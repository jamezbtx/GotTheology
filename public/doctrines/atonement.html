Doctrines

Doctrine

Atonement

Christ died as a real sacrifice for sin—both camps preach a cross of infinite worth; they part over whether the Father intended that death for every person or for the elect in particular.

Atonement is the work by which the death of Jesus deals with guilt. Scripture calls him the Lamb who takes away the sin of the world, a ransom, a propitiation, the one who lays down his life for the sheep. Arminian and Reformed teachers agree the cross is substitutionary in some real sense and that no one is saved apart from it. They disagree about the design: a sufficient provision for all, applied to believers, or a particular redemption that infallibly secures a people.

Arminian

Arminian teaching starts from the texts that sound universal: Christ tasted death for every man; he is the propitiation for the sins of the whole world; God wants all to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth. The Remonstrance holds both halves together: the Son obtained redemption for all, and no one enjoys it except the believer.

Watson’s “remissible” is the key pastoral word. The cross really makes forgiveness available; it does not, by itself, make every person forgiven. Application is by faith—itself the gift of preventing grace, yet resistible. A sincere gospel offer to every hearer is therefore not a bluff.

This is not a thin “possibility” that leaves the cross uncertain. Classical Arminians preach a finished sacrifice of infinite worth. What they refuse is a design that, from eternity, withholds that sacrifice’s saving intent from the non-elect. The fork is intent and application, not whether Jesus’ blood is enough.

Reformed

Reformed teaching often grants the infinite sufficiency of the death (Dort II.3) and then asks a further question: what did the Father actually intend the death to accomplish? If Christ made a satisfaction for all in the same sense, they argue, all would be saved. Particular redemption says the cross did not merely make salvation possible; it secured a people.

“For the sheep,” “for the church,” “those you gave me” are read as naming the intended beneficiaries. Faith itself is purchased and bestowed, so the atonement cannot finally fail for those it was designed to save. Spurgeon’s bridge image is rhetorical, but the claim is doctrinal: an atonement limited in extent, unlimited in effect.

Pastoral Reformed preaching still calls all people to Christ. Many Reformed writers distinguish the sufficient worth of the death, the free offer of the gospel, and the particular design of the covenant. Fair reading keeps those distinctions in view rather than hearing only “Christ did not die for you.”

Key contrast Arminians typically teach a universal provision (every person’s sins remissible) applied by resistible, enabling grace through faith; the Reformed typically teach a particular design that infallibly obtains faith and salvation for the elect, while still confessing the infinite worth of the death and a real gospel offer.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the cross—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside the texts.

Arminian Notable voices

“Jesus Christ, the Saviour of the world, died for all men and for every man, so that he has obtained for them all, by his death on the cross, redemption and the forgiveness of sins; yet that no one actually enjoys this forgiveness of sins except the believer.”
The Remonstrance · Article II (1610)
“When, therefore, it is said that Christ by the grace of God tasted death for every man, and that he is the propitiation for our sins, and not for ours only, but also for the sins of the whole world, it can only, we think, be fairly concluded… that, by the death of Christ, the sins of every man are rendered remissible, and that salvation is consequently attainable by every man.”
Richard Watson · Theological Institutes, Part II, ch. 25
“How freely does God love the world! While we were yet sinners, “Christ died for the ungodly.” While we were dead in our sin, God “spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all.”… The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)

Reformed Notable voices

“The death of the Son of God is the only and most perfect sacrifice and satisfaction for sin; and is of infinite worth and value, abundantly sufficient to expiate the sins of the whole world.”
Canons of Dort · Second Head, Article 3
“This was the sovereign counsel and most gracious will and purpose of God the Father, that the quickening and saving efficacy of the most precious death of his Son should extend to all the elect, for bestowing upon them alone the gift of justifying faith, thereby to bring them infallibly to salvation.”
Canons of Dort · Second Head, Article 8
“We are often told that we limit the atonement of Christ, because we say that Christ has not made a satisfaction for all men, or all men would be saved. Now, our reply to this is, that, on the other hand, our opponents limit it: we do not.… We say Christ so died that He infallibly secured the salvation of a multitude that no man can number, who through Christ’s death not only may be saved, but are saved, must be saved, and cannot by any possibility run the hazard of being anything but saved.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · Sermon 181, “Particular Redemption” (1858)
