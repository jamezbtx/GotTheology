Passage

2 Thessalonians 1

Verses 5–12 · WEB

Paul places the church’s affliction within God’s righteous judgment: Christ will bring relief to his people and judgment upon evil. He then prays that divine power will make their calling fruitful. Both traditions distinguish grace-enabled worthiness from earning salvation through suffering and direct hope toward Christ’s glory.

2 Thessalonians 1:5

5This is an obvious sign of the righteous judgment of God, to the end that you may be counted worthy of God’s Kingdom, for which you also suffer.

Arminian

Suffering for the kingdom does not purchase admission to it. Arminian readers understand enduring faith as fitting those sustained by grace for God’s coming vindication.

Reformed

Reformed readers see endurance as evidence of grace and of God’s righteous verdict, not merit earning the kingdom. Both distinguish being counted worthy from buying salvation through suffering.

2 Thessalonians 1:6

6For it is a righteous thing with God to repay affliction to those who afflict you,

Arminian

God takes the wrongs done to his people seriously. Arminian readers entrust justice to him rather than making persecution an excuse for personal vengeance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise ground hope in God’s righteous judgment. The promise of repayment releases believers from the demand to settle every wrong themselves.

2 Thessalonians 1:7

7and to give relief to you who are afflicted with us when the Lord Jesus is revealed from heaven with his mighty angels in flaming fire,

Arminian

The promised relief is tied to Jesus’ revelation, not a guarantee of immediate comfort. Arminian readers find courage to endure through hope in his return.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s certain coming to vindicate his afflicted people. Both traditions can wait for relief without denying the present reality of suffering.

2 Thessalonians 1:8

8punishing those who don’t know God, and to those who don’t obey the Good News of our Lord Jesus,

Arminian

The verse connects judgment with refusing God and the gospel. Arminian readers emphasize accountable unbelief and the urgency of responding to God’s gracious summons.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm responsibility for refusing the gospel. Divine sovereignty does not make disobedience innocent or diminish the seriousness of Christ’s judgment.

2 Thessalonians 1:9

9who will pay the penalty: eternal destruction from the face of the Lord and from the glory of his might,

Arminian

The warning presents judgment as a dreadful, lasting loss before the Lord’s glory. Arminian readers urge repentance rather than treating salvation as something people can safely neglect.

Reformed

Reformed readers also understand this as a solemn warning of final judgment. Its pastoral force is to direct people to Christ, not to encourage speculation or delight in others’ ruin.

2 Thessalonians 1:10

10when he comes in that day to be glorified in his saints and to be admired among all those who have believed, because our testimony to you was believed.

Arminian

Christ will be admired among those who believed the apostolic testimony. Arminian readers emphasize faith’s reception of grace, with all glory belonging to the Savior.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s saving work displayed in his people at his return. Both recognize the preached testimony and genuine belief as central, rather than salvation apart from faith.

2 Thessalonians 1:11

11To this end we also pray always for you that our God may count you worthy of your calling, and fulfill every desire of goodness and work of faith with power,

Arminian

Paul prays for God to empower good desires and works of faith. Arminian readers see active obedience depending throughout on grace, not on unaided moral effort.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God supplying the power that makes faith fruitful. Prayer is a fitting means of seeking this work, not a substitute for the obedience Paul desires.

2 Thessalonians 1:12

12that the name of our Lord Jesus may be glorified in you, and you in him, according to the grace of our God and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

The goal is Christ’s honor in believers and their participation in his glory. Arminian readers stress that even fruitful human response remains dependent on divine grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise locate both present holiness and future glory in grace. The church’s calling is to display Christ, not to claim the credit for its endurance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
