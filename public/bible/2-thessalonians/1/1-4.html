Passage

2 Thessalonians 1

Verses 1–4 · WEB

Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy greet a church whose faith and mutual love are growing amid persecution. Thanksgiving credits God rather than congratulating self-sufficiency. Both traditions recognize grace bearing fruit in endurance; this opening prepares for the assurance of righteous judgment and relief that follows.

2 Thessalonians 1:1

1Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy, to the assembly of the Thessalonians in God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ:

Arminian

The assembly belongs to the Father and the Lord Jesus, not to its persecutors. Arminian readers see a gracious relationship in which believers are called to continue in trust.

Reformed

The assembly’s identity rests in the Father and the Son. Reformed readers emphasize God’s sustaining care for his people; both traditions find comfort in this shared belonging.

2 Thessalonians 1:2

2Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Grace and peace come from God, not from human achievement. Arminian readers receive this greeting as provision for a life of dependent faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see grace as the source of peace. The greeting directs a pressured church toward the Father and Christ rather than self-reliance.

2 Thessalonians 1:3

3We are bound to always give thanks to God for you, brothers, even as it is appropriate, because your faith grows exceedingly, and the love of each and every one of you toward one another abounds,

Arminian

Growing faith and mutual love are reasons to thank God. Arminian readers see grace bearing fruit through believers’ continuing response, never a reason for self-congratulation.

Reformed

Paul credits God for the church’s growth. Reformed readers see divine grace producing active faith and love, sharing the conviction that spiritual maturity should bless others.

2 Thessalonians 1:4

4so that we ourselves boast about you in the assemblies of God for your perseverance and faith in all your persecutions and in the afflictions which you endure.

Arminian

Perseverance under affliction is living trust rather than mere survival. Arminian readers commend this grace-enabled endurance while urging believers to keep trusting Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see endurance as evidence of God’s preserving work. Like Arminian readers, they honor faithful suffering without treating pain itself as a spiritual achievement.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
