Passage

Deuteronomy 4

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Chapter 4 turns from history to exhortation. Moses urges Israel to hear and keep God’s statutes as their wisdom before the nations, warning them from Baal Peor and grounding everything in the day at Horeb when they heard God’s voice but saw no form.

Deuteronomy 4:1

1Now, Israel, listen to the statutes and to the ordinances which I teach you, to do them, that you may live and go in and possess the land which the LORD, the God of your fathers, gives you.

Arminian

Listen, do, live, and possess are linked. Arminian readers stress that the covenant promises are genuinely conditioned on obedient response, which is enabled by God’s grace but not automatic.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see obedience here as the way of life within a covenant already established by grace, not the means of earning it. They frequently connect this with the law’s third use as a guide for the redeemed.

Deuteronomy 4:2

2You shall not add to the word which I command you, neither shall you take away from it, that you may keep the commandments of the LORD your God which I command you.

Arminian

Neither adding nor subtracting guards the integrity of God’s word. Arminian readers, in the Wesleyan tradition, stress Scripture as the sufficient rule of faith and practice that all may read and obey.

Reformed

Many Reformed readers draw on this verse, among others, for the regulative principle of worship (compare Westminster Confession 21.1), though Reformed churches apply it with varying strictness. Other Protestants generally read the verse as guarding the sufficiency of God’s word without that specific application.

Deuteronomy 4:3

3Your eyes have seen what the LORD did because of Baal Peor; for the LORD your God has destroyed all the men who followed Baal Peor from among you.

Arminian

Baal Peor showed that the covenant people could perish through idolatry. Arminian readers treat the episode as a real warning that belonging to God’s people does not guarantee persevering faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers read Baal Peor as a sobering example of judgment within the visible covenant community. They often distinguish outward membership in Israel from the inward faithfulness that verse 4 describes.

Deuteronomy 4:4

4But you who were faithful to the LORD your God are all alive today.

Arminian

Those who held fast to the LORD are alive. Arminian readers note that the difference between life and death followed a real choice made by each person in the face of temptation.

Reformed

The word translated faithful suggests clinging to God. Reformed readers often see such clinging as the fruit of God’s grace, while still fully the people’s own act.

Deuteronomy 4:5

5Behold, I have taught you statutes and ordinances, even as the LORD my God commanded me, that you should do so in the middle of the land where you go in to possess it.

Arminian

Moses teaches only what God commanded him, modeling faithful transmission. Arminian readers emphasize that God’s commands are given to be understood and done by ordinary people in ordinary life.

Reformed

Moses’ authority is derivative: as the LORD my God commanded me. Reformed readers stress the mediated character of revelation and the obligation of teachers to deliver only what God has spoken.

Deuteronomy 4:6

6Keep therefore and do them; for this is your wisdom and your understanding in the sight of the peoples who shall hear all these statutes and say, “Surely this great nation is a wise and understanding people.”

Arminian

Obedience becomes a public witness. Arminian readers often stress that Israel’s faithfulness was meant to draw the nations, reflecting God’s concern for all peoples. Wisdom here is practical and visible.

Reformed

The law’s wisdom is visible to outsiders. Reformed readers often connect this with the goodness of God’s moral law, which reflects his character and commends itself even to those outside the covenant.

Deuteronomy 4:7

7For what great nation is there that has a god so near to them as the LORD our God is whenever we call on him?

Arminian

A God near whenever we call is a God who hears and responds. Arminian readers find in this the relational heart of the covenant and a ground for confident, persistent prayer.

Reformed

God’s nearness is his gift, not Israel’s achievement. Reformed readers see it fulfilled supremely in Christ, through whom God’s people draw near (Hebrews 4:16). Nearness is covenantal.

Deuteronomy 4:8

8What great nation is there that has statutes and ordinances so righteous as all this law which I set before you today?

Arminian

The righteousness of the statutes is a privilege that increases responsibility. Arminian readers note that greater light brings greater accountability, a theme common in Wesleyan teaching.

Reformed

Israel’s laws are called righteous, a claim Reformed readers often connect with Romans 7:12, where the law is holy, righteous, and good, while recognizing that the law cannot itself give life.

Deuteronomy 4:9

9Only be careful, and keep your soul diligently, lest you forget the things which your eyes saw, and lest they depart from your heart all the days of your life; but make them known to your children and your children’s children—

Arminian

Keep your soul diligently warns against forgetting. Arminian readers see spiritual decline as a real danger that believers must guard against through watchful memory and teaching.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the means God uses to preserve faith across generations, especially teaching children. They often connect this with covenant nurture in the home and church.

Deuteronomy 4:10

10the day that you stood before the LORD your God in Horeb, when the LORD said to me, “Assemble the people to me, and I will make them hear my words, that they may learn to fear me all the days that they live on the earth, and that they may teach their children.”

Arminian

Horeb was meant to teach fear of the LORD for a lifetime. Arminian readers stress that such fear is learned and must be cultivated freely in each person and passed on.

Reformed

God assembles the people to hear his words. Reformed readers highlight God’s initiative in gathering and teaching his people, with the word as the central means of grace.

Deuteronomy 4:11

11You came near and stood under the mountain. The mountain burned with fire to the heart of the sky, with darkness, cloud, and thick darkness.

Arminian

The burning mountain with darkness and cloud displays both nearness and hiddenness. Arminian readers note that God draws Israel near while guarding his holiness. Approach is real but bounded.

Reformed

The fire and thick darkness express God’s majesty and transcendence. Reformed readers often connect this scene with Hebrews 12:18-24, contrasting Sinai with the gathering at Mount Zion.

Deuteronomy 4:12

12The LORD spoke to you out of the middle of the fire: you heard the voice of words, but you saw no form; you only heard a voice.

Arminian

Israel heard a voice but saw no form. Arminian readers stress that God’s chosen means of relating to Israel was speech that calls for understanding and response.

Reformed

The absence of form grounds the prohibition of images that follows. Reformed readers often see the priority of hearing over seeing as basic to biblical worship.

Deuteronomy 4:13

13He declared to you his covenant, which he commanded you to perform, even the ten commandments. He wrote them on two stone tablets.

Arminian

The covenant is something Israel was commanded to perform. Arminian readers stress the covenant’s mutual commitments and the real obligations it placed on Israel. Duties flow from relationship.

Reformed

God declares his covenant and writes the commandments himself. Reformed readers often see the Ten Commandments as a summary of the moral law that continues to guide believers.

Deuteronomy 4:14

14The LORD commanded me at that time to teach you statutes and ordinances, that you might do them in the land where you go over to possess it.

Arminian

Moses is charged to teach, not just to repeat. Arminian readers note the pastoral task of helping people understand and apply God’s word in new settings, such as the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish the Ten Words, written by God, from the further statutes Moses taught for life in the land, a distinction many use in discussing moral, ceremonial, and civil law.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
