Passage

Deuteronomy 4

Verses 15–31 · WEB

Because Israel saw no form at Horeb, they must make no image of God and must not worship the heavenly bodies. Moses warns of exile if they turn to idols, yet promises that those who seek the LORD from exile with all their heart will find a merciful God who remembers his covenant.

Deuteronomy 4:15

15Be very careful, for you saw no kind of form on the day that the LORD spoke to you in Horeb out of the middle of the fire,

Arminian

The warning rests on what Israel actually experienced at Horeb: a voice and no shape. Wesleyan readers stress that God reveals himself in ways that call for a listening, obedient heart, and that guarding this memory is the people’s own watchful task.

Reformed

Since God showed no form, any image claims to know what he withheld. Reformed churches commonly read this verse with the second commandment, as the Heidelberg Catechism does in its questions on images (Questions 96-98), treating worship through images as forbidden.

Deuteronomy 4:16

16lest you corrupt yourselves, and make yourself a carved image in the form of any figure, the likeness of male or female,

Arminian

Moses calls image-making a way of corrupting yourselves. Arminian readers often emphasize that idolatry harms the worshiper, bending desire toward what can be controlled, and that people remain responsible to refuse it.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that an image does not merely add a symbol to worship but misrepresents God. Calvin argued along these lines in his treatment of images in the Institutes (Book 1, chapter 11), and many Reformed writers follow him.

Deuteronomy 4:17

17the likeness of any animal that is on the earth, the likeness of any winged bird that flies in the sky,

Arminian

Land animals and birds are named one by one. Arminian readers note that even the most impressive creatures are fellow creatures, and that honoring them as gods betrays the relationship God offers.

Reformed

The sequence of earth, sky, and water creatures echoes the creation account. Reformed readers often hear Romans 1:23 behind it, where people exchange the glory of the incorruptible God for images of creatures.

Deuteronomy 4:18

18the likeness of anything that creeps on the ground, the likeness of any fish that is in the water under the earth;

Arminian

By naming even creeping things and fish, Moses closes every loophole. Wesleyan preaching often presses this comprehensiveness: nothing in our lives may quietly take the place that belongs to God alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the Creator-creature distinction that runs through their theology. Because God made everything listed, nothing in the list can represent him without lowering him to its level.

Deuteronomy 4:19

19and lest you lift up your eyes to the sky, and when you see the sun and the moon and the stars, even all the army of the sky, you are drawn away and worship them, and serve them, which the LORD your God has allotted to all the peoples under the whole sky.

Arminian

The danger of being drawn away by the heavens shows that idolatry often begins with the eyes and with admiration. Arminian readers stress that resisting such drift is a real choice Israel must keep making.

Reformed

The verse says God allotted the sun, moon, and stars to all peoples. Interpreters differ on whether this means the lights were given for all nations’ use or that the nations were left to their worship under God’s providence. Reformed readers commonly hold that providence over the nations does not mean approval.

Deuteronomy 4:20

20But the LORD has taken you, and brought you out of the iron furnace, out of Egypt, to be to him a people of inheritance, as it is today.

Arminian

Brought out of the iron furnace, Israel was rescued before it was commanded. Arminian readers emphasize that redemption is the motive for obedience and that God desires a people who answer his love with loyal love.

Reformed

A people of inheritance is God’s own possession by his redeeming act. Reformed readers see this as a picture of grace that owns and keeps its people, later applied to the church in passages like 1 Peter 2:9.

Deuteronomy 4:21

21Furthermore the LORD was angry with me for your sakes, and swore that I should not go over the Jordan, and that I should not go in to that good land which the LORD your God gives you for an inheritance;

Arminian

Moses repeats that God was angry with him for your sakes. Arminian readers note that he uses his own loss as a pastoral warning, showing that even the most faithful servant’s failures carry real consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that Moses’ discipline becomes instruction for the people. God’s dealings with one servant serve the good of the whole community, a pattern they often see in God’s providence.

Deuteronomy 4:22

22but I must die in this land. I must not go over the Jordan, but you shall go over and possess that good land.

Arminian

Moses accepts dying outside the land while urging others to enter. Arminian readers find here a model of humble service that continues to seek others’ good even after personal hopes are denied.

Reformed

I must die in this land, but you shall go over: the contrast shows that God’s promise does not depend on any single leader. Reformed readers see God’s purposes carried forward beyond the life of his servant.

Deuteronomy 4:23

23Be careful, lest you forget the covenant of the LORD your God, which he made with you, and make yourselves a carved image in the form of anything which the LORD your God has forbidden you.

Arminian

Forgetting the covenant is the first step toward making an image. Arminian readers emphasize memory as an active, chosen discipline, sustained by grace, that keeps believers from drifting.

Reformed

Reformed readers link forgetting the covenant with forgetting the God who made it with you. They often stress the covenant’s gracious origin, which makes idolatry not only disobedience but ingratitude.

Deuteronomy 4:24

24For the LORD your God is a devouring fire, a jealous God.

Arminian

God’s jealousy is the passion of covenant love that refuses rivals. Arminian readers stress that this love is real and personal, and that God’s holiness is not opposed to his goodness toward all.

Reformed

Hebrews 12:29 takes up this verse for the church. Reformed readers emphasize God’s holiness and exclusive claim, which call for reverence and awe in worship under the new covenant as under the old.

Deuteronomy 4:25

25When you father children and children’s children, and you have been long in the land, and then corrupt yourselves, and make a carved image in the form of anything, and do that which is evil in the LORD your God’s sight to provoke him to anger,

Arminian

The warning looks to generations long settled in the land. Arminian readers stress that prosperity and time do not secure faithfulness; each generation must choose afresh to serve the LORD.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the verse as anticipating Israel’s later history of idolatry. They often note that God’s foreknowledge of the apostasy stands alongside the genuine warning and Israel’s full responsibility.

Deuteronomy 4:26

26I call heaven and earth to witness against you today, that you will soon utterly perish from off the land which you go over the Jordan to possess it. You will not prolong your days on it, but will utterly be destroyed.

Arminian

Calling heaven and earth as witnesses frames the covenant like a solemn legal agreement. Arminian readers stress that the threatened loss of the land is real and depends on Israel’s response.

Reformed

Reformed readers take the covenant sanctions with full seriousness while noting that the same chapter promises mercy after judgment (4:29-31). Judgment is real, but it is not God’s last word for his people.

Deuteronomy 4:27

27The LORD will scatter you among the peoples, and you will be left few in number among the nations where the LORD will lead you away.

Arminian

Exile is presented as a consequence God will bring if Israel chooses idols. Arminian readers note the responsive character of God’s judgments, which follow the people’s persistent rebellion.

Reformed

The LORD scatters and the LORD leads away: even exile is in God’s hand. Reformed readers stress that judgment falls within his providence and purpose, never outside his control.

Deuteronomy 4:28

28There you will serve gods, the work of men’s hands, wood and stone, which neither see, nor hear, nor eat, nor smell.

Arminian

Those who chose idols are left to serve them. Arminian readers note the moral irony: God allows people to experience the emptiness of what they preferred, often as a summons to return.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect the description of senseless gods with Psalm 115 and Isaiah 44, where idolaters become like what they worship. Judgment hands people over to their chosen gods.

Deuteronomy 4:29

29But from there you shall seek the LORD your God, and you will find him when you search after him with all your heart and with all your soul.

Arminian

The promise that seekers will find God is open to scattered Israel without restriction. Arminian readers stress that wholehearted seeking is a genuine response that people, enabled by grace, can and must make.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the promise as real while often seeing wholehearted seeking as itself a fruit of God’s work, connecting this verse with 30:6, where the LORD circumcises the heart so that Israel may love him.

Deuteronomy 4:30

30When you are in oppression, and all these things have come on you, in the latter days you shall return to the LORD your God and listen to his voice.

Arminian

Return and listen describe repentance as a real human turning back to God. Arminian readers stress that God welcomes this turning and that oppression can become the occasion of it.

Reformed

In the latter days points to restoration within God’s plan. Reformed readers often link it with the renewal promised in Deuteronomy 30 and the new covenant in Jeremiah 31, where God himself writes his law on the heart.

Deuteronomy 4:31

31For the LORD your God is a merciful God. He will not fail you nor destroy you, nor forget the covenant of your fathers which he swore to them.

Arminian

God is merciful and will not fail you. Arminian readers emphasize God’s steady readiness to receive returning sinners, a mercy that reflects his love for all and his patience with the unfaithful.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that hope rests on the oath God swore to the fathers. Israel’s restoration depends finally on God’s covenant faithfulness rather than on the strength of Israel’s repentance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
