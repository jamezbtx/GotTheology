Passage

Deuteronomy 4

Verses 32–40 · WEB

Moses invites Israel to search all history for anything like their experience: a people who heard God speak from fire and lived, and a nation God took for himself out of another. He grounds this in God’s love for the patriarchs and calls Israel to know that the LORD alone is God and to obey.

Deuteronomy 4:32

32For ask now of the days that are past, which were before you, since the day that God created man on the earth, and from the one end of the sky to the other, whether there has been anything as great as this thing is, or has been heard like it?

Arminian

Moses invites Israel to search history and the whole sky for a parallel. Arminian readers note that faith is not blind; God provides evidence to which people can reasonably respond.

Reformed

The appeal to creation and all history sets Israel’s story within God’s universal rule. Reformed readers see in Israel’s uniqueness the mark of God’s free and singular purpose.

Deuteronomy 4:33

33Did a people ever hear the voice of God speaking out of the middle of the fire, as you have heard, and live?

Arminian

To hear God from the fire and live is presented as unheard of. Arminian readers stress that God made himself known in order to be received in trust and obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that sinners survived God’s voice only by his merciful condescension. The verse prepares for Israel’s request for a mediator in 5:24-27. Fear and grace meet here.

Deuteronomy 4:34

34Or has God tried to go and take a nation for himself from among another nation, by trials, by signs, by wonders, by war, by a mighty hand, by an outstretched arm, and by great terrors, according to all that the LORD your God did for you in Egypt before your eyes?

Arminian

God’s trials, signs, and mighty hand were aimed at taking a nation for himself. Arminian readers stress that God’s mighty acts open a relationship Israel was called to accept and live out.

Reformed

The exodus is a paradigm of redemption: God acts in power to claim a people from bondage. Reformed readers see this as a pattern of salvation fulfilled in Christ’s redeeming work.

Deuteronomy 4:35

35It was shown to you so that you might know that the LORD is God. There is no one else besides him.

Arminian

The goal of these acts was knowledge: the LORD is God, and there is no other. Arminian readers stress that revelation aims at understanding and willing trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the confession of one God as foundational, and the fact that Israel knew it because it was shown to them, not discovered by them.

Deuteronomy 4:36

36Out of heaven he made you to hear his voice, that he might instruct you. On earth he made you to see his great fire; and you heard his words out of the middle of the fire.

Arminian

God spoke from heaven that he might instruct. Arminian readers stress his desire to teach and form his people, addressing their minds and hearts. Instruction implies a learner.

Reformed

Heaven and earth are joined in God’s self-disclosure: the voice from above and the fire below. Reformed readers often see in this a pattern that reaches its fullness when the Word becomes flesh.

Deuteronomy 4:37

37Because he loved your fathers, therefore he chose their offspring after them, and brought you out with his presence, with his great power, out of Egypt;

Arminian

Arminian readers typically read this as God’s love for the patriarchs leading to the choice of their offspring as a nation, an election to covenant privilege and vocation in history. Many point to Baal Peor (4:3), where members of the chosen nation perished, as showing that this election did not guarantee each individual’s salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm that the election here is national, yet they stress that it springs from God’s free love rather than from anything in Israel, as 7:7-8 makes explicit. Many see this unconditioned love as a pattern that also illuminates God’s choice of individuals to salvation.

Deuteronomy 4:38

38to drive out nations from before you greater and mightier than you, to bring you in, to give you their land for an inheritance, as it is today.

Arminian

God drives out greater nations to bring Israel in. Arminian readers note that the gift of the land came with ongoing conditions, as the surrounding warnings make clear.

Reformed

The nations were greater and mightier than Israel. Reformed readers stress that the gift was undeserved, a point Moses presses later when he denies that Israel received the land for its righteousness (9:4-6).

Deuteronomy 4:39

39Know therefore today, and take it to heart, that the LORD himself is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath. There is no one else.

Arminian

Know and take it to heart: belief must move from the head to the heart. Wesleyan readers stress heartfelt conviction that shapes daily life, not just correct ideas.

Reformed

The LORD himself is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath. Reformed readers stress God’s sovereign rule over all places, which leaves no room for rival powers in either realm.

Deuteronomy 4:40

40You shall keep his statutes and his commandments which I command you today, that it may go well with you and with your children after you, and that you may prolong your days in the land which the LORD your God gives you for all time.

Arminian

Obedience and well-being are linked for Israel and its children. Arminian readers emphasize the conditional nature of covenant blessing and the real stakes of each generation’s response.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the call to keep the statutes as the grateful response of a redeemed people, through which God gives blessing within a covenant already founded on grace.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
