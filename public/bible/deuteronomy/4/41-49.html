Passage

Deuteronomy 4

Verses 41–49 · WEB

A short narrative notice records Moses setting apart three cities of refuge east of the Jordan, followed by a heading (4:44-49) that introduces the law Moses sets before Israel in the following chapters.

Deuteronomy 4:41

41Then Moses set apart three cities beyond the Jordan toward the sunrise,

Arminian

Moses provides cities of refuge east of the Jordan before he dies. Arminian readers see in this God’s concern that justice protect the innocent, and they note Moses acting on commands already given (Numbers 35).

Reformed

The setting apart of refuge cities shows God’s law providing for justice tempered with mercy. Reformed readers note Moses’ faithful completion of a task God had assigned.

Deuteronomy 4:42

42that the man slayer might flee there, who kills his neighbor unintentionally and didn’t hate him in time past, and that fleeing to one of these cities he might live:

Arminian

The law distinguishes unintentional killing from malice. Arminian readers stress that intent and heart matter to God, a theme they connect with God’s justice toward all people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s law distinguishes degrees of guilt. Many Christian interpreters in both traditions see refuge cities as a picture of fleeing to Christ for refuge (compare Hebrews 6:18), though the typology is not explicit.

Deuteronomy 4:43

43Bezer in the wilderness, in the plain country, for the Reubenites; and Ramoth in Gilead for the Gadites; and Golan in Bashan for the Manassites.

Arminian

Bezer, Ramoth, and Golan serve the three eastern tribes. Arminian readers note that refuge is made accessible to all regions, reflecting a God who makes mercy reachable.

Reformed

One city for each eastern region shows care in God’s provision. Reformed readers see the distribution as part of the law’s ordered concern for justice throughout the land.

Deuteronomy 4:44

44This is the law which Moses set before the children of Israel.

Arminian

This is the law introduces the speech that follows. Arminian readers note that the law is set before the people, offered for their response rather than imposed without address.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this heading as introducing the covenant stipulations of chapters 5-26, given to a people already redeemed from Egypt. The law follows redemption in the book’s order.

Deuteronomy 4:45

45These are the testimonies, and the statutes, and the ordinances which Moses spoke to the children of Israel when they came out of Egypt,

Arminian

Testimonies, statutes, and ordinances together describe the breadth of God’s instruction. Arminian readers stress that the law covers all of life, inviting holiness in every area.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that these terms are often used in Scripture for God’s whole revealed will. They see the law as a comprehensive guide for the covenant people.

Deuteronomy 4:46

46beyond the Jordan, in the valley opposite Beth Peor, in the land of Sihon king of the Amorites, who lived at Heshbon, whom Moses and the children of Israel struck when they came out of Egypt.

Arminian

The setting near Beth Peor recalls both victory over Sihon and the sin at Baal Peor. For Arminian readers, receiving the law in a place that recalls both grace and danger keeps the choice before Israel vivid.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that the law is given after victory, so that obedience responds to deliverance. The location ties the law to God’s acts on Israel’s behalf.

Deuteronomy 4:47

47They took possession of his land and the land of Og king of Bashan, the two kings of the Amorites, who were beyond the Jordan toward the sunrise;

Arminian

The defeat of Sihon and Og is again remembered. Arminian readers stress that recalling God’s past deeds strengthens trust for the challenges ahead in Canaan.

Reformed

Reformed readers note how often Scripture repeats these two victories. The repetition keeps God’s faithfulness before his people. Remembering them also reassures a new generation that God fights for Israel, as 3:22 promised.

Deuteronomy 4:48

48from Aroer, which is on the edge of the valley of the Arnon, even to Mount Siyon (also called Hermon),

Arminian

The WEB prints Mount Siyon, also called Hermon, a name distinct from Zion in Jerusalem. Arminian readers note the care for accurate geography in recording God’s gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the territory extends to Hermon, matching 3:8. The boundaries mark the full extent of what God gave east of the Jordan.

Deuteronomy 4:49

49and all the Arabah beyond the Jordan eastward, even to the sea of the Arabah, under the slopes of Pisgah.

Arminian

The Arabah and the slopes of Pisgah close the description. Arminian readers note the poignancy, since Pisgah is where Moses will see the land he cannot enter.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the geographical frame returns to Pisgah, linking the law’s introduction to Moses’ coming death and the transfer of leadership. The chapter ends where 3:27 pointed.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
