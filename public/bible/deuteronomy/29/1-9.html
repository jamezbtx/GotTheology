Passage

Deuteronomy 29

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Deuteronomy 29 opens a covenant renewal in the land of Moab, a covenant made in addition to the one at Horeb, as Moses’ long address nears its end. In the Hebrew text, WEB 29:1 is numbered 28:69, closing the previous chapter, so the Hebrew verse numbers in chapter 29 run one lower than the English ones. Moses reminds Israel of the wonders in Egypt, the forty years in which their clothes and sandals did not wear out, and the victories over Sihon and Og, yet he says that the LORD has not given them a heart to know, eyes to see, and ears to hear, to this day. Arminian and Reformed readers commonly agree that God’s faithfulness was plain, that Israel was responsible for its dullness, and that true understanding is God’s gift; they differ in how they explain verse 4, which touches the question of enabling grace.

Deuteronomy 29:1

1These are the words of the covenant which the LORD commanded Moses to make with the children of Israel in the land of Moab, in addition to the covenant which he made with them in Horeb.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the covenant in Moab as God renewing his relationship with a new generation on the edge of the land. The covenant at Horeb is not replaced but restated and applied.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Moab covenant as a renewal of the one covenant relationship God established with Israel. Readers in both traditions commonly see the verse as a heading for the renewal that follows, and the Hebrew numbering (28:69) reflects a different chapter division, not a different text.

Deuteronomy 29:2

2Moses called to all Israel, and said to them: Your eyes have seen all that the LORD did in the land of Egypt to Pharaoh, and to all his servants, and to all his land;

Arminian

Arminian readers note that Moses appeals to what the people saw with their own eyes in Egypt. God’s acts are public evidence that calls for trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the recital of God’s works as the foundation of covenant obligation. Both traditions commonly read the verse as grounding obedience in what God has already done.

Deuteronomy 29:3

3the great trials which your eyes saw, the signs, and those great wonders.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the great trials, signs and wonders as abundant evidence of God’s power and care. The people had every reason to believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the signs as displays of God’s glory before Israel and Egypt alike. Readers in both traditions commonly see the verse as preparing for the striking contrast in verse 4.

Deuteronomy 29:4

4But the LORD has not given you a heart to know, eyes to see, and ears to hear, to this day.

Arminian

Arminian readers commonly read this as an honest description of Israel’s spiritual dullness despite all they had seen, a condition for which they were responsible (compare Matthew 13:15). Many understand God’s gift of understanding as offered and enabling, but not yet received in fullness by a people who resisted it.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly read the verse as showing that spiritual understanding comes only as God gives it, which Paul echoes when he speaks of Israel’s eyes and ears (Romans 11:8). They hold that Israel was still fully responsible for its unbelief, while the remedy lay in God’s gift of a new heart (30:6).

Deuteronomy 29:5

5I have led you forty years in the wilderness. Your clothes have not grown old on you, and your sandals have not grown old on your feet.

Arminian

Arminian readers see God’s care in the wilderness, where clothes and sandals did not wear out, as tender provision (compare Deuteronomy 8:4). Daily needs were met by God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the forty years as a long lesson in God’s faithful providence. Both traditions commonly see the verse as calling for trust in the God who sustained his people.

Deuteronomy 29:6

6You have not eaten bread, neither have you drunk wine or strong drink, that you may know that I am the LORD your God.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the lack of ordinary bread and wine was meant to teach Israel to know the LORD as their God. Hardship can be a means of learning dependence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God providing in ways that drew attention to himself, a lesson also taught in Deuteronomy 8:3 and quoted by Jesus (Matthew 4:4). Readers in both traditions commonly see the purpose of knowing God as central.

Deuteronomy 29:7

7When you came to this place, Sihon the king of Heshbon and Og the king of Bashan came out against us to battle, and we struck them.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the victories over Sihon and Og as further evidence of God’s faithfulness on the journey (compare Numbers 21:24, 33). God fought for his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see these victories as fulfillments of God’s promises. Both traditions commonly read the verse as part of the recital that calls Israel to trust and obey.

Deuteronomy 29:8

8We took their land, and gave it for an inheritance to the Reubenites, and to the Gadites, and to the half-tribe of the Manassites.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the land east of the Jordan was given as an inheritance to Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh. God’s promises were already being fulfilled.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the allotment as a pledge of the fuller inheritance to come. Readers in both traditions commonly see the verse as encouraging confidence in God’s word.

Deuteronomy 29:9

9Therefore keep the words of this covenant and do them, that you may prosper in all that you do.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the call to keep the covenant as the fitting response to all God has done. Obedience and blessing belong together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the exhortation as resting on grace already shown, with blessing as God’s gift in the way of faithfulness. Both traditions commonly read the verse as summarizing the purpose of the recital.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
