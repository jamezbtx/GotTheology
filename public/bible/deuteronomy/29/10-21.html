Passage

Deuteronomy 29

Verses 10–21 · WEB

Moses gathers the whole community, from leaders to children, wives, foreigners, woodcutters and water carriers, to enter the covenant and oath that the LORD makes with them that day. The covenant extends also to those not present, generally understood as future generations. Moses then warns against anyone whose heart turns to idols and who imagines he will have peace while walking in the stubbornness of his heart; such a person faces the covenant curses. Readers in both traditions commonly see here the breadth of God’s covenant, the danger of presumption, and the seriousness of idolatry. The warnings concern Israel’s covenant life, and both traditions generally apply them to believers by analogy rather than reading them as a full doctrine of salvation.

Deuteronomy 29:10

10All of you stand today in the presence of the LORD your God: your heads, your tribes, your elders, and your officers, even all the men of Israel,

Arminian

Arminian readers see the whole community standing before the LORD as each person being personally addressed. No one is too important or too insignificant to be included.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant gathering as an expression of God’s dealings with his people as a body. Readers in both traditions commonly see the verse as highlighting the inclusiveness of the covenant assembly.

Deuteronomy 29:11

11your little ones, your wives, and the foreigners who are in the middle of your camps, from the one who cuts your wood to the one who draws your water,

Arminian

Arminian readers are moved by the inclusion of children, wives and foreigners, down to woodcutters and water carriers. God’s covenant reaches the vulnerable and the marginal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the inclusion of children as part of God’s covenant dealings with families (compare Acts 2:39), a point that informs differing views on baptism within Protestantism. Both traditions commonly treasure the breadth of welcome in this verse.

Deuteronomy 29:12

12that you may enter into the covenant of the LORD your God, and into his oath, which the LORD your God makes with you today,

Arminian

Arminian readers see entering into the covenant and oath as a real, personal commitment. The people are invited to respond.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the LORD as the one who makes the covenant and binds himself by oath. Readers in both traditions commonly see God’s initiative and the people’s response joined in this verse.

Deuteronomy 29:13

13that he may establish you today as his people, and that he may be your God, as he spoke to you and as he swore to your fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob.

Arminian

Arminian readers see God’s purpose to establish Israel as his people and be their God as rooted in his promise to the patriarchs. The relationship is personal and covenantal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant formula as central to God’s saving purposes throughout Scripture. Both traditions commonly see the verse as expressing the heart of the covenant.

Deuteronomy 29:14

14Neither do I make this covenant and this oath with you only,

Arminian

Arminian readers note that Moses makes the covenant not only with those present. Its reach goes beyond one generation, so later hearers are also addressed by this oath.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant as extending across generations. Readers in both traditions commonly read this verse together with the next.

Deuteronomy 29:15

15but with those who stand here with us today before the LORD our God, and also with those who are not here with us today

Arminian

Arminian readers see the inclusion of those not present as future generations who will also be called to respond. Each generation must take up the covenant.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant as embracing descendants yet unborn. Both traditions commonly read those not present as later generations, though some interpreters include absent members of that generation.

Deuteronomy 29:16

16(for you know how we lived in the land of Egypt, and how we came through the middle of the nations through which you passed;

Arminian

Arminian readers note that Moses reminds Israel of their experiences among the nations. Memory helps guard against imitation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the recollection as preparing for the warning against idolatry. Readers in both traditions commonly see the verse as setting the context for what follows.

Deuteronomy 29:17

17and you have seen their abominations and their idols of wood, stone, silver, and gold, which were among them);

Arminian

Arminian readers see the idols of wood, stone, silver and gold as lifeless substitutes for the living God. Israel had seen them firsthand.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the description of idols as abominations as underscoring God’s exclusive claim. Both traditions commonly read the verse as warning against being attracted to what Israel had witnessed.

Deuteronomy 29:18

18lest there should be among you man, woman, family, or tribe whose heart turns away today from the LORD our God, to go to serve the gods of those nations; lest there should be among you a root that produces bitter poison;

Arminian

Arminian readers see the warning against any heart turning away as recognizing a real possibility of apostasy within the covenant community. The image of a root producing bitter poison shows how one person’s defection can spread.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the warning as a means God uses to guard his people, and they note that Hebrews 12:15 echoes the image of a bitter root. Readers in both traditions commonly take the warning seriously, while interpreting its implications for perseverance within their broader frameworks.

Deuteronomy 29:19

19and it happen, when he hears the words of this curse, that he bless himself in his heart, saying, “I shall have peace, though I walk in the stubbornness of my heart,” to destroy the moist with the dry.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the person who blesses himself while walking in stubbornness as presuming on God’s mercy. False security is a real danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the self-deceived heart that claims peace while rejecting God’s ways. Both traditions commonly read the verse as a warning against presumption, with the closing phrase about the moist and the dry generally understood as total destruction, though its exact sense is debated.

Deuteronomy 29:20

20The LORD will not pardon him, but then the LORD’s anger and his jealousy will smoke against that man, and all the curse that is written in this book will fall on him, and the LORD will blot out his name from under the sky.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the stern warning that the LORD will not pardon such a person as reflecting the seriousness of deliberate, persistent rebellion. God’s patience is not license.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s just anger against the presumptuous idolater. Readers in both traditions commonly read the verse as describing judgment on unrepentant rebellion within Israel’s covenant setting.

Deuteronomy 29:21

21The LORD will set him apart for evil out of all the tribes of Israel, according to all the curses of the covenant written in this book of the law.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the individual singled out for judgment as showing personal accountability. The covenant community does not shield the rebel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God distinguishing the guilty person from the tribes for judgment. Both traditions commonly read the verse as showing that covenant membership does not guarantee blessing for the unrepentant.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
