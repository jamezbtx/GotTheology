Passage

Deuteronomy 29

Verses 22–29 · WEB

Moses looks ahead to a time when future generations and foreigners will see the land devastated like Sodom and Gomorrah and ask why the LORD has done this. The answer will be that Israel abandoned the covenant and served other gods, so the LORD uprooted them and thrust them into another land. The chapter closes with a well-known saying: the secret things belong to the LORD, but the things revealed belong to us and our children, that we may do all the words of this law. Arminian and Reformed readers commonly read this as shared ground: God’s judgments are just and explainable, and his people are to focus on obeying what he has revealed. Both traditions generally appeal to verse 29 to encourage humility about God’s hidden purposes and faithfulness to his revealed word.

Deuteronomy 29:22

22The generation to come—your children who will rise up after you, and the foreigner who will come from a far land—will say, when they see the plagues of that land, and the sicknesses with which the LORD has made it sick,

Arminian

Arminian readers see future generations and foreigners noticing the devastation as a warning to all. Judgment is public, as blessing is.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s judgments as a testimony to the nations. Readers in both traditions commonly read the verse as part of a prophetic warning.

Deuteronomy 29:23

23that all of its land is sulfur, salt, and burning, that it is not sown, doesn’t produce, nor does any grass grow in it, like the overthrow of Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, and Zeboiim, which the LORD overthrew in his anger, and in his wrath.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the comparison with Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah and Zeboiim as stressing the severity of judgment (compare Genesis 19:24; Hosea 11:8). The land itself bears the marks.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the imagery of sulfur, salt and burning as describing God’s righteous wrath. Both traditions commonly read the verse as a sobering warning, not a prediction to be pressed in every detail.

Deuteronomy 29:24

24Even all the nations will say, “Why has the LORD done this to this land? What does the heat of this great anger mean?”

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the nations ask why the LORD has done this. God’s dealings with Israel are meant to be understood.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the question as an occasion for declaring God’s justice. Readers in both traditions commonly see the verse as preparing for the explanation that follows.

Deuteronomy 29:25

25Then men will say, “Because they abandoned the covenant of the LORD, the God of their fathers, which he made with them when he brought them out of the land of Egypt,

Arminian

Arminian readers see the answer, that Israel abandoned the covenant, as placing responsibility squarely on the people. Judgment follows unfaithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see covenant-breaking as the cause of judgment, with God remaining faithful. Both traditions commonly read the verse as vindicating God’s justice.

Deuteronomy 29:26

26and went and served other gods and worshiped them, gods that they didn’t know and that he had not given to them.

Arminian

Arminian readers see serving other gods as a deliberate choice to turn from the God who delivered them. That these were gods they had not known underlines the folly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see idolatry as the root sin that broke the covenant. Readers in both traditions commonly see the verse as describing the essence of Israel’s failure.

Deuteronomy 29:27

27Therefore the LORD’s anger burned against this land, to bring on it all the curses that are written in this book.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the LORD’s anger as a response to persistent covenant violation. The curses written in the book come to pass.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to his word in judgment as well as blessing. Both traditions commonly read the verse as showing that God’s warnings are serious.

Deuteronomy 29:28

28The LORD rooted them out of their land in anger, in wrath, and in great indignation, and thrust them into another land, as it is today.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see exile as the painful consequence of rejecting God. Yet chapter 30 will show that judgment is not the final word.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the uprooting as God’s righteous judgment, preparing for the promise of restoration in 30:1–10. Readers in both traditions commonly read the verse as pointing ahead to God’s mercy.

Deuteronomy 29:29

29The secret things belong to the LORD our God; but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever, that we may do all the words of this law.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the distinction between secret and revealed things as calling for humility about God’s hidden purposes and focus on obedience. What God has revealed is enough to live by.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the verse as guarding against speculation into God’s hidden counsel, while affirming that his revealed will is the rule for life (compare Romans 11:33). Both traditions commonly appeal to this verse, and it serves as shared ground for discussing God’s purposes with reverence.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
