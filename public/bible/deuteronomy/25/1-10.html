Passage

Deuteronomy 25

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Moses continues a series of laws for life in the land. Judges must acquit the righteous and condemn the wicked, and a beating must be limited to forty strokes so the guilty person is not degraded. An ox treading grain must not be muzzled. If a man dies childless, his brother should marry the widow so the dead man’s name continues; if the brother refuses, the widow publicly shames him before the elders.

Deuteronomy 25:1

1If there is a controversy between men, and they come to judgment and the judges judge them, then they shall justify the righteous and condemn the wicked.

Arminian

Judges must “justify the righteous and condemn the wicked.” Justice means declaring people to be what they are. Proverbs 17:15 condemns reversing this. Classical Arminians affirm that God’s justification of sinners through Christ upholds rather than violates this principle (Romans 3:26).

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same legal language and the same tension resolved in Christ, in whom God is both just and the justifier of the one who has faith (Romans 3:26). Classical Arminians and the Reformed share this forensic understanding of justification.

Deuteronomy 25:2

2It shall be, if the wicked man is worthy to be beaten, that the judge shall cause him to lie down and to be beaten before his face, according to his wickedness, by number.

Arminian

Punishment is proportioned to the offense and carried out in the judge’s presence, by number. The law restrains anger and private vengeance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see proportion and oversight in punishment, carried out before the judge rather than in private anger. Both traditions read this as shared ground about restrained justice.

Deuteronomy 25:3

3He may sentence him to no more than forty stripes. He shall not give more, lest if he should give more and beat him more than that many stripes, then your brother will be degraded in your sight.

Arminian

No more than forty strokes, so that “your brother” is not degraded. Even a guilty person keeps his dignity as a member of the covenant people. Paul later received “forty stripes minus one” five times (2 Corinthians 11:24), reflecting a later practice of stopping short of the limit.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same concern for human dignity and the same link to 2 Corinthians 11:24. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 25:4

4You shall not muzzle the ox when he treads out the grain.

Arminian

The working ox must not be muzzled. God cares about animals and about fair reward for labor. Paul applies the verse to supporting those who preach the gospel (1 Corinthians 9:9–10; 1 Timothy 5:18).

Reformed

Reformed readers draw the same New Testament applications. Both traditions see Paul drawing out a principle already present in the law. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 25:5

5If brothers dwell together, and one of them dies and has no son, the wife of the dead shall not be married outside to a stranger. Her husband’s brother shall go in to her, and take her as his wife, and perform the duty of a husband’s brother to her.

Arminian

If a man dies without a son, his brother is to marry the widow. This custom, called levirate marriage, protected the widow and preserved the family line and its land. Genesis 38 shows an earlier form of the practice.

Reformed

Reformed readers describe the custom the same way and note Genesis 38. Both traditions treat this as covenant law for Israel’s family and land, not a rule binding on the church.

Deuteronomy 25:6

6It shall be that the firstborn whom she bears shall succeed in the name of his brother who is dead, that his name not be blotted out of Israel.

Arminian

The firstborn son continues the dead brother’s name, so it is not blotted out of Israel. Name and inheritance mattered because the land was God’s gift to each family.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The Sadducees later used this law to question the resurrection (Matthew 22:23–33). This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 25:7

7If the man doesn’t want to take his brother’s wife, then his brother’s wife shall go up to the gate to the elders, and say, “My husband’s brother refuses to raise up to his brother a name in Israel. He will not perform the duty of a husband’s brother to me.”

Arminian

If the brother refuses, the widow may appeal to the elders at the gate. The law gives the vulnerable woman a public voice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same protection for the widow: she may bring her case to the elders at the gate, so the refusal cannot stay hidden. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 25:8

8Then the elders of his city shall call him, and speak to him. If he stands and says, “I don’t want to take her,”

Arminian

The elders summon the brother and speak with him. He is given the chance to reconsider before the public rite of shame proceeds, so the refusal is deliberate, not hasty.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the elders’ counsel as a chance for the man to change his mind. The law prefers persuasion first; the public shaming comes only if he still refuses. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 25:9

9then his brother’s wife shall come to him in the presence of the elders, and loose his sandal from off his foot, and spit in his face. She shall answer and say, “So shall it be done to the man who does not build up his brother’s house.”

Arminian

If he still refuses, the widow removes his sandal and spits in his face, declaring the shame of not building up his brother’s house. The refusal is treated as a failure of family duty. Ruth 4 shows a related sandal custom, though the details differ.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same link to Ruth 4, where Boaz acts as redeemer. Many in both traditions see Boaz as a picture of Christ the Redeemer, while recognizing that Ruth’s situation is not a simple case of this law.

Deuteronomy 25:10

10His name shall be called in Israel, “The house of him who had his sandal removed.”

Arminian

His family will be known as “the house of him who had his sandal removed.” The penalty is public shame, not physical punishment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a lasting public name as the penalty, not bodily harm. The household bears the memory of a duty refused. This is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
