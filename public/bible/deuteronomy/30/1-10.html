Passage

Deuteronomy 30

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Chapter 30 looks beyond the curses of exile to restoration. When Israel calls these things to mind among the nations and returns to the LORD with all heart and soul, he will have compassion, gather them from the farthest places, bring them back to the land, and do them good. At the center stands the promise that the LORD will circumcise their heart and their children’s heart to love him with all heart and soul, that they may live. Arminian and Reformed readers commonly read this as a gracious promise of restoration and inward renewal that later Scripture develops in the new covenant (Jeremiah 31:33; Ezekiel 36:26; Romans 2:29). They differ mainly in how they relate God’s heart-circumcising work in verse 6 to Israel’s returning in verses 2, 8 and 10.

Deuteronomy 30:1

1It shall happen, when all these things have come on you, the blessing and the curse, which I have set before you, and you shall call them to mind among all the nations where the LORD your God has driven you,

Arminian

Arminian readers see the people calling things to mind among the nations as the first step of repentance. Exile can become a place of reflection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God using judgment to bring his people to remembrance. Readers in both traditions commonly see the verse as opening the promise of restoration (compare Deuteronomy 4:29–31).

Deuteronomy 30:2

2and return to the LORD your God and obey his voice according to all that I command you today, you and your children, with all your heart and with all your soul,

Arminian

Arminian readers see returning to the LORD with all heart and soul as a genuine response that God invites and enables. Repentance is wholehearted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wholehearted return as the fruit of God’s gracious work, described in verse 6. Both traditions commonly see the verse as describing true repentance, not mere outward change.

Deuteronomy 30:3

3that then the LORD your God will release you from captivity, have compassion on you, and will return and gather you from all the peoples where the LORD your God has scattered you.

Arminian

Arminian readers rejoice that God will release, have compassion, and gather his scattered people. His mercy answers their return.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s compassion and gathering as acts of covenant faithfulness (compare Leviticus 26:40–42). Readers in both traditions commonly treasure the verse as a promise of restoration.

Deuteronomy 30:4

4If your outcasts are in the uttermost parts of the heavens, from there the LORD your God will gather you, and from there he will bring you back.

Arminian

Arminian readers see God’s reach extending to the uttermost parts of the heavens. No one is too far for God to bring back.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the promise as showing the limitless scope of God’s power to gather his people (compare Nehemiah 1:9). Both traditions commonly read the verse as encouragement to those who feel far from God.

Deuteronomy 30:5

5The LORD your God will bring you into the land which your fathers possessed, and you will possess it. He will do you good, and increase your numbers more than your fathers.

Arminian

Arminian readers see restoration to the land and increase as God’s generous blessing. He does more than restore what was lost.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the promise as fulfilling God’s covenant purposes for his people. Readers in both traditions commonly read the verse as part of the hope of restoration, with differing views on how it is fulfilled.

Deuteronomy 30:6

6The LORD your God will circumcise your heart, and the heart of your offspring, to love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul, that you may live.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the LORD circumcising the heart as his gracious work of inner renewal, enabling love for him. They commonly hold that this grace works with human response, so that the promise stands alongside the call to return in verse 2 and the command of Deuteronomy 10:16.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the circumcision of the heart as God’s effectual work that produces the love and return the passage calls for (compare Ezekiel 36:26; Colossians 2:11). They commonly read the commands of 10:16 and 30:2 as stating duty, with this verse showing how God brings it about. The same difference of emphasis is discussed at Deuteronomy 10:16.

Deuteronomy 30:7

7The LORD your God will put all these curses on your enemies and on those who hate you, who persecuted you.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the curses turned on enemies and persecutors as God’s justice on those who oppressed his people. God defends the restored.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness in reversing the curses. Both traditions commonly read the verse as part of the reversal of Israel’s fortunes.

Deuteronomy 30:8

8You shall return and obey the LORD’s voice, and do all his commandments which I command you today.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the restored people returning, obeying and doing God’s commandments as a free response to grace. Renewal leads to obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same obedience as the fruit of the circumcised heart in verse 6. Readers in both traditions commonly see the verse as showing that true renewal results in a changed life.

Deuteronomy 30:9

9The LORD your God will make you prosperous in all the work of your hand, in the fruit of your body, in the fruit of your livestock, and in the fruit of your ground, for good; for the LORD will again rejoice over you for good, as he rejoiced over your fathers,

Arminian

Arminian readers rejoice that the LORD will again rejoice over his people for good. God delights in their restoration and takes pleasure in blessing them once more.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s rejoicing as an expression of his covenant love (compare Zephaniah 3:17). Both traditions commonly treasure the image of God rejoicing over his people.

Deuteronomy 30:10

10if you will obey the LORD your God’s voice, to keep his commandments and his statutes which are written in this book of the law, if you turn to the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the conditions in this verse as showing that blessing is tied to genuine obedience and wholehearted turning. The covenant involves real responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the conditions as describing the path of blessing that God’s renewing grace secures. Readers in both traditions commonly read the verse as joining promise and obligation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
