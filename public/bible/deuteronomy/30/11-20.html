Passage

Deuteronomy 30

Verses 11–20 · WEB

Moses insists that the commandment he gives is not too hard or too distant: it is not in heaven or beyond the sea, but very near, in the mouth and in the heart. He then sets before Israel life and prosperity, death and evil, calls heaven and earth as witnesses, and urges them to choose life by loving the LORD, obeying his voice, and clinging to him, for he is their life. Paul later draws on verses 11–14 to describe the nearness of the word of faith in Christ (Romans 10:6–8). Arminian and Reformed readers commonly agree that this is a real and urgent call, that the choice matters, and that God himself is the life offered. They differ mainly in how they understand the human ability to choose life in relation to the promise of a circumcised heart in verse 6.

Deuteronomy 30:11

11For this commandment which I command you today is not too hard for you or too distant.

Arminian

Arminian readers see Moses’ statement that the commandment is not too hard or too distant as affirming that God’s call can be answered. God does not demand the impossible from those he enables.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly read the verse as stressing that God’s word has been plainly revealed and brought near, so Israel cannot plead ignorance. Many also read it in light of 30:6, where God provides the heart to keep his word.

Deuteronomy 30:12

12It is not in heaven, that you should say, “Who will go up for us to heaven, bring it to us, and proclaim it to us, that we may do it?”

Arminian

Arminian readers see the denial that the word is in heaven as removing excuses based on distance. God has made his will accessible.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God taking the initiative to reveal his word rather than leaving people to search for it. Both traditions commonly note that Paul applies this language to Christ, who has come down (Romans 10:6).

Deuteronomy 30:13

13Neither is it beyond the sea, that you should say, “Who will go over the sea for us, bring it to us, and proclaim it to us, that we may do it?”

Arminian

Arminian readers see the denial that the word is beyond the sea as stressing again that God has brought his word near. No heroic quest is required.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same emphasis on God’s gracious revelation. Readers in both traditions commonly see Paul’s use of these lines in Romans 10:7 as applying them to Christ’s resurrection.

Deuteronomy 30:14

14But the word is very near to you, in your mouth and in your heart, that you may do it.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the word near in the mouth and heart as available for faith and obedience. Paul calls it the word of faith which is preached (Romans 10:8).

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the nearness of the word as pointing to the gospel received by faith (Romans 10:8–9). Both traditions commonly read Paul’s use of the passage as showing that God’s word comes near in Christ.

Deuteronomy 30:15

15Behold, I have set before you today life and prosperity, and death and evil.

Arminian

Arminian readers see life and prosperity, death and evil set before Israel as a genuine choice with real consequences. The options are stark and clear.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the setting of these options as God’s solemn covenant summons. Readers in both traditions commonly read the verse as stressing the seriousness of the moment.

Deuteronomy 30:16

16For I command you today to love the LORD your God, to walk in his ways and to keep his commandments, his statutes, and his ordinances, that you may live and multiply, and that the LORD your God may bless you in the land where you go in to possess it.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the command to love, walk and keep God’s ways as the path to life and blessing. Love is the heart of obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the commandments as describing the life of a people who belong to God. Both traditions commonly see love for God as the root of the obedience described.

Deuteronomy 30:17

17But if your heart turns away, and you will not hear, but are drawn away and worship other gods, and serve them,

Arminian

Arminian readers see the warning about a heart turning away as a real possibility. People can be drawn away from God, and the verse treats that turning as a genuine danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the warning as a means God uses to guard his people from idolatry. Readers in both traditions commonly take the warning seriously.

Deuteronomy 30:18

18I declare to you today that you will surely perish. You will not prolong your days in the land where you pass over the Jordan to go in to possess it.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the declaration that the disobedient will surely perish as a sober statement of covenant consequences. Rejection of God has real outcomes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to his word in warning as well as promise. Both traditions commonly read the verse in its setting of life in the land.

Deuteronomy 30:19

19I call heaven and earth to witness against you today that I have set before you life and death, the blessing and the curse. Therefore choose life, that you may live, you and your descendants,

Arminian

Arminian readers commonly see the call to choose life as a real, grace-enabled choice that people can make or refuse, with heaven and earth called as witnesses to its seriousness (compare Joshua 24:15). God sincerely calls and truly enables a response.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly see the call as a sincere command and the means God uses to bring his people to life, while holding that fallen hearts choose life only as God circumcises them (30:6). The command shows what is required and calls for real response.

Key contrast Both traditions generally agree that “choose life” is a sincere call and that those who choose life do so freely. Arminian readers commonly see grace enabling every hearer to answer this call, while Reformed readers commonly see the call made effective in those whose hearts God renews.

Deuteronomy 30:20

20to love the LORD your God, to obey his voice, and to cling to him; for he is your life, and the length of your days, that you may dwell in the land which the LORD swore to your fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, to give them.

Arminian

Arminian readers see loving, obeying and clinging to the LORD as the content of choosing life. He is your life, the true center of the promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God himself as the life offered, not merely the land (compare Psalm 36:9; John 11:25). Readers in both traditions commonly treasure this verse as pointing to God as the source and goal of life.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
