Passage

Deuteronomy 18

Verses 1–8 · WEB

Deuteronomy 18:1–8 turns to the priests and Levites, the tribe of Levi that receives no territorial inheritance because “The LORD is their inheritance” (18:2; compare 10:9; Numbers 18:20). Verses 3–5 set out the priests’ due from sacrifices and first fruits, grounded in the LORD’s choice of Levi “to stand to minister in the LORD’s name” (18:5). Verses 6–8 protect a Levite who comes from any town to serve at the central sanctuary, giving him an equal share with the Levites already serving there. The passage ties Israel’s worship to the LORD’s provision and the people’s faithful giving.

Deuteronomy 18:1

1The priests and the Levites—all the tribe of Levi—shall have no portion nor inheritance with Israel. They shall eat the offerings of the LORD made by fire and his portion.

Arminian

“The priests and the Levites—all the tribe of Levi” receive no tribal land. They live from “the offerings of the LORD made by fire and his portion.” Their support comes through Israel’s worship.

Arminian readers see God’s generosity in providing for those set apart to serve him, through the people’s faithful giving.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the LORD shares his own portion with those who serve at his altar. Their life depends on the God they serve, not on owning land.

Shared ground: both traditions read the arrangement as tying ministry and worship together. The New Testament applies the principle that those who serve should be supported (compare 1 Corinthians 9:13–14).

Deuteronomy 18:2

2They shall have no inheritance among their brothers. The LORD is their inheritance, as he has spoken to them.

Arminian

“The LORD is their inheritance, as he has spoken to them.” Levi’s lack of land is not a loss but a privilege. This echoes Numbers 18:20, where the LORD tells Aaron, “I am your portion and your inheritance among the children of Israel.”

Arminian readers see an invitation to find one’s security in God himself rather than in possessions.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God himself is the highest good and inheritance of his people. Levi’s portion pictures what is true of all who belong to him.

Shared ground: both traditions read “The LORD is their inheritance” as a rich theme that the Psalms and later Scripture apply more widely to believers who treasure God above everything.

Deuteronomy 18:3

3This shall be the priests’ due from the people, from those who offer a sacrifice, whether it be ox or sheep, that they shall give to the priest: the shoulder, the two cheeks, and the inner parts.

Arminian

The priests’ due from sacrifices is set out: “the shoulder, the two cheeks, and the inner parts.” These are specific portions from offerings of oxen or sheep. Some of these portions differ from those named in Leviticus (compare Leviticus 7:31–34), and interpreters discuss how the two lists relate.

Arminian readers see fairness in a clear, fixed due, so priests are supported without exploiting worshipers.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s order in a fixed portion. The priests’ share is defined by God, which protects both priests and worshipers (contrast the abuse at Shiloh in 1 Samuel 2:12–17).

Shared ground: both traditions recognize that the relation between this list and the Levitical portions is discussed, and both read the verse as providing orderly support for those who serve.

Deuteronomy 18:4

4You shall give him the first fruits of your grain, of your new wine, and of your oil, and the first of the fleece of your sheep.

Arminian

The people must also give “the first fruits of your grain, of your new wine, and of your oil, and the first of the fleece of your sheep.” The first and best of the harvest and flock goes to the LORD’s servants.

Arminian readers see firstfruits giving as grateful acknowledgment that every good harvest comes from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that firstfruits confess God’s ownership of all things. Giving the first portion declares that the whole belongs to him.

Shared ground: both traditions see firstfruits as an act of worship and trust, and both note that the New Testament uses firstfruits imagery for Christ’s resurrection and for God’s people.

Deuteronomy 18:5

5For the LORD your God has chosen him out of all your tribes to stand to minister in the LORD’s name, him and his sons forever.

Arminian

The reason for the priests’ support is that “the LORD your God has chosen him out of all your tribes to stand to minister in the LORD’s name, him and his sons forever.” The office rests on God’s choice.

Arminian readers see the choice here as God’s appointment of Levi to a particular service for the good of all Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the LORD’s choice establishes the priesthood. No one takes the office for himself; God appoints those who serve him.

Shared ground: both traditions read this as election to a vocation and office within Israel, not a statement about individual salvation. The verse does not raise the classical debate about election to salvation.

Deuteronomy 18:6

6If a Levite comes from any of your gates out of all Israel where he lives, and comes with all the desire of his soul to the place which the LORD shall choose,

Arminian

A Levite from any town may come “with all the desire of his soul to the place which the LORD shall choose.” His willing desire to serve is respected.

Arminian readers value the emphasis on the Levite’s own wholehearted desire. God welcomes the willing servant who comes to minister before him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Levite’s desire as a fitting response to God’s calling of Levi to serve. A heart drawn to God’s house is a gift to be honored.

Shared ground: both traditions affirm that sincere desire to serve God is good and should not be blocked by those already serving at the sanctuary.

Deuteronomy 18:7

7then he shall minister in the name of the LORD his God, as all his brothers the Levites do, who stand there before the LORD.

Arminian

The Levite who comes “shall minister in the name of the LORD his God, as all his brothers the Levites do.” He stands on equal footing with those already serving “before the LORD.”

Arminian readers see here a guard against an exclusive priestly class that keeps out other qualified Levites.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that service is “in the name of the LORD.” Ministers serve as representatives of God, not as owners of the office.

Shared ground: both traditions read the verse as protecting fairness among God’s servants and as stressing that all ministry is done before the LORD and in his name.

Deuteronomy 18:8

8They shall have like portions to eat, in addition to that which comes from the sale of his family possessions.

Arminian

The newly arrived Levite receives “like portions to eat,” in addition to “the sale of his family possessions.” The Hebrew behind the last phrase is compact and its exact sense is debated; WEB takes it as income from selling family property.

Arminian readers see God’s concern that no servant of his be shortchanged because of where he came from.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the equal portion as just. Service at the sanctuary is supported by the LORD’s provision, shared equally among those who serve.

Shared ground: both traditions acknowledge the uncertainty in the wording, and both read the verse as ensuring equal support for Levites who serve before the LORD.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
