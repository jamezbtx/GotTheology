Passage

Deuteronomy 18

Verses 15–22 · WEB

Deuteronomy 18:15–22 gives the LORD’s promise of a prophet “like me” (Moses) and the test for true and false prophets. The promise answers Israel’s plea at Horeb not to hear God’s voice directly again (18:16; compare 5:23–29; Exodus 20:19). The LORD will put his words in the prophet’s mouth, and Israel must listen (18:15, 18–19). A prophet who speaks presumptuously or in the name of other gods is to die (18:20), and a prediction that fails shows a word the LORD has not spoken (18:21–22; compare 13:1–5). Within Israel the promise sustained a line of prophets, yet Deuteronomy itself ends by noting that no prophet like Moses had arisen (34:10). The New Testament identifies Jesus as the prophet like Moses (Acts 3:22; 7:37). Classical Arminian and Reformed interpreters share this messianic reading.

Deuteronomy 18:15

15The LORD your God will raise up to you a prophet from among you, of your brothers, like me. You shall listen to him.

Arminian

“The LORD your God will raise up to you a prophet from among you, of your brothers, like me. You shall listen to him.” The prophet will be an Israelite and will speak as Moses did. In context, this provides for continuing prophetic guidance in Israel. Yet Deuteronomy ends by saying no prophet had arisen “like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face” (34:10), which keeps the promise looking forward.

Arminian readers, with the New Testament, see the promise fulfilled in Jesus (Acts 3:22; 7:37).

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the promise as pointing to Christ, who fulfills it fully. Many also see it as covering the line of true prophets God would raise up in Israel.

Shared ground: both traditions read Peter’s use of this verse, “The Lord God will raise up a prophet for you from among your brothers, like me” (Acts 3:22), as identifying Jesus as the prophet like Moses. At the transfiguration the Father’s words “Listen to him” (Matthew 17:5) echo this verse.

Deuteronomy 18:16

16This is according to all that you desired of the LORD your God in Horeb in the day of the assembly, saying, “Let me not hear again the LORD my God’s voice, neither let me see this great fire any more, that I not die.”

Arminian

The promise answers Israel’s request at Horeb: “Let me not hear again the LORD my God’s voice, neither let me see this great fire any more, that I not die.” The people asked for a mediator (5:23–27; Exodus 20:19).

Arminian readers see God’s compassion in granting the people’s request. He meets their weakness by providing a prophet to speak his words.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the need for a mediator between the holy God and sinful people. Israel’s fear at Horeb showed that sinners cannot stand before God’s unmediated presence.

Shared ground: both traditions see the request for a mediator as answered in the prophetic office and finally in Christ, through whom God “has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son” (Hebrews 1:2).

Deuteronomy 18:17

17The LORD said to me, “They have well said that which they have spoken.

Arminian

The LORD tells Moses, “They have well said that which they have spoken.” He approves the people’s request for a mediator. Deuteronomy 5:28 records the same approval: “They have well said all that they have spoken.”

Arminian readers note that God approved the people’s response. He takes human requests seriously and answers them.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God approves the request and turns it into a promise. In 5:29 the LORD adds, “Oh that there were such a heart in them,” which shows that the people needed more than a good request.

Shared ground: both traditions read the verse as God graciously approving the request for a mediator and providing for it.

Deuteronomy 18:18

18I will raise them up a prophet from among their brothers, like you. I will put my words in his mouth, and he shall speak to them all that I shall command him.

Arminian

“I will raise them up a prophet from among their brothers, like you. I will put my words in his mouth, and he shall speak to them all that I shall command him.” The true prophet speaks God’s words, not his own.

Arminian readers see God’s faithfulness in continuing to speak to his people, so they can know his will and respond to it.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the prophet’s authority lies in God’s words placed in his mouth. Christ fulfills this as the one who speaks the Father’s words perfectly.

Shared ground: both traditions read the verse as defining true prophecy as speaking God’s word and as fulfilled in Jesus. Both note Jesus’ statement about Moses: “he wrote about me” (John 5:46).

Deuteronomy 18:19

19It shall happen, that whoever will not listen to my words which he shall speak in my name, I will require it of him.

Arminian

The LORD warns that “whoever will not listen to my words which he shall speak in my name, I will require it of him.” Rejecting the prophet is rejecting God, and God will hold the hearer accountable.

Arminian readers see real responsibility here: hearers can listen or refuse, and they are answerable to God for their response.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm full human accountability for hearing God’s word. They note that Acts 3:23 applies this warning to rejecting Christ: “every soul that will not listen to that prophet will be utterly destroyed from among the people.”

Shared ground: both traditions affirm that people are responsible for how they respond to God’s word spoken through his prophet. The verse does not itself raise the debate about how grace relates to that response.

Deuteronomy 18:20

20But the prophet who speaks a word presumptuously in my name, which I have not commanded him to speak, or who speaks in the name of other gods, that same prophet shall die.”

Arminian

The prophet “who speaks a word presumptuously in my name, which I have not commanded him to speak, or who speaks in the name of other gods” is to die. Two kinds of false prophecy are named: false words in the LORD’s name and words in the name of other gods (compare 13:1–5).

Arminian readers see God’s protection of his people from deception.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the gravity of claiming divine authority falsely. To put words in God’s mouth is a serious offense against his holiness and against his people.

Shared ground: both traditions read the penalty in Israel’s covenant setting, and both see a lasting warning against speaking for God without his word.

Deuteronomy 18:21

21You may say in your heart, “How shall we know the word which the LORD has not spoken?”

Arminian

Moses anticipates the people’s question: “How shall we know the word which the LORD has not spoken?” Discernment is needed because not everyone who claims to speak for God does.

Arminian readers note that God gives his people means to test claims, expecting them to exercise discernment.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God provides objective tests rather than leaving his people to subjective impressions.

Shared ground: both traditions see the need for testing prophecy as an abiding concern, carried into the New Testament’s call to discern true and false teaching.

Deuteronomy 18:22

22When a prophet speaks in the LORD’s name, if the thing doesn’t follow, nor happen, that is the thing which the LORD has not spoken. The prophet has spoken it presumptuously. You shall not be afraid of him.

Arminian

“When a prophet speaks in the LORD’s name, if the thing doesn’t follow, nor happen, that is the thing which the LORD has not spoken.” A failed prediction marks a false prophet. “You shall not be afraid of him.”

Arminian readers note that this test works alongside 13:1–5, where even a fulfilled sign does not validate a prophet who leads people to other gods.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that the test is not the only one. Fulfillment is necessary but not sufficient; faithfulness to the LORD and his word is also required (13:1–5). Jeremiah 28:9 applies the same test of fulfillment.

Shared ground: both traditions read the two tests together and see God freeing his people from fear of false prophets.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
