Passage

Deuteronomy 18

Verses 9–14 · WEB

Deuteronomy 18:9–14 forbids Israel to imitate the religious practices of the nations of Canaan once they enter the land. It lists child sacrifice (“pass through the fire,” compare Leviticus 18:21; 2 Kings 23:10) together with a range of divination, sorcery and consultation of the dead. These practices are called “abominations” (18:9, 12), and they are part of the reason given for the nations’ dispossession (18:12). Israel is to be “blameless with the LORD your God” (18:13). The passage sets up 18:15–22: Israel is not to seek hidden knowledge by these means, because the LORD will speak to them through his own prophet.

Deuteronomy 18:9

9When you have come into the land which the LORD your God gives you, you shall not learn to imitate the abominations of those nations.

Arminian

When Israel enters the land, they must “not learn to imitate the abominations of those nations.” The danger is gradual cultural absorption of practices opposed to the LORD.

Arminian readers see a call to watchfulness. God’s people must actively choose not to learn the ways of the world around them.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God’s people are set apart by his word. What they learn and imitate must come from the LORD, not from surrounding religious practices.

Shared ground: both traditions read the verse as a warning that God’s people can be shaped by surrounding practices, and both see a lasting call to holiness.

Deuteronomy 18:10

10There shall not be found with you anyone who makes his son or his daughter to pass through the fire, one who uses divination, one who tells fortunes, or an enchanter, or a sorcerer,

Arminian

The list begins with anyone “who makes his son or his daughter to pass through the fire,” a practice linked elsewhere with child sacrifice to Molech (Leviticus 18:21; 2 Kings 23:10). It continues with divination, fortune telling, enchantment and sorcery. The exact distinctions between these terms are not always clear.

Arminian readers see these practices as attempts to manipulate the spiritual world or gain hidden knowledge apart from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see these practices as forms of false worship that seek power or knowledge apart from God’s revealed word.

Shared ground: both traditions read the list as forbidding every attempt to control the future or the spirit world by occult means. Both note that 2 Kings 21:6 records Manasseh doing many of these very things.

Deuteronomy 18:11

11or a charmer, or someone who consults with a familiar spirit, or a wizard, or a necromancer.

Arminian

The list continues: charmers, those who consult “a familiar spirit,” wizards and necromancers, who seek to consult the dead. Isaiah later asks, “Should they consult the dead on behalf of the living?” (Isaiah 8:19).

Arminian readers see in this a call to trust God for guidance rather than seeking it from forbidden sources.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the sufficiency of God’s word. Isaiah’s answer is “Turn to the law and to the covenant!” (Isaiah 8:20).

Shared ground: both traditions agree that seeking guidance from the dead or from spirits is forbidden. God’s people are to seek him and his revealed word instead.

Deuteronomy 18:12

12For whoever does these things is an abomination to the LORD. Because of these abominations, the LORD your God drives them out from before you.

Arminian

“For whoever does these things is an abomination to the LORD.” These abominations are given as part of the reason the LORD drives out the nations before Israel. Their dispossession is presented as judgment, not as favoritism.

Arminian readers note that judgment falls because of practiced sin. The same standard will apply to Israel if they adopt these practices.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress God’s holiness. The LORD acts as Judge of the nations, and Israel receives the land by his grace, not by its own righteousness (compare 9:4–6).

Shared ground: both traditions read the verse as showing that God judges these practices, and that Israel is warned against them for the same reason.

Deuteronomy 18:13

13You shall be blameless with the LORD your God.

Arminian

“You shall be blameless with the LORD your God.” The word suggests wholeness and integrity, an undivided loyalty to the LORD rather than a mixture of his worship with occult practice.

Arminian readers see a call to wholehearted devotion that God enables and expects of his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same call to integrity before God. They note that the word speaks of covenant faithfulness and sincerity, not sinless perfection.

Shared ground: in context, both traditions read “blameless” as undivided loyalty to the LORD in contrast with the divided practices just listed. Wesleyan readers have often connected “blameless” and “perfect” language with Christian perfection, while Reformed readers typically take the word here as covenant faithfulness and sincerity rather than sinless perfection.

Deuteronomy 18:14

14For these nations that you shall dispossess listen to those who practice sorcery and to diviners; but as for you, the LORD your God has not allowed you so to do.

Arminian

The nations “listen to those who practice sorcery and to diviners,” but “the LORD your God has not allowed you so to do.” Israel’s difference from the nations is grounded in the LORD’s decision.

Arminian readers see God’s protective love in withholding what would harm his people and offering something better.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God draws the boundary for his people. What he has not allowed, they must not do, and he provides the better way.

Shared ground: both traditions read the verse as the turning point to 18:15. The LORD forbids occult guidance because he will speak to his people through the prophet he raises up.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
