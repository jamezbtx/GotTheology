Passage

Deuteronomy 31

Verses 14–23 · WEB

At the Tent of Meeting, God tells Moses that after his death Israel will turn to other gods and break the covenant, bringing judgment on themselves. He commands Moses to write a song as a witness, saying, “I know their ways and what they are doing today,” and then commissions Joshua. Arminian and Reformed readers explain God’s foreknowledge of this apostasy differently, but the text holds Israel fully responsible.

Deuteronomy 31:14

14The LORD said to Moses, “Behold, your days approach that you must die. Call Joshua, and present yourselves in the Tent of Meeting, that I may commission him.” Moses and Joshua went, and presented themselves in the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

God tells Moses his death is near and calls him and Joshua to the Tent of Meeting for Joshua’s commissioning. Arminian readers see God personally overseeing the leadership transition.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the commissioning as God’s act, not merely Moses’ choice. The setting at the Tent of Meeting marks the moment as solemn and divinely sanctioned.

Deuteronomy 31:15

15The LORD appeared in the Tent in a pillar of cloud, and the pillar of cloud stood over the Tent’s door.

Arminian

The LORD appears in a pillar of cloud at the tent door. Arminian readers see God’s visible presence confirming what is about to be said and done.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the cloud signaling God’s authority over the occasion. The image recalls God’s guidance and presence throughout the wilderness journey. Joshua’s commission happens in God’s presence.

Deuteronomy 31:16

16The LORD said to Moses, “Behold, you shall sleep with your fathers. This people will rise up and play the prostitute after the strange gods of the land where they go to be among them, and will forsake me and break my covenant which I have made with them.

Arminian

God tells Moses that after his death the people will “play the prostitute after the strange gods” and break the covenant. Arminian readers see God foreseeing a free and culpable turning away by Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God declaring what will come to pass within his sovereign purpose, while Israel remains fully guilty. The text itself presents the apostasy as Israel’s own forsaking of God.

Deuteronomy 31:17

17Then my anger shall be kindled against them in that day, and I will forsake them, and I will hide my face from them, and they shall be devoured, and many evils and troubles shall come on them; so that they will say in that day, ‘Haven’t these evils come on us because our God is not among us?’

Arminian

God’s anger will burn, he will hide his face, and troubles will come, so the people will ask, “Haven’t these evils come on us because our God is not among us?” Arminian readers see judgment answering Israel’s choices.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s withdrawal as just response to covenant breaking. The people’s anticipated question shows that they will recognize, at least partly, the cause of their distress.

Deuteronomy 31:18

18I will surely hide my face in that day for all the evil which they have done, in that they have turned to other gods.

Arminian

God will surely hide his face because of the evil done in turning to other gods. Arminian readers see the hiding of God’s face as a consequence Israel brings on itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s judgment directly tied to idolatry. The verse states the reason plainly: Israel’s turning to other gods. Divine hiddenness here is judgment, not indifference or absence of power.

Deuteronomy 31:19

19“Now therefore write this song for yourselves, and teach it to the children of Israel. Put it in their mouths, that this song may be a witness for me against the children of Israel.

Arminian

Moses is to write a song, teach it, and put it in the people’s mouths as a witness against them. Arminian readers see God providing a memorable warning that could call Israel back.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the song as a means God appoints for testimony and conviction. Teaching it by heart makes it available long after Moses is gone.

Deuteronomy 31:20

20For when I have brought them into the land which I swore to their fathers, flowing with milk and honey, and they have eaten and filled themselves, and grown fat, then they will turn to other gods, and serve them, and despise me, and break my covenant.

Arminian

Once fed and fat in the good land, Israel will turn to other gods and break the covenant. Arminian readers see prosperity as a setting in which the heart may freely turn away, echoing chapter 8.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the heart’s tendency to forget God in plenty. The verse anticipates the pattern the Song describes in 32:15. Fullness becomes the occasion of forgetting, as Moses warned before.

Deuteronomy 31:21

21It will happen, when many evils and troubles have come on them, that this song will testify before them as a witness; for it will not be forgotten out of the mouths of their descendants; for I know their ways and what they are doing today, before I have brought them into the land which I promised them.”

Arminian

When troubles come, the song will testify, for God says, “I know their ways and what they are doing today.” Arminian readers see foreknowledge that does not cause Israel’s sin, with Israel fully responsible.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s foreknowledge as grounded in his decree, while Israel’s responsibility remains real. God’s foresight does not excuse Israel; the song is given precisely to hold them accountable.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm that God foreknew Israel’s apostasy and that his foresight does not excuse Israel, since the song is given to hold them accountable. Classical Arminian readers see foreknowledge without causation, with Israel’s turning a real and culpable free act. Reformed readers see God’s foreknowledge as grounded in his decree, while insisting human responsibility remains real. Each camp holds a range of views on how foreknowledge and freedom relate.

Deuteronomy 31:22

22So Moses wrote this song the same day, and taught it to the children of Israel.

Arminian

Moses writes the song that same day and teaches it to Israel. Arminian readers see prompt obedience that equips the people with a lasting witness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Moses fulfilling God’s command immediately. The verse connects chapter 31’s narrative with the Song recorded in chapter 32. The same day that the command is given, the witness is put in place.

Deuteronomy 31:23

23He commissioned Joshua the son of Nun, and said, “Be strong and courageous; for you shall bring the children of Israel into the land which I swore to them. I will be with you.”

Arminian

God commissions Joshua, saying, “Be strong and courageous; for you shall bring the children of Israel into the land which I swore to them. I will be with you.” Arminian readers see God’s personal pledge to Joshua.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God directly installing Joshua as leader. The promise “I will be with you” grounds Joshua’s courage in God’s presence. The new leader receives the same charge Moses gave in 31:7.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
