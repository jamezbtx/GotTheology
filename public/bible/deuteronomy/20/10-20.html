Passage

Deuteronomy 20

Verses 10–20 · WEB

The law distinguishes between distant cities and the cities of the nations in the land Israel is to inherit. Distant cities are first offered peace on terms of forced labor; if they resist, the men are killed and the rest taken as plunder. For the cities of the Canaanite nations, the command is to destroy everything that breathes, and the stated reason is that otherwise they would teach Israel their abominable worship. The section ends by forbidding Israel to cut down fruit trees during a siege. Readers in both traditions find 20:16–18 morally difficult and read it as a one-time judgment within redemptive history, not a model for today.

Deuteronomy 20:10

10When you draw near to a city to fight against it, then proclaim peace to it.

Arminian

When approaching a city, Israel must first proclaim peace. Arminian readers see a preference for peace over bloodshed. Both traditions agree that this applies to distant cities (20:15), not to the Canaanite cities addressed in 20:16–18.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the offer of peace as a restraint on war. Shared ground: the law requires an offer of terms before battle, limiting violence where possible, though the terms are harsh by modern standards.

Deuteronomy 20:11

11It shall be, if it gives you answer of peace and opens to you, then it shall be that all the people who are found therein shall become forced laborers to you, and shall serve you.

Arminian

If the city accepts, its people become forced laborers. Arminian readers acknowledge that the terms are harsh. Both traditions agree that the law reflects ancient Near Eastern practice while limiting killing.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the terms preserve life, though at the cost of freedom. Shared ground: the law is set in its historical context, and readers should not treat it as a timeless ideal for international relations.

Deuteronomy 20:12

12If it will make no peace with you, but will make war against you, then you shall besiege it.

Arminian

If the city refuses peace and makes war, Israel must besiege it. Arminian readers see the city’s choice determining its fate. Both traditions agree that the offer of peace precedes any siege.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the refusal as bringing the consequences of war upon the city. Shared ground: the city’s response to the offer of peace shapes what follows, and the law does not permit attack without that offer.

Deuteronomy 20:13

13When the LORD your God delivers it into your hand, you shall strike every male of it with the edge of the sword;

Arminian

When God gives the city into Israel’s hand, every male is to be killed. Arminian readers state the severity plainly. Both traditions agree that this harsh command is part of Israel’s ancient laws of war and is not a model for Christian conduct.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the victory is attributed to God. The command reflects warfare in its time. Shared ground: both traditions read this within the civil laws of ancient Israel, not as a pattern for the church or modern nations.

Deuteronomy 20:14

14but the women, the little ones, the livestock, and all that is in the city, even all its plunder, you shall take for plunder for yourself. You may use the plunder of your enemies, which the LORD your God has given you.

Arminian

Women, children, livestock, and plunder may be taken. Arminian readers note that the law spares non-combatants in distant cities. Both traditions agree that Deuteronomy 21:10–14 later regulates the treatment of captive women.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the provision as distinguishing between combatants and non-combatants. Shared ground: the law sets limits on killing, while still reflecting the harsh realities of ancient war. The text still regards the spoil as something “the LORD your God has given.”

Deuteronomy 20:15

15Thus you shall do to all the cities which are very far off from you, which are not of the cities of these nations.

Arminian

These rules apply to distant cities, not to the cities of the nations in the land. Arminian readers note the important distinction. Both traditions agree that the following verses address a different and unique situation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the distinction as crucial for interpreting the chapter. Shared ground: the rules for distant cities differ from the command concerning the Canaanite nations, which follows. Reading the two cases together keeps the chapter’s argument clear.

Deuteronomy 20:16

16But of the cities of these peoples that the LORD your God gives you for an inheritance, you shall save alive nothing that breathes;

Arminian

In the cities of the nations in the land, Israel must save alive nothing that breathes. Arminian readers state the command plainly and acknowledge its moral difficulty. Many see it as a unique judgment tied to the land’s conquest. Some interpreters read the language as partly hyperbolic.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm God’s right to judge, citing passages such as Genesis 15:16 and Deuteronomy 9:4–5 on the nations’ wickedness. They treat this as a unique act in redemptive history. Shared ground: neither tradition views it as a model for today.

Deuteronomy 20:17

17but you shall utterly destroy them: the Hittite, the Amorite, the Canaanite, the Perizzite, the Hivite, and the Jebusite, as the LORD your God has commanded you;

Arminian

Israel must utterly destroy the Hittite, Amorite, Canaanite, Perizzite, Hivite, and Jebusite as commanded. Arminian readers note that this list matches 7:1 except for the Girgashite. Both traditions agree that the command applies to specific nations at a specific time.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command as God’s judgment on specific peoples. Joshua’s narrative also records exceptions such as Rahab (Joshua 6:25) and the Gibeonites (Joshua 9). Shared ground: the command is limited to the conquest of Canaan.

Deuteronomy 20:18

18that they not teach you to follow all their abominations, which they have done for their gods; so would you sin against the LORD your God.

Arminian

The reason given is so the nations will not teach Israel their abominable practices. Arminian readers see the concern for Israel’s faithfulness. Both traditions agree that the text grounds the command in protecting Israel from idolatry (compare 12:31; 18:9–12).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command as protecting the covenant people from corruption. Shared ground: the reason the text gives concerns false worship rather than ancestry, and both traditions read it in light of the danger of idolatry to Israel’s covenant life.

Deuteronomy 20:19

19When you shall besiege a city a long time, in making war against it to take it, you shall not destroy its trees by wielding an ax against them; for you may eat of them. You shall not cut them down, for is the tree of the field man, that it should be besieged by you?

Arminian

During a long siege, Israel must not cut down fruit trees. Arminian readers see care for creation even in war. The rhetorical question about the tree is difficult in Hebrew, and translations vary. Both traditions agree that the law limits destruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law as reflecting God’s care for creation and for future provision. Shared ground: war does not license unlimited destruction, and fruit trees are protected because they sustain life.

Deuteronomy 20:20

20Only the trees that you know are not trees for food, you shall destroy and cut them down. You shall build bulwarks against the city that makes war with you, until it falls.

Arminian

Only non-fruit trees may be cut down to build siege works. Arminian readers note the practical balance. Both traditions agree that the law permits necessary use while protecting resources for the future.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom in distinguishing between trees. Shared ground: the chapter ends with a restraint on destruction, reminding Israel that even in war God’s law limits what may be done.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
