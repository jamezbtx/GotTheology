Passage

Deuteronomy 12

Verses 15–28 · WEB

Alongside the central place for holy gifts, Moses allows ordinary meat to be killed and eaten within the gates, according to the LORD’s blessing, by unclean and clean alike, as of the gazelle and the deer. Blood, however, may not be eaten; it is to be poured out on the earth like water. Tithes, firstborn, vows, free will offerings, and the wave offering may not be eaten at home; they are to be eaten before the LORD at the place he shall choose, with the household and the Levite, who must not be forsaken. When the LORD enlarges the border and the people desire meat, they may eat it at home, and if the chosen place is too far, they may kill from herd and flock and eat within the gates, still without the blood, “for the blood is the life.” Holy things and vows still go to the chosen place and to the LORD’s altar. Observing these words will be for their good and their children’s, when they do what is good and right in the LORD’s eyes. Both traditions read the section as constructive law: a distinction between common meat and sanctuary offerings, a standing ban on eating blood, and care for the Levite, all as shared ground.

Deuteronomy 12:15

15Yet you may kill and eat meat within all your gates, after all the desire of your soul, according to the LORD your God’s blessing which he has given you. The unclean and the clean may eat of it, as of the gazelle and the deer.

Arminian

They may kill and eat meat within all their gates, after the desire of the soul, according to the blessing the LORD has given. The unclean and the clean may eat of it, as of the gazelle and the deer.

Arminian readers hear a generous permission. Ordinary hunger may be met at home, under God’s blessing, and ritual purity does not bar a person from this common meat, any more than from game.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same liberty, expressly tied to the LORD’s blessing. Gazelle and deer, animals not brought as altar sacrifices in the usual way, are the comparison for this household meat.

Both traditions take the verse as opening a lawful local slaughter, distinct from the sanctuary gifts of verses 6 and 17, without a doctrinal fork.

Deuteronomy 12:16

16Only you shall not eat the blood. You shall pour it out on the earth like water.

Arminian

Only they shall not eat the blood. “You shall pour it out on the earth like water.”

Arminian readers hear a sharp limit inside the permission. Meat may be eaten at home; blood may not. It is to be poured out, not consumed.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same ban, which verse 23 will explain. The pouring out on the earth treats the blood as not for the table.

Both traditions read a standing prohibition, in line with earlier biblical bans on eating blood, and they do not invent a contrast over it.

Deuteronomy 12:17

17You may not eat within your gates the tithe of your grain, or of your new wine, or of your oil, or the firstborn of your herd or of your flock, nor any of your vows which you vow, nor your free will offerings, nor the wave offering of your hand;

Arminian

They may not eat within their gates the tithe of grain, new wine, or oil, or the firstborn of herd or flock, or vowed gifts, free will offerings, or the wave offering of the hand.

Arminian readers see the home table clearly bounded. What has been vowed or set apart as holy is not converted into an ordinary meal at the gate.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the detailed list, matching the sanctuary gifts of verse 6. Local permission does not swallow up what belongs at the chosen place.

Both traditions take the verse as protecting sacred portions from being secularized at home.

Deuteronomy 12:18

18but you shall eat them before the LORD your God in the place which the LORD your God shall choose: you, your son, your daughter, your male servant, your female servant, and the Levite who is within your gates. You shall rejoice before the LORD your God in all that you put your hand to.

Arminian

Those holy foods are to be eaten “before the LORD your God in the place which the LORD your God shall choose” by the household and the Levite within the gates. They shall rejoice before him in all they put their hand to.

Arminian readers hear again the joy of verse 7, now applied to the reserved gifts. The Levite is at the table, and the place is still the one God shall choose.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise keep the chosen place in view, unnamed here as in verse 5, and the command to rejoice with those who have no land portion.

Both traditions remain on the shared ground already marked: God’s choice of place, household joy, and care for the Levite.

Deuteronomy 12:19

19Be careful that you don’t forsake the Levite as long as you live in your land.

Arminian

They must be careful not to forsake the Levite as long as they live in the land.

Arminian readers hear a lasting duty, not a single feast invitation. As long as they live in the land, the Levite is not to be abandoned.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same duration. The Levite’s lack of inheritance (verse 12) makes this care a standing part of life in the land.

Both traditions take the warning as straightforward covenant ethics, without a fork.

Deuteronomy 12:20

20When the LORD your God enlarges your border, as he has promised you, and you say, “I want to eat meat,” because your soul desires to eat meat, you may eat meat, after all the desire of your soul.

Arminian

When the LORD enlarges their border, as he has promised, and they say, “I want to eat meat,” because the soul desires meat, they may eat meat after all the desire of the soul.

Arminian readers hear desire named without shame, once the LORD has enlarged the border as promised. Wanting meat is met by permission, not by a new high place.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the enlargement is the LORD’s promise kept, and that the quoted wish to eat meat is allowed under that blessing.

Both traditions read the verse as a provision for a wider land and ordinary appetite, still governed by the rules that follow.

Deuteronomy 12:21

21If the place which the LORD your God shall choose to put his name is too far from you, then you shall kill of your herd and of your flock, which the LORD has given you, as I have commanded you; and you may eat within your gates, after all the desire of your soul.

Arminian

If “the place which the LORD your God shall choose to put his name” is too far, they may kill of the herd and flock the LORD has given, as commanded, and eat within the gates after the desire of the soul.

Arminian readers hear a merciful adjustment for distance. Distance from the chosen place does not force either hunger or a local altar for common meat.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the herd and flock are what the LORD has given, and that the farness of the chosen place is the reason for home slaughter, not a reason to invent another sanctuary.

Both traditions keep the earlier caution: the place remains the one God shall choose, still unnamed in this chapter.

Deuteronomy 12:22

22Even as the gazelle and as the deer is eaten, so you shall eat of it. The unclean and the clean may eat of it alike.

Arminian

They shall eat it as the gazelle and the deer are eaten; the unclean and the clean may eat of it alike.

Arminian readers hear the comparison of verse 15 repeated. Common meat, treated like game, is open to clean and unclean persons alike.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same likeness to gazelle and deer. This is not altar flesh, and the purity rule that bounds holy meals does not bound this one in the same way.

Both traditions take the verse as clarifying the character of the home meal, on shared descriptive ground.

Deuteronomy 12:23

23Only be sure that you don’t eat the blood; for the blood is the life. You shall not eat the life with the meat.

Arminian

They must not eat the blood, “for the blood is the life.” They shall not eat the life with the meat.

Arminian readers hear the reason now stated in the text itself: the blood is the life, and the life is not to be eaten with the meat. The ban is more than a custom.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise start from the verse’s own explanation. Earlier law also ties life and blood together, though the wording is not identical in every passage, and those lines are not treated as if they were quoted here.

Both traditions accept the stated reason and the ban, without forcing a further theory of blood as a point of contrast.

Deuteronomy 12:24

24You shall not eat it. You shall pour it out on the earth like water.

Arminian

They shall not eat it. “You shall pour it out on the earth like water.”

Arminian readers hear the practical act again: pour the blood out, as in verse 16. The life is returned to the earth, not taken as food.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the repeated command. Eating is forbidden; pouring out is required.

Both traditions take the repetition as emphasis, not as a new rule.

Deuteronomy 12:25

25You shall not eat it, that it may go well with you and with your children after you, when you do that which is right in the LORD’s eyes.

Arminian

They shall not eat it, that it may go well with them and with their children after them, when they do what is right in the LORD’s eyes.

Arminian readers hear the ban tied to a hopeful end: well-being for them and their children, in a life that is right in the LORD’s eyes. Obedience at the table has a future.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress “in the LORD’s eyes.” What is right is measured by him, and the promised good extends to the children after them.

Both traditions read a motive of lasting good, on shared ground.

Deuteronomy 12:26

26Only your holy things which you have, and your vows, you shall take and go to the place which the LORD shall choose.

Arminian

Only their holy things and their vows shall they take and go to “the place which the LORD shall choose.”

Arminian readers hear the sanctuary obligation left intact. Distance and home meat do not cancel the duty to bring holy things and vows to the chosen place.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the shorter formula here, “the place which the LORD shall choose” still without a name. Holy things travel to that place; common meat may stay at home.

Both traditions keep the distinction of the whole section, and the same caution about not over-identifying the site.

Deuteronomy 12:27

27You shall offer your burnt offerings, the meat and the blood, on the LORD your God’s altar. The blood of your sacrifices shall be poured out on the LORD your God’s altar, and you shall eat the meat.

Arminian

Burnt offerings, meat and blood, are to be offered on the LORD’s altar. The blood of sacrifices shall be poured out on that altar, and they shall eat the meat.

Arminian readers hear the difference from verses 23–24: sacrificial blood goes on the altar, not only on the earth, and the meat of those offerings is eaten in the appointed way.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the altar as the LORD’s. Blood of sacrifices is poured out there; the home pouring of common blood is not a substitute for this altar act.

Both traditions take the verse as sanctuary procedure, constructive and shared.

Deuteronomy 12:28

28Observe and hear all these words which I command you, that it may go well with you and with your children after you forever, when you do that which is good and right in the LORD your God’s eyes.

Arminian

They are to observe and hear all these words, that it may go well with them and their children after them forever, when they do what is good and right in the LORD their God’s eyes.

Arminian readers hear a closing appeal for the whole section: hearing and doing, for lasting good, measured as good and right in God’s eyes.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same standard and the same hope, “forever,” for the children after them. The words commanded are to be observed, not revised.

Both traditions take the verse as a hinge into the final warning of verses 29–32, still on shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
