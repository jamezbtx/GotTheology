Passage

Deuteronomy 12

Verses 1–14 · WEB

A new body of statutes begins, to be observed in the land the LORD, the God of the fathers, has given them all the days they live on the earth. Israel must destroy the Canaanite places of worship on mountains, hills, and under every green tree, breaking altars, dashing pillars, burning Asherah poles, and cutting off the names of those gods. They must not serve the LORD in that way. They are to seek the place which the LORD their God shall choose out of all their tribes to put his name, and there bring offerings, tithes, vows, and firstborn, and eat and rejoice before him. The present arrangement, in which every man does whatever is right in his own eyes, will not continue once they have the rest and inheritance God gives; then burnt offerings and the commanded gifts go to the chosen place, and the household is to rejoice there with the Levite, who has no portion with them. Both traditions read the chapter as shared ground: one LORD, not the many high places of the nations, and a place of his choosing rather than theirs. The text itself does not name that place; later identifications with sites such as Shiloh or Jerusalem are common in the wider canon’s story, but should not be treated as settled by this chapter alone.

Deuteronomy 12:1

1These are the statutes and the ordinances which you shall observe to do in the land which the LORD, the God of your fathers, has given you to possess all the days that you live on the earth.

Arminian

These are the statutes and ordinances to be observed in the land which the LORD, the God of their fathers, has given them to possess, all the days they live on the earth.

Arminian readers hear a law for life in the given land, for the whole span of their days on the earth. The God of the fathers is the giver, and the statutes are to be done there.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same gift and duration. The statutes belong to possessed land and to ongoing life, not only to the moment of entry.

Both traditions take the verse as the heading of the laws that follow, on shared ground.

Deuteronomy 12:2

2You shall surely destroy all the places in which the nations that you shall dispossess served their gods: on the high mountains, and on the hills, and under every green tree.

Arminian

They must surely destroy all the places in which the nations they dispossess served their gods: on the high mountains, on the hills, and “under every green tree.”

Arminian readers hear a thorough break with the land’s former worship. Every such place, not only a few famous shrines, is to be destroyed.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the completeness: mountains, hills, and every green tree. The LORD will not share the land with those sites of service to other gods.

Both traditions read a command to clear the pagan high places, without assigning a settled extra symbolism to each kind of site.

Deuteronomy 12:3

3You shall break down their altars, dash their pillars in pieces, and burn their Asherah poles with fire. You shall cut down the engraved images of their gods. You shall destroy their name out of that place.

Arminian

They shall break down altars, dash pillars, burn Asherah poles, cut down engraved images, and destroy the names of those gods out of that place.

Arminian readers see even the names targeted. Objects and memory alike are to be removed, so that the old worship has no standing in the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the total dismantling. Altars, pillars, poles, images, and names are all under the ban of this command.

Both traditions take the verse as practical iconoclasm in the land, shared ground rather than a later intramural dispute about images in the church, which this text does not adjudicate.

Deuteronomy 12:4

4You shall not do so to the LORD your God.

Arminian

“You shall not do so to the LORD your God.”

Arminian readers hear a clear limit: the LORD is not to be worshiped in the manner of those destroyed places, as if any hill or tree were good enough, or as if his service could copy theirs.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same prohibition, which verse 31 will repeat in connection with the nations’ abominations. The LORD’s worship is not to be modeled on the cult just condemned.

Both traditions take the line as a ban on treating the LORD as one more high-place god, and they do not force a later liturgical controversy into the verse.

Deuteronomy 12:5

5But to the place which the LORD your God shall choose out of all your tribes, to put his name there, you shall seek his habitation, and you shall come there.

Arminian

Instead they shall seek “the place which the LORD your God shall choose out of all your tribes, to put his name there,” his habitation, and they shall come there.

Arminian readers hear a place of God’s choosing, not Israel’s. They are to seek his habitation and come, rather than pick a convenient height of their own.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the LORD’s choice among the tribes, and the putting of his name there. The sanctuary is located by his will.

Both traditions refuse to treat this chapter as if it already named the site. Later narrative will record the name as placed at particular centers, and readers have often thought of Shiloh or of Jerusalem, but those identifications are not stated here and should not be presented as the consensus meaning of the verse itself.

Deuteronomy 12:6

6You shall bring your burnt offerings, your sacrifices, your tithes, the wave offering of your hand, your vows, your free will offerings, and the firstborn of your herd and of your flock there.

Arminian

There they shall bring burnt offerings, sacrifices, tithes, the wave offering of the hand, vows, free will offerings, and the firstborn of herd and flock.

Arminian readers see the main stream of Israel’s gifts directed to that one place. Vow and freewill, required and voluntary, all go there.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same full list. What is holy to the LORD is not to be offered at the old high places, but brought to the place of his name.

Both traditions take the verse as a table of sanctuary gifts, on shared descriptive ground.

Deuteronomy 12:7

7There you shall eat before the LORD your God, and you shall rejoice in all that you put your hand to, you and your households, in which the LORD your God has blessed you.

Arminian

There they shall eat before the LORD their God and rejoice in all they put their hand to, they and their households, in which the LORD has blessed them.

Arminian readers hear worship as a shared meal of joy before God, not only as destruction of pagan sites. The household is included, and the blessing already given is the occasion of rejoicing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress eating and rejoicing before the LORD, in the blessing he has given. The chosen place is a place of gladness, not only of slaughter.

Both traditions take joy and household fellowship as part of the commanded worship, without a fork between the traditions.

Deuteronomy 12:8

8You shall not do all the things that we do here today, every man whatever is right in his own eyes;

Arminian

They shall not do all the things that they do here today, “every man whatever is right in his own eyes;”

Arminian readers hear a present, temporary pattern being ended. In the wilderness, practice has been more open; it will not remain the rule once the land’s rest is given.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same contrast between “here today” and the coming order. What is right in a man’s own eyes is not the standard for the LORD’s worship in the land.

Both traditions read the verse as a transition, not as praise of private judgment, and they do not import later uses of the phrase as if they were already quoted here.

Deuteronomy 12:9

9for you haven’t yet come to the rest and to the inheritance which the LORD your God gives you.

Arminian

The reason is that they have not yet come “to the rest and to the inheritance” which the LORD their God gives them.

Arminian readers hear rest and inheritance as still ahead, gifts the LORD gives. The present looseness of verse 8 is tied to being not-yet-home.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that rest and inheritance are what God gives. The verse locates the new sanctuary order after that gift, not before it.

Both traditions take rest and inheritance here as the land’s promised settlement, and they do not treat a later typological use of “rest” as the stated meaning of this line.

Deuteronomy 12:10

10But when you go over the Jordan and dwell in the land which the LORD your God causes you to inherit, and he gives you rest from all your enemies around you, so that you dwell in safety,

Arminian

When they go over the Jordan, dwell in the land the LORD causes them to inherit, and he gives them rest from all enemies so that they dwell in safety, the new order can begin.

Arminian readers hear a sequence: crossing, dwelling, rest, and safety are God’s gifts, and they prepare for the worship described next.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the LORD causes them to inherit and gives rest from enemies. Safety in the land is his doing.

Both traditions read the verse as the condition of time and peace in which the chosen-place law of verse 11 will operate.

Deuteronomy 12:11

11then it shall happen that to the place which the LORD your God shall choose, to cause his name to dwell there, there you shall bring all that I command you: your burnt offerings, your sacrifices, your tithes, the wave offering of your hand, and all your choice vows which you vow to the LORD.

Arminian

Then, to “the place which the LORD your God shall choose, to cause his name to dwell there,” they shall bring all that is commanded: burnt offerings, sacrifices, tithes, the wave offering, and choice vows.

Arminian readers hear the same chosen place, now described as the dwelling of his name, after rest has been given. The gifts of verse 6 are gathered there in the time of settlement.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the purpose clause: he chooses the place to cause his name to dwell. The emphasis remains on his choice, not on Israel’s site selection.

Both traditions keep the caution already stated at verse 5: this chapter does not name the site, and later identifications should not be read back as if they were written here.

Deuteronomy 12:12

12You shall rejoice before the LORD your God—you, and your sons, your daughters, your male servants, your female servants, and the Levite who is within your gates, because he has no portion nor inheritance with you.

Arminian

They shall rejoice before the LORD: they, sons, daughters, male and female servants, and the Levite within their gates, because he has no portion or inheritance with them.

Arminian readers hear a wide table: the whole household and the landless Levite are included in the rejoicing. Joy before God is not for heads of houses only.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the reason: the Levite has no portion or inheritance with them, so he must not be left out of the sanctuary feast.

Both traditions take the verse as a command of inclusive joy, which verse 19 will underline, on shared ground.

Deuteronomy 12:13

13Be careful that you don’t offer your burnt offerings in every place that you see;

Arminian

They must be careful not to offer burnt offerings “in every place that you see;”

Arminian readers hear a curb on convenience. A striking height or a familiar tree is not enough to make a burnt offering lawful.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same care. Sight and preference do not authorize an altar.

Both traditions read the warning as the practical edge of verses 4–5, still without a contrast between the traditions.

Deuteronomy 12:14

14but in the place which the LORD chooses in one of your tribes, there you shall offer your burnt offerings, and there you shall do all that I command you.

Arminian

Burnt offerings are to be offered “in the place which the LORD chooses in one of your tribes,” and there they shall do all that he commands.

Arminian readers hear both the restriction and the completeness: one chosen place, in one of the tribes, and all the commanded worship done there.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note “in one of your tribes,” The choice is particular, not a license for many centers, though the place is still unnamed in this chapter.

Both traditions close the unit on the same shared ground as verse 5, without declaring a later city to be the certain reference of this verse alone.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
