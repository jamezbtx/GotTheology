Passage

Deuteronomy 9

Verses 22–29 · WEB

Moses lists other places where Israel provoked the LORD: Taberah, Massah, Kibroth Hattaavah, and Kadesh Barnea, where they refused to go up and take the land. He then recalls his forty days of intercession, appealing to God’s promise to the patriarchs, Israel’s identity as his redeemed inheritance, and the honor of God’s name among the nations. Both traditions see shared ground in Israel’s persistent rebellion, God’s covenant faithfulness, and the power of intercession grounded in God’s own promises.

Deuteronomy 9:22

22At Taberah, at Massah, and at Kibroth Hattaavah you provoked the LORD to wrath.

Arminian

Taberah, Massah, and Kibroth Hattaavah were places of complaint and judgment (Num 11:1–3; Exod 17:1–7; Num 11:31–34). Arminian readers note that the list shows a repeated pattern of rebellion.

Each name preserves a memory of failure.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the persistence of sin across the wilderness journey.

The place names serve as a catalogue of Israel’s need for grace.

Deuteronomy 9:23

23When the LORD sent you from Kadesh Barnea, saying, “Go up and possess the land which I have given you,” you rebelled against the commandment of the LORD your God, and you didn’t believe him or listen to his voice.

Arminian

At Kadesh Barnea Israel refused to go up, did not believe God, and did not listen (Num 13–14; Deut 1:19–33). Arminian readers stress that unbelief was a real refusal of a real command and promise.

Hebrews 3:16–19 says they could not enter because of unbelief.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see unbelief as the root sin (Heb 3:19).

The command came with a promise, “which I have given you,” yet the people still refused.

Deuteronomy 9:24

24You have been rebellious against the LORD from the day that I knew you.

Arminian

Moses says they have been rebellious from the day he knew them. Arminian readers note the sweeping summary.

Moses speaks from long personal experience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a sober judgment on the human heart apart from grace.

The verse reinforces 9:6: Israel receives the land despite, not because of, its record.

Deuteronomy 9:25

25So I fell down before the LORD the forty days and forty nights that I fell down, because the LORD had said he would destroy you.

Arminian

Moses fell down before the LORD the forty days and nights. Arminian readers note that he returns to the intercession of verse 18 to describe its content.

Whether these forty-day periods are one or more is discussed by interpreters.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the intercession now described in detail.

The reason was God’s declared intent to destroy them, which Moses opposed in prayer.

Deuteronomy 9:26

26I prayed to the LORD, and said, “Lord GOD, don’t destroy your people and your inheritance that you have redeemed through your greatness, that you have brought out of Egypt with a mighty hand.

Arminian

Moses prays: do not destroy your people, your inheritance, whom you redeemed. Arminian readers note that Moses appeals to God’s own saving action.

Israel belongs to God because he redeemed them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see intercession grounded in God’s prior grace. Redemption is the basis of the appeal, not the people’s merit.

The address “Lord GOD” expresses reverent dependence.

Deuteronomy 9:27

27Remember your servants, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Don’t look at the stubbornness of this people, nor at their wickedness, nor at their sin,

Arminian

Moses asks God to remember Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and not look at the people’s stubbornness, wickedness, or sin. Arminian readers note that Moses does not excuse the people.

He appeals to the covenant promises.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the appeal to God’s oath as central (cf. Exod 32:13). Moses does not plead the people’s goodness.

God’s faithfulness to the fathers outweighs the children’s sin. The prayer asks God to look away from their stubbornness for the sake of his promise.

Deuteronomy 9:28

28lest the land you brought us out from say, ‘Because the LORD was not able to bring them into the land which he promised to them, and because he hated them, he has brought them out to kill them in the wilderness.’

Arminian

Moses fears that Egypt would say the LORD could not bring them in or hated them. Arminian readers note the concern for God’s reputation among the nations (cf. Num 14:13–16).

God’s honor is tied to his people’s fate.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s glory as a ground of prayer (cf. Ezek 36:22). Moses prays for God’s name, not only for the people’s survival.

The nations’ accusation would deny either God’s power or his love, and Moses asks God to vindicate both.

Deuteronomy 9:29

29Yet they are your people and your inheritance, which you brought out by your great power and by your outstretched arm.”

Arminian

Yet they are your people and your inheritance, brought out by your power. Arminian readers note the closing appeal to belonging.

God’s investment in Israel is the reason to spare them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the climax of the prayer: Israel is God’s inheritance (Deut 32:9).

The “outstretched arm” recalls the exodus as God’s mighty work.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
