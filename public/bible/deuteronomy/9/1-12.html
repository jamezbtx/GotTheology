Passage

Deuteronomy 9

Verses 1–12 · WEB

As Israel prepares to cross the Jordan against nations stronger than itself, Moses assures them that the LORD goes ahead of them as a devouring fire, but warns them not to think the land is a reward for their righteousness, since they are a stiff-necked people. He then begins to recall their rebellion at Horeb, where he received the tablets after forty days on the mountain while the people made a molten image. Both traditions read this as shared ground on grace: the gift of the land rests on God’s promise and on the nations’ wickedness, not on Israel’s merit.

Deuteronomy 9:1

1Hear, Israel! You are to pass over the Jordan today, to go in to dispossess nations greater and mightier than yourself, cities great and fortified up to the sky,

Arminian

Moses calls Israel to hear: they are about to cross the Jordan against nations greater and mightier than they are. Arminian readers note that he names the danger honestly instead of minimizing it.

The phrase “up to the sky” echoes the spies’ report a generation earlier (Deut 1:28). The same obstacles now face a new generation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Moses begins with Israel’s weakness, not its strength. The nations are greater and mightier, so victory will plainly be God’s work.

The call to “hear” recalls the Shema (Deut 6:4). Israel’s life depends on listening to God’s word.

Deuteronomy 9:2

2a people great and tall, the sons of the Anakim, whom you know, and of whom you have heard say, “Who can stand before the sons of Anak?”

Arminian

The Anakim were the tall people whose reputation had terrified the earlier generation (Num 13:28, 33). Arminian readers note that the saying “Who can stand before the sons of Anak?” shows how fear had become proverbial.

Moses names that fear in order to answer it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same giants that once produced unbelief at Kadesh (Num 13–14). The question is whether Israel will again measure the enemy rather than God.

The proverb is answered in the next verse: the LORD goes before them.

Deuteronomy 9:3

3Know therefore today that the LORD your God is he who goes over before you as a devouring fire. He will destroy them and he will bring them down before you. So you shall drive them out and make them perish quickly, as the LORD has spoken to you.

Arminian

The LORD goes before Israel as a devouring fire (cf. Deut 4:24). Arminian readers stress both sides of the verse: God will destroy the nations, and Israel will drive them out.

Divine action and human obedience work together. Deuteronomy 7:22 speaks of driving them out little by little, and interpreters discuss how that relates to “quickly” here.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the LORD himself goes ahead as a consuming fire (cf. Heb 12:29). Israel’s action follows and depends on his.

“As the LORD has spoken” grounds the victory in God’s promise. The relation to the “little by little” of Deuteronomy 7:22 is discussed by interpreters.

Deuteronomy 9:4

4Don’t say in your heart, after the LORD your God has thrust them out from before you, “For my righteousness the LORD has brought me in to possess this land;” because the LORD drives them out before you because of the wickedness of these nations.

Arminian

Moses warns Israel not to say in their hearts that God brought them in because of their righteousness. Arminian readers see a guard against pride: the gift of the land is not a reward for merit.

The nations are driven out because of their wickedness, which God had long tolerated (Gen 15:16; Lev 18:24–25).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a decisive rejection of merit. God’s gifts are never earned, a principle the New Testament applies to salvation (Titus 3:5).

Both traditions agree here. The land is given by grace, and the nations’ judgment is just.

Deuteronomy 9:5

5Not for your righteousness or for the uprightness of your heart do you go in to possess their land; but for the wickedness of these nations the LORD your God does drive them out from before you, and that he may establish the word which the LORD swore to your fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob.

Arminian

Not for your righteousness or the uprightness of your heart, but because of the nations’ wickedness and God’s oath to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Arminian readers note that God’s faithfulness to his promise is the positive ground of the gift.

Israel’s response still matters for how they live in the land, but it is not why they receive it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see two grounds named: God’s just judgment on the nations and his sworn promise to the patriarchs (Gen 12:7; 26:3; 28:13).

Grace rests on God’s word, not on the recipients’ worth. This is shared ground for both traditions.

Deuteronomy 9:6

6Know therefore that the LORD your God doesn’t give you this good land to possess for your righteousness, for you are a stiff-necked people.

Arminian

For the third time Moses says the land is not given for Israel’s righteousness, and he adds that they are stiff-necked. Arminian readers note the bluntness: the people are reminded of their failures to keep them humble.

The phrase “stiff-necked” comes from the golden-calf story (Exod 32:9; 33:3, 5).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repetition as emphatic. God gives to a stubborn people, which highlights that the gift is grace.

Both traditions read the verse as a warning against pride and a reminder that God’s goodness is undeserved (cf. Eph 2:8–9).

Deuteronomy 9:7

7Remember, and don’t forget, how you provoked the LORD your God to wrath in the wilderness. From the day that you left the land of Egypt until you came to this place, you have been rebellious against the LORD.

Arminian

Moses calls Israel to remember how they provoked the LORD from Egypt until now. Arminian readers note that memory is a tool for humility and faithfulness.

Rebellion was not a single lapse but a pattern the people must own.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the persistent sinfulness of the human heart displayed in Israel’s history (cf. Ps 106).

Remembering sin is meant to drive the people to depend on God’s mercy rather than on themselves.

Deuteronomy 9:8

8Also in Horeb you provoked the LORD to wrath, and the LORD was angry with you to destroy you.

Arminian

At Horeb the LORD was angry enough to destroy them. Arminian readers note that Horeb, the place of covenant-making, became the place of their worst rebellion.

The seriousness of the sin matches the privilege they had received.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gravity of sin committed at the very mountain where God revealed himself.

Only God’s mercy, through Moses’ intercession, prevented destruction.

Deuteronomy 9:9

9When I had gone up onto the mountain to receive the stone tablets, even the tablets of the covenant which the LORD made with you, then I stayed on the mountain forty days and forty nights. I neither ate bread nor drank water.

Arminian

Moses stayed on the mountain forty days and nights without bread or water to receive the tablets. Arminian readers note his devotion and the extraordinary character of the encounter (cf. Exod 24:18; 34:28).

The tablets are called the tablets of the covenant, which the LORD made with the people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses as covenant mediator, receiving God’s law on the people’s behalf.

The forty days recall later forty-day periods, such as Elijah’s journey (1 Kings 19:8) and Jesus’ fast (Matt 4:2).

Deuteronomy 9:10

10The LORD delivered to me the two stone tablets written with God’s finger. On them were all the words which the LORD spoke with you on the mountain out of the middle of the fire in the day of the assembly.

Arminian

The tablets were written with God’s finger and contained all the words spoken from the fire. Arminian readers note the divine origin of the commandments (Exod 31:18).

The law is God’s own word, not Moses’ invention.

Reformed

Reformed readers also stress the divine authorship of the Ten Commandments.

“The day of the assembly” recalls Sinai as the gathering of God’s people to hear his voice (Deut 4:10).

Deuteronomy 9:11

11It came to pass at the end of forty days and forty nights that the LORD gave me the two stone tablets, even the tablets of the covenant.

Arminian

At the end of forty days the LORD gave Moses the tablets. Arminian readers note the solemn repetition of “the tablets of the covenant.”

The moment of receiving the covenant sets up the tragedy that follows.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant in hand at the very moment the people break it.

The contrast between God’s gift and the people’s sin is deliberate.

Deuteronomy 9:12

12The LORD said to me, “Arise, get down quickly from here; for your people whom you have brought out of Egypt have corrupted themselves. They have quickly turned away from the way which I commanded them. They have made a molten image for themselves!”

Arminian

God tells Moses to go down quickly, for “your people” have corrupted themselves and made a molten image. Arminian readers note how quickly they turned from the commanded way.

The same account appears in Exodus 32:7–8.

Reformed

Reformed readers note God’s phrase “your people whom you have brought out,” which some read as God distancing himself from the rebels.

The speed of the apostasy shows how deep the inclination to idolatry runs (Ps 106:19–20; Acts 7:41).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
