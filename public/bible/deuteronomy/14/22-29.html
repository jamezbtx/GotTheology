Passage

Deuteronomy 14

Verses 22–29 · WEB

The chapter turns from what Israel may eat to what Israel must give. Each year Israel is to tithe its produce and eat the tithe, with the firstborn of its herds and flocks, “before the LORD your God” at the place he chooses. If the distance is too great, the tithe may be turned into money and spent at the sanctuary on whatever the household desires, so that the whole family can rejoice before God. The Levite, who has no land inheritance, must not be forgotten. Every third year, the tithe is stored in the towns for the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow (compare 26:12–15). How this tithe relates to the Levitical tithe of Numbers 18:21–24 is debated, and later Jewish tradition counted more than one tithe. Both traditions read the passage as teaching that worship, joy, and generosity belong together, and that God’s people honor him by caring for those without land or support. Both agree that the specific tithe system belonged to Israel’s covenant in the land, though they discuss how its principles bear on Christian giving.

Deuteronomy 14:22

22You shall surely tithe all the increase of your seed, that which comes out of the field year by year.

Arminian

“You shall surely tithe all the increase of your seed, that which comes out of the field year by year.” The tithe acknowledges that the harvest comes from God.

Arminian readers note that regular giving trains God’s people in grateful dependence on him.

Reformed

The annual tithe is a recurring confession that the land and its fruit belong to the LORD.

Reformed readers stress that giving is a response to God’s ownership and provision. Both traditions agree that the verse teaches gratitude, and they discuss how its principle shapes Christian generosity, which the New Testament describes in terms of cheerful, willing giving (compare 2 Cor 9:7).

Deuteronomy 14:23

23You shall eat before the LORD your God, in the place which he chooses to cause his name to dwell, the tithe of your grain, of your new wine, and of your oil, and the firstborn of your herd and of your flock; that you may learn to fear the LORD your God always.

Arminian

The tithe and the firstborn are eaten “before the LORD your God, in the place which he chooses to cause his name to dwell,” “that you may learn to fear the LORD your God always.” The tithe is a shared meal in God’s presence, not only a payment.

Arminian readers note that the purpose is formative: worship teaches Israel to fear God.

Reformed

The meal at the chosen place ties the tithe to the central worship commanded in chapter 12.

Reformed readers stress that God uses ordinary means, such as eating together in his presence, to shape his people’s reverence. Both traditions agree that fearing God here includes awe joined with joy and fellowship.

Deuteronomy 14:24

24If the way is too long for you, so that you are not able to carry it because the place which the LORD your God shall choose to set his name there is too far from you, when the LORD your God blesses you,

Arminian

The law makes room for distance: “If the way is too long for you, so that you are not able to carry it,” especially “when the LORD your God blesses you” with a large harvest.

Arminian readers note that God’s law is practical and considerate of his people’s circumstances.

Reformed

God’s blessing creates the problem of a large harvest, and God’s law solves it.

Reformed readers stress that God anticipates his people’s needs in his commands. Both traditions agree that the provision shows God’s kindness in making worship possible for every Israelite household.

Deuteronomy 14:25

25then you shall turn it into money, bind up the money in your hand, and shall go to the place which the LORD your God shall choose.

Arminian

The tithe may be turned into money and carried to “the place which the LORD your God shall choose.” The change of form does not change its dedication.

Arminian readers note that faithfulness lies in the purpose of the gift, not in its exact form.

Reformed

The money remains devoted to the LORD on the journey.

Reformed readers stress that the purpose of worship governs the means. Both traditions agree that the verse shows flexibility in the form of obedience while keeping its goal intact.

Deuteronomy 14:26

26You shall trade the money for whatever your soul desires: for cattle, or for sheep, or for wine, or for strong drink, or for whatever your soul asks of you. You shall eat there before the LORD your God, and you shall rejoice, you and your household.

Arminian

At the sanctuary the money may be spent “for whatever your soul desires: for cattle, or for sheep, or for wine, or for strong drink, or for whatever your soul asks of you.” Then “you shall rejoice, you and your household.” The tithe becomes a feast of joy.

Arminian readers note that God delights in his people’s enjoyment of his gifts in his presence.

Reformed

Joy is commanded, not just permitted.

Reformed readers stress that God is glorified when his people rejoice in him and his gifts. Both traditions agree that the inclusion of “wine” and “strong drink” must be read alongside Scripture’s warnings against drunkenness, and that the main point is rejoicing before God with the household.

Deuteronomy 14:27

27You shall not forsake the Levite who is within your gates, for he has no portion nor inheritance with you.

Arminian

“You shall not forsake the Levite who is within your gates, for he has no portion nor inheritance with you.” The Levites served God and depended on the people’s faithfulness.

Arminian readers note that joy in God’s presence must include care for others.

Reformed

The Levite’s lack of land inheritance made him dependent on Israel’s obedience.

Reformed readers stress that God provides for those who serve him through the gifts of his people. Both traditions often connect this with the New Testament principle that those who serve the gospel should be supported (compare 1 Cor 9:13–14).

Deuteronomy 14:28

28At the end of every three years you shall bring all the tithe of your increase in the same year, and shall store it within your gates.

Arminian

“At the end of every three years you shall bring all the tithe of your increase in the same year, and shall store it within your gates.” In that year the tithe stays in the local towns rather than going to the sanctuary.

Arminian readers note that generosity is to reach the local community regularly, not only on special occasions.

Reformed

The third-year tithe is stored locally for distribution to those in need.

Reformed readers stress that God builds care for the poor into the regular rhythm of Israel’s life. Both traditions agree that the precise relation of this tithe to the others in the Pentateuch is debated, while its purpose here is clear.

Deuteronomy 14:29

29The Levite, because he has no portion nor inheritance with you, as well as the foreigner living among you, the fatherless, and the widow who are within your gates shall come, and shall eat and be satisfied; that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hand which you do.

Arminian

The Levite, “the foreigner living among you, the fatherless, and the widow” are to “eat and be satisfied; that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hand which you do.” These four groups often appear together in Deuteronomy as those without land or protection.

Arminian readers note that God links his blessing to his people’s generosity toward the vulnerable.

Reformed

God’s concern for the vulnerable reflects his own character as the one who defends the fatherless and the widow and loves the foreigner (10:18).

Reformed readers stress that generosity is the fruit of God’s grace, not a way to earn his blessing. Both traditions agree that the verse shows God’s heart for the poor and that the church is called to the same care (compare Jas 1:27).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
