Passage

Deuteronomy 14

Verses 1–21 · WEB

Chapter 14 begins with Israel’s identity: “You are the children of the LORD your God” and “a holy people to the LORD your God.” From that identity flow two sets of practices. First, Israel must not imitate mourning rites of cutting and shaving (compare Lev 19:28; 21:5), which seem to have been associated with the religious practices of surrounding peoples. Second, Israel is to keep a diet that distinguishes clean from unclean animals, closely paralleling Leviticus 11. Land animals must part the hoof and chew the cud, water creatures must have fins and scales, and certain birds and winged creeping things are forbidden. The chapter does not state why each animal is clean or unclean. Interpreters have proposed hygiene, association with pagan worship, symbolic ideas of wholeness and order, or simply a test of obedience that marked Israel off from the nations (compare Lev 20:24–26). No single theory has won general agreement. Several of the Hebrew animal names, especially among the birds, are uncertain, so the English names are best treated as approximate. Both traditions agree that these laws taught Israel’s holiness and separation to God. Both also agree that they are fulfilled in Christ: Jesus declared “all foods clean” (Mark 7:19), Peter heard, “What God has cleansed, you must not call unclean” (Acts 10:15), and Paul calls food laws “a shadow of the things to come; but the body is Christ’s” (Col 2:17).

Deuteronomy 14:1

1You are the children of the LORD your God. You shall not cut yourselves, nor make any baldness between your eyes for the dead.

Arminian

“You are the children of the LORD your God.” Because of this relationship, Israel must not “cut yourselves, nor make any baldness between your eyes for the dead.” The prohibited rites appear to have been mourning customs among neighboring peoples, though their exact meaning is not stated. Elijah’s contest shows the prophets of Baal cutting themselves in worship (1 Kings 18:28).

Arminian readers note that belonging to God reshapes even how his people grieve.

Reformed

Israel’s sonship is the ground of the command, not its reward.

Reformed readers stress that God’s adoption of Israel calls for a distinct way of life. Both traditions agree that the verse does not forbid grief itself but forbids practices tied to other religions, and they connect it with Paul’s words that believers need not “grieve like the rest, who have no hope” (1 Thess 4:13).

Deuteronomy 14:2

2For you are a holy people to the LORD your God, and the LORD has chosen you to be a people for his own possession, above all peoples who are on the face of the earth.

Arminian

“For you are a holy people to the LORD your God, and the LORD has chosen you to be a people for his own possession, above all peoples who are on the face of the earth.” The verse repeats 7:6 almost word for word. Holiness here is first a status given by God, then a calling to live out.

Arminian readers generally read this election as God’s choice of Israel as a people for covenant service and blessing. They note that individual Israelites still had to respond in faith and obedience.

Reformed

Israel’s holiness rests on God’s choice and possession, not on its merit (compare 7:7–8).

Reformed readers agree that this is the national election of Israel. They generally distinguish it from the election of individuals to salvation, while seeing both as flowing from God’s free love. Both traditions agree that the verse grounds Israel’s distinct practices in God’s prior choice, and that the New Testament applies similar language to the church (1 Pet 2:9).

Deuteronomy 14:3

3You shall not eat any abominable thing.

Arminian

“You shall not eat any abominable thing.” This heading introduces the food laws that follow.

Arminian readers note that God’s people were to show their holiness even at the table, in the ordinary choices of daily life.

Reformed

The word “abominable” marks what was unfit for Israel as God’s holy people.

Reformed readers stress that the food laws belong to the ceremonial law, which served Israel until Christ. Both traditions agree that these foods were unclean for Israel by God’s appointment, not because creation itself is evil, and that Jesus has declared “all foods clean” (Mark 7:19).

Deuteronomy 14:4

4These are the animals which you may eat: the ox, the sheep, the goat,

Arminian

The permitted domestic animals come first: “the ox, the sheep, the goat.” These were also Israel’s main sacrificial animals, so the table and the altar overlapped.

Arminian readers note that God begins with what he allows, which shows that the law is not only a list of prohibitions but a provision for his people.

Reformed

The list begins with the familiar livestock that fed Israel and were offered to God.

Reformed readers see God’s generous provision in what he permits. Both traditions agree that the clean animals were a daily reminder that Israel lived before God in every part of life.

Deuteronomy 14:5

5the deer, the gazelle, the roebuck, the wild goat, the ibex, the antelope, and the chamois.

Arminian

Seven wild animals are permitted: “the deer, the gazelle, the roebuck, the wild goat, the ibex, the antelope, and the chamois.” Several of these Hebrew names are rare, and the exact species is uncertain for some of them, so translations differ. Names such as “the chamois” are best read as traditional approximations.

Arminian readers note that God’s permission extends beyond the farm to the animals of the wild.

Reformed

The list shows that the clean category included game as well as livestock.

Reformed readers note that the uncertainty of some identifications does not affect the law’s purpose of marking Israel as distinct. Both traditions agree that these names should be held loosely as identifications and that the general principle is clear: such animals were permitted for food.

Deuteronomy 14:6

6Every animal that parts the hoof, and has the hoof split in two and chews the cud, among the animals, you may eat.

Arminian

The general rule for land animals is given: “Every animal that parts the hoof, and has the hoof split in two and chews the cud, among the animals, you may eat.” Both signs must be present.

Arminian readers note that the rule gave Israelites a clear, practical way to apply the law to animals not named in the lists.

Reformed

The text gives the criteria but does not give a reason for them.

Reformed readers note that interpreters have proposed several explanations, and no single one is agreed on. Both traditions agree that the rule served to mark out Israel’s distinct life under God, and that this function is fulfilled in Christ.

Deuteronomy 14:7

7Nevertheless these you shall not eat of them that chew the cud, or of those who have the hoof split: the camel, the hare, and the rabbit. Because they chew the cud but don’t part the hoof, they are unclean to you.

Arminian

Animals with only one sign are excluded: “the camel, the hare, and the rabbit. Because they chew the cud but don’t part the hoof, they are unclean to you.” The hare and the animal translated “rabbit” (often identified as the rock hyrax) are not true ruminants, though their chewing motion resembles it. The law describes them by appearance, not by modern classification.

Arminian readers note that the law spoke in the everyday categories Israel could observe.

Reformed

The verse applies the rule to borderline cases.

Reformed readers note that the law uses ordinary observation, not modern biology, to draw its lines. Both traditions agree that this is a matter of descriptive language and does not undercut the text’s truthfulness, and that the identification of the third animal is uncertain.

Deuteronomy 14:8

8The pig, because it has a split hoof but doesn’t chew the cud, is unclean to you. You shall not eat their meat. You shall not touch their carcasses.

Arminian

“The pig, because it has a split hoof but doesn’t chew the cud, is unclean to you.” Israel must neither eat its meat nor touch its carcass. The pig became one of the best-known marks of Jewish distinctiveness.

Arminian readers note that obeying this command visibly set Israel apart from its neighbors.

Reformed

The pig fails the test of having both signs.

Reformed readers stress that the uncleanness was assigned by God for Israel’s covenant life, not because the animal was evil in itself. Both traditions agree that Christ’s declaration of all foods clean ends this distinction for believers. Theories linking the ban to health or to pagan sacrifice are possibilities only, since the text gives no reason.

Deuteronomy 14:9

9These you may eat of all that are in the waters: you may eat whatever has fins and scales.

Arminian

For water creatures, the rule is simple: “you may eat whatever has fins and scales.” Most common fish meet the test.

Arminian readers note that, as with land animals, God gives a general principle that Israel could apply with confidence.

Reformed

The criteria again concern visible features.

Reformed readers note that the reason for the criterion is not stated, and that proposed explanations remain theories. Both traditions agree that the rule is part of the ceremonial law that distinguished Israel.

Deuteronomy 14:10

10You shall not eat whatever doesn’t have fins and scales. It is unclean to you.

Arminian

“You shall not eat whatever doesn’t have fins and scales. It is unclean to you.” This would exclude shellfish and similar creatures.

Arminian readers note that the phrase “unclean to you” limits the status to Israel’s covenant life, not to the creature’s goodness as part of God’s creation.

Reformed

The phrase “to you” marks the uncleanness as relational and covenantal.

Reformed readers connect this with Paul’s statement, “nothing is unclean of itself” (Rom 14:14). Both traditions agree that the creatures themselves are good (Gen 1:31) and that the law marked out Israel’s distinct calling.

Deuteronomy 14:11

11Of all clean birds you may eat.

Arminian

“Of all clean birds you may eat.” The law begins the bird section with permission, then lists the exceptions.

Arminian readers note that permission is the default, and the restrictions are specific and limited.

Reformed

God’s provision is generous, with only named exceptions.

Reformed readers note that, unlike land and water creatures, birds are handled by a list rather than a rule. Both traditions agree that the list approach shows that the purpose was God’s appointment for Israel, not a natural principle Israel could discover on its own.

Deuteronomy 14:12

12But these are they of which you shall not eat: the eagle, the vulture, the osprey,

Arminian

The forbidden birds begin: “the eagle, the vulture, the osprey.” Many of the forbidden birds are birds of prey or scavengers, which some interpreters connect with contact with blood and dead bodies. This is a common suggestion, but the text does not give it.

Arminian readers note that the reason remains God’s to give, and the text calls Israel to obey without a full explanation.

Reformed

The Hebrew names in this list are not all certainly identified, and translations vary.

Reformed readers stress that the main point is clear even where the species are uncertain. Both traditions agree that any explanation about blood or carrion should be offered as a possibility, not as the text’s stated reason.

Deuteronomy 14:13

13the red kite, the falcon, the kite of any kind,

Arminian

The list continues with “the red kite, the falcon, the kite of any kind.” The phrase “of any kind” indicates that whole groups of related birds are included.

Arminian readers note that the phrase extends the command to related species not individually named.

Reformed

The exact birds behind these Hebrew names are uncertain, and English versions differ.

Reformed readers note that the lists teach the principle of separation more than precise classification. Both traditions agree that the identifications should be held loosely.

Deuteronomy 14:14

14every raven of any kind,

Arminian

The law forbids “every raven of any kind.” The raven is a scavenger, the bird Noah sent from the ark (Gen 8:7).

Arminian readers note that even a bird God used to feed Elijah (1 Kings 17:4–6) was unclean for eating, which shows that uncleanness is not a moral judgment on the creature.

Reformed

The phrase “of any kind” brings in the whole crow family.

Reformed readers note that God cares for the raven (compare Luke 12:24) even while it was forbidden as food. Both traditions agree that clean and unclean categories concern Israel’s ritual life, not God’s regard for his creatures.

Deuteronomy 14:15

15the ostrich, the owl, the seagull, the hawk of any kind,

Arminian

The list includes “the ostrich, the owl, the seagull, the hawk of any kind.” Some of these identifications are uncertain, and versions differ especially for the ostrich and the seagull.

Arminian readers note that the specific identity of each bird matters less for the reader than the shape of the law.

Reformed

The uncertainty in identifying several of these birds is widely recognized.

Reformed readers stress that the purpose of the list was clear to Israel even if some details are obscure to modern readers. Both traditions agree that the law marked out Israel’s distinct life before God.

Deuteronomy 14:16

16the little owl, the great owl, the horned owl,

Arminian

Three kinds of owl are named: “the little owl, the great owl, the horned owl.” Owls are nocturnal birds of prey, often associated with ruins and desolate places in the prophets.

Arminian readers note that the law covered birds of the night as well as the day.

Reformed

The precise species are uncertain, and the English names are approximate.

Reformed readers note that the details of the list are less important than the principle that God set the boundaries of Israel’s table. Both traditions agree that the list is part of the ceremonial law fulfilled in Christ.

Deuteronomy 14:17

17the pelican, the vulture, the cormorant,

Arminian

The list goes on with “the pelican, the vulture, the cormorant.” The WEB uses “vulture” here and also in 14:12, reflecting two different Hebrew words that are hard to distinguish in English.

Arminian readers note that the repetition in English shows how difficult it is to match ancient bird names with modern species.

Reformed

These are mostly fish-eating or carrion-eating birds, though identifications vary.

Reformed readers stress that the uncertain names do not obscure the law’s purpose. Both traditions agree that the list served Israel’s holiness and is not binding on the church.

Deuteronomy 14:18

18the stork, the heron after its kind, the hoopoe, and the bat.

Arminian

The list ends with “the stork, the heron after its kind, the hoopoe, and the bat.” The bat is not a bird in modern classification, but the Hebrew category covers flying creatures.

Arminian readers note that the law groups creatures by how they appear and move, not by modern biology.

Reformed

Including the bat shows that the category is flying creatures, not birds in the modern sense.

Reformed readers note that this is a matter of descriptive language, not an error. Both traditions agree that the text speaks in Israel’s everyday categories and that some identifications in the list remain uncertain.

Deuteronomy 14:19

19All winged creeping things are unclean to you. They shall not be eaten.

Arminian

“All winged creeping things are unclean to you. They shall not be eaten.” This refers to flying insects. Leviticus 11:21–22 allows certain kinds, such as locusts, as exceptions.

Arminian readers note that Deuteronomy gives a summary here, while Leviticus supplies more detail.

Reformed

The verse summarizes the rule for swarming winged creatures.

Reformed readers read Deuteronomy and Leviticus together as complementary accounts of the same law. Both traditions agree that the two passages should be harmonized rather than set against each other.

Deuteronomy 14:20

20Of all clean birds you may eat.

Arminian

“Of all clean birds you may eat.” The WEB uses the same wording as 14:11. The Hebrew word here is broader and can mean clean flying creatures, which would include the permitted winged insects of Leviticus 11:21–22.

Arminian readers note that the section closes, as it began, with God’s permission.

Reformed

The repetition frames the bird and insect section with permission at both ends.

Reformed readers note that the framing stresses God’s generosity within the limits he set. Both traditions agree that the verse closes the list of flying creatures and that the precise scope of the Hebrew term is debated.

Deuteronomy 14:21

21You shall not eat of anything that dies of itself. You may give it to the foreigner living among you who is within your gates, that he may eat it; or you may sell it to a foreigner; for you are a holy people to the LORD your God. You shall not boil a young goat in its mother’s milk.

Arminian

Israel must not eat “anything that dies of itself,” but may give it to the resident foreigner or sell it to a foreigner, “for you are a holy people to the LORD your God.” The law binds Israel’s holiness, not other nations. The verse ends: “You shall not boil a young goat in its mother’s milk” (also in Exod 23:19; 34:26). The reason for this last command is not stated. Suggestions include rejection of a pagan ritual, a humane concern, or a refusal to mix the means of life with death. None is certain, and the proposed link to a specific pagan rite is disputed. Later Jewish practice developed from it the separation of meat and dairy.

Arminian readers note that the command reflects God’s concern for the order of life, whatever its precise reason.

Reformed

The distinction between Israel and the foreigner shows that these laws marked Israel’s covenant identity, not universal moral duty.

Reformed readers stress that the food laws were ceremonial and are fulfilled in Christ. Both traditions agree that the reason for the kid-in-milk command is uncertain, and that Christ’s work makes all foods clean (Mark 7:19; Acts 10:15; Col 2:16–17). Both also treat the food laws as shared ground, not a point of dispute between them.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
