Passage

Deuteronomy 33

Verses 1–5 · WEB

Before his death Moses, the man of God, blesses Israel, opening with a poetic picture of the LORD coming from Sinai, Seir, and Paran with his holy ones, giving his law, and loving his people. The prologue speaks of the law Moses commanded as Jacob’s inheritance and of a king in Jeshurun when the tribes gathered, a phrase read either of the LORD or of Moses. Both traditions read the passage as shared ground celebrating God’s revelation, his love for his people, and his kingship, while acknowledging that the Hebrew of these verses is difficult in places.

Deuteronomy 33:1

1This is the blessing with which Moses the man of God blessed the children of Israel before his death.

Arminian

The chapter is introduced as the blessing Moses, the man of God, gave Israel before his death. Arminian readers note the parallel with Jacob’s blessing of his sons (Gen 49), as a leader’s last words shape the people’s future.

The title “man of God” marks Moses as God’s spokesman (cf. Josh 14:6; Ps 90 title).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a father-like blessing from the covenant mediator at the end of his life.

The blessing expresses God’s purposes for his people, not merely Moses’ wishes. It is spoken in faith that God will do what he has promised.

Deuteronomy 33:2

2He said, “The LORD came from Sinai, and rose from Seir to them. He shone from Mount Paran. He came from the ten thousands of holy ones. At his right hand was a fiery law for them.

Arminian

The LORD comes from Sinai, Seir, and Paran, with holy ones and a fiery law at his right hand. Arminian readers note similar poetic pictures of God marching from the south (Judg 5:4–5; Hab 3:3).

The Hebrew is obscure, and the WEB footnote gives a different reading from another manuscript. Translations vary considerably.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s majestic self-revelation at Sinai. The New Testament speaks of the law being given through angels (Acts 7:53; Gal 3:19; Heb 2:2), and many readers connect that with the holy ones here.

Because the Hebrew is uncertain, both traditions hold such connections loosely.

Deuteronomy 33:3

3Yes, he loves the people. All his saints are in your hand. They sat down at your feet. Each receives your words.

Arminian

The verse begins, “Yes, he loves the people.” Arminian readers note that God’s love is the ground of the law and the covenant (cf. Deut 7:7–8).

The people sit at his feet and receive his words, a picture of attentive discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s love for his people and his keeping care: “All his saints are in your hand.” Christians often recall Jesus’ promise that no one can snatch his sheep from his hand (John 10:28), as a reading.

The Hebrew of this verse is difficult, and translations differ on its details.

Deuteronomy 33:4

4Moses commanded us a law, an inheritance for the assembly of Jacob.

Arminian

“Moses commanded us a law, an inheritance for the assembly of Jacob.” Arminian readers note that the law is called an inheritance, a treasured gift passed down (cf. Ps 119:111).

The first-person “us” suggests the voice of the people speaking about Moses.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the law as a gift to be cherished, not a burden.

The shift in voice, with the people speaking about Moses, is one of several features interpreters note in the poem’s composition.

Deuteronomy 33:5

5He was king in Jeshurun, when the heads of the people were gathered, all the tribes of Israel together.

Arminian

“He was king in Jeshurun” can be read of the LORD or of Moses. Arminian readers often note that the LORD’s kingship over Israel is a theme elsewhere (Exod 15:18; Judg 8:23; 1 Sam 8:7).

Others read it of Moses as the leader when the tribes gathered. Jeshurun is a poetic name for Israel (Deut 32:15; Isa 44:2).

Reformed

Reformed readers also weigh both readings. Many readers prefer the reading that the LORD became king at Sinai, when the tribes were gathered.

Both readings are held in both traditions. Either way, Israel’s life is ordered under God’s rule.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
