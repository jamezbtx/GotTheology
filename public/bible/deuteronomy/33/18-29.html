Passage

Deuteronomy 33

Verses 18–29 · WEB

Moses blesses Zebulun and Issachar, Gad, Dan, Naphtali, and Asher, then closes with praise: there is no one like Israel’s God, who rides the heavens to help them, whose everlasting arms are underneath them, and who makes them a people saved by the LORD. Several place and image references are uncertain. Both traditions read this as shared ground celebrating God’s protection and salvation of his people, and the closing verses have long been loved pastorally.

Deuteronomy 33:18

18About Zebulun he said, “Rejoice, Zebulun, in your going out; and Issachar, in your tents.

Arminian

Zebulun is to rejoice in going out and Issachar in their tents. Arminian readers note the pairing, as in Jacob’s blessing (Gen 49:13–15).

One tribe’s activity and the other’s settled life are both blessed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God blessing different callings: going out in trade or travel and staying in the tents at home.

Both are occasions for rejoicing, since every lawful calling can be lived before God. The pairing of these two tribes echoes Jacob’s blessing (Gen 49:13–15).

Deuteronomy 33:19

19They will call the peoples to the mountain. There they will offer sacrifices of righteousness, for they will draw out the abundance of the seas, the hidden treasures of the sand.”

Arminian

They will call peoples to the mountain, offer sacrifices of righteousness, and draw wealth from the seas and sand. Arminian readers note the worship and prosperity combined.

The mountain is not named; some suggest Tabor (cf. Judg 4:6), but the identification is uncertain.

Reformed

Reformed readers see right worship (“sacrifices of righteousness,” cf. Ps 4:5; 51:19) linked with God’s provision.

Some Christian readers see a foreshadowing of the nations coming to worship (cf. Isa 2:3), as a reading.

Deuteronomy 33:20

20About Gad he said, “He who enlarges Gad is blessed. He dwells as a lioness, and tears the arm and the crown of the head.

Arminian

Blessed is he who enlarges Gad; Gad dwells like a lioness. Arminian readers note Gad’s strength and expanded territory east of the Jordan (Num 32).

The image recalls Jacob’s blessing (Gen 49:19).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the one who enlarges Gad, so Gad’s expansion is a gift rather than an achievement.

The lioness image suggests fierce defense of the territory God gave. The tribe’s strength is meant to serve the whole people, as the next verse shows.

Deuteronomy 33:21

21He provided the first part for himself, for the lawgiver’s portion was reserved for him. He came with the heads of the people. He executed the righteousness of the LORD, His ordinances with Israel.”

Arminian

Gad chose the first portion and carried out the LORD’s righteousness with Israel. Arminian readers connect this with Gad’s settlement east of the Jordan and its promise to fight alongside the other tribes (Num 32:20–22; Josh 22:1–4).

The phrase about the lawgiver’s portion is obscure.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Gad keeping its commitment to the whole people, coming with the heads of the people and carrying out the LORD’s righteousness.

The verse honors faithfulness to covenant obligations even after receiving one’s own portion first.

Deuteronomy 33:22

22About Dan he said, “Dan is a lion’s cub that leaps out of Bashan.”

Arminian

Dan is a lion’s cub that leaps from Bashan. Arminian readers note the image of sudden strength.

Dan later migrated north (Judg 18), though the connection with Bashan here is discussed.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see vigor in the image of a lion’s cub, as in Jacob’s blessing of Dan (Gen 49:16–17).

The blessing is brief. Its link with Bashan and with Dan’s later migration is discussed, so readers should not press the geography.

Deuteronomy 33:23

23About Naphtali he said, “Naphtali, satisfied with favor, full of the LORD’s blessing, Possess the west and the south.”

Arminian

Naphtali is satisfied with favor and full of the LORD’s blessing, to possess the west and the south. Arminian readers note the fullness of the blessing.

The geographical terms are debated; Naphtali’s territory lay in Galilee.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s abundant favor.

Christians sometimes recall that Jesus’ ministry began in the region of Naphtali (Matt 4:13–16; Isa 9:1–2), as a reading.

Deuteronomy 33:24

24About Asher he said, “Asher is blessed with children. Let him be acceptable to his brothers. Let him dip his foot in oil.

Arminian

Asher is blessed with children, acceptable to his brothers, dipping his foot in oil. Arminian readers note the image of prosperity, perhaps olive oil (cf. Gen 49:20).

Asher’s blessing is abundance shared in harmony.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see blessing in fruitfulness, family, and goodwill among the tribes.

The oil image suggests abundance so great that one could dip a foot in it. Being “acceptable to his brothers” shows that blessing includes harmony within God’s people.

Deuteronomy 33:25

25Your bars will be iron and bronze. As your days, so your strength will be.

Arminian

Asher’s bars will be iron and bronze; as your days, so your strength. Arminian readers note the promise of security and sustaining strength.

The Hebrew of the second line is uncertain, but the WEB rendering has been a source of comfort.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s promise to sustain his people day by day. Christians often apply the line to God’s grace being sufficient in weakness (2 Cor 12:9), as an application.

Strength is given according to need, and the bars of iron and bronze picture secure defenses.

Deuteronomy 33:26

26“There is no one like God, Jeshurun, who rides on the heavens for your help, in his excellency on the skies.

Arminian

There is no one like God, who rides the heavens for Jeshurun’s help. Arminian readers note the closing praise (cf. Exod 15:11; Ps 68:4, 33).

The blessings end by turning to God himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the incomparable majesty of God and his commitment to help his people. He “rides on the heavens” for their sake.

The poem’s climax is God himself, not the tribes. Every blessing named earlier flows from him.

Deuteronomy 33:27

27The eternal God is your dwelling place. Underneath are the everlasting arms. He thrust out the enemy from before you, and said, ‘Destroy!’

Arminian

The eternal God is your dwelling place; underneath are the everlasting arms. Arminian readers find deep comfort in God as refuge and support (cf. Ps 90:1).

God also drives out enemies before his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s sustaining care (cf. Isa 46:4). This verse has long been loved pastorally in both traditions.

The everlasting arms beneath speak of security that does not fail. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 33:28

28Israel dwells in safety, the fountain of Jacob alone, In a land of grain and new wine. Yes, his heavens drop down dew.

Arminian

Israel dwells safely, the fountain of Jacob alone, in a land of grain and wine. Arminian readers note the promise of security and abundance (cf. Num 23:9).

God’s blessing includes rain and harvest.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s people set apart and secure in his provision (cf. Jer 23:6).

The dew from heaven is a sign of God’s favor in a land that depended on rain. Israel’s safety rests on God, not on its own strength.

Deuteronomy 33:29

29You are happy, Israel! Who is like you, a people saved by the LORD, the shield of your help, the sword of your excellency? Your enemies will submit themselves to you. You will tread on their high places.”

Arminian

Happy is Israel, a people saved by the LORD, their shield and sword. Arminian readers note the blessing of salvation (cf. Ps 33:12; 144:15).

God protects and fights for his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see salvation as God’s work: “a people saved by the LORD.”

God is both shield and sword (cf. Gen 15:1). This closing praise is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
