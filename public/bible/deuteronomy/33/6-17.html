Passage

Deuteronomy 33

Verses 6–17 · WEB

Moses blesses Reuben, Judah, Levi, Benjamin, and Joseph in turn: Reuben is to live, Judah is to be helped against enemies, Levi is to teach and serve at the altar, Benjamin is to dwell securely with the LORD, and Joseph is to receive the richest gifts of land and strength through Ephraim and Manasseh. Simeon is not mentioned in the chapter, and interpreters discuss why. Both traditions read these blessings as shared ground about God’s varied gifts and callings among his people.

Deuteronomy 33:6

6“Let Reuben live, and not die; Nor let his men be few.”

Arminian

Reuben’s blessing is a prayer that he live and not die, and that his men not be few. Arminian readers note the contrast with Jacob’s words about Reuben losing his preeminence (Gen 49:3–4; 1 Chron 5:1).

Grace preserves a tribe that had fallen.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s mercy in sustaining Reuben despite Jacob’s judgment. The prayer for survival may reflect Reuben’s exposed position east of the Jordan (Num 32), though the text does not say so.

Simeon is not named anywhere in the chapter. Many connect this with Jacob’s word that Simeon would be scattered (Gen 49:5–7) and with Simeon’s inheritance lying within Judah (Josh 19:1, 9), but the reason for the omission is discussed and not stated in the text.

Deuteronomy 33:7

7This is for Judah. He said, “Hear, LORD, the voice of Judah. Bring him in to his people. With his hands he contended for himself. You shall be a help against his adversaries.”

Arminian

Moses prays that the LORD hear Judah’s voice, bring him in to his people, and help him against his adversaries. Arminian readers note the dependence on God in Judah’s struggles. Jacob’s blessing had promised Judah leadership (Gen 49:8–12).

Simeon’s absence from the list is sometimes linked with Judah, since Simeon’s territory lay within Judah’s (Josh 19:9). That explanation is one proposal among several.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Judah’s future kingship in the background, which Christians connect with the Messiah from Judah (Gen 49:10; Rev 5:5), as a reading.

The phrase “bring him in to his people” is obscure, and interpreters differ on its meaning.

Deuteronomy 33:8

8About Levi he said, “Your Thummim and your Urim are with your godly one, whom you proved at Massah, with whom you contended at the waters of Meribah.

Arminian

Levi’s blessing begins with the Urim and Thummim, used for discerning God’s will (Exod 28:30). Arminian readers note the reference to Massah and Meribah, places of testing (Exod 17:7; Num 20:13).

Who the “godly one” is, whether the tribe or Aaron or Moses, is discussed by interpreters.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priestly tribe entrusted with means of seeking God’s guidance.

The mention of testing suggests that God’s servants are proved before they are entrusted.

Deuteronomy 33:9

9He said of his father, and of his mother, ‘I have not seen him.’ He didn’t acknowledge his brothers, nor did he know his own children; for they have observed your word, and keep your covenant.

Arminian

Levi did not acknowledge father, mother, brothers, or children because they kept God’s word and covenant. Arminian readers connect this with the Levites’ loyalty at the golden calf (Exod 32:26–29).

Jesus speaks of loving him more than family (Matt 10:37; Luke 14:26).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Exodus 32:26–29 behind the verse. Covenant loyalty took priority over family ties.

The Levites are praised because they observed God’s word and kept his covenant, which shows that their calling rested on faithfulness, not only on descent.

Deuteronomy 33:10

10They shall teach Jacob your ordinances, and Israel your law. They shall put incense before you, and whole burnt offering on your altar.

Arminian

Levi will teach Israel God’s ordinances and law and offer incense and burnt offerings. Arminian readers stress the teaching role of the priests (Lev 10:11; Mal 2:6–7).

Worship and instruction belong together.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the ministry of word and worship. Teaching God’s law was central to the priestly calling (Neh 8:7–8).

Christians often draw from this the importance of teaching in the church.

Deuteronomy 33:11

11LORD, bless his skills. Accept the work of his hands. Strike through the hips of those who rise up against him, of those who hate him, that they not rise again.”

Arminian

Moses prays that the LORD bless Levi’s skills, accept his work, and strike his enemies. Arminian readers note that the priests’ work depends on God’s acceptance.

The prayer for protection reflects the opposition priestly faithfulness might provoke.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the one who makes ministry acceptable and protects his servants.

The verse ends Levi’s blessing with a prayer for God’s defense of his ministers.

Deuteronomy 33:12

12About Benjamin he said, “The beloved of the LORD will dwell in safety by him. He covers him all day long. He dwells between his shoulders.”

Arminian

Benjamin, the beloved of the LORD, will dwell safely by him; God covers him and dwells between his shoulders. Arminian readers note the tender image of God’s protection.

The phrase “between his shoulders” may picture being carried, or may refer to Benjamin’s territory near the later temple site (cf. Josh 18:16, 28). Interpreters differ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s protective love for his people.

The verse is often read devotionally as a picture of resting securely in God’s care.

Deuteronomy 33:13

13About Joseph he said, “His land is blessed by the LORD, for the precious things of the heavens, for the dew, for the deep that couches beneath,

Arminian

Joseph’s land is blessed by the LORD with dew from heaven and water from the deep. Arminian readers note the parallel with Jacob’s blessing of Joseph (Gen 49:25).

The blessing is generous and agricultural.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the source of every good gift in creation.

The “precious things” repeated in the following verses stress abundance.

Deuteronomy 33:14

14for the precious things of the fruits of the sun, for the precious things that the moon can yield,

Arminian

The blessing includes the fruits of the sun and what the moon yields. Arminian readers note the seasonal rhythms of growth.

God’s provision comes through the created order.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence working through sun and moon (cf. Gen 1:14–18).

The imagery affirms the goodness of creation’s gifts.

Deuteronomy 33:15

15for the best things of the ancient mountains, for the precious things of the everlasting hills,

Arminian

The best things of the ancient mountains and everlasting hills. Arminian readers note the poetic stress on permanence and abundance (cf. Gen 49:26).

Joseph’s land is pictured as richly endowed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s ancient purposes reflected in the land’s gifts.

The “everlasting hills” suggest stability and long-lasting blessing.

Deuteronomy 33:16

16for the precious things of the earth and its fullness, the good will of him who lived in the bush. Let this come on the head of Joseph, on the crown of the head of him who was separated from his brothers.

Arminian

The precious things of the earth and the good will of him who lived in the bush. Arminian readers note the reference to the burning bush (Exod 3:2–4), as the WEB footnote indicates.

God’s favor is the greatest gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the LORD who revealed himself in the bush as the source of Joseph’s blessing.

Joseph, separated from his brothers (Gen 49:26), receives special blessing.

Deuteronomy 33:17

17Majesty belongs to the firstborn of his herd. His horns are the horns of the wild ox. With them he will push all the peoples to the ends of the earth. They are the ten thousands of Ephraim. They are the thousands of Manasseh.”

Arminian

Joseph’s majesty is like a firstborn bull, with horns of the wild ox, through Ephraim’s ten thousands and Manasseh’s thousands. Arminian readers note the imagery of strength (cf. Num 23:22; 24:8).

Ephraim, the younger, is named with the larger number (cf. Gen 48:19–20).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign choice of the younger over the elder, a recurring biblical pattern (Gen 48:19–20).

The blessing ends with Joseph’s strength as God’s gift.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
