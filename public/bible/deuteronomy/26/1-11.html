Passage

Deuteronomy 26

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Once Israel is settled in the land, each family is to bring the first of its harvest in a basket to the place God chooses. The worshiper confesses before the LORD a short history of redemption: from a wandering ancestor to slavery in Egypt, to God’s mighty rescue, to the gift of a land flowing with milk and honey. Then the household rejoices together with the Levite and the foreigner.

Deuteronomy 26:1

1It shall be, when you have come in to the land which the LORD your God gives you for an inheritance, possess it, and dwell in it,

Arminian

The law looks ahead to life in the land God gives as an inheritance. Worship will flow from receiving that gift, once the people possess and dwell in it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same order: God gives the land, the people possess it, and then they bring firstfruits in grateful worship. Grace precedes the offering. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 26:2

2that you shall take some of the first of all the fruit of the ground, which you shall bring in from your land that the LORD your God gives you. You shall put it in a basket, and shall go to the place which the LORD your God shall choose to cause his name to dwell there.

Arminian

The first of the harvest is put in a basket and brought to the place God chooses for his name. The firstfruits acknowledge that the whole harvest belongs to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Paul uses firstfruits imagery for Christ’s resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:20). This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 26:3

3You shall come to the priest who shall be in those days, and tell him, “I profess today to the LORD your God, that I have come to the land which the LORD swore to our fathers to give us.”

Arminian

The worshiper tells the priest that he has come into the land God swore to give. Each Israelite personally confesses God’s faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same personal confession of a corporate promise: each worshiper stands before the priest and says that God has kept the oath to the fathers. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 26:4

4The priest shall take the basket out of your hand, and set it down before the LORD your God’s altar.

Arminian

The priest takes the basket and sets it before the altar of the LORD. The gift is presented to God, not kept as the priest’s own, though the priesthood serves in the offering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the priest acting as a minister, placing the basket before the LORD’s altar. The offering is directed to God. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 26:5

5You shall answer and say before the LORD your God, “My father was a Syrian ready to perish. He went down into Egypt, and lived there, few in number. There he became a great, mighty, and populous nation.

Arminian

“My father was a Syrian ready to perish.” The ancestor is usually identified as Jacob, who lived in Aram with Laban. The Hebrew word rendered “ready to perish” can also mean “wandering.” Israel’s story begins in weakness.

Reformed

Reformed readers share the identification with Jacob and note the same range of meaning. Both traditions see grace in God choosing a small, struggling family.

Deuteronomy 26:6

6The Egyptians mistreated us, afflicted us, and imposed hard labor on us.

Arminian

The Egyptians mistreated, afflicted, and imposed hard labor on Israel. The confession remembers suffering honestly, not skipping from promise to plenty.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the confession naming Egypt’s oppression in full. Gratitude for redemption includes telling the truth about the bondage God ended. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 26:7

7Then we cried to the LORD, the God of our fathers. The LORD heard our voice, and saw our affliction, our toil, and our oppression.

Arminian

Israel cried out, and the LORD heard, saw, and knew their suffering. God’s compassion moves him to act (compare Exodus 3:7).

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same link to Exodus 3, where the LORD hears, sees, and comes down to deliver. The firstfruits liturgy retells that mercy. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 26:8

8The LORD brought us out of Egypt with a mighty hand, with an outstretched arm, with great terror, with signs, and with wonders;

Arminian

God brought them out with a mighty hand, an outstretched arm, terror, signs, and wonders. Redemption is God’s work from start to finish.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the same point. Both traditions treat the exodus as a pattern of salvation fulfilled in Christ. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 26:9

9and he has brought us into this place, and has given us this land, a land flowing with milk and honey.

Arminian

God brought them into this place and gave them a land flowing with milk and honey. Redemption leads to inheritance, and the gift is described in the language of the promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the land is God’s gift, described in the words of the promise. Redemption and inheritance belong together in the confession. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 26:10

10Now, behold, I have brought the first of the fruit of the ground, which you, the LORD, have given me.” You shall set it down before the LORD your God, and worship before the LORD your God.

Arminian

The worshiper says, “I have brought the first of the fruit of the ground, which you, the LORD, have given me,” then bows in worship. Giving back is a response to receiving.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same logic: God gives the fruit, and his people return the first of it with worship. The offering is thanksgiving, not payment that earns the land. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 26:11

11You shall rejoice in all the good which the LORD your God has given to you, and to your house, you, and the Levite, and the foreigner who is among you.

Arminian

The household rejoices with the Levite and the foreigner. Gratitude becomes shared celebration that includes those without land.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see commanded joy that includes the Levite and the foreigner, those without a share of land. Thanksgiving is to be shared, not kept in the family alone. This is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
