Passage

Deuteronomy 13

Verses 1–5 · WEB

Deuteronomy 12 called Israel to worship the LORD only in the place he would choose and in the way he commanded. Chapter 13 now guards that worship against those who would lead Israel to other gods. The first case is a prophet or a dreamer whose sign or wonder actually comes to pass. A fulfilled sign does not validate a message that says, “Let’s go after other gods.” What the prophet says about God is the test, not what he can do. In Hebrew Bibles, English 12:32 is numbered 13:1, so English 13:1–5 are 13:2–6 there. Both traditions agree that the LORD “is testing you” (13:3) in order to expose and prove Israel’s love, not because he lacks knowledge. They also agree that the death penalty belonged to Israel’s theocratic covenant, where the nation was God’s holy people under his direct rule. Both see the New Testament applying this charge to guard the truth through church discipline and spiritual discernment, not through civil penalties.

Deuteronomy 13:1

1If a prophet or a dreamer of dreams arises among you, and he gives you a sign or a wonder,

Arminian

“If a prophet or a dreamer of dreams arises among you” names the two familiar channels of revelation in Israel, prophetic speech and dreams (compare Num 12:6). The case assumes he “gives you a sign or a wonder,” something that looks like divine backing.

Arminian readers note that God’s people are expected to weigh what they hear, not simply accept a speaker because of his gifts or powers.

Reformed

The danger arises “among you,” from within the covenant community, not from an outside nation.

Reformed readers stress that Scripture never treats signs as self-authenticating apart from the word God has already spoken. Both traditions agree that the passage teaches Israel to test claims of revelation by God’s revealed truth.

Deuteronomy 13:2

2and the sign or the wonder comes to pass, of which he spoke to you, saying, “Let’s go after other gods” (which you have not known) “and let’s serve them,”

Arminian

The sign “comes to pass,” yet the message is “Let’s go after other gods” (which you have not known). Fulfillment alone does not prove a prophet true. Deuteronomy 18:20–22 adds that a prophet whose word fails has not spoken from the LORD; together the two passages give a failed prediction and a false god as separate tests.

Arminian readers see here a call for responsible discernment, since a real wonder can still serve a false message.

Reformed

The phrase “which you have not known” contrasts these gods with the LORD, who made himself known to Israel in redemption and covenant.

Reformed readers stress that faithfulness to the God who has revealed himself is the decisive test of any message. Both traditions connect the warning with Jesus’ words that false prophets will “show great signs and wonders” (Matt 24:24), and with Paul’s warning about “signs and lying wonders” (2 Thess 2:9).

Deuteronomy 13:3

3you shall not listen to the words of that prophet, or to that dreamer of dreams; for the LORD your God is testing you, to know whether you love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul.

Arminian

Israel must not listen, “for the LORD your God is testing you, to know whether you love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul.” The phrase echoes the Shema (6:5). The test exposes whether Israel’s love is real.

Arminian readers stress that the test is a genuine call to faithfulness, in which Israel’s response truly matters. They agree that “to know” does not mean God lacks knowledge; it means the test brings Israel’s heart into the open.

Reformed

God permits even a deceiving sign to serve his purpose of proving his people.

Reformed readers stress that God’s sovereign rule extends over the false prophet’s success without making God the author of the deception. Both traditions agree that God is not ignorant of Israel’s heart, that the testing is for Israel’s sake, and that love for God is expressed in refusing rival claims. This is largely shared ground.

Deuteronomy 13:4

4You shall walk after the LORD your God, fear him, keep his commandments, and obey his voice. You shall serve him, and cling to him.

Arminian

The positive charge follows: “You shall walk after the LORD your God, fear him, keep his commandments, and obey his voice. You shall serve him, and cling to him.” The verbs answer the false prophet’s “Let’s go after other gods” with whole-life loyalty to the LORD.

Arminian readers note that clinging to God is presented as Israel’s continuing responsibility in the covenant relationship.

Reformed

The verse gathers covenant loyalty into six verbs, ending with “cling to him,” a word used of close attachment.

Reformed readers stress that such clinging is the fruit of the covenant God who first redeemed Israel. Both traditions agree that true worship is defined by love, reverence, and obedience toward the God who has spoken.

Deuteronomy 13:5

5That prophet, or that dreamer of dreams, shall be put to death, because he has spoken rebellion against the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt and redeemed you out of the house of bondage, to draw you aside out of the way which the LORD your God commanded you to walk in. So you shall remove the evil from among you.

Arminian

The prophet “shall be put to death, because he has spoken rebellion against the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt.” His crime is treason against Israel’s divine King and Redeemer, and the verdict closes with “So you shall remove the evil from among you.” Elsewhere in Deuteronomy, the penalty follows legal process (compare 17:6).

Arminian readers note that the warning shows that turning aside from the LORD was a real danger for members of the covenant people.

Reformed

The penalty belongs to Israel’s theocracy, in which the nation was both the worshiping community and the civil order under God’s direct rule.

Reformed readers commonly distinguish Israel’s civil laws, which expired with that nation’s unique status, from the moral principles they express. Arminian and Wesleyan readers commonly use the same distinction (compare Wesley’s Article VI). Both traditions note that Paul applies the formula of this verse to church discipline: “Put away the wicked man from among yourselves” (1 Cor 5:13). The church guards its fellowship by exclusion, not by the sword.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
