Passage

Deuteronomy 13

Verses 12–18 · WEB

The third case concerns a whole Israelite city drawn into idolatry by “certain wicked fellows” from among its people. Before anything is done, Israel must “inquire, investigate, and ask diligently” and confirm that the report is true and certain. If it is, the city is to be devoted to destruction, the same treatment given to the Canaanite cities (compare 7:1–5; 20:16–18). Its plunder is to be burned as a whole offering to the LORD, and it is never to be rebuilt. Idolatry inside Israel is treated as seriously as idolatry outside it. Both traditions read this as a law of Israel’s theocratic covenant in the land, not a pattern for the church or for civil states. Both note that the New Testament points instead to the church’s spiritual discipline and to God’s final judgment. The closing promise of mercy and multiplication shows that the aim is the restoration of covenant blessing.

Deuteronomy 13:12

12If you hear about one of your cities, which the LORD your God gives you to dwell there, that

Arminian

The case begins with a report: “If you hear about one of your cities, which the LORD your God gives you to dwell there.” The city is Israel’s own, given by God.

Arminian readers note that a city receiving God’s gift could still turn away from him, which shows that the covenant blessings could be forfeited.

Reformed

The city is described as God’s gift, which makes its apostasy a betrayal of the giver.

Reformed readers stress that the land belonged to the LORD and that its cities were held under his covenant terms. Both traditions agree that the verse sets the case firmly inside Israel, not among the nations.

Deuteronomy 13:13

13certain wicked fellows have gone out from among you and have drawn away the inhabitants of their city, saying, “Let’s go and serve other gods,” which you have not known,

Arminian

The verse speaks of “certain wicked fellows” who “have gone out from among you and have drawn away the inhabitants of their city.” The Hebrew phrase behind “wicked fellows” is rendered in other versions as sons of Belial or worthless men. Their message repeats the earlier invitation: “Let’s go and serve other gods.”

Arminian readers note that a few determined people can lead many astray, which makes leadership and influence morally weighty.

Reformed

The leaders come from “among you,” which shows that apostasy can arise inside the covenant community.

Reformed readers distinguish between outward membership in the covenant people and true faithfulness to God. Both traditions agree that those who draw others into idolatry bear a special guilt.

Deuteronomy 13:14

14then you shall inquire, investigate, and ask diligently. Behold, if it is true, and the thing certain, that such abomination was done among you,

Arminian

Israel must “inquire, investigate, and ask diligently.” Only if “it is true, and the thing certain” may the community act. Three verbs and a double confirmation require careful judgment.

Arminian readers stress the fairness of the process and the responsibility of the judges to establish the facts.

Reformed

The demand for thorough inquiry guards against rumor and false accusation.

Reformed readers note that God’s law protects justice even when dealing with the gravest crime. Both traditions agree that the verse models due process and that hasty judgment is ruled out.

Deuteronomy 13:15

15you shall surely strike the inhabitants of that city with the edge of the sword, destroying it utterly, with all that is therein and its livestock, with the edge of the sword.

Arminian

The guilty city is to be struck “with the edge of the sword, destroying it utterly, with all that is therein and its livestock.” The phrase “destroying it utterly” translates the language of devoting something to God for destruction. This is the treatment prescribed for the Canaanite cities.

Arminian readers note that God shows no partiality, so Israel’s cities that adopt Canaanite worship face Canaanite judgment.

Reformed

The ban expresses God’s holy judgment on idolatry within the land he claims as his own.

Reformed readers read the command as a unique part of Israel’s theocratic and redemptive-historical setting. Both traditions agree that it is not a pattern for Christian action and that the church has no warrant to use the sword in matters of faith. Many in both traditions see such judgments as foreshadowing God’s final judgment.

Deuteronomy 13:16

16You shall gather all its plunder into the middle of its street, and shall burn with fire the city, with all of its plunder, to the LORD your God. It shall be a heap forever. It shall not be built again.

Arminian

The plunder is gathered and burned “to the LORD your God,” and the city becomes “a heap forever. It shall not be built again.” No one may profit from the judgment.

Arminian readers note that the destruction is not a reward for the victors but a solemn act of judgment before God.

Reformed

The burned city is offered wholly to God, like a whole offering.

Reformed readers stress that the ban removes any motive of greed from the act. Both traditions agree that the permanent ruin was meant to be a lasting warning to Israel of the seriousness of idolatry.

Deuteronomy 13:17

17Nothing of the devoted thing shall cling to your hand, that the LORD may turn from the fierceness of his anger and show you mercy, and have compassion on you and multiply you, as he has sworn to your fathers,

Arminian

“Nothing of the devoted thing shall cling to your hand.” The aim is “that the LORD may turn from the fierceness of his anger and show you mercy, and have compassion on you and multiply you, as he has sworn to your fathers.” Achan’s sin in Joshua 7 shows what happens when this command is broken.

Arminian readers note that God’s mercy is linked to the people’s obedience in carrying out his word.

Reformed

The verse moves from wrath to mercy and compassion and to the oath sworn to the fathers.

Reformed readers stress that God’s mercy rests on his sworn promise to the patriarchs. Both traditions agree that the verse shows that God’s goal in judgment is to remove what provokes his anger and restore his people to blessing.

Deuteronomy 13:18

18when you listen to the LORD your God’s voice, to keep all his commandments which I command you today, to do that which is right in the LORD your God’s eyes.

Arminian

The promise holds “when you listen to the LORD your God’s voice, to keep all his commandments which I command you today, to do that which is right in the LORD your God’s eyes.” The chapter ends where it began, with listening to the right voice.

Arminian readers stress that the covenant blessing is joined to the people’s continued faithfulness.

Reformed

Listening to God’s voice is the opposite of listening to the false prophet (13:3).

Reformed readers stress that obedience is the response God requires of the people he has already redeemed. Both traditions agree that the chapter’s goal is a people who hear and do what is right in God’s eyes, and they see that goal fulfilled in a people who follow Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
