Passage

Deuteronomy 13

Verses 6–11 · WEB

The second case moves from public prophets to private relationships. The enticer may be the closest relative or friend, “your friend who is as your own soul,” and the invitation is secret: “Let’s go and serve other gods.” Israel is not to pity, spare, or conceal such a person, and the one enticed is to be first in carrying out the sentence. The law confronts the pull of family loyalty against loyalty to the LORD. Both traditions read the passage as showing how seriously God regards his people being led away from him. Both note that Jesus teaches that loyalty to him outranks loyalty to family (Matt 10:37). Both also agree that the stoning penalty belonged to Israel’s theocratic order and that the New Testament directs the church to deal with such seduction through teaching and discipline, not civil punishment.

Deuteronomy 13:6

6If your brother, the son of your mother, or your son, or your daughter, or the wife of your bosom, or your friend who is as your own soul, entices you secretly, saying, “Let’s go and serve other gods”—which you have not known, you, nor your fathers;

Arminian

The list moves closer and closer: brother, son, daughter, “the wife of your bosom,” and “your friend who is as your own soul.” The enticement comes “secretly.”

Arminian readers note that the deepest human bonds can become channels of temptation, which is why personal loyalty to God must come first.

Reformed

The phrase “which you have not known, you, nor your fathers” stresses that these gods have no history of redemption with Israel.

Reformed readers stress that God alone is the one who acted for Israel’s fathers. Both traditions agree that the closest relationships do not change what is owed to the LORD.

Deuteronomy 13:7

7of the gods of the peoples who are around you, near to you, or far off from you, from the one end of the earth even to the other end of the earth—

Arminian

The gods may belong to peoples “near to you, or far off from you, from the one end of the earth even to the other end of the earth.” Whether familiar or exotic, they are equally excluded.

Arminian readers note that the attraction of the new and distant can be as dangerous as the pressure of the familiar.

Reformed

The universal scope of the verse leaves no god outside the prohibition.

Reformed readers see the exclusive claim of the LORD over all the earth’s gods. Both traditions agree that Israel’s loyalty belongs to the LORD alone, whatever form the alternative takes.

Deuteronomy 13:8

8you shall not consent to him nor listen to him; neither shall your eye pity him, neither shall you spare, neither shall you conceal him;

Arminian

Five commands pile up: “you shall not consent to him nor listen to him; neither shall your eye pity him, neither shall you spare, neither shall you conceal him.” Each closes a way of protecting the enticer.

Arminian readers note that the law expects a real, responsible choice by the one enticed, who must refuse both the invitation and the urge to cover it up.

Reformed

Natural affection must not override faithfulness to God in this case.

Reformed readers stress that concealment would make the hearer a partner in the rebellion. Both traditions agree that covering up a call to apostasy harms the whole community, and both read the severity in light of Israel’s theocratic setting.

Deuteronomy 13:9

9but you shall surely kill him. Your hand shall be first on him to put him to death, and afterwards the hands of all the people.

Arminian

The command is stark: “you shall surely kill him. Your hand shall be first on him to put him to death, and afterwards the hands of all the people.” The one who heard the enticement bears witness first, and then the community acts. Deuteronomy 17:6–7 sets the same pattern, requiring two or three witnesses and placing the witnesses’ hands first.

Arminian readers note that responsibility is personal before it is communal.

Reformed

The witness’s hand comes first so that the accuser bears the weight of the verdict, a check against false or careless charges.

Reformed readers read the verse within the judicial procedure of Deuteronomy rather than as private vengeance. Both traditions agree that this belonged to Israel’s theocratic law and is not a pattern for Christian action. In the church, the matching response is discipline and exclusion.

Deuteronomy 13:10

10You shall stone him to death with stones, because he has sought to draw you away from the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.

Arminian

The method is stoning, and the reason is that “he has sought to draw you away from the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.” The offense is measured against God’s act of redemption.

Arminian readers note that the gravity of apostasy lies in turning from the God who freely rescued Israel.

Reformed

The appeal to the exodus grounds the law in grace already shown.

Reformed readers stress that God’s redemption establishes his exclusive claim on his people. Both traditions agree that the penalty belonged to Israel’s theocratic covenant, and that the redemptive motive behind it now shapes the church’s concern to guard believers from being drawn away from Christ.

Deuteronomy 13:11

11All Israel shall hear, and fear, and shall not do any more wickedness like this among you.

Arminian

“All Israel shall hear, and fear, and shall not do any more wickedness like this among you.” The punishment has a public, deterrent purpose.

Arminian readers note that God uses warnings and examples to keep his people from sin, and that such warnings assume a real possibility of falling.

Reformed

The aim is the purity of the whole community.

Reformed readers see such warnings as one of the means by which God keeps his people faithful. Both traditions agree that the verse expresses God’s concern to protect the covenant people from spreading idolatry, and they find a parallel in the fear that fell on the church after judgment in Acts 5:11.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
