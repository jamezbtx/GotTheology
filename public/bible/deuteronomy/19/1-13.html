Passage

Deuteronomy 19

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Moses’ second address continues with laws for life in the land. Here he commands Israel to set apart three cities of refuge west of the Jordan, adding to the three east of the Jordan in 4:41–43, and to keep the roads to them open so that someone who kills a neighbor accidentally can escape the avenger of blood. The law separates accidental killing from murder: a deliberate killer who flees to a refuge city is handed over by his own city’s elders. Numbers 35 and Joshua 20 give parallel accounts.

Deuteronomy 19:1

1When the LORD your God cuts off the nations whose land the LORD your God gives you, and you succeed them and dwell in their cities and in their houses,

Arminian

The law looks ahead to Israel living in cities and houses it did not build, given by God. Arminian readers note that the gift of the land brings responsibility for justice within it. Both traditions agree that Deuteronomy repeatedly ties possession of the land to faithful obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God is the one who “cuts off the nations” and gives the land; Israel receives what grace provides. Shared ground: the cities of refuge belong to the ordering of life in the land God gives, and the gift comes before the obligations.

Deuteronomy 19:2

2you shall set apart three cities for yourselves in the middle of your land, which the LORD your God gives you to possess.

Arminian

Three cities are to be set apart in the middle of the land. Arminian readers see God’s provision of mercy built into Israel’s geography. Both traditions note that Moses had already set apart three cities east of the Jordan (4:41–43), and that Joshua 20:7–8 later names all six.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the refuge cities as part of God’s design for a just society, where protection is available to the innocent. Shared ground: the cities are central and accessible, reflecting God’s concern that mercy be within reach.

Deuteronomy 19:3

3You shall prepare the way, and divide the borders of your land which the LORD your God causes you to inherit into three parts, that every man slayer may flee there.

Arminian

Israel must prepare the roads and divide the land into three regions so that any manslayer can reach a city. Arminian readers see a practical commitment to making refuge accessible. Both traditions agree that the law cares not only about principle but also about distance and access.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the prepared way a picture of God’s provision that removes obstacles to refuge. Some readers draw a typological line to Christ as refuge (compare Hebrews 6:18), though that is an application, not the law’s own claim. Shared ground: refuge must be reachable.

Deuteronomy 19:4

4This is the case of the man slayer who shall flee there and live: Whoever kills his neighbor unintentionally, and didn’t hate him in time past—

Arminian

The refuge applies to someone who kills a neighbor unintentionally, without prior hatred. Arminian readers note the law’s attention to intent and moral responsibility. Both traditions agree that the distinction between accidental and deliberate killing is foundational to justice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law weighs the heart: prior hatred marks murder, its absence marks accident. Shared ground: guilt is not measured only by outcome, and the law protects the person who caused a death without malice.

Deuteronomy 19:5

5as when a man goes into the forest with his neighbor to chop wood and his hand swings the ax to cut down the tree, and the head slips from the handle and hits his neighbor so that he dies—he shall flee to one of these cities and live.

Arminian

The example of an ax head flying off while cutting wood makes the principle concrete. Arminian readers see a realistic accident that could happen to anyone. Both traditions agree that the example clarifies what “unintentionally” means in practice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law uses ordinary life to define a legal category, so judges could apply it consistently. Shared ground: an accident, however tragic, is not murder, and the one responsible may flee to a refuge city and live.

Deuteronomy 19:6

6Otherwise, the avenger of blood might pursue the man slayer while hot anger is in his heart and overtake him, because the way is long, and strike him mortally, even though he was not worthy of death, because he didn’t hate him in time past.

Arminian

Without a nearby refuge, the avenger of blood might overtake the manslayer in hot anger and kill him though he did not deserve death. Arminian readers note the law’s realism about human anger. Both traditions agree that the refuge cities restrain revenge.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law placing a check on the role of the avenger, a kinsman who pursued justice for the dead (compare Numbers 35:19). Shared ground: God’s law protects the innocent from vengeance driven by passion rather than judgment.

Deuteronomy 19:7

7Therefore I command you to set apart three cities for yourselves.

Arminian

Moses repeats the command to set apart three cities. Arminian readers note that the repetition underlines its importance. Both traditions agree that the command flows from the preceding concern to prevent unjust bloodshed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repetition as typical of Deuteronomy’s urgent, preaching style. Shared ground: the refuge cities are not optional; Israel must establish them as part of faithful life in the land.

Deuteronomy 19:8

8If the LORD your God enlarges your border, as he has sworn to your fathers, and gives you all the land which he promised to give to your fathers;

Arminian

If God enlarges Israel’s borders as he swore to the fathers, the law anticipates growth. Arminian readers see God’s promise unfolding in response to the people’s faithfulness. Both traditions agree that the promise to the patriarchs (Genesis 15:18) frames the possibility of expansion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sworn promise as the basis for the enlargement. Shared ground: the land is given according to God’s oath to the fathers, and the law adjusts its provisions as the promise is fulfilled.

Deuteronomy 19:9

9and if you keep all this commandment to do it, which I command you today, to love the LORD your God, and to walk ever in his ways, then you shall add three cities more for yourselves, in addition to these three.

Arminian

If Israel keeps the commandment to love God and walk in his ways, three more cities are to be added. Arminian readers note the conditional link between obedience and enlargement. Interpreters differ on whether these three were ever established or how they relate to the six named in Joshua 20; the text leaves it open.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that love for God is the heart of the commandment, consistent with 6:5. Shared ground: whether or not the additional cities were established, the principle stands: as the community grows, access to refuge must grow with it.

Deuteronomy 19:10

10This is so that innocent blood will not be shed in the middle of your land which the LORD your God gives you for an inheritance, leaving blood guilt on you.

Arminian

The purpose is that innocent blood not be shed in the land, leaving bloodguilt on Israel. Arminian readers see corporate responsibility: the community can share guilt for injustice it permits. Both traditions agree that bloodshed defiles the land (compare Numbers 35:33).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the land as God’s gift, which must not be polluted by injustice. Shared ground: the whole community bears responsibility for preventing the death of the innocent, and refuge cities are one means God provides.

Deuteronomy 19:11

11But if any man hates his neighbor, lies in wait for him, rises up against him, strikes him mortally so that he dies, and he flees into one of these cities;

Arminian

If someone hates a neighbor, lies in wait, and kills him, then flees to a refuge city, the refuge does not protect him. Arminian readers stress that deliberate murder forfeits protection. Both traditions agree that refuge is for the innocent, not a hiding place for the guilty.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the marks of murder: hatred, ambush, and a deliberate blow. Shared ground: the refuge cities cannot be abused to shelter murderers, and the law distinguishes clearly between misfortune and malice.

Deuteronomy 19:12

12then the elders of his city shall send and bring him there, and deliver him into the hand of the avenger of blood, that he may die.

Arminian

The murderer’s own city elders must bring him back and hand him to the avenger of blood. Arminian readers note that local leaders bear responsibility for justice. Both traditions agree that the process involves community authority, not private action alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the elders acting as God’s appointed magistrates. Numbers 35:24–25 describes the congregation judging between the slayer and the avenger. Shared ground: justice for murder is carried out through recognized authority.

Deuteronomy 19:13

13Your eye shall not pity him, but you shall purge the innocent blood from Israel that it may go well with you.

Arminian

Israel must not pity the murderer but purge innocent blood so it may go well with them. Arminian readers note that misplaced pity can perpetuate injustice. Both traditions agree that the verse reflects the seriousness of murder in God’s eyes (Genesis 9:6).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command as upholding the sanctity of human life made in God’s image. Shared ground: justice for murder is required for the community’s well-being, and both traditions read this within Israel’s civil law, applied in different ways today.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
