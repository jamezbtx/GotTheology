Passage

Deuteronomy 22

Verses 13–30 · WEB

These laws address marriage and sexual conduct. They cover a husband who falsely accuses his new wife of not being a virgin (verses 13–21), adultery (verse 22), cases involving a betrothed woman in the city or in the field (verses 23–27), a man who seizes an unbetrothed virgin (verses 28–29), and a prohibition on taking one’s father’s wife (verse 30, Hebrew 23:1). The penalties are severe, and some provisions are difficult for modern readers. Read in their setting, several aim to protect women. A slanderous husband is fined and loses the right to divorce. A woman forced in the field is declared innocent, with her case compared to murder, and the law presumes she cried out. The meaning and purpose of verses 28–29 are debated. Both traditions read these laws as Israel’s civil code within the covenant, fulfilled in Christ and not binding as civil law today. Both hold that the moral principles endure: sexual faithfulness, justice for the falsely accused, and compassion and protection for victims.

Deuteronomy 22:13

13If any man takes a wife, and goes in to her, hates her,

Arminian

A man marries a woman, has relations with her, and then turns against her. The case begins with hatred.

Arminian readers note that the man’s change of heart is his own responsibility, and the law holds him accountable for what he does with it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law exposes the danger of a hardened heart in marriage. What begins in covenant can be betrayed by contempt.

Both traditions affirm that marriage is a covenant meant for love and faithfulness, not a relationship that can be discarded on a whim (compare Malachi 2:14–16).

Deuteronomy 22:14

14accuses her of shameful things, gives her a bad name, and says, “I took this woman, and when I came near to her, I didn’t find in her the tokens of virginity;”

Arminian

He accuses her of shameful conduct and gives her a bad name, claiming she was not a virgin. The charge threatens her life and her family’s honor.

Arminian readers note the gravity of slander, which can destroy a person’s life and reputation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the ninth commandment forbids false witness (Exodus 20:16), and this law applies it to marriage.

Both traditions see the law as protecting the woman from false accusation, a principle of justice that remains binding.

Deuteronomy 22:15

15then the young lady’s father and mother shall take and bring the tokens of the young lady’s virginity to the elders of the city in the gate.

Arminian

Her parents bring evidence of her virginity to the elders at the gate. Her family stands with her.

Arminian readers note that the law gives the accused woman a defense and requires public judgment, not private decision.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law presumes the woman’s family will defend her and that evidence matters in God’s justice.

Both traditions note that the evidence described reflects ancient customs, and that the principle of fair process for the accused carries over.

Deuteronomy 22:16

16The young lady’s father shall tell the elders, “I gave my daughter to this man as his wife, and he hates her.

Arminian

Her father tells the elders that he gave his daughter in marriage and the man has turned against her. The father speaks for his daughter.

Arminian readers note that the law gives the woman an advocate in a public setting where she might otherwise have no voice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the father’s testimony reflects the household structure of ancient Israel. God’s law worked within that structure to protect the vulnerable.

Both traditions read the verse as showing God’s concern that the falsely accused have defenders.

Deuteronomy 22:17

17Behold, he has accused her of shameful things, saying, ‘I didn’t find in your daughter the tokens of virginity;’ and yet these are the tokens of my daughter’s virginity.” They shall spread the cloth before the elders of the city.

Arminian

The father declares that the accusation is false and presents the evidence before the elders. The truth is brought into the open.

Arminian readers note that justice requires truthful evidence and that false accusations must be answered publicly.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s law upholds the innocent through ordered procedures. Truth is vindicated before the community.

Both traditions affirm that slander must be exposed, a principle that remains binding for Christians in every setting.

Deuteronomy 22:18

18The elders of that city shall take the man and chastise him.

Arminian

The elders take the man and discipline him. The false accuser faces consequences.

Arminian readers note that the law holds the slanderer accountable for his actions and does not allow him to escape responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s justice punishes false witness, protecting the community from the destructive power of lies. The civil penalty expressed the moral seriousness of the ninth commandment.

Both traditions agree that those who falsely accuse must be held to account.

Deuteronomy 22:19

19They shall fine him one hundred shekels of silver, and give them to the father of the young lady, because he has given a bad name to a virgin of Israel. She shall be his wife. He may not put her away all his days.

Arminian

He is fined one hundred shekels, paid to her father, for defaming a virgin of Israel; she remains his wife, and he may never divorce her. The WEB footnote gives the shekel as about ten grams.

Arminian readers note that the penalty protects her from abandonment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law secures her place and provision, though modern readers may find the continuing marriage troubling. In the ancient setting, it guarded her from destitution.

Both traditions acknowledge this tension honestly while seeing the law’s protective aim.

Deuteronomy 22:20

20But if this thing is true, that the tokens of virginity were not found in the young lady,

Arminian

If the accusation is true and evidence of virginity is not found, the case proceeds differently. The law considers both possibilities.

Arminian readers note that the law takes truth seriously in both directions, protecting the innocent and addressing real wrongdoing.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law assumes the importance of sexual faithfulness before marriage within Israel’s covenant.

Both traditions read the verse carefully, recognizing the difficulty of evidence in such cases and the potential for injustice.

Deuteronomy 22:21

21then they shall bring out the young lady to the door of her father’s house, and the men of her city shall stone her to death with stones, because she has done folly in Israel, to play the prostitute in her father’s house. So you shall remove the evil from among you.

Arminian

The young woman is brought to her father’s house and stoned, for she has acted shamefully in Israel. The penalty is severe.

Arminian readers acknowledge the severity of this law and read it within Israel’s covenant setting, not as a pattern for today.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the penalty as part of Israel’s civil code, fulfilled in Christ and not binding now. They note that the law treated sexual unfaithfulness as an offense against the covenant community.

Both traditions affirm sexual faithfulness and point to Christ’s mercy toward sinners.

Deuteronomy 22:22

22If a man is found lying with a woman married to a husband, then they shall both die, the man who lay with the woman and the woman. So you shall remove the evil from Israel.

Arminian

If a man is found with a married woman, both must die. Adultery is treated as a grave offense.

Arminian readers note that both parties are held responsible; the law does not place guilt on the woman alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that adultery breaks the covenant of marriage, which mirrors God’s covenant with his people (compare Hosea 2; Ephesians 5:25–32).

Both traditions read the death penalty as part of Israel’s civil law, fulfilled in Christ, while affirming that adultery remains a serious sin that Christ forgives and calls his people to forsake.

Deuteronomy 22:23

23If there is a young lady who is a virgin pledged to be married to a husband, and a man finds her in the city, and lies with her,

Arminian

If a betrothed virgin is found with a man in the city, the case is considered. Betrothal was legally binding in Israel.

Arminian readers note that the law treats betrothal as a real covenant commitment, as Joseph’s situation with Mary shows (Matthew 1:18–19).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that betrothal carried the weight of marriage, so the offense is treated as adultery.

Both traditions read the verse within the ancient setting, where the presumption about consent was shaped by the location.

Deuteronomy 22:24

24then you shall bring them both out to the gate of that city, and you shall stone them to death with stones; the lady, because she didn’t cry, being in the city; and the man, because he has humbled his neighbor’s wife. So you shall remove the evil from among you.

Arminian

Both are brought to the city gate and stoned: the woman because she did not cry out in the city, and the man because he humbled his neighbor’s wife. The law presumes that a cry in the city would be heard.

Arminian readers note that the law tries to distinguish consent from coercion, though modern readers recognize that silence does not always mean consent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the limits of this presumption: fear, threats, or isolation can silence a victim even in a town. The law’s aim was justice, and Christians today must apply that aim with full attention to how coercion actually works.

Both traditions read the verse honestly within its setting and affirm that victims must not be blamed.

Deuteronomy 22:25

25But if the man finds the lady who is pledged to be married in the field, and the man forces her and lies with her, then only the man who lay with her shall die;

Arminian

If a man forces a betrothed woman in the field, only the man dies. The law recognizes coercion.

Arminian readers note that the law places full responsibility on the man who uses force. The woman bears no guilt for what was done to her.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the location in the field changes the presumption: no one could hear her cry. Justice requires attention to circumstances.

Both traditions affirm that the guilt lies with the attacker, and that the law protects the woman as a victim.

Deuteronomy 22:26

26but to the lady you shall do nothing. There is in the lady no sin worthy of death; for as when a man rises against his neighbor and kills him, even so is this matter;

Arminian

Nothing is to be done to the woman, for she has committed no sin worthy of death; the case is like a man rising against his neighbor and killing him. The analogy to murder is striking.

Arminian readers note that the law declares her innocent and treats the assault as a grave violation of her person.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the comparison to murder shows how seriously God regards sexual violence. She is a victim, not an offender.

Both traditions affirm compassion and justice for victims, a principle that remains binding.

Deuteronomy 22:27

27for he found her in the field, the pledged to be married lady cried, and there was no one to save her.

Arminian

He found her in the field; she cried out, and there was no one to save her. The law presumes her innocence.

Arminian readers note that the law gives her the benefit of the doubt, assuming she resisted.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God sees the cry of the helpless even when no human rescuer is present (compare Psalm 34:17–18).

Both traditions see here God’s compassion for victims of violence, and they apply it to the church’s care for those who have suffered abuse.

Deuteronomy 22:28

28If a man finds a lady who is a virgin, who is not pledged to be married, grabs her and lies with her, and they are found,

Arminian

If a man seizes an unbetrothed virgin and lies with her, and they are discovered, the law addresses the case. The Hebrew verb differs from the one in verse 25, and interpreters debate whether this describes rape or seduction.

Arminian readers note the interpretive difficulty and hold the meaning cautiously.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the debate and compare Exodus 22:16–17, which addresses enticement and lets the father refuse the marriage. They read the two laws together rather than in isolation.

Both traditions acknowledge that the verse is hard and avoid simplistic readings.

Deuteronomy 22:29

29then the man who lay with her shall give to the lady’s father fifty shekels of silver. She shall be his wife, because he has humbled her. He may not put her away all his days.

Arminian

The man pays fifty shekels to her father, must marry her, and may never divorce her. The WEB footnote gives the shekel as about ten grams.

Arminian readers note that in the ancient setting the law aimed to secure her provision and status, but modern readers rightly feel the difficulty of the arrangement.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that if Exodus 22:16–17 applies, the father could refuse the marriage, which would protect her from an unwanted union.

Both traditions read the law as protective in aim, while acknowledging the real interpretive difficulty.

Deuteronomy 22:30

30A man shall not take his father’s wife, and shall not uncover his father’s skirt.

Arminian

A man must not take his father’s wife or uncover his father’s skirt. In the Hebrew text this verse is numbered 23:1.

Arminian readers note that the prohibition protects family boundaries and the honor of the father’s marriage (compare Leviticus 18:8).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul confronted this very sin in the church at Corinth (1 Corinthians 5:1), showing that the moral principle carried over into the new covenant.

Both traditions affirm the moral principle of sexual faithfulness within family relationships.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
