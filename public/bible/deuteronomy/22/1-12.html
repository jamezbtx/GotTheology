Passage

Deuteronomy 22

Verses 1–12 · WEB

A series of brief laws covers neighborly care and the order of creation. Israelites must return a neighbor’s lost animals and goods, and help lift a fallen animal, rather than hiding from responsibility. They must not wear the clothing of the other sex, must spare the mother bird when taking young from a nest, must build a railing on a flat roof, and must avoid certain mixtures of seed, plow animals, and fabric. They are also to wear tassels on their cloaks. Some laws have a plain moral sense; others, such as the mixtures, likely served as signs of Israel’s distinctness, and their exact rationale is debated. Both traditions read these as Israel’s covenant laws, fulfilled in Christ, while seeing lasting principles of love of neighbor, care for creation, and attentiveness to God’s order.

Deuteronomy 22:1

1You shall not see your brother’s ox or his sheep go astray and hide yourself from them. You shall surely bring them again to your brother.

Arminian

An Israelite must not ignore a neighbor’s straying ox or sheep but must bring it back. Indifference is forbidden.

Arminian readers note that love of neighbor is an active responsibility; one cannot hide from opportunities to do good.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law teaches care for a neighbor’s property as an expression of love (compare Exodus 23:4–5, which extends this even to an enemy’s animal).

Both traditions see here a principle that carries over directly: love does not look away (compare Luke 10:30–37).

Deuteronomy 22:2

2If your brother isn’t near to you, or if you don’t know him, then you shall bring it home to your house, and it shall be with you until your brother comes looking for it, and you shall restore it to him.

Arminian

If the owner is far away or unknown, the finder keeps the animal safely until it is claimed. Care may require inconvenience.

Arminian readers note that the law asks for patient, costly kindness, not merely a passing gesture.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law provides an orderly way of protecting property until it can be restored. Stewardship of another’s goods is a trust.

Both traditions read the verse as practical love that takes time and effort, a pattern Christians are called to imitate.

Deuteronomy 22:3

3So you shall do with his donkey. So you shall do with his garment. So you shall do with every lost thing of your brother’s, which he has lost and you have found. You may not hide yourself.

Arminian

The same applies to a donkey, a garment, or any lost thing. The finder may not hide himself.

Arminian readers note that the principle extends to every kind of loss; love does not choose only convenient cases.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law trains the conscience to see a neighbor’s loss as one’s own concern. Honesty includes returning what is not ours.

Both traditions agree that this principle remains binding in substance: Christians must return lost property and resist the temptation to keep it.

Deuteronomy 22:4

4You shall not see your brother’s donkey or his ox fallen down by the way, and hide yourself from them. You shall surely help him to lift them up again.

Arminian

An Israelite must help lift a neighbor’s fallen donkey or ox. Help is required, not optional.

Arminian readers note that love is shown in practical help, especially when someone is struggling, and that the call to act is addressed to every Israelite personally.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law commands active mercy as part of covenant life. Grace received becomes grace extended.

Both traditions apply this to bearing one another’s burdens (Galatians 6:2), seeing in the law a picture of Christian love.

Deuteronomy 22:5

5A woman shall not wear men’s clothing, neither shall a man put on women’s clothing; for whoever does these things is an abomination to the LORD your God.

Arminian

A woman must not wear men’s clothing, nor a man women’s clothing; whoever does so is an abomination to the LORD. The exact scope of the law is debated.

Arminian readers note that some interpreters connect it with pagan cultic practices, while others read it as preserving the distinction between male and female.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note both readings and affirm the creation of humanity as male and female (Genesis 1:27).

Both traditions read the verse within Israel’s covenant setting, avoid using it to shame people, and hold that its application today requires care and humility.

Deuteronomy 22:6

6If you come across a bird’s nest on the way, in any tree or on the ground, with young ones or eggs, and the hen sitting on the young, or on the eggs, you shall not take the hen with the young.

Arminian

If someone finds a bird’s nest with a mother sitting on eggs or young, he must not take the mother with the young. Restraint applies to the smallest creatures.

Arminian readers note that God’s care extends to creatures and calls his people to responsible stewardship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law reflects God’s providential care for all he made (compare Matthew 10:29).

Both traditions see here a principle of care for creation that carries over, though they hold specific environmental applications with prudence.

Deuteronomy 22:7

7You shall surely let the hen go, but the young you may take for yourself, that it may be well with you, and that you may prolong your days.

Arminian

The mother must be released, though the young may be taken, so that it may go well and life may be prolonged. A promise accompanies the command.

Arminian readers note that the promise connects obedience with blessing, encouraging faithful conduct even in small matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the same promise attaches to honoring parents (Deuteronomy 5:16). God’s blessing rests on the whole fabric of obedience.

Both traditions read the promise as a general pattern of covenant blessing rather than a guarantee for every case.

Deuteronomy 22:8

8When you build a new house, then you shall make a railing around your roof, so that you don’t bring blood on your house if anyone falls from there.

Arminian

When building a new house, an Israelite must put a railing around the roof so that no one falls and bloodguilt does not come on the house. Prevention is a duty.

Arminian readers note that loving one’s neighbor includes taking reasonable precautions to protect others.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law applies the commandment against murder to negligence: failing to prevent foreseeable harm is a moral failure.

Both traditions see a principle that carries over directly: we are responsible for the safety of those affected by what we build and do.

Deuteronomy 22:9

9You shall not sow your vineyard with two kinds of seed, lest all the fruit be defiled, the seed which you have sown, and the increase of the vineyard.

Arminian

A vineyard must not be sown with two kinds of seed, or the whole yield will be forfeited. The law concerns mixing.

Arminian readers note that this law, along with the next two, belongs to a group of rules about separation that marked Israel as distinct.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Leviticus 19:19 contains similar laws, and that their exact rationale is debated; many interpreters see them as teaching holiness and separation through everyday life.

Both traditions read these laws as fulfilled in Christ, not binding today, while retaining the principle of holiness.

Deuteronomy 22:10

10You shall not plow with an ox and a donkey together.

Arminian

An ox and a donkey must not plow together. The animals differ in strength and kind.

Arminian readers note that the law may also reflect kindness to animals, since an unequal yoke burdens both.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul uses the image of an unequal yoke in 2 Corinthians 6:14 to warn believers about binding partnerships with unbelievers, though he does not cite this verse.

Both traditions treat that application as an analogy rather than the verse’s original meaning.

Deuteronomy 22:11

11You shall not wear clothes of wool and linen woven together.

Arminian

Clothing of wool and linen woven together must not be worn. The law again concerns mixture.

Arminian readers note that Israel’s distinctness was expressed even in clothing, a daily reminder of their calling to holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priests’ garments did combine materials (compare Exodus 28), which suggests the law marked a boundary between holy and common use. Interpreters debate this.

Both traditions read the verse as part of the ceremonial distinctions fulfilled in Christ.

Deuteronomy 22:12

12You shall make yourselves fringes on the four corners of your cloak with which you cover yourself.

Arminian

Israelites must make tassels on the four corners of their cloaks. The WEB footnote gives tassels as an alternative to fringes.

Arminian readers note that Numbers 15:37–41 explains the tassels as reminders to remember and do God’s commandments.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Jesus wore such a garment; a woman touched its edge for healing (Matthew 9:20), and he warned against enlarging such fringes for show (Matthew 23:5).

Both traditions see the tassels as a call to remember God’s word, a principle that remains while the form is not required.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
