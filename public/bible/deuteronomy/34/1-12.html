Passage

Deuteronomy 34

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Moses climbs Mount Nebo, sees the whole land God promised to the patriarchs, and dies in Moab at the LORD’s word; he is buried in an unknown place, mourned thirty days, and succeeded by Joshua, who is full of the spirit of wisdom through Moses’ laying on of hands. The book closes by saying no prophet like Moses has since arisen, whom the LORD knew face to face. Jewish and Christian tradition has often held that Joshua or a later inspired writer recorded this account of Moses’ death, while other views of the book’s composition exist; both traditions read the chapter as shared ground, and many Christians see the promised prophet like Moses fulfilled in Christ, as a reading.

Deuteronomy 34:1

1Moses went up from the plains of Moab to Mount Nebo, to the top of Pisgah, that is opposite Jericho. The LORD showed him all the land of Gilead to Dan,

Arminian

Moses climbs Mount Nebo, to the top of Pisgah, and the LORD shows him the land. Arminian readers note that God fulfills his word that Moses would see the land (Deut 3:27; 32:49).

The view stretches from Gilead to Dan.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s grace in letting Moses see the promise even though he cannot enter.

The LORD himself shows him the land, a personal act of kindness. The survey from Gilead to Dan takes in the whole land at once, so Moses sees the full scope of what God promised.

Deuteronomy 34:2

2and all Naphtali, and the land of Ephraim and Manasseh, and all the land of Judah, to the Western Sea,

Arminian

The view includes Naphtali, Ephraim, Manasseh, and Judah to the Western Sea. Arminian readers note the sweep of the promised territory.

The Western Sea is the Mediterranean.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the inheritance laid out before the mediator who led the people to its edge.

The tribal names anticipate the land’s later distribution (Josh 13–19). Moses sees in advance what Joshua will apportion.

Deuteronomy 34:3

3and the south, and the Plain of the valley of Jericho the city of palm trees, to Zoar.

Arminian

The view includes the south, the plain of Jericho, the city of palm trees, to Zoar. Arminian readers note the WEB footnote, which gives “Negev” as an alternative for “south.”

Jericho is called the city of palm trees elsewhere (Judg 1:16; 2 Chron 28:15).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the full extent of the promised land shown to Moses, from north to south.

Zoar recalls the story of Lot (Gen 19:22). The survey reminds readers that God keeps every part of his promise.

Deuteronomy 34:4

4The LORD said to him, “This is the land which I swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, saying, ‘I will give it to your offspring.’ I have caused you to see it with your eyes, but you shall not go over there.”

Arminian

God says this is the land sworn to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, but Moses will not cross over. Arminian readers note the consequence of Moses’ failure at Meribah (Num 20:12; Deut 32:51–52).

God’s promise stands, though Moses does not enter.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to his oath and his justice toward Moses held together.

Moses dies in faith, seeing the promise from afar, like the patriarchs (Heb 11:13, 39–40). The promise does not depend on any one leader.

Deuteronomy 34:5

5So Moses the servant of the LORD died there in the land of Moab, according to the LORD’s word.

Arminian

Moses, the servant of the LORD, dies in Moab according to the LORD’s word. Arminian readers note the honorable title “servant of the LORD” (cf. Josh 1:1–2; Num 12:7).

His death comes at God’s appointed time.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereignty over life and death: Moses dies “according to the LORD’s word.”

Hebrews honors Moses as faithful in God’s house as a servant, while Christ is faithful as a Son (Heb 3:5–6).

Deuteronomy 34:6

6He buried him in the valley in the land of Moab opposite Beth Peor, but no man knows where his tomb is to this day.

Arminian

He buried Moses in a valley in Moab, but no one knows his tomb. Arminian readers note that the text seems to imply God himself buried Moses.

Jude 9 refers to a dispute over Moses’ body. The unknown grave may have prevented veneration, though the text does not say so.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s care in burying his servant, which the text attributes to “he.”

Moses later appears with Elijah at the transfiguration (Matt 17:3), so his story does not end in the unknown grave. Jude 9 also refers to Moses’ body.

Deuteronomy 34:7

7Moses was one hundred twenty years old when he died. His eye was not dim, nor his strength gone.

Arminian

Moses was 120 years old, his eye not dim nor his strength gone. Arminian readers note his vigor to the end.

In Deuteronomy 31:2 Moses said he could no longer go out and come in; interpreters discuss how these fit together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sustaining grace throughout Moses’ long life.

His death did not come from failing strength but at God’s word (v. 5). Deuteronomy 31:2 speaks of his limits, and interpreters discuss how the two statements fit.

Deuteronomy 34:8

8The children of Israel wept for Moses in the plains of Moab thirty days, until the days of weeping in the mourning for Moses were ended.

Arminian

Israel wept for Moses in the plains of Moab thirty days. Arminian readers note that this matches the mourning for Aaron (Num 20:29).

Grief is fitting and honorable, but it has a limit, and then the people move on to the next step of God’s purpose.

Reformed

Reformed readers see honor given to God’s servant by the whole people.

The days of mourning end, and the people move forward under Joshua. The work of God continues beyond the death of his greatest servants.

Deuteronomy 34:9

9Joshua the son of Nun was full of the spirit of wisdom, for Moses had laid his hands on him. The children of Israel listened to him, and did as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

Joshua was full of the spirit of wisdom because Moses laid hands on him, and the people listened. Arminian readers note the commissioning described in Numbers 27:18–23 and Deuteronomy 31:7–8, 23.

Leadership passes to Joshua with God’s equipping.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God providing a leader for his people. The laying on of hands later marks commissioning in the church (Acts 6:6; 1 Tim 4:14).

The people’s obedience to Joshua continues their obedience to God’s command through Moses.

Deuteronomy 34:10

10Since then, there has not arisen a prophet in Israel like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face,

Arminian

No prophet like Moses has arisen, whom the LORD knew face to face. Arminian readers note the uniqueness of Moses’ relationship with God (Exod 33:11; Num 12:6–8).

Deuteronomy 18:15–18 promised a prophet like Moses, and Acts 3:22 and 7:37 apply that promise to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Deuteronomy 18:15–18 fulfilled in Christ, who is worthy of more glory than Moses (Heb 3:1–6).

These are readings both traditions share. The phrase “since then” looks back from a later time.

Deuteronomy 34:11

11in all the signs and the wonders which the LORD sent him to do in the land of Egypt, to Pharaoh, and to all his servants, and to all his land,

Arminian

Moses did signs and wonders in Egypt before Pharaoh and his servants. Arminian readers note the plagues and the exodus (Exod 7–12).

God worked through Moses in unique ways.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s power displayed through his servant in Egypt (Deut 4:34).

The text stresses that the LORD sent Moses to do these signs. The signs confirmed Moses’ authority before Pharaoh and before Israel.

Deuteronomy 34:12

12and in all the mighty hand, and in all the awesome deeds, which Moses did in the sight of all Israel.

Arminian

The book ends with Moses’ mighty hand and awesome deeds before Israel. Arminian readers note the closing tribute.

The Torah ends looking back at Moses and forward to the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the mighty deeds as ultimately God’s, done through Moses (Deut 26:8).

The Pentateuch closes with Israel waiting on the edge of the promise, looking back on God’s acts and forward to their fulfillment.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
