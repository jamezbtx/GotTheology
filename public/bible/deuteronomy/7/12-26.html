Passage

Deuteronomy 7

Verses 12–26 · WEB

If Israel listens and obeys, God will keep his covenant love, bless them with fruitfulness and health, and drive out the nations little by little. Moses answers Israel’s fear with memory of the exodus and the promise of God’s presence, and he warns them not to covet the silver and gold on idols, lest Israel become devoted to destruction itself. The emphasis falls on God’s power, Israel’s faithfulness, and the danger of idolatry.

Deuteronomy 7:12

12It shall happen, because you listen to these ordinances and keep and do them, that the LORD your God will keep with you the covenant and the loving kindness which he swore to your fathers.

Arminian

If Israel listens and keeps these ordinances, the LORD will keep his covenant and loving kindness. Arminian readers see genuine covenant conditions, in which Israel’s response affects its experience of blessing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm real covenant obligations, often distinguishing the Sinai administration’s blessings in the land from the ground of eternal salvation. The verse ties covenant blessing to listening and obedience.

Deuteronomy 7:13

13He will love you, bless you, and multiply you. He will also bless the fruit of your body and the fruit of your ground, your grain and your new wine and your oil, the increase of your livestock and the young of your flock, in the land which he swore to your fathers to give you.

Arminian

God promises to love, bless, and multiply Israel in family, crops, and livestock. Arminian readers see God’s generous intention to bless his people in every area of life in the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see covenant blessings expressed in concrete, earthly terms suited to Israel’s life in Canaan. The list reflects the promises made to the fathers, which the verse explicitly recalls.

Deuteronomy 7:14

14You will be blessed above all peoples. There won’t be male or female barren among you, or among your livestock.

Arminian

Israel will be blessed above all peoples, with no barrenness among people or animals. Arminian readers see blessing that reverses the barrenness experienced by the patriarchal families, signaling God’s favor.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see promised fruitfulness as covenant blessing. The promise is addressed to Israel as a nation in the land, which shapes how Reformed readers apply it today.

Deuteronomy 7:15

15The LORD will take away from you all sickness; and he will put none of the evil diseases of Egypt, which you know, on you, but will lay them on all those who hate you.

Arminian

The LORD will take away sickness and lay Egypt’s diseases on Israel’s enemies. Arminian readers see God as healer, echoing Exodus 15:26, where he calls himself the LORD who heals.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a covenant promise tied to Israel’s life in the land. The reference to the diseases of Egypt recalls the plagues and the deliverance Israel had witnessed.

Deuteronomy 7:16

16You shall consume all the peoples whom the LORD your God shall deliver to you. Your eye shall not pity them. You shall not serve their gods; for that would be a snare to you.

Arminian

Israel must consume the peoples God delivers, show no pity, and not serve their gods, which would be a snare. Arminian readers see the command again tied to the danger of idolatry spelled out in the text.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the command as part of the specific judgment of these nations. The stated rationale, that their gods would be a snare, keeps the focus on Israel’s faithfulness to the LORD.

Deuteronomy 7:17

17If you shall say in your heart, “These nations are more than I; how can I dispossess them?”

Arminian

Israel may think, “These nations are more than I; how can I dispossess them?” Arminian readers see Moses anticipating honest fear and addressing it pastorally rather than dismissing it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the anticipated question exposing human weakness, which God answers with reminders of his power. The verse names the fear explicitly before giving the remedy.

Deuteronomy 7:18

18you shall not be afraid of them. You shall remember well what the LORD your God did to Pharaoh and to all Egypt:

Arminian

Israel must not fear but remember what the LORD did to Pharaoh and Egypt. Arminian readers see memory of past deliverance strengthening present trust in God’s promises.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see remembrance of God’s works as a means of strengthening faith. Deuteronomy repeatedly calls Israel to remember, making memory central to covenant faithfulness.

Deuteronomy 7:19

19the great trials which your eyes saw, the signs, the wonders, the mighty hand, and the outstretched arm, by which the LORD your God brought you out. So shall the LORD your God do to all the peoples of whom you are afraid.

Arminian

The trials, signs, wonders, mighty hand, and outstretched arm that Israel saw will be repeated against the feared peoples. Arminian readers see God’s past faithfulness offered as evidence for future trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the God of the exodus pledging the same power for the conquest. The verse links what Israel saw with its own eyes to what God promises to do next.

Deuteronomy 7:20

20Moreover the LORD your God will send the hornet among them, until those who are left, and hide themselves, perish from before you.

Arminian

God will send the hornet among the nations until those hiding perish. Arminian readers see God fighting for Israel through means beyond Israel’s control. Israel is not left to fight alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see divine agency in the conquest. Interpreters differ on whether the hornet is literal or figurative, and the text itself does not specify.

Deuteronomy 7:21

21You shall not be scared of them; for the LORD your God is among you, a great and awesome God.

Arminian

Israel must not be terrified, for the LORD is among them, great and awesome. Arminian readers see God’s presence as the decisive reason for courage.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s presence among his people as their security. The phrase “a great and awesome God” emphasizes his majesty in contrast to the nations Israel fears.

Deuteronomy 7:22

22The LORD your God will cast out those nations before you little by little. You may not consume them at once, lest the animals of the field increase on you.

Arminian

God will drive out the nations little by little, lest wild animals multiply. Arminian readers see God’s wise pacing, which also leaves room for Israel’s continued trust and obedience over time.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see providential ordering of the conquest for Israel’s good. The verse shows that God’s gradual method has a stated practical reason. Even the pace of victory is under God’s care.

Deuteronomy 7:23

23But the LORD your God will deliver them up before you, and will confuse them with a great confusion, until they are destroyed.

Arminian

God will deliver the nations and throw them into great confusion until destroyed. Arminian readers see the outcome credited to God’s action, keeping Israel from boasting.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God as the decisive actor in judgment. The text repeatedly assigns the victory to the LORD rather than to Israel’s military strength.

Deuteronomy 7:24

24He will deliver their kings into your hand, and you shall make their name perish from under the sky. No one will be able to stand before you until you have destroyed them.

Arminian

God will hand over their kings, and Israel will blot out their names; none will stand before Israel. Arminian readers see the promise of victory joined to the warnings about idolatry that frame it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a promise grounded in God’s power. Both the promise and its limits must be read alongside Deuteronomy 9:4–6, which denies that Israel’s righteousness is the reason for its success.

Deuteronomy 7:25

25You shall burn the engraved images of their gods with fire. You shall not covet the silver or the gold that is on them, nor take it for yourself, lest you be snared in it; for it is an abomination to the LORD your God.

Arminian

Israel must burn the idols and not covet their silver or gold, which is an abomination. Arminian readers see the danger that greed for precious metals could draw Israel into idolatry.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the warning against coveting what God has devoted to destruction. The later account of Achan in Joshua 7 illustrates the danger described here.

Deuteronomy 7:26

26You shall not bring an abomination into your house and become a devoted thing like it. You shall utterly detest it. You shall utterly abhor it; for it is a devoted thing.

Arminian

Israel must not bring an abomination home and become devoted to destruction like it, but utterly detest it. Arminian readers see the warning that Israel could share the fate of what it brings in.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the seriousness of contact with devoted things. The verse closes the chapter by turning the language of utter destruction back on Israel as a warning.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
