Passage

Deuteronomy 7

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Moses commands Israel to destroy the seven nations of Canaan, refuse covenants and intermarriage with them, and tear down their altars, because their gods would turn Israel away. The text grounds this in the danger of idolatry and, in canonical context, in the nations’ wickedness (9:4–5; compare Genesis 15:16), and it warns that the same judgment could fall on Israel. The chapter then explains Israel’s election: God chose the fewest of peoples because he loved them and kept his oath to the fathers. Arminian and Reformed readers differ on how this national election relates to individual election to salvation.

Deuteronomy 7:1

1When the LORD your God brings you into the land where you go to possess it, and casts out many nations before you—the Hittite, the Girgashite, the Amorite, the Canaanite, the Perizzite, the Hivite, and the Jebusite—seven nations greater and mightier than you;

Arminian

Moses names seven nations in the land, all greater and mightier than Israel. Arminian readers note that the victory is framed from the start as God’s work, since Israel is the weaker party and must trust his promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that the LORD himself brings Israel in and casts out the nations. The text’s own emphasis is that possession of the land depends on God’s action, not on Israel’s strength or numbers.

Deuteronomy 7:2

2and when the LORD your God delivers them up before you, and you strike them, then you shall utterly destroy them. You shall make no covenant with them, nor show mercy to them.

Arminian

When God delivers the nations, Israel must utterly destroy them, make no covenant, and show no mercy. Arminian readers take the command seriously in its setting, reading it with 9:4–5, which ties it to the nations’ wickedness rather than Israel’s righteousness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the command as a specific act of divine judgment, often linking it with Genesis 15:16 on the iniquity of the Amorites. Christian readers in both traditions commonly treat it as limited to this moment in redemptive history, not as a pattern for later believers.

Deuteronomy 7:3

3You shall not make marriages with them. You shall not give your daughter to his son, nor shall you take his daughter for your son.

Arminian

Israel must not intermarry with these nations. Arminian readers see the prohibition aimed at religious allegiance, as the next verse explains, rather than at ethnicity, noting that Rahab and Ruth were later received into Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the stated purpose of protecting Israel from idolatry. Some Reformed interpreters connect the principle with Paul’s concern in 2 Corinthians 6:14 about partnerships with unbelievers, while noting the different covenant setting.

Deuteronomy 7:4

4For that would turn away your sons from following me, that they may serve other gods. So the LORD’s anger would be kindled against you, and he would destroy you quickly.

Arminian

Intermarriage would turn Israel’s children to other gods and bring the LORD’s anger on Israel itself. Arminian readers note that the same judgment could fall on Israel, so the command is not a charter of ethnic favoritism.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the text warning Israel that its own covenant standing does not shield it from judgment for idolatry. The verse makes the danger explicit: the issue is worship of other gods.

Deuteronomy 7:5

5But you shall deal with them like this: you shall break down their altars, dash their pillars in pieces, cut down their Asherah poles, and burn their engraved images with fire.

Arminian

Israel must break altars, smash pillars, cut down Asherah poles, and burn images. Arminian readers see the command centered on removing idolatry from the land God is giving, so Israel’s worship remains undivided.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the destruction of idolatrous objects as required by the first and second commandments. The verse’s focus falls on objects of worship, which the chapter returns to in verses 25–26.

Deuteronomy 7:6

6For you are a holy people to the LORD your God. The LORD your God has chosen you to be a people for his own possession, above all peoples who are on the face of the earth.

Arminian

Israel is a holy people, chosen by the LORD to be his own possession above all peoples. Arminian readers typically see here the election of a nation to covenant privilege and vocation, a people set apart to serve God’s purposes among the nations.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that Israel’s national election is in view. They often note that 1 Peter 2:9 applies similar language to the church, calling believers a people for God’s own possession.

Deuteronomy 7:7

7The LORD didn’t set his love on you nor choose you, because you were more in number than any people; for you were the fewest of all peoples;

Arminian

God did not choose Israel because they were numerous; they were the fewest of all peoples. Classical Arminian readers usually stress that this is corporate election of Israel as a people and that it does not settle the eternal destiny of each Israelite.

Reformed

Reformed readers grant that national election is the subject, but see the explicit denial of any qualifying ground in Israel as consistent with, or illustrative of, God’s unconditional election of individuals to salvation.

Key contrast Classical Arminian readers tend to see corporate and vocational election here: God chose Israel as a nation for covenant privilege and service, without settling the eternal destiny of each Israelite. Reformed readers grant that national election is in view but see its ground in God’s unmerited love (7:7–8) as consistent with, or illustrative of, sovereign unconditional election of individuals. Neither camp is uniform, and readers within each tradition weigh the corporate and individual dimensions of this passage differently.

Deuteronomy 7:8

8but because the LORD loves you, and because he desires to keep the oath which he swore to your fathers, the LORD has brought you out with a mighty hand and redeemed you out of the house of bondage, from the hand of Pharaoh king of Egypt.

Arminian

God acted because he loves Israel and keeps his oath to the fathers, redeeming them from Egypt. Arminian readers see covenant love and faithfulness to promises given to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as the ground of the exodus.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the cause located wholly in God’s love and oath, not in Israel. Many Reformed readers see this unmerited love as a window onto the character of God’s electing grace more broadly.

Deuteronomy 7:9

9Know therefore that the LORD your God himself is God, the faithful God, who keeps covenant and loving kindness to a thousand generations with those who love him and keep his commandments,

Arminian

The LORD is the faithful God who keeps covenant and loving kindness to a thousand generations of those who love him and keep his commandments. Arminian readers see God’s faithfulness matched by a covenant relationship in which love and obedience are genuine responses.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s steadfast covenant love, and commonly read the love and obedience mentioned as themselves fruits of his grace. The text itself joins God’s faithfulness and the people’s faithful response.

Deuteronomy 7:10

10and repays those who hate him to their face, to destroy them. He will not be slack to him who hates him. He will repay him to his face.

Arminian

God repays those who hate him to their face and will not be slack. Arminian readers see personal accountability before a just God, with judgment answering persistent rejection of him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s justice toward those who hate him. The contrast between a thousand generations of love and direct repayment underscores both God’s mercy and his justice.

Deuteronomy 7:11

11You shall therefore keep the commandments, the statutes, and the ordinances which I command you today, to do them.

Arminian

Israel must keep the commandments, statutes, and ordinances given that day. Arminian readers see obedience as the fitting response of a redeemed and chosen people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the law given to a people already chosen and redeemed, so obedience responds to grace rather than earning it. The word “therefore” links obedience to the preceding description of who God is.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
