Passage

Deuteronomy 15

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Deuteronomy 15:1–11 sets out the seventh-year release of debts owed by fellow Israelites. Moses holds together the promise that obedience will bring such blessing that ‘there will be no poor with you’ and the realistic admission that ‘the poor will never cease out of the land’. Then he commands open-handed lending even when the release year is near. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that the passage roots generosity in God’s own giving and calls for a willing heart, not grudging compliance.

Deuteronomy 15:1

1At the end of every seven years, you shall cancel debts.

Arminian

‘At the end of every seven years, you shall cancel debts.’ Arminians see God building mercy into Israel’s economic life, so that no one would stay trapped in debt forever.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the seven-year rhythm extends the Sabbath principle (Exodus 23:10–11; Leviticus 25) into lending. Both traditions agree that the law reflects God’s ownership of the land and his care for the poor.

Deuteronomy 15:2

2This is the way it shall be done: every creditor shall release that which he has lent to his neighbor. He shall not require payment from his neighbor and his brother, because the LORD’s release has been proclaimed.

Arminian

The release is ‘the LORD’s release’, so creditors must not press a neighbor for payment. Arminians see the creditor’s obedience as a free response to God’s authority over Israel’s wealth.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters note that calling it ‘the LORD’s release’ grounds it in God’s rights rather than human goodwill. Both traditions acknowledge that interpreters differ on whether the debt was canceled outright or payment suspended for the year.

Deuteronomy 15:3

3Of a foreigner you may require it; but whatever of yours is with your brother, your hand shall release.

Arminian

A foreigner may be required to pay, but a brother must be released. Arminians see the law as distinguishing covenant obligations within Israel, likely including commercial lending to traders who were not Israelites.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the release expresses covenant brotherhood. Both traditions note that the Old Testament elsewhere commands love and justice for the foreigner living in Israel (10:19), so the verse is not a license for harshness.

Deuteronomy 15:4

4However there will be no poor with you (for the LORD will surely bless you in the land which the LORD your God gives you for an inheritance to possess)

Arminian

‘There will be no poor with you’, because the LORD will bless the land. Arminians see God’s ideal for his people: a community whose obedience leads to shared abundance.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the verse states God’s purpose and promise. Both traditions read it together with verse 11 and see the tension between them as resolved by the condition stated in verse 5.

Deuteronomy 15:5

5if only you diligently listen to the LORD your God’s voice, to observe to do all this commandment which I command you today.

Arminian

The blessing depends on diligently listening to the LORD’s voice and doing the commandment. Arminians emphasize the real conditionality of covenant blessing, which calls for responsive obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers also affirm the conditional form of covenant blessing within the Mosaic covenant and see obedience as the fruit of God’s grace. Both traditions agree that Israel was truly called to obey and truly accountable for its response.

Deuteronomy 15:6

6For the LORD your God will bless you, as he promised you. You will lend to many nations, but you will not borrow. You will rule over many nations, but they will not rule over you.

Arminian

Israel will lend to many nations and not borrow, and rule rather than be ruled. Arminians see the promise of blessing tied to faithfulness, held out as a real hope.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and note that the promise echoes 28:12–13. Both traditions read the verse as part of the Mosaic covenant’s blessings for obedience and agree on its meaning.

Deuteronomy 15:7

7If a poor man, one of your brothers, is with you within any of your gates in your land which the LORD your God gives you, you shall not harden your heart, nor shut your hand from your poor brother;

Arminian

Do not harden your heart or shut your hand to a poor brother. Arminians see that the command reaches the heart, not merely outward action.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the law addresses inward attitude as well as outward deed. Both traditions connect this with the New Testament call to open-handed love (1 John 3:17).

Deuteronomy 15:8

8but you shall surely open your hand to him, and shall surely lend him sufficient for his need, which he lacks.

Arminian

Open your hand and lend enough for his need. Arminians see generous provision as the natural expression of love for a neighbor in need.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the measure is the neighbor’s real need. Both traditions note that Jesus’ teaching on lending (Luke 6:34–35) builds on this kind of generosity.

Deuteronomy 15:9

9Beware that there not be a wicked thought in your heart, saying, “The seventh year, the year of release, is at hand,” and your eye be evil against your poor brother and you give him nothing; and he cry to the LORD against you, and it be sin to you.

Arminian

Beware of the ‘wicked thought’ of refusing a loan because the release year is near. Arminians see God exposing self-interested calculation and calling for integrity of heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the law reaches the motives behind decisions. Both traditions note the warning that the poor man’s cry to the LORD makes the refusal a sin, and they agree on the verse.

Deuteronomy 15:10

10You shall surely give, and your heart shall not be grieved when you give to him, because it is for this thing the LORD your God will bless you in all your work and in all that you put your hand to.

Arminian

Give without a grieving heart, for the LORD will bless your work. Arminians see cheerful giving as a free, willing response to God’s goodness (compare 2 Corinthians 9:7).

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that God values willing giving and promises his blessing. Both traditions read the promise as encouragement to generosity, not as a formula for gain.

Deuteronomy 15:11

11For the poor will never cease out of the land. Therefore I command you to surely open your hand to your brother, to your needy, and to your poor, in your land.

Arminian

‘The poor will never cease out of the land’, so open your hand to the needy. Arminians see realism about a fallen world joined to a continuing duty of generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Both traditions note that Jesus alludes to this verse in Mark 14:7, and both read it as a call to ongoing generosity rather than an acceptance of poverty.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
