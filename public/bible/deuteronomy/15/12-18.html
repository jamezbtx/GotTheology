Passage

Deuteronomy 15

Verses 12–18 · WEB

Deuteronomy 15:12–18 regulates the release of a Hebrew man or woman who has sold himself or herself into service. After six years the servant goes free in the seventh, and not empty-handed: the master must supply him generously from flock, threshing floor, and wine press, remembering Israel’s own redemption from slavery in Egypt. A servant who chooses to stay out of love may become a permanent servant by the awl through the ear. The law parallels Exodus 21:2–11 and adds that women are included. Both traditions read it as ancient Israelite debt-service law shaped by the memory of redemption.

Deuteronomy 15:12

12If your brother, a Hebrew man, or a Hebrew woman, is sold to you and serves you six years, then in the seventh year you shall let him go free from you.

Arminian

A Hebrew man or woman sold into service goes free in the seventh year. Arminians see God limiting debt-service so that it could not become permanent oppression.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and note that Deuteronomy explicitly includes women, which goes beyond Exodus 21:2. Both traditions read the passage as regulating ancient debt service, not endorsing the later chattel slavery of the modern era.

Deuteronomy 15:13

13When you let him go free from you, you shall not let him go empty.

Arminian

The freed servant must not go out empty. Arminians see release that sets a person up to make a fresh start, not merely to leave.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the law aims at the servant’s real restoration. Both traditions see God’s generosity reflected in the master’s duty.

Deuteronomy 15:14

14You shall furnish him liberally out of your flock, out of your threshing floor, and out of your wine press. As the LORD your God has blessed you, you shall give to him.

Arminian

Supply him liberally from flock, threshing floor, and wine press, as the LORD has blessed you. Arminians see the master’s gifts as a grateful sharing of what God gave him.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the measure of giving is God’s blessing. Both traditions read the verse as teaching that what we receive from God shapes what we owe to others.

Deuteronomy 15:15

15You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and the LORD your God redeemed you. Therefore I command you this thing today.

Arminian

Remember you were a slave in Egypt and the LORD redeemed you. Arminians see redemption as the motive for mercy: those set free are to set others free.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that grace received is the ground of obedience. Both traditions see the same pattern in the New Testament, where Christ’s redemption motivates love for others (Ephesians 4:32).

Deuteronomy 15:16

16It shall be, if he tells you, “I will not go out from you,” because he loves you and your house, because he is well with you,

Arminian

A servant may refuse to leave ‘because he loves you and your house’. Arminians see a relationship of mutual goodwill, where service can become a freely chosen bond.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the law assumes the servant was treated well. Both traditions read the verse as protecting the servant’s choice and agree on its meaning.

Deuteronomy 15:17

17then you shall take an awl, and thrust it through his ear to the door, and he shall be your servant forever. Also to your female servant you shall do likewise.

Arminian

An awl through the ear at the door makes him a servant forever, and the same for a female servant. Arminians see a public, freely chosen commitment marked by a lasting sign.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Both traditions note that some interpreters connect Psalm 40:6 with this rite, while others think that verse means something else, so they treat the link cautiously.

Deuteronomy 15:18

18It shall not seem hard to you when you let him go free from you, for he has been double the value of a hired hand as he served you six years. The LORD your God will bless you in all that you do.

Arminian

Do not resent freeing him, for he served you at double a hired hand’s value. Arminians see God appealing to fairness and gratitude so the master obeys gladly.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and note the promise of God’s blessing. Both traditions read the phrase about ‘double’ as describing the servant’s value, though interpreters differ on its exact sense.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
