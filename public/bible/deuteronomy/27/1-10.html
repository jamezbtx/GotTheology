Passage

Deuteronomy 27

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Moses and the elders command Israel to renew the covenant once they cross the Jordan: plastered stones inscribed with the law, an altar of uncut stones on Mount Ebal, and offerings with rejoicing. Joshua 8:30-35 records the fulfillment. Verse 9 declares that Israel has become the LORD’s people today.

Deuteronomy 27:1

1Moses and the elders of Israel commanded the people, saying, “Keep all the commandment which I command you today.

Arminian

Moses joins the elders in giving the command, so the covenant is not one man’s project but the community’s charge. Arminian readers note that keeping all the commandment is presented as a responsibility Israel accepts and must sustain through the generations.

Reformed

The command to keep all the commandment comes after forty years of God’s care in the wilderness. Reformed readers commonly stress this order: God redeems and sustains first, and the call to obedience answers grace already received.

Deuteronomy 27:2

2It shall be on the day when you shall pass over the Jordan to the land which the LORD your God gives you, that you shall set yourself up great stones, and coat them with plaster.

Arminian

Setting up the stones on the very day of crossing ties entry into the land to public commitment to God’s word. Wesleyan readers see a fitting picture: new beginnings with God call for an immediate, visible pledge of obedience.

Reformed

The land is described as what the LORD your God gives you, even as Israel must cross and set up stones. Reformed readers see gift and duty held together, with the gift logically first.

Deuteronomy 27:3

3You shall write on them all the words of this law, when you have passed over, that you may go in to the land which the LORD your God gives you, a land flowing with milk and honey, as the LORD, the God of your fathers, has promised you.

Arminian

Writing all the words of this law makes God’s will accessible to every Israelite who passes by. Arminian readers value this public, readable law as a sign that God addresses all his people and expects them to understand.

Reformed

The law is written as Israel enters a land promised to the fathers, linking the covenant at Moab with the oath to Abraham. Reformed readers often stress this continuity of one gracious purpose across the covenants.

Deuteronomy 27:4

4It shall be, when you have crossed over the Jordan, that you shall set up these stones, which I command you today, on Mount Ebal, and you shall coat them with plaster.

Arminian

Mount Ebal, later the mountain of the curse (27:13), is where the law is displayed and the altar is built. Arminian readers find a sober reminder that God’s word confronts people with real consequences as well as real promises.

Reformed

The WEB follows the Masoretic text in naming Ebal; the Samaritan Pentateuch reads Gerizim, a well-known textual variant. Reformed readers, like most interpreters, generally follow the Masoretic reading while acknowledging the variant.

Deuteronomy 27:5

5There you shall build an altar to the LORD your God, an altar of stones. You shall not use any iron tool on them.

Arminian

No iron tool may shape the altar stones, echoing Exodus 20:25. Arminian readers often draw the lesson that approaching God depends on his provision rather than on human craftsmanship or display.

Reformed

Many Reformed interpreters see the ban on tooled stones as excluding human embellishment from God’s appointed worship. They often relate it to the conviction that worship should follow what God prescribes.

Deuteronomy 27:6

6You shall build the LORD your God’s altar of uncut stones. You shall offer burnt offerings on it to the LORD your God.

Arminian

Burnt offerings on the altar express wholehearted dedication. Arminian readers note that the covenant renewal includes consecration, a people offering themselves to the God who brought them in.

Reformed

Uncut stones and burnt offerings together point to atonement and consecration that God has provided. Reformed readers frequently see in the altar a pointer to the sacrifice of Christ, where acceptance with God is grounded.

Deuteronomy 27:7

7You shall sacrifice peace offerings, and shall eat there. You shall rejoice before the LORD your God.

Arminian

Peace offerings and a shared meal mean that the covenant is celebrated with joy before God. Wesleyan readers stress that obedience and gladness belong together in the life God desires for his people.

Reformed

Eating before the LORD signals fellowship restored and enjoyed. Reformed readers often link this covenant meal with the communion God grants his people, later expressed in the Lord’s Supper.

Deuteronomy 27:8

8You shall write on the stones all the words of this law very plainly.”

Arminian

Very plainly stresses clarity. Arminian readers see God’s will as something ordinary people can read and grasp, removing any excuse that the law was hidden or obscure.

Reformed

The plain writing of the law fits the Reformed stress on the clarity of Scripture in what is necessary, while still allowing that some passages need careful study.

Deuteronomy 27:9

9Moses and the Levitical priests spoke to all Israel, saying, “Be silent and listen, Israel! Today you have become the people of the LORD your God.

Arminian

Today you have become the people of the LORD: the covenant is renewed with a new generation. Arminian readers stress that each generation must take the covenant as its own through faith and obedience.

Reformed

Israel had long been God’s people, yet today they become his people in this renewal. Reformed readers see covenant identity confirmed and sealed afresh, grounded in God’s prior choice and redemption.

Deuteronomy 27:10

10You shall therefore obey the LORD your God’s voice, and do his commandments and his statutes, which I command you today.”

Arminian

Therefore obey: covenant identity leads to obedience. Arminian readers emphasize that belonging to God and continuing to walk with him are bound together and that the walk is a genuine human response.

Reformed

The therefore makes obedience the fruit of being God’s people, not the means of becoming so. Reformed readers often cite this order as basic to the law’s role in the life of the redeemed.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
