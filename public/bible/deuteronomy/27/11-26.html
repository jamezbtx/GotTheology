Passage

Deuteronomy 27

Verses 11–26 · WEB

Six tribes are to stand on Mount Gerizim for blessing and six on Mount Ebal for the curse, while the Levites pronounce twelve curses and the people answer Amen. Several curses target sins done in secret, and the last (27:26) sums up the whole law; Paul cites it in Galatians 3:10.

Deuteronomy 27:11

11Moses commanded the people the same day, saying,

Arminian

Moses gives the instructions on the same day, keeping blessing and curse together with the covenant renewal. Arminian readers note that God sets both possibilities clearly before his people.

Reformed

The same day links the ceremony on the two mountains to the covenant declaration of 27:9. Reformed readers see the sanctions as part of one covenant administration given to a redeemed people.

Deuteronomy 27:12

12“These shall stand on Mount Gerizim to bless the people, when you have crossed over the Jordan: Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Joseph, and Benjamin.

Arminian

Six tribes stand on Gerizim to bless. The text gives no reason for the grouping, and Arminian readers resist speculation, focusing instead on blessing as a genuine offer set before the whole people.

Reformed

The tribes on Gerizim descend from Leah and Rachel, though the text does not explain the arrangement. Reformed readers note that blessing is pronounced by God’s appointment, not chosen by the tribes themselves.

Deuteronomy 27:13

13These shall stand on Mount Ebal for the curse: Reuben, Gad, Asher, Zebulun, Dan, and Naphtali.

Arminian

Six tribes stand on Ebal for the curse, including Reuben, the firstborn. Arminian readers note that standing on Ebal does not doom those tribes; the curses fall on whoever does the deeds named.

Reformed

The grouping on Ebal includes the sons of the handmaids along with Reuben and Zebulun. Reformed readers avoid assigning hidden meaning and focus on the public, covenantal character of the ritual.

Deuteronomy 27:14

14With a loud voice, the Levites shall say to all the men of Israel,

Arminian

The Levites speak with a loud voice to all the men of Israel, so no one can claim not to have heard. Arminian readers see clear proclamation as essential to genuine accountability.

Reformed

The Levites, teachers of the law, lead the liturgy. Reformed readers often connect this with the ordained ministry of the word, through which God addresses and binds his people.

Deuteronomy 27:15

15‘Cursed is the man who makes an engraved or molten image, an abomination to the LORD, the work of the hands of the craftsman, and sets it up in secret.’ All the people shall answer and say, ‘Amen.’

Arminian

The first curse targets a secret image. Arminian readers note that the curse reaches into hidden places, calling for inward integrity before a God who sees what others cannot.

Reformed

Image-making heads the list, echoing the first two commandments. Reformed readers see idolatry as the root sin from which other wrongs grow, a pattern they also find in Romans 1.

Deuteronomy 27:16

16‘Cursed is he who dishonors his father or his mother.’ All the people shall say, ‘Amen.’

Arminian

Dishonoring parents is cursed, echoing the fifth commandment. Arminian readers stress that family faithfulness is part of covenant holiness and a sphere where daily choices matter.

Reformed

Honoring parents is grounded in God’s order for society. Reformed readers often treat the fifth commandment broadly, covering all relations of authority and care. The people’s Amen makes the standard their own.

Deuteronomy 27:17

17‘Cursed is he who removes his neighbor’s landmark.’ All the people shall say, ‘Amen.’

Arminian

Moving a landmark steals land quietly. Arminian readers see God’s concern for the weak and for justice in everyday dealings, where secret wrongs harm neighbors.

Reformed

The land was God’s gift to each family (19:14). Reformed readers see stealing a boundary as an offense against God’s distribution, not just against a neighbor.

Deuteronomy 27:18

18‘Cursed is he who leads the blind astray on the road.’ All the people shall say, ‘Amen.’

Arminian

Misleading the blind abuses vulnerability. Arminian readers stress God’s protective love for those who cannot defend themselves and his call to active compassion. Readers sometimes apply the principle to misleading anyone who is vulnerable or easily deceived.

Reformed

Leviticus 19:14 forbids putting a stumbling block before the blind. Reformed readers note that God’s law protects the vulnerable and that he himself takes up their cause.

Deuteronomy 27:19

19‘Cursed is he who withholds justice from the foreigner, fatherless, and widow.’ All the people shall say, ‘Amen.’

Arminian

Foreigner, fatherless, and widow are named together as God’s special concern. Arminian readers often stress that justice for the marginalized reflects God’s love for all people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this as an application of the moral law and of the character of God, who executes justice for the fatherless and widow (10:18). Perverting their justice is cursed.

Deuteronomy 27:20

20‘Cursed is he who lies with his father’s wife, because he dishonors his father’s bed.’ All the people shall say, ‘Amen.’

Arminian

Sexual sin with a father’s wife violates family order. Arminian readers stress that holiness extends to the most private areas of life, governed by God’s good design.

Reformed

The phrase dishonors his father’s bed links this curse to Leviticus 18:8. Reformed readers see sexual boundaries as part of God’s moral law, still binding in substance.

Deuteronomy 27:21

21‘Cursed is he who lies with any kind of animal.’ All the people shall say, ‘Amen.’

Arminian

Bestiality is cursed, a sin that defies the created order. Arminian readers note that God’s law guards the dignity of human beings made in his image.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this prohibition with Leviticus 18:23 and see in it the moral law’s defense of the distinction between humans and animals. The curse protects that boundary.

Deuteronomy 27:22

22‘Cursed is he who lies with his sister, his father’s daughter or his mother’s daughter.’ All the people shall say, ‘Amen.’

Arminian

Sexual relations with a sister, whether full or half, are cursed. Arminian readers see God protecting the family from exploitation and preserving trust within households.

Reformed

The detail of father’s or mother’s daughter closes loopholes (Leviticus 18:9). Reformed readers note how the law anticipates attempts to evade it. Half-siblings are not excepted.

Deuteronomy 27:23

23‘Cursed is he who lies with his mother-in-law.’ All the people shall say, ‘Amen.’

Arminian

Relations with a mother-in-law are cursed. Arminian readers stress that marriage creates real bonds of kinship that must be honored. The curse treats kin by marriage as true family, worthy of the same protection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in this a recognition that marriage creates kinship as real as blood relationship, a principle found also in Leviticus 20:14. Kinship by marriage counts.

Deuteronomy 27:24

24‘Cursed is he who secretly kills his neighbor.’ All the people shall say, ‘Amen.’

Arminian

Secret murder is cursed. Arminian readers stress that God sees what is hidden and that human life is precious because people bear his image. Hidden violence is not hidden from him.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with the sixth commandment and with Genesis 9:6. The curse affirms that God avenges innocent blood even when human courts cannot.

Deuteronomy 27:25

25‘Cursed is he who takes a bribe to kill an innocent person.’ All the people shall say, ‘Amen.’

Arminian

Taking a bribe to kill the innocent corrupts justice. Arminian readers stress that God hates the abuse of power and money against the defenseless. Corrupt courts offend him deeply.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this as a sin against both justice and life, connecting it with laws against bribery (16:19) and God’s impartial character. Innocent blood cries out to him.

Deuteronomy 27:26

26‘Cursed is he who doesn’t uphold the words of this law by doing them.’ All the people shall say, ‘Amen.’ ”

Arminian

Paul cites this verse in Galatians 3:10 to show that those who rely on works of the law are under a curse. Arminian readers commonly stress Paul’s target: reliance on law-keeping for justification rather than faith in Christ. Both traditions affirm Christ redeemed us from the curse (Galatians 3:13).

Reformed

Paul’s wording, close to the Greek Old Testament, stresses continuing in all things. Reformed readers often see here the law’s demand for perfect obedience, which Christ fulfilled and whose curse he bore (Galatians 3:13). Both traditions agree that the curse is removed in Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
