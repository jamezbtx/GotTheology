Passage

Deuteronomy 16

Verses 9–17 · WEB

Deuteronomy 16:9–17 covers the other two pilgrimage feasts, Weeks and Booths, and then the rule that all males appear before the LORD three times a year. Both feasts are marked by rejoicing that includes children, servants, Levites, foreigners, orphans, and widows, and by gifts in proportion to God’s blessing. Both traditions see here joyful worship that overflows into care for the vulnerable, and many readers link the Feast of Weeks with Pentecost (Acts 2:1).

Deuteronomy 16:9

9You shall count for yourselves seven weeks. From the time you begin to put the sickle to the standing grain you shall begin to count seven weeks.

Arminian

Count seven weeks from the start of the grain harvest. Arminians see the harvest season framed by worship, from first cut to completion.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree. Both traditions note that the count gives the feast its name and that it later became known as Pentecost.

Deuteronomy 16:10

10You shall keep the feast of weeks to the LORD your God with a tribute of a free will offering of your hand, which you shall give according to how the LORD your God blesses you.

Arminian

Keep the Feast of Weeks with a freewill offering in proportion to God’s blessing. Arminians see giving as a willing response, matched to what God has given.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the measure is God’s blessing. Both traditions read the verse as teaching proportional, grateful giving.

Deuteronomy 16:11

11You shall rejoice before the LORD your God: you, your son, your daughter, your male servant, your female servant, the Levite who is within your gates, the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow who are among you, in the place which the LORD your God shall choose to cause his name to dwell there.

Arminian

Rejoice before the LORD with your household, servants, Levites, foreigners, orphans, and widows. Arminians see God’s joy reaching the vulnerable and the overlooked.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the feast’s joy includes everyone in the community. Both traditions see the verse as a model of worship that welcomes the needy.

Deuteronomy 16:12

12You shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt. You shall observe and do these statutes.

Arminian

Remember you were a slave in Egypt, and keep these statutes. Arminians see memory of redemption again motivating obedience and generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Both traditions read the verse as grounding the feast’s inclusiveness in Israel’s own experience of rescue.

Deuteronomy 16:13

13You shall keep the feast of booths seven days, after you have gathered in from your threshing floor and from your wine press.

Arminian

Keep the Feast of Booths for seven days after the harvest is gathered. Arminians see thanksgiving at the end of the agricultural year.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree. Both traditions note that Leviticus 23:42–43 links the booths with Israel’s dwelling in the wilderness.

Deuteronomy 16:14

14You shall rejoice in your feast, you, your son, your daughter, your male servant, your female servant, the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow who are within your gates.

Arminian

Rejoice in your feast with the whole community, including the vulnerable. Arminians see shared joy as part of worship itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the feast’s joy is to be shared widely. Both traditions agree on the verse. The list repeats verse 11, which shows that the same welcome applies at both harvest feasts.

Deuteronomy 16:15

15You shall keep a feast to the LORD your God seven days in the place which the LORD chooses, because the LORD your God will bless you in all your increase and in all the work of your hands, and you shall be altogether joyful.

Arminian

Seven days at the chosen place, because God will bless you, and ‘you shall be altogether joyful’. Arminians see joy as a fitting response to God’s blessing.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that joy is commanded and grounded in God’s goodness. Both traditions agree on the verse. The feast is held at the chosen place, as with Passover and Weeks.

Deuteronomy 16:16

16Three times in a year all of your males shall appear before the LORD your God in the place which he chooses: in the feast of unleavened bread, in the feast of weeks, and in the feast of booths. They shall not appear before the LORD empty.

Arminian

All males appear three times a year, at Unleavened Bread, Weeks, and Booths, and not empty-handed. Arminians see regular pilgrimage as a rhythm of devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Both traditions note that this matches Exodus 23:14–17 and 34:23, and both read “They shall not appear before the LORD empty” as calling for offerings.

Deuteronomy 16:17

17Every man shall give as he is able, according to the LORD your God’s blessing which he has given you.

Arminian

Every man gives as he is able, according to the LORD’s blessing. Arminians see giving measured by ability and gratitude.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree. Both traditions connect the principle with 2 Corinthians 8:12 and agree on the verse. No fixed amount is set, so the gift reflects each household’s blessing.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
