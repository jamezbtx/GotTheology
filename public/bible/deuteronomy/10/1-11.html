Passage

Deuteronomy 10

Verses 1–11 · WEB

The LORD tells Moses to cut two new tablets and make an ark, and he writes the Ten Commandments again, which Moses places in the ark. A parenthetical travel note mentions Aaron’s death at Moserah and the setting apart of Levi to carry the ark, minister, and bless, with the LORD as Levi’s inheritance; Moses then recalls that the LORD heard his intercession and sent the people on to possess the land. Both traditions see shared ground in God’s grace renewing the broken covenant, and the relation of the itinerary to Numbers 20 and 33 is discussed by interpreters.

Deuteronomy 10:1

1At that time the LORD said to me, “Cut two stone tablets like the first, and come up to me onto the mountain, and make an ark of wood.

Arminian

The LORD tells Moses to cut new tablets and make an ark. Arminian readers note that God restores the covenant after the people broke it.

The renewal is God’s initiative (cf. Exod 34:1).

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace following judgment. God does not abandon the covenant his people broke.

The ark will hold the tablets as a sign of God’s covenant and presence among them (Exod 25:16, 22).

Deuteronomy 10:2

2I will write on the tablets the words that were on the first tablets which you broke, and you shall put them in the ark.”

Arminian

God will write the same words on the new tablets. Arminian readers note that the covenant terms are unchanged.

God’s standards do not change even though the people failed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same law renewed. God does not lower his standard but restores the relationship.

The words are written by God again, as the first time.

Deuteronomy 10:3

3So I made an ark of acacia wood, and cut two stone tablets like the first, and went up onto the mountain, having the two tablets in my hand.

Arminian

Moses makes an ark of acacia wood and cuts the tablets. Arminian readers note Moses’ obedience.

Exodus 37:1 says Bezalel made the ark; interpreters discuss how the two accounts relate.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses as the one who carries out God’s command, whether directly or through others.

The acacia ark matches the description in Exodus 25:10.

Deuteronomy 10:4

4He wrote on the tablets, according to the first writing, the ten commandments, which the LORD spoke to you on the mountain out of the middle of the fire in the day of the assembly; and the LORD gave them to me.

Arminian

God writes the Ten Commandments on the tablets as before. Arminian readers note that this verse names them explicitly as the ten commandments.

The commandments are the core of the covenant.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the moral law restated by God’s own hand, “according to the first writing.” The renewal does not revise the content.

The Ten Commandments hold a central place in the catechesis of both traditions, which commonly treat them as a summary of God’s moral will.

Deuteronomy 10:5

5I turned and came down from the mountain, and put the tablets in the ark which I had made; and there they are as the LORD commanded me.

Arminian

Moses puts the tablets in the ark, and there they remain. Arminian readers note the phrase “there they are,” which speaks of their continued presence.

Later Scripture also says the ark held the tablets (1 Kings 8:9; Heb 9:4).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law kept at the heart of Israel’s worship, inside the ark that stood in the Most Holy Place.

The ark becomes the place where God’s law and his presence meet. The tablets are preserved there “as the LORD commanded,” stressing faithful obedience.

Deuteronomy 10:6

6(The children of Israel traveled from Beeroth Bene Jaakan to Moserah. There Aaron died, and there he was buried; and Eleazar his son ministered in the priest’s office in his place.

Arminian

A parenthetical note says Israel traveled to Moserah, where Aaron died and Eleazar succeeded him. Arminian readers note that Numbers places Aaron’s death at Mount Hor (Num 20:22–29; 33:38) and lists the stations in a different order (Num 33:30–39).

Interpreters have proposed several explanations, such as Moserah being a region near Mount Hor; the relationship is discussed.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note the differences from Numbers and treat them as a matter interpreters discuss rather than settle.

The point is the continuity of the priesthood: Aaron’s sin (9:20) did not end the priestly line, and Eleazar continues it.

Deuteronomy 10:7

7From there they traveled to Gudgodah; and from Gudgodah to Jotbathah, a land of brooks of water.

Arminian

They traveled to Gudgodah and then Jotbathah, described as a land of brooks of water. Arminian readers note the mention of water after so many wilderness hardships.

The locations cannot be identified with confidence, and no attempt is made to place them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s provision along the journey, even in a brief travel note.

The relation of these stations to the list in Numbers 33:32–33 is discussed by interpreters. The parenthesis keeps the focus on God’s care rather than on precise geography.

Deuteronomy 10:8

8At that time the LORD set apart the tribe of Levi to bear the ark of the LORD’s covenant, to stand before the LORD to minister to him, and to bless in his name, to this day.

Arminian

The LORD set apart Levi to carry the ark, minister, and bless. Arminian readers note the connection with the Levites’ faithfulness at the golden calf (Exod 32:26–29).

The tribe’s service is defined.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God freely choosing Levi for holy service (Num 3:5–13). The tribe is set apart to carry the ark, stand before the LORD, and bless in his name.

Blessing in God’s name recalls the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:23–27, which places God’s name on the people.

Deuteronomy 10:9

9Therefore Levi has no portion nor inheritance with his brothers; the LORD is his inheritance, according as the LORD your God spoke to him.)

Arminian

Levi has no inheritance; the LORD is his inheritance. Arminian readers note that this means dependence on God and on the people’s offerings (Num 18:20; Deut 18:1–2).

It is a privilege, not a deprivation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a picture of having God himself as one’s portion (cf. Ps 16:5; 73:26).

Christians often apply this to all believers as a royal priesthood (1 Pet 2:9).

Deuteronomy 10:10

10I stayed on the mountain, as at the first time, forty days and forty nights; and the LORD listened to me that time also. The LORD would not destroy you.

Arminian

Moses stayed forty days as at first, and the LORD listened again and did not destroy them. Arminian readers note the repeated emphasis on God’s responsiveness to prayer.

The parenthesis ends, and the narrative resumes.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s mercy given through intercession. “The LORD would not destroy you” is the decisive outcome of Moses’ prayer.

The phrase “that time also” recalls 9:19, presenting God’s repeated willingness to hear his appointed mediator.

Deuteronomy 10:11

11The LORD said to me, “Arise, take your journey before the people; and they shall go in and possess the land which I swore to their fathers to give to them.”

Arminian

The LORD tells Moses to lead the people to possess the land sworn to their fathers. Arminian readers note that the journey resumes after forgiveness.

God’s purpose moves forward.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s oath to the fathers as the reason the journey continues after such grievous sin.

The covenant promise stands despite the people’s failure. The command to “arise” echoes 9:12, but now Moses goes down to lead, not to confront idolatry.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
