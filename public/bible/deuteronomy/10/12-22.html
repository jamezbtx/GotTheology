Passage

Deuteronomy 10

Verses 12–22 · WEB

Moses asks what the LORD requires: to fear him, walk in his ways, love and serve him with all the heart and soul, and keep his commandments for Israel’s good. He grounds this in the LORD’s ownership of all things, his love and choice of the patriarchs and their descendants, his justice for the fatherless, widow, and foreigner, and his great works for Israel, and he calls them to circumcise their hearts and love the foreigner. Both traditions share the call to wholehearted love and justice; they differ mainly on how the command to circumcise the heart (10:16) relates to God’s promise to do it (Deut 30:6).

Deuteronomy 10:12

12Now, Israel, what does the LORD your God require of you, but to fear the LORD your God, to walk in all his ways, to love him, and to serve the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul,

Arminian

What does the LORD require? To fear him, walk in his ways, love him, and serve him with all heart and soul. Arminian readers note the relational nature of the requirement and its echo in Micah 6:8.

The question invites a willing response.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the summary of covenant duty, which Jesus later echoes in the great commandment (Matt 22:37).

The requirement is total: all heart and soul.

Deuteronomy 10:13

13to keep the LORD’s commandments and statutes, which I command you today for your good?

Arminian

Keep the commandments, which are for your good. Arminian readers stress that God’s law is given for human flourishing (cf. Deut 6:24).

Obedience is not arbitrary but beneficial.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the law as good and given in love, “for your good.”

The commandments are not burdensome to those who love God (1 John 5:3). Reformed theology often speaks of the law as a guide for grateful living among God’s people.

Deuteronomy 10:14

14Behold, to the LORD your God belongs heaven, the heaven of heavens, and the earth, with all that is therein.

Arminian

Heaven, the highest heavens, and the earth belong to the LORD. Arminian readers note the vastness of God’s ownership (cf. Ps 24:1).

This sets the stage for the surprise of verse 15.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s absolute sovereignty over all creation. Even “the heaven of heavens” cannot contain him (1 Kings 8:27).

The owner of everything chose one people to be his own (Exod 19:5), which sets up the wonder of verse 15.

Deuteronomy 10:15

15Only the LORD had a delight in your fathers to love them, and he chose their offspring after them, even you above all peoples, as it is today.

Arminian

Yet the LORD delighted in the fathers and chose their offspring above all peoples. Arminian readers usually read this as God’s corporate, national choice of Israel for a role in his purposes, grounded in his love and promise (Deut 7:6–8).

They caution against reading it as the individual election of each Israelite to salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s free, unmerited love and choice (Deut 7:7–8). Many see the pattern of election here, though they recognize the verse speaks of Israel corporately.

Both traditions read it as grace: God’s love, not Israel’s merit, explains the choice (cf. Rom 11:28).

Deuteronomy 10:16

16Circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart, and be no more stiff-necked.

Arminian

Circumcise the foreskin of your heart and stop being stiff-necked. Arminian readers see a real command that calls for a real inward response, made possible by God’s grace (cf. Jer 4:4; Ezek 18:31).

Deuteronomy 30:6 promises that God will circumcise the heart, which Arminian readers read as God’s work that people receive and cooperate with, not as overriding their will.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command as exposing the people’s inability, since the stiff-necked heart cannot renew itself (cf. Deut 29:4).

They read Deuteronomy 30:6 as God’s promise to do what he commands, by effectual regeneration (Jer 31:33; Ezek 36:26; Rom 2:29; Col 2:11).

Key contrast Both agree that the command is real, that God’s grace is necessary for a changed heart, and that Deuteronomy 30:6 promises God’s own work. Arminian readers hold that the command implies a grace-enabled response, so the promised circumcision of the heart is received through cooperation that can be refused. Reformed readers hold that the command exposes human inability, so the promised circumcision of the heart is God’s effectual regeneration that produces the response he commands.

Deuteronomy 10:17

17For the LORD your God, he is God of gods and Lord of lords, the great God, the mighty, and the awesome, who doesn’t respect persons or take bribes.

Arminian

The LORD is God of gods and Lord of lords, great, mighty, and awesome, showing no partiality and taking no bribes. Arminian readers note that God’s impartiality is the basis of justice.

The New Testament repeats the title (Rev 19:16) and God’s impartiality (Acts 10:34; Rom 2:11).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s supremacy over every rival power and his perfect justice.

His refusal of bribes shows that his favor cannot be bought (2 Chron 19:7). Israel’s judges are to reflect the same impartiality (Deut 16:19).

Deuteronomy 10:18

18He executes justice for the fatherless and widow and loves the foreigner in giving him food and clothing.

Arminian

He executes justice for the fatherless and widow and loves the foreigner. Arminian readers stress God’s special care for the vulnerable (Ps 68:5; 146:9).

God’s character sets the pattern for his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s justice and compassion joined. He provides food and clothing for the foreigner.

True religion reflects this concern for orphans and widows (James 1:27). This is shared ground across both traditions.

Deuteronomy 10:19

19Therefore love the foreigner, for you were foreigners in the land of Egypt.

Arminian

Love the foreigner, for you were foreigners in Egypt. Arminian readers note that memory of redemption grounds compassion (Lev 19:34; Exod 23:9).

Israel’s ethics flow from its history.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see grace motivating love. Jesus’ teaching extends this to welcoming the stranger (Matt 25:35).

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 10:20

20You shall fear the LORD your God. You shall serve him. You shall cling to him, and you shall swear by his name.

Arminian

Fear, serve, cling to, and swear by the LORD. Arminian readers note the call to steadfast loyalty (cf. Josh 23:8).

Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 6:13, a parallel, in Matthew 4:10.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wholehearted devotion required: fear, service, clinging, and oaths in God’s name alone.

“Cling” is the language of close covenant attachment (cf. Deut 13:4). Swearing by his name means acknowledging him alone as the God who guarantees truth.

Deuteronomy 10:21

21He is your praise, and he is your God, who has done for you these great and awesome things which your eyes have seen.

Arminian

He is your praise and your God, who did great things you have seen. Arminian readers note that the people’s own eyes witnessed God’s works.

Praise is the response to experienced grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God himself as both the object and the content of Israel’s praise (cf. Jer 17:14).

Worship rests on what God has done in history, the great and awesome things the people saw, not on abstract ideas about him.

Deuteronomy 10:22

22Your fathers went down into Egypt with seventy persons; and now the LORD your God has made you as the stars of the sky for multitude.

Arminian

Seventy went down to Egypt; now Israel is as numerous as the stars. Arminian readers note the fulfillment of the promise to Abraham (Gen 15:5; 22:17).

The number seventy matches Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5; Acts 7:14 gives seventy-five, following another textual tradition.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to his promise across generations.

The chapter ends with evidence of God’s grace in Israel’s growth.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
