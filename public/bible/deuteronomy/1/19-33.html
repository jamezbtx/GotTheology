Passage

Deuteronomy 1

Verses 19–33 · WEB

Moses retells the events at Kadesh Barnea. The LORD had set the land before Israel and told them not to fear. The people asked to send spies, who brought back good fruit and a good report of the land, yet Israel refused to go up, accused God of hating them, and was terrified by reports of giants. Moses reminded them that God had fought for them and carried them like a son, but they still did not believe.

Deuteronomy 1:19

19We traveled from Horeb and went through all that great and terrible wilderness which you saw, by the way to the hill country of the Amorites, as the LORD our God commanded us; and we came to Kadesh Barnea.

Arminian

Israel crossed the great and terrible wilderness, as God commanded, and reached Kadesh Barnea. God had brought them safely through every danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same evidence of God’s care. Israel traveled from Horeb through “that great and terrible wilderness” by the way God commanded, and they came safely to Kadesh Barnea; the danger of the road is named so that the safe arrival can be seen as his doing.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:20

20I said to you, “You have come to the hill country of the Amorites, which the LORD our God gives to us.

Arminian

Moses tells them they have reached the land “which the LORD our God gives to us.” The gift is presented as already in hand.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the certainty of the gift. Moses tells them they have come to the hill country of the Amorites, “which the LORD our God gives to us”—the land spoken of as already given, not as a prize they must first earn.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:21

21Behold, the LORD your God has set the land before you. Go up, take possession, as the LORD the God of your fathers has spoken to you. Don’t be afraid, neither be dismayed.”

Arminian

Moses urges them to go up and take possession, as the LORD the God of their fathers has spoken, without fear or dismay. Faith here means acting on a word already given, not waiting for a safer feeling.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the command assumes the promise. “The LORD your God has set the land before you” precedes “Go up, take possession,” so courage is grounded in what God has already done, not in Israel’s estimate of its own strength.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:22

22You came near to me, everyone of you, and said, “Let’s send men before us, that they may search the land for us, and bring back to us word of the way by which we must go up, and the cities to which we shall come.”

Arminian

Here the people propose sending spies. Numbers 13:1–2 records God commanding the mission; the two accounts are usually read as complementary, with God approving the people’s request.

Reformed

Reformed readers hold the same harmonizing view. Here the people propose sending men to search the land; Numbers 13:1–2 records God’s command to send them. The two are commonly read as complementary, with the LORD approving a request the people had made.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:23

23The thing pleased me well. I took twelve of your men, one man for every tribe.

Arminian

Moses approves and chooses twelve men, one per tribe. The plan itself was not sinful; the mission becomes a test only when the report is later met with unbelief.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The sin came later, in unbelief. Taking one man from every tribe made the search a national act, so the later refusal would be a refusal after testimony from every tribe’s own representative.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:24

24They turned and went up into the hill country, and came to the valley of Eshcol, and spied it out.

Arminian

The spies go up into the hill country, come to the valley of Eshcol, and spy it out. Moses compresses the longer story of Numbers 13, keeping the visit to Eshcol as the emblem of what they actually saw.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the verse the same way. The valley of Eshcol is named because of the cluster taken there; Moses points the new generation back to a place whose very name records the land’s plenty.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:25

25They took some of the fruit of the land in their hands and brought it down to us, and brought us word again, and said, “It is a good land which the LORD our God gives to us.”

Arminian

The spies bring fruit and say, “It is a good land which the LORD our God gives to us.” Moses highlights the good part of the report, which made Israel’s refusal less excusable.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same emphasis. The spies bring fruit in their hands and say, “It is a good land which the LORD our God gives to us.” Moses highlights this true part of the report, which made Israel’s later refusal less excusable.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:26

26Yet you wouldn’t go up, but rebelled against the commandment of the LORD your God.

Arminian

Yet Israel would not go up, but rebelled against the commandment of the LORD their God. Refusing to receive a gift already set before them is not treated as caution; it is named as rebellion.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that unbelief is active disobedience, not mere hesitation. “You wouldn’t go up” is paired with rebellion against the LORD’s commandment, so the failure is measured against a clear word, not against a vague hope.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:27

27You murmured in your tents, and said, “Because the LORD hated us, he has brought us out of the land of Egypt, to deliver us into the hand of the Amorites to destroy us.

Arminian

In their tents they murmured that the LORD had brought them out of Egypt because he hated them, to hand them to the Amorites. Unbelief distorts God’s redeeming love into a charge of hatred, and it does so in private speech as well as in public complaint.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see unbelief as slander against God’s character. The exodus itself is recast as a plot to destroy them, so the issue is not only fear of the Amorites, but a false account of the God who had already saved them.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:28

28Where are we going up? Our brothers have made our heart melt, saying, ‘The people are greater and taller than we. The cities are great and fortified up to the sky. Moreover we have seen the sons of the Anakim there!’ ”

Arminian

The spies’ words made their hearts melt: people greater and taller than they, cities fortified up to the sky, and the sons of the Anakim. Fear was spread through speech, as “our brothers” turned a true difficulty into a melting of courage.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same power of discouraging speech. The report of size, walls, and Anakim is allowed to outweigh the good word of verse 25, and Moses traces the melting of the heart to what “our brothers” had said.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:29

29Then I said to you, “Don’t be terrified. Don’t be afraid of them.

Arminian

Moses told them not to be terrified or afraid of them. The command is pastoral as well as authoritative: he meets the melted heart with a word against terror, before he goes on to the reasons in the next verses.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. “Don’t be terrified. Don’t be afraid of them” is spoken into a fear already stirred by the report; Moses does not deny the enemies, but he will not let terror have the last word.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:30

30The LORD your God, who goes before you, he will fight for you, according to all that he did for you in Egypt before your eyes,

Arminian

God goes before them and will fight for them, as he did in Egypt before their eyes. Past deliverance is the basis for present trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise ground faith in God’s past acts. The LORD who goes before them will fight for them, “according to all that he did for you in Egypt before your eyes,” so present courage is asked on the basis of a deliverance they had themselves seen.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:31

31and in the wilderness where you have seen how that the LORD your God carried you, as a man carries his son, in all the way that you went, until you came to this place.”

Arminian

God carried Israel in the wilderness “as a man carries his son.” The image is tender and personal. Arminian readers emphasize God’s fatherly love as the context of every command.

Reformed

Reformed readers also treasure the image of God’s fatherly care, which they see as grounding Israel’s adoption as his son (compare Exodus 4:22). This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:32

32Yet in this thing you didn’t believe the LORD your God,

Arminian

“Yet in this thing you didn’t believe the LORD your God.” Unbelief is named as the root problem. After the reminder of Egypt and of the wilderness guidance, the failure is stated in a single charge: they did not believe him in this matter of the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Hebrews 3:19 makes the same diagnosis. “In this thing” points to the refusal to go up; the issue is unbelief toward the LORD himself, not a mere misreading of the spies’ details.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:33

33who went before you on the way, to seek out a place for you to pitch your tents in: in fire by night, to show you by what way you should go, and in the cloud by day.

Arminian

God went before them in fire and cloud to find them places to camp. The guide they refused to trust had guided them every day.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same irony. The God they would not believe is the one who had gone before them in fire by night and in the cloud by day, seeking out places for them to camp and showing them the way.

This is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
