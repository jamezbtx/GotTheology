Passage

Deuteronomy 1

Verses 34–46 · WEB

The LORD swore that no one of that evil generation would see the land except Caleb, and later Joshua, along with the children the people had feared for. Moses himself was barred. When Israel then tried to fight against God’s word, they were routed by the Amorites, and their weeping was not heard.

Deuteronomy 1:34

34The LORD heard the voice of your words and was angry, and swore, saying,

Arminian

The LORD heard the voice of their words, was angry, and swore an oath. God takes his people’s words seriously; the murmuring in the tents is not treated as a private mood that leaves no mark.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same seriousness. What Israel said is heard, and the LORD’s anger takes the form of an oath, so the coming sentence will rest on his sworn word, not on a passing mood.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:35

35“Surely not one of these men of this evil generation shall see the good land which I swore to give to your fathers,

Arminian

Not one of “this evil generation” shall see the good land which God swore to give to their fathers. The judgment matches their refusal: they would not go up to see it, and they will not see it.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way. Interpreters in both traditions note that this exclusion concerned the land and do not draw from it alone conclusions about each person’s final state. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:36

36except Caleb the son of Jephunneh. He shall see it. I will give the land that he has trodden on to him and to his children, because he has wholly followed the LORD.”

Arminian

Caleb the son of Jephunneh is excepted because he “has wholly followed the LORD.” He shall see the land, and it will be given to him and to his children. Arminian readers see faithful obedience honored by God, a real exception within the sworn sentence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Caleb’s wholehearted following as the fruit of grace and honored by God. Both traditions treat him as an example.

Deuteronomy 1:37

37Also the LORD was angry with me for your sakes, saying, “You also shall not go in there.

Arminian

God was angry with Moses “for your sakes,” and Moses too will not enter. Numbers 20:12 gives Moses’ own failure at Meribah as the reason. Interpreters reconcile the two by seeing the people’s provocation as the occasion of Moses’ sin, or by reading Deuteronomy’s wording as emphasis.

Reformed

Reformed readers hold the same range of harmonizations. Numbers 20:12 names Moses’ failure at Meribah, while this verse says the LORD was angry with him “for your sakes.” The people’s provocation and Moses’ own act are commonly read together rather than set against each other.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:38

38Joshua the son of Nun, who stands before you, shall go in there. Encourage him, for he shall cause Israel to inherit it.

Arminian

Joshua the son of Nun, who stands before Moses, shall go in and cause Israel to inherit the land. Moses is told to encourage him. Leadership passes to the next generation, and the present leader’s task includes strengthening the one who will finish the work.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Joshua is named as the one who shall cause Israel to inherit the land, and Moses is commanded to encourage him, so the transfer of leadership is itself part of God’s provision, not a mere human succession.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:39

39Moreover your little ones, whom you said would be captured or killed, your children, who today have no knowledge of good or evil, shall go in there. I will give it to them, and they shall possess it.

Arminian

The little ones, who “have no knowledge of good or evil,” will receive the land. Some Arminian interpreters cite this verse in discussing God’s treatment of young children, though the verse itself concerns entering Canaan.

Reformed

Some Reformed interpreters also cite it in that discussion, and views differ within the tradition. Both traditions agree the verse’s main point is God’s faithfulness to the next generation.

Deuteronomy 1:40

40But as for you, turn, and take your journey into the wilderness by the way to the Red Sea.”

Arminian

The rebels must turn and take their journey into the wilderness by the way to the Red Sea. The journey reverses: instead of going up to the land, they are sent back toward the wilderness they had already crossed.

Reformed

Reformed readers read this the same way. The command is specific—the way to the Red Sea—and it marks the end of the attempt to enter from Kadesh. The path forward is now the path of the sworn wandering.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:41

41Then you answered and said to me, “We have sinned against the LORD. We will go up and fight, according to all that the LORD our God commanded us.” Every man of you put on his weapons of war, and presumed to go up into the hill country.

Arminian

Israel confesses sin and arms for battle, presuming to go up. Confession without submission to God’s current word becomes presumption.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same distinction. Israel says, “We have sinned,” then puts on weapons and presumes to go up. Confession that will not accept God’s present word—don’t go up—turns into a new act of self-will.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:42

42The LORD said to me, “Tell them, ‘Don’t go up and don’t fight; for I am not among you, lest you be struck before your enemies.’ ”

Arminian

God warns, “Don’t go up and don’t fight; for I am not among you, lest you be struck before your enemies.” Without his presence, the battle they now want to fight will fail. Arminian readers hear a clear word that still can be refused, as the next verse shows they refused it.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The LORD tells Moses to warn them, “Don’t go up and don’t fight; for I am not among you, lest you be struck before your enemies.” Without his presence, the battle they now propose will fail.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:43

43So I spoke to you, and you didn’t listen; but you rebelled against the commandment of the LORD, and were presumptuous, and went up into the hill country.

Arminian

They do not listen, rebel again, and go up presumptuously. Unbelief and presumption are two forms of the same self-will.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way. Moses spoke, and they did not listen; they rebelled against the commandment, were presumptuous, and went up into the hill country. Unbelief and presumption are two forms of the same self-will.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:44

44The Amorites, who lived in that hill country, came out against you and chased you as bees do, and beat you down in Seir, even to Hormah.

Arminian

The Amorites who lived in that hill country came out, chased them as bees do, and beat them down in Seir even to Hormah. The defeat confirms the warning that the LORD was not among them.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The comparison “as bees do” pictures a sudden, overwhelming pursuit, and the flight as far as Hormah shows that the presumption ended exactly as God had said.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:45

45You returned and wept before the LORD, but the LORD didn’t listen to your voice, nor turn his ear to you.

Arminian

They weep before the LORD, but he does not listen. The verse concerns that particular venture; God refused to reverse the sentence he had pronounced.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the refusal as God holding to his judgment, not as a statement that repentance is never heard. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:46

46So you stayed in Kadesh many days, according to the days that you remained.

Arminian

They stayed in Kadesh “many days, according to the days that you remained.” The phrasing is unusual and does not give an exact length; Moses simply marks a long delay at the place where the refusal had happened.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way. The verse does not fix a number of days, and interpreters do not agree on how long the stay lasted; what is clear is that Israel remained at Kadesh through a prolonged period after the failed ascent.

This is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
