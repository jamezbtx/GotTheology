Passage

Deuteronomy 1

Verses 1–8 · WEB

Deuteronomy opens beyond the Jordan in Moab, in the fortieth year after the exodus, after Israel’s victories over Sihon and Og. Moses begins to explain the law by recalling how the LORD told Israel at Horeb (Sinai) to leave the mountain and take possession of the land promised to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

Deuteronomy 1:1

1These are the words which Moses spoke to all Israel beyond the Jordan in the wilderness, in the Arabah opposite Suf, between Paran, Tophel, Laban, Hazeroth, and Dizahab.

Arminian

The book presents itself as Moses’ words to all Israel beyond the Jordan. Several place names here are not securely identified today. The setting is the edge of the promised land, where a new generation must decide whether to trust God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the setting and the uncertainty about some locations. Both traditions read Deuteronomy as covenant renewal for the generation about to enter the land. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:2

2It is eleven days’ journey from Horeb by the way of Mount Seir to Kadesh Barnea.

Arminian

Horeb to Kadesh Barnea is only an eleven-day journey. The note quietly sets up the contrast with the forty years Israel actually spent, a delay caused by unbelief.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same observation. Sin turned a short journey into a long one. Eleven days from Horeb by the way of Mount Seir to Kadesh Barnea is set beside the forty years that actually followed, so the delay is not blamed on the distance.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:3

3In the fortieth year, in the eleventh month, on the first day of the month, Moses spoke to the children of Israel according to all that the LORD had given him in commandment to them,

Arminian

Moses speaks in the fortieth year, eleventh month, first day, and he speaks what the LORD commanded. His authority is derived from God’s word, not his own.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the same point: Deuteronomy is God’s instruction through Moses. The date in the fortieth year, the eleventh month, on the first day, and the claim that Moses spoke “according to all that the LORD had given him in commandment,” place the whole address under that word.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:4

4after he had struck Sihon the king of the Amorites who lived in Heshbon, and Og the king of Bashan who lived in Ashtaroth, at Edrei.

Arminian

The speech follows the defeat of Sihon and Og (Numbers 21:21–35). These recent victories serve as evidence that God can give Israel the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the victories as proof of God’s faithfulness, an encouragement for the conquest ahead. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:5

5Beyond the Jordan, in the land of Moab, Moses began to declare this law, saying,

Arminian

In Moab, Moses begins to “declare” or explain the law. Deuteronomy is not simply a repetition; it is exposition and application for a new generation.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The book models teaching that applies God’s word to God’s people in their situation. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:6

6“The LORD our God spoke to us in Horeb, saying, ‘You have lived long enough at this mountain.

Arminian

God told Israel they had stayed long enough at Horeb. The covenant given at the mountain was meant to send them forward, not keep them in place.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same movement from covenant to mission. “You have lived long enough at this mountain” is the LORD’s own word at Horeb: the law given there was meant to send Israel forward into the land, not to leave them camped at the mountain.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:7

7Turn, and take your journey, and go to the hill country of the Amorites and to all the places near there: in the Arabah, in the hill country, in the lowland, in the South, by the seashore, in the land of the Canaanites, and in Lebanon as far as the great river, the river Euphrates.

Arminian

The boundaries are broad: hill country, lowland, the South, the coast, Lebanon, and the Euphrates. The scope echoes the promise to Abraham in Genesis 15:18.

Reformed

Reformed readers draw the same link to Genesis 15. Both traditions note that Israel never fully occupied these boundaries for long, which interpreters handle in different ways. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:8

8Behold, I have set the land before you. Go in and possess the land which the LORD swore to your fathers—to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob—to give to them and to their offspring after them.’ ”

Arminian

“I have set the land before you. Go in and possess.” The gift is sworn to the patriarchs, yet Israel must act to receive it. God’s promise and Israel’s obedience are both real.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm both God’s sworn promise and Israel’s responsibility to obey. They stress that the oath to the fathers guarantees the outcome. Both traditions hold gift and response together.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
