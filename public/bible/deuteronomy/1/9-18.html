Passage

Deuteronomy 1

Verses 9–18 · WEB

Moses recalls how Israel’s growth made it impossible for him to lead and judge them alone. At his suggestion, the people chose wise and respected men from each tribe, whom he appointed as leaders and judges. He charged them to judge fairly, without partiality or fear, because judgment belongs to God.

Deuteronomy 1:9

9I spoke to you at that time, saying, “I am not able to bear you myself alone.

Arminian

Moses admits he cannot carry the people alone. Leadership in God’s community is meant to be shared. Exodus 18 and Numbers 11 describe related moments.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same humility and the same cross-references. Moses cannot bear the people alone; Exodus 18 and Numbers 11 describe related moments when shared leadership was provided for a growing nation.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:10

10The LORD your God has multiplied you, and behold, you are today as the stars of the sky for multitude.

Arminian

Israel has become as numerous as the stars, echoing God’s promise to Abraham (Genesis 15:5). The burden Moses feels is the result of God’s blessing.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the fulfillment of the Abrahamic promise. “You are today as the stars of the sky for multitude” echoes Genesis 15:5; the very size that burdens Moses is evidence that God has already kept his word.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:11

11May the LORD, the God of your fathers, make you a thousand times as many as you are and bless you, as he has promised you!

Arminian

Moses blesses the people and prays that God would multiply them a thousand times more. He does not resent their growth; he welcomes it.

Reformed

Reformed readers read this as a pastor’s blessing grounded in God’s promise. Moses asks the God of their fathers to make them a thousand times as many and to bless them “as he has promised you,” welcoming the growth he cannot carry alone.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:12

12How can I myself alone bear your problems, your burdens, and your strife?

Arminian

Moses names the problems, burdens, and strife that come with a large community. Conflict is a normal part of life together, and it needs structure.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that God provides order for the life of his people. Problems, burdens, and strife are named honestly; a large community needs more than one man if justice and care are to reach everyone.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:13

13Take wise men of understanding who are respected among your tribes, and I will make them heads over you.”

Arminian

The people are to choose wise, understanding, respected men, and Moses will appoint them. Character comes before position.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same priority on character, echoed in New Testament qualifications for leaders (1 Timothy 3; Titus 1). This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:14

14You answered me, and said, “The thing which you have spoken is good to do.”

Arminian

The people agree that the plan is good: “The thing which you have spoken is good to do.” The appointment of leaders is a shared act of the community, not a structure imposed without their consent.

Reformed

Reformed readers read this the same way. The people themselves judge the proposal good, so the later system of judges rests on a word they had accepted, not on a surprise arrangement.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:15

15So I took the heads of your tribes, wise and respected men, and made them heads over you, captains of thousands, captains of hundreds, captains of fifties, captains of tens, and officers, according to your tribes.

Arminian

Moses appoints the heads of the tribes, wise and respected men, as captains of thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens, and as officers according to their tribes. The layered structure allows each person to be heard and cared for, rather than leaving every case at Moses’ feet.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see orderly, layered leadership. The heads are taken from the tribes themselves and set in ranks, so authority is both local and accountable, and no tribe is left without its own officers.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:16

16I commanded your judges at that time, saying, “Hear cases between your brothers and judge righteously between a man and his brother, and the foreigner who is living with him.

Arminian

Judges must hear cases fairly between a man and his brother, and also the foreigner living with him. Justice belongs to everyone in the community, not only to native Israelites, and the judges are commanded to hear before they decide.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same concern for impartial justice, including for the vulnerable. The foreigner is named beside the brother, so righteousness in court is not allowed to stop at the family or the tribe.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:17

17You shall not show partiality in judgment; you shall hear the small and the great alike. You shall not be afraid of the face of man, for the judgment is God’s. The case that is too hard for you, you shall bring to me, and I will hear it.”

Arminian

No partiality, no fear of people, “for the judgment is God’s.” Judges act as God’s servants and answer to him. Hard cases go up to Moses.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the same ground: human judges represent God’s justice. James 2:1–9 applies the ban on partiality to the church. This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 1:18

18I commanded you at that time all the things which you should do.

Arminian

Moses commanded them at that time all the things they should do. The new generation is reminded that God’s instructions were given clearly; they are not being asked to invent a path, but to keep what had already been set out.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The verse closes the unit on judges by stressing that the whole duty was commanded “at that time,” so later failure cannot be excused as a lack of instruction.

This is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
