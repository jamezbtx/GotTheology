Passage

Deuteronomy 8

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Moses calls Israel to remember the forty years in which God humbled, tested, and fed them with manna, teaching that “man lives by every word that proceeds out of the LORD’s mouth,” words Jesus quotes in Matthew 4:4. God disciplined them as a father disciplines a son and now brings them into a rich land, where they must bless him when they eat and are full. Much of this chapter is common ground for Arminian and Reformed readers.

Deuteronomy 8:1

1You shall observe to do all the commandments which I command you today, that you may live, and multiply, and go in and possess the land which the LORD swore to your fathers.

Arminian

Israel must observe all the commandments so that they may live, multiply, and possess the land. Arminian readers see obedience as the path to enjoying covenant blessings in the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see obedience as the way of life within the covenant, given to a people already redeemed. The verse ties life in the land to keeping God’s word.

Deuteronomy 8:2

2You shall remember all the way which the LORD your God has led you these forty years in the wilderness, that he might humble you, to test you, to know what was in your heart, whether you would keep his commandments or not.

Arminian

Israel must remember how God led them forty years to humble and test them, to know what was in their hearts. Arminian readers see a real test that revealed Israel’s heart, classically held alongside God’s full foreknowledge.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see testing as God’s way of exposing and training his people’s hearts, consistent with his exhaustive knowledge. The purpose stated is to show whether Israel would keep his commandments.

Deuteronomy 8:3

3He humbled you, allowed you to be hungry, and fed you with manna, which you didn’t know, neither did your fathers know, that he might teach you that man does not live by bread only, but man lives by every word that proceeds out of the LORD’s mouth.

Arminian

God humbled Israel, let them hunger, and fed them with manna to teach that “man lives by every word that proceeds out of the LORD’s mouth.” Arminian readers see dependence on God’s word as the lesson of the wilderness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the sufficiency of God’s word. Jesus quotes this verse in Matthew 4:4 and Luke 4:4 when tempted in the wilderness, a connection readers in both traditions commonly highlight.

Deuteronomy 8:4

4Your clothing didn’t grow old on you, neither did your foot swell, these forty years.

Arminian

Israel’s clothing did not wear out, nor did their feet swell, for forty years. Arminian readers see God’s quiet, daily care sustaining his people through hardship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see providence attending to ordinary needs. The verse adds personal detail to the larger account of God’s provision in the wilderness. Small mercies are part of the testimony.

Deuteronomy 8:5

5You shall consider in your heart that as a man disciplines his son, so the LORD your God disciplines you.

Arminian

As a father disciplines his son, so the LORD disciplines Israel. Arminian readers see discipline as an expression of fatherly love aimed at maturity. The aim is growth, not rejection.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s discipline as proof of his care. Hebrews 12:5–11 develops the theme of God disciplining his children, though it quotes Proverbs 3 rather than this verse.

Deuteronomy 8:6

6You shall keep the commandments of the LORD your God, to walk in his ways, and to fear him.

Arminian

Israel must keep the commandments, walk in God’s ways, and fear him. Arminian readers see obedience, conduct, and reverence held together as a whole-life response to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see these three phrases describing covenant faithfulness. The verse draws a practical conclusion from the lessons of the wilderness. The wilderness lessons are meant to shape daily life in the land.

Deuteronomy 8:7

7For the LORD your God brings you into a good land, a land of brooks of water, of springs, and underground water flowing into valleys and hills;

Arminian

God is bringing Israel into a good land with brooks, springs, and underground water. Arminian readers see the generosity of God’s gift contrasting with the dry wilderness Israel has known.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the goodness of the land as a sign of God’s faithfulness to his promise. The emphasis on water recalls the thirst and complaints of the wilderness journey.

Deuteronomy 8:8

8a land of wheat, barley, vines, fig trees, and pomegranates; a land of olive trees and honey;

Arminian

The land has wheat, barley, vines, figs, pomegranates, olives, and honey. Arminian readers see abundant provision prepared by God for his people’s enjoyment. What was promised is now close at hand.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s bounty described in rich detail. The list echoes the produce the spies brought back in Numbers 13, reminding Israel of what earlier unbelief forfeited.

Deuteronomy 8:9

9a land in which you shall eat bread without scarcity, you shall not lack anything in it; a land whose stones are iron, and out of whose hills you may dig copper.

Arminian

Israel will eat without scarcity, lack nothing, and find iron and copper in the hills. Arminian readers see God supplying both daily bread and resources for work and building.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see comprehensive provision in the land. The verse sets up the danger in the following section, where abundance may lead to forgetting God.

Deuteronomy 8:10

10You shall eat and be full, and you shall bless the LORD your God for the good land which he has given you.

Arminian

Israel will eat and be full and must bless the LORD for the good land. Arminian readers see gratitude as the proper response to satisfaction, guarding against complacency.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see thanksgiving as the fitting acknowledgment that God is the giver. The command to “bless the LORD” after eating makes gratitude part of enjoying God’s gifts.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
