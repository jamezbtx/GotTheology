Passage

Deuteronomy 8

Verses 11–20 · WEB

Moses warns that abundance can lift the heart in pride, so that Israel forgets the God who rescued, led, and fed them and says, “My power and the might of my hand has gotten me this wealth.” Israel must remember that God gives the power to get wealth. If they turn to other gods, they will perish like the nations. Arminian and Reformed readers alike treat the warning as serious, while framing its relation to perseverance differently.

Deuteronomy 8:11

11Beware lest you forget the LORD your God, in not keeping his commandments, his ordinances, and his statutes, which I command you today;

Arminian

Israel must beware of forgetting the LORD by failing to keep his commandments. Arminian readers see a real danger that a blessed people could drift from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the warning seriously as a means of keeping God’s people faithful. The verse defines forgetting God concretely as neglecting his commands.

Deuteronomy 8:12

12lest, when you have eaten and are full, and have built fine houses and lived in them;

Arminian

The danger comes when Israel has eaten, is full, and lives in fine houses. Arminian readers see prosperity itself as a setting that tests the heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see comfort exposing the heart’s tendency toward self-reliance. The verse begins a description of blessings that may become occasions for pride. Ease can be as testing as hardship.

Deuteronomy 8:13

13and when your herds and your flocks multiply, and your silver and your gold is multiplied, and all that you have is multiplied;

Arminian

Herds, flocks, silver, gold, and all possessions multiply. Arminian readers see the very blessings God promised becoming potential snares if received without gratitude. Gifts can quietly replace the giver.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see that increase calls for humility and remembrance. The repeated word “multiplied” emphasizes how complete the prosperity will be. Abundance raises the stakes of remembering.

Deuteronomy 8:14

14then your heart might be lifted up, and you forget the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage;

Arminian

Israel’s heart might be lifted up, and they might forget the God who brought them out of Egypt. Arminian readers see pride as the root of forgetting, a danger to any blessed people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the heart’s pride as the core problem. The verse anchors Israel’s identity in the exodus as a remedy against forgetting. Memory of rescue checks pride.

Deuteronomy 8:15

15who led you through the great and terrible wilderness, with venomous snakes and scorpions, and thirsty ground where there was no water; who poured water for you out of the rock of flint;

Arminian

God led Israel through a terrible wilderness with snakes, scorpions, and thirst, and brought water from flint rock. Arminian readers see past dangers recalled to deepen dependence on God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s protection and provision in the wilderness. The reference to water from the rock recalls Exodus 17 and Numbers 20. The journey itself is evidence of God’s care.

Deuteronomy 8:16

16who fed you in the wilderness with manna, which your fathers didn’t know, that he might humble you, and that he might prove you, to do you good at your latter end;

Arminian

God fed Israel with manna to humble and prove them, to do them good in the end. Arminian readers see testing with a gracious goal, aimed at Israel’s ultimate good.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s purposes in hardship directed toward his people’s good. The phrase “at your latter end” shows that the testing looked toward blessing, not destruction.

Deuteronomy 8:17

17and lest you say in your heart, “My power and the might of my hand has gotten me this wealth.”

Arminian

Israel must not say, “My power and the might of my hand has gotten me this wealth.” Arminian readers see a warning against self-made pride that forgets God’s role.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the temptation to claim credit for what God has given. The quoted thought captures in Israel’s own imagined words the error the chapter warns against.

Deuteronomy 8:18

18But you shall remember the LORD your God, for it is he who gives you power to get wealth, that he may establish his covenant which he swore to your fathers, as it is today.

Arminian

Israel must remember that God gives power to get wealth to establish his covenant. Arminian readers see human effort enabled by God, so that success becomes an occasion for gratitude.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God as the source of every ability. The verse connects wealth to God’s covenant purposes rather than to personal glory. Ability itself is a gift.

Deuteronomy 8:19

19It shall be, if you shall forget the LORD your God, and walk after other gods, and serve them and worship them, I testify against you today that you shall surely perish.

Arminian

If Israel forgets the LORD and serves other gods, they will surely perish. Arminian readers see a real and solemn warning that covenant privilege can be lost through apostasy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the warning seriously, often reading it as addressed to the nation as a whole and as a means God uses to keep his people faithful. The verse names idolatry as the specific path to ruin.

Deuteronomy 8:20

20As the nations that the LORD makes to perish before you, so you shall perish, because you wouldn’t listen to the LORD your God’s voice.

Arminian

Like the nations God destroys, Israel will perish if it will not listen to his voice. Arminian readers see God’s impartial justice, which does not exempt Israel from judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see that Israel’s election does not excuse disobedience. The verse links back to chapter 7, applying the fate of the nations to Israel if it imitates them.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
