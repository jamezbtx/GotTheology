Passage

Deuteronomy 2

Verses 26–37 · WEB

Moses sends Sihon king of Heshbon an offer of peace, asking only to pass through on the highway and pay for food and water. Sihon refuses, because the LORD hardened his spirit to deliver him into Israel’s hand. Sihon attacks at Jahaz, and Israel defeats him, devotes his cities to destruction, takes the livestock, and occupies the land from the Arnon to Gilead, avoiding Ammon as God commanded.

Deuteronomy 2:26

26I sent messengers out of the wilderness of Kedemoth to Sihon king of Heshbon with words of peace, saying,

Arminian

Moses sends messengers from the wilderness of Kedemoth to Sihon with words of peace. Even with the land already promised, Israel first offers peaceful passage, rather than opening with an attack.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same offer. Both traditions see this as consistent with the law’s later instruction to offer peace before battle (Deuteronomy 20:10).

Deuteronomy 2:27

27“Let me pass through your land. I will go along by the highway. I will turn neither to the right hand nor to the left.

Arminian

Israel asks to pass through on the highway, turning neither to the right hand nor to the left. The request is modest: a straight passage, not a wandering through Sihon’s fields and towns.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way. The offer to keep to the highway, without turning aside, presents Sihon with a limited request and leaves him without the excuse that Israel had come to raid the land.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:28

28You shall sell me food for money, that I may eat; and give me water for money, that I may drink. Just let me pass through on my feet,

Arminian

Israel will pay money for food and water and only pass through on foot. The request mirrors the fair dealing already required toward Edom, and it asks for sale, not for tribute or plunder.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. “Sell me food for money… and give me water for money” and “just let me pass through on my feet” keep the approach to Sihon in the same honest terms used with Edom.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:29

29as the children of Esau who dwell in Seir, and the Moabites who dwell in Ar, did to me, until I pass over the Jordan into the land which the LORD our God gives us.”

Arminian

Moses points to Edom and Moab as examples, and to Israel’s goal across the Jordan. Numbers 20:14–21 says Edom refused passage; interpreters often reconcile the two by suggesting that, although Edom refused passage through its land, Edomites along the border still sold food and water to Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same tension and similar harmonizations. Moses cites Esau’s children and the Moabites as having done this until Israel should pass the Jordan. Numbers 20:14–21 says Edom refused passage through its land; many suggest that border Edomites still sold food and water, even if transit through the country was refused.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:30

30But Sihon king of Heshbon would not let us pass by him, for the LORD your God hardened his spirit and made his heart obstinate, that he might deliver him into your hand, as it is today.

Arminian

Sihon refuses because “the LORD your God hardened his spirit and made his heart obstinate.” Arminian readers typically read this as judicial hardening: God confirms and gives over a king already set against him, in line with the judgment on Amorite sin anticipated in Genesis 15:16. Sihon remains responsible for his refusal.

Reformed

Reformed readers typically read the verse as God’s sovereign purpose working through Sihon’s willing sin: God ordained the refusal to bring about Israel’s victory, while Sihon acted freely and is justly accountable. They see the same pattern in Pharaoh (Exodus 9:12; Romans 9:18).

Key contrast Both traditions affirm that God hardened Sihon, that Sihon was guilty, and that God used his refusal to give Israel the land. Classical Arminians typically read the hardening as God’s judicial response to Sihon’s own prior hostility. Reformed interpreters typically read it as God’s decree working through Sihon’s free choice, so that divine purpose and human responsibility are both fully real.

Deuteronomy 2:31

31The LORD said to me, “Behold, I have begun to deliver up Sihon and his land before you. Begin to possess, that you may inherit his land.”

Arminian

The LORD says he has begun to deliver up Sihon and his land, and Israel must begin to possess it, in order to inherit it. The gift is already under way, and the people are called to take it up, not to stand still and watch.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same pairing of gift and action. “I have begun to deliver up Sihon” is joined to “Begin to possess,” so the inheritance is both God’s delivery and Israel’s obedient taking of what he gives.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:32

32Then Sihon came out against us, he and all his people, to battle at Jahaz.

Arminian

Sihon comes out against Israel, he and all his people, to battle at Jahaz. He chooses war after peace was offered, meeting a request for passage with a full military advance.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Sihon’s attack is his own act. He comes out to Jahaz with all his people, after words of peace and an offer to buy food and water, so the battle is one he has opened.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:33

33The LORD our God delivered him up before us; and we struck him, his sons, and all his people.

Arminian

The LORD their God delivered Sihon up before them, and Israel struck him, his sons, and all his people. The victory is credited to God first, then to the blow Israel struck.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way. “The LORD our God delivered him up before us” is the ground of the victory; Israel’s striking of the king, his sons, and his people is told as the outworking of that delivery.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:34

34We took all his cities at that time, and utterly destroyed every inhabited city, with the women and the little ones. We left no one remaining.

Arminian

Israel devotes every inhabited city to destruction, including women and children. This practice, often called herem, is one of Scripture’s hardest moral questions. Many Arminian interpreters treat it as a unique, limited act of divine judgment on long-standing sin, not a pattern for other wars or for the church.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters likewise treat it as God’s judgment through Israel at a particular point in redemptive history, not a model for later believers. Both traditions acknowledge the difficulty and differ internally on how to explain it; neither treats it as permission for violence today.

Deuteronomy 2:35

35Only the livestock we took for plunder for ourselves, with the plunder of the cities which we had taken.

Arminian

Only the livestock and plunder are kept. The note shows that this herem allowed spoil, unlike some later cases (Joshua 6:17–19).

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way. Only the livestock and the plunder of the taken cities are kept. This herem allowed spoil, unlike some later cases such as Jericho (Joshua 6:17–19), a difference the text itself records rather than leaving it to later inference.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:36

36From Aroer, which is on the edge of the valley of the Arnon, and the city that is in the valley, even to Gilead, there was not a city too high for us. The LORD our God delivered up all before us.

Arminian

From Aroer to Gilead, no city was too strong, because “the LORD our God delivered up all before us.” The fear of fortified cities (1:28) is answered.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same contrast with the earlier fear. From Aroer on the edge of the Arnon valley, and the city in the valley, even to Gilead, “there was not a city too high for us,” because the LORD delivered up all before them—an answer to the report of cities fortified up to the sky (1:28).

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:37

37Only to the land of the children of Ammon you didn’t come near: all the banks of the river Jabbok, and the cities of the hill country, and wherever the LORD our God forbade us.

Arminian

Israel did not come near the land of the children of Ammon, the banks of the Jabbok, the cities of the hill country, or anywhere the LORD their God forbade them. Victory over Sihon did not become a license to take whatever they could.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that obedience includes respecting God’s boundaries. The campaign stops where the LORD has forbidden them to go, so the same God who delivered Sihon also limited the conquest.

This is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
