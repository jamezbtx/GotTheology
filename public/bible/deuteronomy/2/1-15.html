Passage

Deuteronomy 2

Verses 1–15 · WEB

After years around Mount Seir, the LORD tells Israel to turn north. They must pass Edom (Esau’s descendants) and Moab (Lot’s descendants) peacefully, because God has given those peoples their lands. Notes in parentheses describe earlier peoples those nations displaced. Israel crosses the brook Zered thirty-eight years after Kadesh, once the generation of fighting men has died, as the LORD swore.

Deuteronomy 2:1

1Then we turned, and took our journey into the wilderness by the way to the Red Sea, as the LORD spoke to me; and we encircled Mount Seir many days.

Arminian

Israel turns into the wilderness as God commanded and circles Mount Seir for many days. Even the years of judgment are lived under God’s direction.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same point: God governs the wandering as well as the conquest. Israel turns into the wilderness by the way to the Red Sea, as the LORD had spoken, and encircles Mount Seir many days, so even the years of judgment are lived under his direction.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:2

2The LORD spoke to me, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks again to Moses. After the long circling of Mount Seir, a new word signals a new stage of the journey; Israel does not move on by restlessness, but by a command.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way. The next instructions about Edom, Moab, and Ammon will come as the LORD’s speech, so the change of direction is his, not Moses’ improvisation.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:3

3“You have encircled this mountain long enough. Turn northward.

Arminian

“You have encircled this mountain long enough.” The phrasing echoes 1:6. God moves his people forward when his time comes.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same echo of 1:6. “You have encircled this mountain long enough. Turn northward.” When God’s time comes, he moves his people forward with the same kind of word that had once sent them on from Horeb.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:4

4Command the people, saying, ‘You are to pass through the border of your brothers, the children of Esau, who dwell in Seir; and they will be afraid of you. Therefore be careful.

Arminian

Israel must pass Edom, “your brothers,” the descendants of Esau, who will be afraid. Israel is to be careful not to exploit that fear.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Kinship with Esau shapes how Israel must act. They will pass the border of “your brothers, the children of Esau,” who will be afraid, and they are told to be careful, not to turn that fear into an advantage.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:5

5Don’t contend with them; for I will not give you any of their land, no, not so much as for the sole of the foot to tread on, because I have given Mount Seir to Esau for a possession.

Arminian

God forbids conflict with Edom because he has given Mount Seir to Esau. God assigns lands to nations other than Israel too (compare Acts 17:26).

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize God’s sovereign rule over all nations’ boundaries. Both traditions agree that Israel’s inheritance is a gift with limits set by God.

Deuteronomy 2:6

6You shall purchase food from them for money, that you may eat. You shall also buy water from them for money, that you may drink.’ ”

Arminian

Israel must purchase food and buy water from Edom for money. Fair dealing is required even with a people who fear them; hunger on the road does not license taking supplies by force.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same ethic. Food and water are to be paid for, so the passage through a brother’s land is conducted as honest trade, not as a raid under the cover of God’s promise.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:7

7For the LORD your God has blessed you in all the work of your hands. He has known your walking through this great wilderness. These forty years, the LORD your God has been with you. You have lacked nothing.

Arminian

God has blessed their work, known their walk through the wilderness, and been with them forty years; they lacked nothing. Even the years of judgment were full of provision.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to his people even under discipline. He has blessed the work of their hands, known their walking through the great wilderness, and been with them forty years; they have lacked nothing, even in the years of judgment.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:8

8So we passed by from our brothers, the children of Esau, who dwell in Seir, from the way of the Arabah from Elath and from Ezion Geber. We turned and passed by the way of the wilderness of Moab.

Arminian

Israel passes by Edom by the Arabah road from Elath and Ezion Geber, then toward Moab. They obey the command not to fight.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way. Israel passes by the children of Esau from the Arabah road of Elath and Ezion Geber, then turns along the wilderness of Moab, obeying the command not to fight and taking the route God had opened.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:9

9The LORD said to me, “Don’t bother Moab, neither contend with them in battle; for I will not give you any of his land for a possession, because I have given Ar to the children of Lot for a possession.”

Arminian

God forbids war with Moab because he has given Ar to the descendants of Lot. God remembers his relationship with Abraham’s nephew.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same sovereign assignment of land. Israel must not bother Moab or contend in battle, because God has given Ar to the children of Lot for a possession; Israel’s inheritance has limits set by that earlier gift.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:10

10(The Emim lived there before, a great and numerous people, and tall as the Anakim.

Arminian

A parenthetical note says the Emim lived there before, a great people as tall as the Anakim. Some interpreters think such notes were added by a later editor; others attribute them to Moses. The point is clear either way.

Reformed

Reformed readers hold a similar range of views on the authorship of these parenthetical notes, whether Mosaic or a later editorial hand. Either way, the Emim are described as a great and numerous people, tall as the Anakim, so Moab’s later possession of the land is not explained by the weakness of those who first lived there.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:11

11These also are considered to be Rephaim, as the Anakim; but the Moabites call them Emim.

Arminian

The Emim were counted among the Rephaim, like the Anakim. If Moab could displace such peoples with God’s help, Israel had no reason to fear the Anakim.

Reformed

Reformed readers draw the same lesson. The Emim were considered Rephaim, as the Anakim were, though the Moabites called them Emim. If Moab could displace such a people with God’s help, Israel had no reason to treat the Anakim as an exception to God’s power.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:12

12The Horites also lived in Seir in the past, but the children of Esau succeeded them. They destroyed them from before them, and lived in their place, as Israel did to the land of his possession, which the LORD gave to them.)

Arminian

The Horites lived in Seir, but Esau’s descendants displaced them, as Israel would in its land. God works in the histories of other nations.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize God’s providence over all peoples. The Horites lived in Seir, but the children of Esau destroyed them and lived in their place, “as Israel did to the land of his possession, which the LORD gave to them.” God works in more histories than Israel’s alone.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:13

13“Now rise up and cross over the brook Zered.” We went over the brook Zered.

Arminian

God commands Israel to rise up and cross the brook Zered, and they do so. The crossing marks a turning point: the long period of wandering toward this border is now brought to an end by obedience to a clear word.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way. The command and the compliance stand in one verse—“Now rise up and cross over the brook Zered.” We went over—so the new stage begins with a word kept, not with a plan of their own.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:14

14The days in which we came from Kadesh Barnea until we had come over the brook Zered were thirty-eight years, until all the generation of the men of war were consumed from the middle of the camp, as the LORD swore to them.

Arminian

Thirty-eight years passed from Kadesh to the Zered, until the whole generation of fighting men had died, as God swore. His word of judgment was fulfilled exactly.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the precise fulfillment of God’s oath (Numbers 14:28–35). From Kadesh Barnea to the brook Zered was thirty-eight years, until the whole generation of the men of war was consumed from the middle of the camp, as the LORD had sworn.

This is shared ground.

Deuteronomy 2:15

15Moreover the LORD’s hand was against them, to destroy them from the middle of the camp, until they were consumed.

Arminian

“The LORD’s hand was against them” until they were consumed. The judgment was God’s own act, not merely natural attrition.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. “The LORD’s hand was against them, to destroy them from the middle of the camp, until they were consumed.” The dying of that generation is described as God’s own act, not as mere natural attrition over time.

This is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
