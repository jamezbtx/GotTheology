Passage

Deuteronomy 23

Verses 15–25 · WEB

This section gathers varied laws: protection for an escaped slave, prohibition of prostitution and of offerings from its earnings, no interest on loans to fellow Israelites, the seriousness of vows freely made, and permission to eat from a neighbor’s field or vineyard without carrying produce away. Together they show covenant life shaped by compassion, integrity, and holiness. Arminian and Reformed readers largely share these readings, with Jesus’ teaching on truthful speech and the disciples plucking grain as natural connections.

Deuteronomy 23:15

15You shall not deliver to his master a servant who has escaped from his master to you.

Arminian

Israel must not return an escaped slave to his master. The context suggests a slave fleeing to Israel, possibly from a foreign master, though the text does not specify.

Arminian readers see compassion for the vulnerable overriding a master’s claims.

Reformed

The law protects someone seeking refuge. The runaway’s safety is placed above the master’s claim.

Reformed readers see God’s concern for the oppressed embedded in Israel’s law. Both traditions read this as shared ground about protecting those who flee mistreatment.

Deuteronomy 23:16

16He shall dwell with you, among you, in the place which he shall choose within one of your gates, where it pleases him best. You shall not oppress him.

Arminian

The escaped slave may live wherever he chooses in Israel, and must not be oppressed. He is given freedom of residence.

Arminian readers see real freedom and dignity granted to the refugee.

Reformed

He chooses his own town, ‘where it pleases him best.’ Oppression is forbidden.

Reformed readers see God’s justice protecting the powerless. Both traditions share this reading, noting that the law gives the person agency and protection rather than treating him as property to be returned.

Deuteronomy 23:17

17There shall be no prostitute of the daughters of Israel, neither shall there be a sodomite of the sons of Israel.

Arminian

No Israelite daughter or son may be a prostitute; the WEB renders the male term ‘sodomite.’ The Hebrew words are related to the word for ‘holy.’

Arminian readers note that some interpreters connect these terms with prostitution linked to pagan worship, while others question how far such practice existed.

Reformed

The law forbids sexual practices associated with the surrounding nations’ religion. The exact meaning of the terms is discussed by interpreters.

Reformed readers likewise avoid overclaiming. Both traditions read the verse carefully and respectfully, as a call to sexual holiness in worship and life.

Deuteronomy 23:18

18You shall not bring the hire of a prostitute, or the wages of a male prostitute, into the house of the LORD your God for any vow; for both of these are an abomination to the LORD your God.

Arminian

Earnings from prostitution may not be brought into the LORD’s house for a vow. The WEB footnotes ‘male prostitute’ as literally ‘dog.’

Arminian readers see that God will not accept worship funded by practices he forbids.

Reformed

Offerings must not come from sources God calls an abomination. Worship and conduct cannot be separated.

Reformed readers see God’s holiness governing what is offered to him. Both traditions read this plainly and respectfully as shared ground about integrity in giving, without speculating beyond the text.

Deuteronomy 23:19

19You shall not lend on interest to your brother: interest of money, interest of food, interest of anything that is lent on interest.

Arminian

Israel must not charge interest to a fellow Israelite on money, food, or anything lent. Loans to a brother are acts of help.

Arminian readers see generosity toward the needy as a covenant duty. Exodus 22:25 and Leviticus 25:35–37 make a similar point.

Reformed

The law protects the poor from being exploited by lending. Help is not to become profit.

Reformed readers see love of neighbor shaping economic life. Both traditions read this as shared ground, while Christians have long discussed how it applies to modern commercial lending.

Deuteronomy 23:20

20You may charge a foreigner interest; but you shall not charge your brother interest, that the LORD your God may bless you in all that you put your hand to, in the land where you go in to possess it.

Arminian

Interest may be charged to a foreigner, but not to a brother, that God may bless Israel. The distinction reflects the covenant community’s obligations.

Arminian readers note the suggestion that commercial dealings with outsiders are in view, since other laws protect the poor foreigner living in Israel.

Reformed

The rule distinguishes covenant kin from outsiders in commerce. Some see commercial lending rather than relief of poverty in view.

Reformed readers likewise read it in context. Both traditions share this reading, noting that the blessing is tied to generosity within God’s people.

Deuteronomy 23:21

21When you vow a vow to the LORD your God, you shall not be slack to pay it, for the LORD your God will surely require it of you; and it would be sin in you.

Arminian

When you make a vow to the LORD, do not delay paying it, for he will require it, and failing would be sin. Vows are serious.

Arminian readers see personal responsibility for promises freely made. Ecclesiastes 5:4–5 gives similar counsel.

Reformed

God holds people to what they promise him. Delay itself is a fault.

Reformed readers see the seriousness of speech before God. Both traditions share this reading and connect it with Jesus’ teaching on simple, truthful speech (Matthew 5:33–37).

Deuteronomy 23:22

22But if you refrain from making a vow, it shall be no sin in you.

Arminian

If you refrain from vowing, it is no sin. Vows are voluntary.

Arminian readers see freedom in the law. God does not demand vows; he asks that vows made be kept.

Reformed

Not vowing is permitted. The danger lies in rash promises.

Reformed readers see wisdom in caution with words before God. Both traditions read this as clear shared ground: devotion is not measured by the number of vows, but by faithfulness to the promises one makes.

Deuteronomy 23:23

23You shall observe and do that which has gone out of your lips. Whatever you have vowed to the LORD your God as a free will offering, which you have promised with your mouth, you must do.

Arminian

Keep what has gone out of your lips; a freewill vow must be fulfilled. Spoken promises bind.

Arminian readers see integrity as faithfulness to one’s own word. The vow was chosen freely, so it must be kept.

Reformed

What is promised voluntarily becomes an obligation. Words carry weight.

Reformed readers see truthful speech as part of honoring God. Both traditions share this reading, summarizing the vow law: freedom in making vows, faithfulness in keeping them.

Deuteronomy 23:24

24When you come into your neighbor’s vineyard, then you may eat your fill of grapes at your own pleasure; but you shall not put any in your container.

Arminian

In a neighbor’s vineyard you may eat your fill of grapes, but not carry any away in a container. Hospitality and property are balanced.

Arminian readers see generosity toward the hungry traveler built into the law.

Reformed

The law allows eating on the spot but forbids taking a harvest. Both the hungry and the owner are protected.

Reformed readers see wise balance between mercy and property rights. Both traditions read this as shared ground about neighborly care with limits.

Deuteronomy 23:25

25When you come into your neighbor’s standing grain, then you may pluck the ears with your hand; but you shall not use a sickle on your neighbor’s standing grain.

Arminian

In a neighbor’s grain field you may pluck ears by hand, but not use a sickle. The same principle applies.

Arminian readers note that Jesus’ disciples plucked grain this way (Matthew 12:1; Mark 2:23); the dispute there concerned the Sabbath, not the act itself.

Reformed

Hand-plucking is allowed; harvesting is not. The law distinguishes need from taking.

Reformed readers likewise note the Gospel scene and its Sabbath focus. Both traditions share this reading of generous but bounded hospitality.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
