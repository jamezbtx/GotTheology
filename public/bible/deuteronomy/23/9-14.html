Passage

Deuteronomy 23

Verses 9–14 · WEB

These verses apply purity rules to Israel’s war camp. A man who becomes ritually unclean through a nighttime emission leaves the camp until evening, and soldiers must relieve themselves outside the camp and cover their waste. The reason is theological: the LORD walks in the middle of the camp, so it must be holy. The uncleanness in view is ritual rather than moral, and both traditions read the passage as shared ground about God’s presence touching ordinary life.

Deuteronomy 23:9

9When you go out and camp against your enemies, then you shall keep yourselves from every evil thing.

Arminian

When Israel goes out to war, the camp must keep itself from every evil thing. Military life is still lived before God.

Arminian readers see holiness required even in the stress and danger of battle.

Reformed

The camp is not exempt from God’s standards. The section that follows explains why.

Reformed readers see God’s presence as the reason for purity in every setting. Both traditions read this as shared ground and note that verse 14 gives the motivation: the LORD walks in the camp.

Deuteronomy 23:10

10If there is among you any man who is not clean by reason of that which happens to him by night, then shall he go outside of the camp. He shall not come within the camp;

Arminian

A man who becomes unclean through a nighttime emission must go outside the camp. The rule echoes Leviticus 15:16.

Arminian readers note that this is ritual uncleanness, not moral sin. Normal bodily events are handled matter-of-factly.

Reformed

The man leaves the camp temporarily. The purity rules of Leviticus are applied to the war camp.

Reformed readers agree that the uncleanness is ritual rather than a moral fault. Both traditions read this as shared ground about God’s holiness in the midst of ordinary life.

Deuteronomy 23:11

11but it shall be, when evening comes, he shall bathe himself in water. When the sun is down, he shall come within the camp.

Arminian

In the evening he bathes, and after sunset he returns to the camp. The exclusion is brief.

Arminian readers see a simple, temporary remedy. The man is restored the same day.

Reformed

Washing and waiting restore him. The pattern matches Leviticus 15.

Reformed readers see God’s provision for restoration built into the law. Both traditions agree that the remedy is ritual, brief, and not a matter of guilt, and read the verse as shared ground.

Deuteronomy 23:12

12You shall have a place also outside of the camp where you go relieve yourself.

Arminian

There must be a place outside the camp for relieving oneself. Sanitation is part of the law.

Arminian readers see God’s concern reaching into the most practical matters of daily life.

Reformed

The law provides for basic hygiene. Holiness is not detached from ordinary bodily needs.

Reformed readers see God’s care for his people’s health and order. Both traditions read this as shared ground: nothing about life in God’s camp is too ordinary for his instruction.

Deuteronomy 23:13

13You shall have a trowel among your weapons. It shall be, when you relieve yourself, you shall dig with it, and shall turn back and cover your excrement;

Arminian

Each soldier must carry a trowel to dig and cover excrement. The instruction is plain and practical.

Arminian readers see that respect for God’s presence includes simple, responsible habits. Faithfulness shows up in small things.

Reformed

The detail is concrete. Weapons and a trowel are carried together.

Reformed readers see holiness applied to practical life. Both traditions read this plainly as shared ground and note that the text grounds even this practical rule in God’s presence, not in hygiene alone.

Deuteronomy 23:14

14for the LORD your God walks in the middle of your camp, to deliver you, and to give up your enemies before you. Therefore your camp shall be holy, that he may not see an unclean thing in you, and turn away from you.

Arminian

The LORD walks in the middle of the camp to deliver Israel, so the camp must be holy, lest he see something unclean and turn away. God’s presence is the reason.

Arminian readers see a relationship in which the people’s conduct matters to God’s nearness.

Reformed

God’s presence brings both deliverance and the demand for holiness. The camp is his dwelling.

Reformed readers see God’s holiness at the center of Israel’s life. Both traditions share this reading, and Christians see the church as God’s dwelling by his Spirit (1 Corinthians 3:16).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
