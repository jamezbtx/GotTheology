Passage

Deuteronomy 24

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Deuteronomy 24 opens with a case about divorce and remarriage (vv. 1–4), which regulates an existing practice by forbidding a man to remarry a wife he divorced after she has married another. What ‘some unseemly thing’ meant was disputed, and Jesus addressed the question, saying Moses allowed divorce because of hardness of heart while pointing back to creation (Matthew 19:3–9; Mark 10:2–12). The section continues with protection for newlyweds, limits on pledges, a ban on kidnapping, and a reminder about skin disease and Miriam. Both traditions treat it as largely shared ground.

Deuteronomy 24:1

1When a man takes a wife and marries her, then it shall be, if she finds no favor in his eyes because he has found some unseemly thing in her, that he shall write her a certificate of divorce, put it in her hand, and send her out of his house.

Arminian

If a husband finds ‘some unseemly thing’ in his wife, he writes a certificate of divorce and sends her away. What the phrase means was debated; Matthew 19:3 shows the question of divorce ‘for any reason’ being put to Jesus.

Arminian readers note that the certificate can be read as protecting the woman by documenting her release.

Reformed

Verses 1–3 can be read as setting out a case, with the main prohibition in verse 4, though the WEB phrasing presents the certificate as required.

Reformed readers note Jesus’ answer that Moses allowed divorce because of hardness of heart (Matthew 19:7–8). Both traditions treat this as shared ground.

Deuteronomy 24:2

2When she has departed out of his house, she may go and be another man’s wife.

Arminian

After leaving, she may become another man’s wife. The certificate made remarriage possible.

Arminian readers see the law recognizing the woman’s need for security and a future in a setting where a woman without a household could be left vulnerable.

Reformed

The law assumes remarriage after divorce. It regulates a practice already present.

Reformed readers see Moses limiting the harm of a fallen practice rather than endorsing it as God’s ideal. Both traditions read this with Jesus’ appeal to creation in view (Matthew 19:4–6; Mark 10:6–9), as shared ground.

Deuteronomy 24:3

3If the latter husband hates her, and writes her a certificate of divorce, puts it in her hand, and sends her out of his house; or if the latter husband dies, who took her to be his wife;

Arminian

If the second husband hates her and divorces her, or dies, the case continues. The law follows realistic circumstances.

Arminian readers see the law dealing honestly with broken situations rather than ignoring them.

Reformed

The verse extends the hypothetical case. Either divorce or death ends the second marriage.

Reformed readers see God’s law addressing real human failures. Both traditions read this plainly as part of a carefully framed case leading to the prohibition in verse 4.

Deuteronomy 24:4

4her former husband, who sent her away, may not take her again to be his wife after she is defiled; for that would be an abomination to the LORD. You shall not cause the land to sin, which the LORD your God gives you for an inheritance.

Arminian

The first husband may not remarry her after she has been ‘defiled,’ for that would be an abomination and would cause the land to sin. Why the remarriage is forbidden is not fully explained.

Arminian readers note suggestions that it discourages hasty divorce and keeps the woman from being passed back and forth like property.

Reformed

Interpreters offer several explanations for the word ‘defiled’ and the rule. The text gives the verdict without detailed reasons.

Reformed readers likewise hold explanations lightly. Both traditions agree that the law restrains abuse of divorce, and that views on divorce and remarriage today vary within each tradition.

Deuteronomy 24:5

5When a man takes a new wife, he shall not go out in the army, neither shall he be assigned any business. He shall be free at home one year, and shall cheer his wife whom he has taken.

Arminian

A newly married man is exempt from military service and other duties for one year, so he can be at home and bring joy to his wife. Marriage is honored.

Arminian readers see God valuing the marriage bond and the wife’s happiness.

Reformed

The law gives a full year for a new marriage to be established. The husband’s duty is to his wife.

Reformed readers see marriage as a covenant worth protecting by public policy. Both traditions read this as clear shared ground, and as a gentle contrast to the divorce case before it.

Deuteronomy 24:6

6No man shall take the mill or the upper millstone as a pledge, for he takes a life in pledge.

Arminian

No one may take a mill or upper millstone as a pledge, for that would take a life in pledge. A family needed its millstone to make daily bread.

Arminian readers see protection of the poor from losing what they need to live.

Reformed

Taking the millstone would cut off a household’s food. The law forbids such security.

Reformed readers see love of neighbor limiting creditors’ rights. Both traditions read this as shared ground about economic mercy, a theme that runs through the chapter’s laws on loans and pledges.

Deuteronomy 24:7

7If a man is found stealing any of his brothers of the children of Israel, and he deals with him as a slave, or sells him, then that thief shall die. So you shall remove the evil from among you.

Arminian

Anyone who kidnaps a fellow Israelite and enslaves or sells him must die. The evil must be removed.

Arminian readers see the gravity of treating a person as property. Exodus 21:16 states a similar law.

Reformed

Kidnapping for slavery is a capital crime. Human freedom is protected.

Reformed readers see the dignity of people made in God’s image. Both traditions read this as shared ground, and 1 Timothy 1:10 lists enslavers among those who act against sound teaching.

Deuteronomy 24:8

8Be careful in the plague of leprosy, that you observe diligently and do according to all that the Levitical priests teach you. As I commanded them, so you shall observe to do.

Arminian

In cases of skin disease, Israel must carefully follow what the Levitical priests teach. The laws of Leviticus 13–14 are in view.

Arminian readers see obedience to God-given instruction for the community’s health and worship.

Reformed

The priests are the appointed teachers in such cases. Their instruction carries God’s authority.

Reformed readers see the value of ordered teaching in God’s community. Both traditions read this as shared ground, and note again that the uncleanness of skin disease is ritual rather than moral.

Deuteronomy 24:9

9Remember what the LORD your God did to Miriam, by the way as you came out of Egypt.

Arminian

Israel must remember what God did to Miriam on the way from Egypt. Numbers 12 tells how she was struck with skin disease after speaking against Moses.

Arminian readers see memory used to encourage careful respect for God’s instructions and appointed leaders.

Reformed

The reminder points to a specific judgment in Miriam’s case. It does not make all skin disease a punishment.

Reformed readers see history teaching reverence. Both traditions share this reading and remember that Miriam was restored after Moses prayed for her.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
