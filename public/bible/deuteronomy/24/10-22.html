Passage

Deuteronomy 24

Verses 10–22 · WEB

The second half of the chapter turns to care for the poor and vulnerable. Lenders must respect borrowers’ homes and return a poor man’s cloak by nightfall; hired workers must be paid the same day; punishment for crimes is individual; justice is owed to the foreigner, fatherless, and widow; and harvests must leave gleanings for those without land. Twice Israel is told to remember they were slaves in Egypt. Both traditions read these laws as shared ground: God’s redemption grounds his people’s compassion.

Deuteronomy 24:10

10When you lend your neighbor any kind of loan, you shall not go into his house to get his pledge.

Arminian

A lender must not enter a neighbor’s house to take a pledge. The borrower’s home is protected.

Arminian readers see dignity preserved for those who need to borrow. Poverty does not cancel privacy.

Reformed

The lender may not seize what he chooses. The borrower keeps control in his own home.

Reformed readers see love of neighbor shaping how debts are handled. Both traditions read this as clear shared ground about treating the poor with respect even in financial dealings.

Deuteronomy 24:11

11You shall stand outside, and the man to whom you lend shall bring the pledge outside to you.

Arminian

The lender stands outside, and the borrower brings out the pledge. The borrower chooses what to give.

Arminian readers see the law guarding the borrower’s honor and freedom. Need does not strip a person of dignity.

Reformed

The arrangement protects the weaker party. The lender waits outside.

Reformed readers see God’s law restraining the power of creditors. Both traditions share this reading, noting how carefully the law protects the dignity of those in need.

Deuteronomy 24:12

12If he is a poor man, you shall not sleep with his pledge.

Arminian

If the borrower is poor, the lender must not keep his pledge overnight. The poor may have only a cloak to give.

Arminian readers see compassion for the poor shaping even the timing of loans.

Reformed

A poor person’s pledge is likely his outer garment. Keeping it overnight would leave him cold.

Reformed readers see God’s care for basic human needs. Both traditions read this as shared ground, with Exodus 22:26–27 giving the same instruction about a neighbor’s garment.

Deuteronomy 24:13

13You shall surely restore to him the pledge when the sun goes down, that he may sleep in his garment and bless you. It shall be righteousness to you before the LORD your God.

Arminian

The pledge must be returned at sunset so the poor man can sleep in his garment and bless the lender. This is counted as righteousness before God.

Arminian readers see mercy toward the poor as genuine righteousness that God notices.

Reformed

Returning the cloak is called “righteousness to you before the LORD.” Kindness to the poor matters to God.

Reformed readers see such righteousness as the fruit of covenant life, not as earning salvation. Both traditions share this reading of mercy as part of faithfulness to God.

Deuteronomy 24:14

14You shall not oppress a hired servant who is poor and needy, whether he is one of your brothers or one of the foreigners who are in your land within your gates.

Arminian

Israel must not oppress a poor and needy hired worker, whether Israelite or foreigner. Workers are protected regardless of origin.

Arminian readers see God’s justice extending to outsiders as well as citizens.

Reformed

The law protects the worker’s vulnerability. Both native and foreigner are named.

Reformed readers see God’s impartial concern for the poor. Both traditions read this as clear shared ground, and Leviticus 19:13 similarly forbids withholding wages from a hired worker.

Deuteronomy 24:15

15In his day you shall give him his wages, neither shall the sun go down on it, for he is poor and sets his heart on it, lest he cry against you to the LORD, and it be sin to you.

Arminian

Wages must be paid the same day, before sunset, because the poor worker depends on them; otherwise he may cry to the LORD against you. God hears the poor.

Arminian readers see God’s attentiveness to the cries of the exploited.

Reformed

Delayed wages would harm someone living day to day. The law requires prompt payment.

Reformed readers see God as defender of the poor. Both traditions share this reading, and James 5:4 echoes the image of withheld wages crying out to the Lord.

Deuteronomy 24:16

16The fathers shall not be put to death for the children, neither shall the children be put to death for the fathers. Every man shall be put to death for his own sin.

Arminian

Parents must not be put to death for children, nor children for parents; each dies for his own sin. Legal punishment is individual.

Arminian readers see personal responsibility affirmed in Israel’s courts. 2 Kings 14:6 shows a king applying this law.

Reformed

The verse governs human judicial penalties. Ezekiel 18:20 develops the principle of individual responsibility.

Reformed readers likewise read it as a rule for human courts. Both traditions agree it limits judicial punishment and is shared ground, while their wider teaching on sin and its effects is drawn from other texts.

Deuteronomy 24:17

17You shall not deprive the foreigner or the fatherless of justice, nor take a widow’s clothing in pledge;

Arminian

Israel must not deny justice to the foreigner or the fatherless, nor take a widow’s clothing as a pledge. The vulnerable are named.

Arminian readers see God’s special concern for those without protection.

Reformed

Foreigner, fatherless, and widow form a recurring trio in Deuteronomy. They lacked family or land to protect them.

Reformed readers see God as the defender of the defenseless. Both traditions read this as clear shared ground about justice for the vulnerable.

Deuteronomy 24:18

18but you shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt, and the LORD your God redeemed you there. Therefore I command you to do this thing.

Arminian

Israel must remember that they were slaves in Egypt and that the LORD redeemed them. That memory grounds the command.

Arminian readers see gratitude for redemption motivating compassion. Those who were rescued should rescue others.

Reformed

Ethics flows from redemption. Israel’s past shapes its present duty.

Reformed readers see grace preceding obedience: God redeemed first, then commanded. Both traditions share this reading, and Christians see the same pattern in the gospel, where mercy received leads to mercy shown.

Deuteronomy 24:19

19When you reap your harvest in your field, and have forgotten a sheaf in the field, you shall not go again to get it. It shall be for the foreigner, for the fatherless, and for the widow, that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hands.

Arminian

A forgotten sheaf must be left for the foreigner, fatherless, and widow, so that God may bless the work of Israel’s hands. Gleaning provides for the poor.

Arminian readers see generosity built into the harvest. Ruth 2 shows this provision at work.

Reformed

The landowner must not go back for what was left. The poor have a share in the harvest.

Reformed readers see God’s blessing linked with generosity. Both traditions read this as clear shared ground, and Leviticus 19:9–10 gives similar gleaning laws.

Deuteronomy 24:20

20When you beat your olive tree, you shall not go over the boughs again. It shall be for the foreigner, for the fatherless, and for the widow.

Arminian

When beating olive trees, Israel must not go over the boughs again; the rest is for the vulnerable. The same principle applies to olives.

Arminian readers see the law’s steady concern for the poor in every kind of harvest.

Reformed

Second gleaning is forbidden. What remains belongs to those in need.

Reformed readers see stewardship that leaves room for others. Both traditions share this reading of the harvest as a gift from God that is to be shared with those who have no land.

Deuteronomy 24:21

21When you harvest your vineyard, you shall not glean it after yourselves. It shall be for the foreigner, for the fatherless, and for the widow.

Arminian

When harvesting the vineyard, Israel must not glean it afterward; the remainder is for the foreigner, fatherless, and widow. The pattern is complete.

Arminian readers see that grain, olives, and grapes are all shared.

Reformed

The threefold repetition covers the main crops of the land. Provision for the poor is comprehensive.

Reformed readers see God’s generosity reflected in his people’s practice. Both traditions read this as clear shared ground, completing the chapter’s concern for those without land or protection.

Deuteronomy 24:22

22You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt. Therefore I command you to do this thing.

Arminian

Israel must remember they were slaves in Egypt; therefore God commands this. The chapter closes with the motivation repeated.

Arminian readers see memory of grace as the root of a generous life.

Reformed

The repetition of verse 18 frames the gleaning laws. Redemption grounds compassion.

Reformed readers see the gospel pattern of grace leading to grateful obedience. Both traditions share this reading, seeing in it a lasting call for God’s people to care for the poor, the stranger, and the fatherless.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
