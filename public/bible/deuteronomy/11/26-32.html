Passage

Deuteronomy 11

Verses 26–32 · WEB

Moses now sets the alternatives in a single pair: “Behold, I set before you today a blessing and a curse:” The blessing is for those who listen to the commandments commanded today; the curse is for those who do not listen, but turn away to go after other gods they have not known. When the LORD brings them into the land, they are to set the blessing on Mount Gerizim and the curse on Mount Ebal, mountains beyond the Jordan to the west, in the Arabah near Gilgal, beside the oaks of Moreh. They are about to pass over the Jordan, possess the land the LORD gives, and dwell in it, and they must observe all the statutes set before them today. Both traditions agree that this choice is real and that it is addressed to a people already redeemed and on the edge of a given land. How that human response is related to God’s saving purpose is discussed at Deuteronomy 30:19; both traditions agree that the offer itself is not a pretense.

Deuteronomy 11:26

26Behold, I set before you today a blessing and a curse:

Arminian

Moses says, “Behold, I set before you today a blessing and a curse:”

Arminian readers take the setting forth as a genuine alternative placed before the people this day. Blessing and curse are both really held out, and the nation’s response, enabled by grace, may go either way.

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear a real blessing and a real curse set before the covenant nation. They typically read the alternative as the condition of the Mosaic administration for life in the land, and they differ among themselves on how that covenant relates to the covenant of grace.

Both traditions insist that the people addressed are already the LORD’s redeemed, standing on the verge of a land he gives, and that the choice is not a way to earn the exodus already accomplished. The same difference of emphasis is discussed at Deuteronomy 30:19.

Deuteronomy 11:27

27the blessing, if you listen to the commandments of the LORD your God, which I command you today;

Arminian

The blessing is “if you listen to the commandments of the LORD your God, which I command you today;”

Arminian readers hear listening as the path into the blessing just set before them. The commandments are today’s word, to be heard and done.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the “if” as a real condition of blessing in the land. Listening is the covenant way of the people to whom the commandments are given.

Both traditions read this verse as the positive half of verse 26, and they do not treat it as a separate contrast from the one discussed at Deuteronomy 30:19.

Deuteronomy 11:28

28and the curse, if you do not listen to the commandments of the LORD your God, but turn away out of the way which I command you today, to go after other gods which you have not known.

Arminian

The curse is if they do not listen, but turn away out of the way commanded today, “to go after other gods which you have not known.”

Arminian readers hear a defined turning: not listening becomes a departure after unknown gods. The curse is not a mystery; it answers a real forsaking of the commanded way.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same content of the curse. Other gods, unknown to this people, are the rival to which a turned-away heart would go, as verse 16 already warned.

Both traditions take the curse as the genuine other side of the blessing, still under the framing of verse 26.

Deuteronomy 11:29

29It shall happen, when the LORD your God brings you into the land that you go to possess, that you shall set the blessing on Mount Gerizim, and the curse on Mount Ebal.

Arminian

When the LORD brings them into the land they go to possess, they shall “set the blessing on Mount Gerizim, and the curse on Mount Ebal.”

Arminian readers see the choice of verses 26–28 about to be planted in the land itself, on two named mountains. Entering the gift includes a public setting of blessing and curse.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the LORD’s bringing them in is assumed: “when the LORD your God brings you into the land” is a when, not an if. The ceremony is commanded for that arrival, and Deuteronomy 27 will spell out the standing on these mountains.

Both traditions take Gerizim and Ebal as the appointed sites of a coming enactment, later also narrated in Joshua 8, without a contrast over the geography.

Deuteronomy 11:30

30Aren’t they beyond the Jordan, behind the way of the going down of the sun, in the land of the Canaanites who dwell in the Arabah near Gilgal, beside the oaks of Moreh?

Arminian

The mountains are identified: they are beyond the Jordan, toward the going down of the sun, in the land of the Canaanites who dwell in the Arabah near Gilgal, beside the oaks of Moreh.

Arminian readers hear a locatable place in the land ahead, near Gilgal and the oaks of Moreh, so that the ceremony will not be an abstraction.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same western, trans-Jordan setting. Genesis 12:6 already places Abram at the oak of Moreh in the region of Shechem; this verse speaks of oaks, in the plural, and the two lines are not forced into a single identical quotation.

Both traditions take the geography as a help to obedience, not as a riddle, and they leave any further site discussion without claiming consensus beyond what is written here.

Deuteronomy 11:31

31For you are to pass over the Jordan to go in to possess the land which the LORD your God gives you, and you shall possess it and dwell in it.

Arminian

They are to pass over the Jordan, go in to possess the land which the LORD their God gives them, and possess it and dwell in it.

Arminian readers hear gift and task together: the LORD gives the land, and they are to go over, possess it, and dwell in it. The life of blessing and curse will be lived as residents, not only as travelers.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the giving. Crossing, possessing, and dwelling rest on what the LORD gives, even as the people must actually enter and live there.

Both traditions read the verse as the imminent future of this sermon, on shared ground.

Deuteronomy 11:32

32You shall observe to do all the statutes and the ordinances which I set before you today.

Arminian

They shall observe to do all the statutes and ordinances which Moses sets before them today.

Arminian readers hear a comprehensive charge at the chapter’s close: all the statutes set before them today are to be done, in the land they are about to enter.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see today’s whole will of God placed before the people to be observed. The line also opens the way into the statutes of chapter 12.

Both traditions take the verse as a hinge from the blessing-and-curse appeal to the laws that follow, without a doctrinal fork.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
