Passage

Deuteronomy 11

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Still on the plains of Moab, Moses calls Israel to love the LORD and to keep his commandments always. He reminds this generation of what their eyes have seen: the LORD’s work in Egypt, the overthrow of Pharaoh’s army in the Red Sea, the wilderness road, and the earth opening under Dathan and Abiram. They are to keep the whole commandment so that they may be strong, possess the land sworn to the fathers, and prolong their days in a land flowing with milk and honey. Unlike Egypt, which was watered by human labor, this land of hills and valleys drinks rain from the sky, and the LORD’s eyes are always on it. Both traditions read the unit as obedience grounded in remembered redemption and in the gift of the land, not as a way of earning the exodus already accomplished.

Deuteronomy 11:1

1Therefore you shall love the LORD your God, and keep his instructions, his statutes, his ordinances, and his commandments, always.

Arminian

Israel is told to love the LORD their God and to keep his instructions, statutes, ordinances, and commandments, always.

Arminian readers hear love and obedience held together as a living response. The call is constant, “always” and it is addressed to a people who already belong to the LORD.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see love of God and the keeping of his words as the fitting life of the covenant people. The “Therefore” looks back to the mercies already recounted in the preceding chapters.

Both traditions take love and obedience here as the way of a redeemed people, not as a ladder by which they first obtain the LORD.

Deuteronomy 11:2

2Know this day—for I don’t speak with your children who have not known, and who have not seen the chastisement of the LORD your God, his greatness, his mighty hand, his outstretched arm,

Arminian

Moses tells them to know this day that he is not speaking with their children who have not known or seen the LORD’s chastisement, his greatness, his mighty hand, and his outstretched arm.

Arminian readers hear a summons to those who have actually witnessed God’s dealing. Knowledge here is not secondhand; it is to be owned by the people who saw.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same address. The generation that has seen the LORD’s hand is made responsible to remember it and to know it today.

Both traditions read the unfinished sentence as beginning a list of mighty acts, completed in the verses that follow, and they do not treat “chastisement” as a point of contrast.

Deuteronomy 11:3

3his signs, and his works, which he did in the middle of Egypt to Pharaoh the king of Egypt, and to all his land;

Arminian

The list continues: his signs and works in the middle of Egypt, done to Pharaoh and to all his land.

Arminian readers see the exodus signs recalled as public acts in Egypt’s own land. The people are to know what God did to a king and a nation, not only what he did for Israel in private.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the signs and works are the LORD’s. Pharaoh and his land were the objects of God’s action, and Israel is to remember that history as God’s.

Both traditions take the verse as part of the remembered exodus, on shared ground.

Deuteronomy 11:4

4and what he did to the army of Egypt, to their horses, and to their chariots; how he made the water of the Red Sea to overflow them as they pursued you, and how the LORD has destroyed them to this day;

Arminian

They are to know what he did to Egypt’s army, horses, and chariots, how he made the water of the Red Sea overflow them as they pursued Israel, and how the LORD has destroyed them to this day.

Arminian readers hear a completed judgment: the pursuers were overflowed, and that destruction still stands. Rescue and judgment happened together at the sea.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the LORD destroyed the army, and that the result remains “to this day.” The memory is of God’s act, not of Israel’s military skill.

Both traditions read the Red Sea as a founding mercy that still underwrites the obedience now required.

Deuteronomy 11:5

5and what he did to you in the wilderness until you came to this place;

Arminian

They are also to know what he did to them in the wilderness until they came to this place.

Arminian readers hear the wilderness years included, not only the departure from Egypt. God’s dealing along the way, to this very place, is part of what they have seen.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same span: from the sea to the plains of Moab, the LORD’s work has accompanied them.

Both traditions take the wilderness as God’s school and provision, without forcing a single inventory of every event into this short line.

Deuteronomy 11:6

6and what he did to Dathan and Abiram, the sons of Eliab, the son of Reuben—how the earth opened its mouth and swallowed them up, with their households, their tents, and every living thing that followed them, in the middle of all Israel;

Arminian

They are to know what he did to Dathan and Abiram, sons of Eliab, son of Reuben: the earth opened its mouth and swallowed them, with their households, tents, and every living thing that followed them, in the middle of all Israel.

Arminian readers hear a warning inside the memory. God’s work includes judgment on rebellion in the camp, seen by the whole people, not only wonders done to Egypt.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the public character of the event, “in the middle of all Israel” and the completeness of the swallowing up. The verse names Dathan and Abiram, as Numbers 16 also does, and does not itself name others involved in that revolt.

Both traditions let the text stand as a remembered judgment on covenant rebellion, without expanding the cast beyond what this verse says.

Deuteronomy 11:7

7but your eyes have seen all of the LORD’s great work which he did.

Arminian

The long sentence closes: “but your eyes have seen all of the LORD’s great work which he did.”

Arminian readers hear the contrast with the children of verse 2. This audience is made up of witnesses. What they have seen is to issue in love and obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the seeing. The LORD’s great work is a given fact of their history, and it grounds the “Therefore” of the next verse.

Both traditions take eyewitness memory as the basis of the charge that follows, not as a merely private spiritual experience.

Deuteronomy 11:8

8Therefore you shall keep the entire commandment which I command you today, that you may be strong, and go in and possess the land that you go over to possess;

Arminian

Therefore they shall keep the entire commandment commanded today, that they may be strong, and go in and possess the land they are going over to possess.

Arminian readers hear a real connection between keeping the whole commandment and being strong to enter. The land is ahead of them as a gift to be possessed, and obedience is the way of a people ready to receive it.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see strength and possession tied to keeping the commandment. The “Therefore” rests on the great work already seen; the land remains what they go over to possess under God’s promise.

Both traditions refuse to turn the verse into a claim that Israel earns the land from scratch. The commandment is given to a people already brought this far.

Deuteronomy 11:9

9and that you may prolong your days in the land which the LORD swore to your fathers to give to them and to their offspring, a land flowing with milk and honey.

Arminian

The purpose continues: that they may prolong their days in the land which the LORD swore to their fathers to give to them and to their offspring, “a land flowing with milk and honey.”

Arminian readers hear long life in the land as a covenant blessing held out to those who keep the commandment, in a land already sworn to the fathers.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the oath to the fathers. The land is gift and promise before it is tenure; prolonged days are enjoyed in what God swore to give.

Both traditions keep the verse within Israel’s life in the land, not as a private guarantee detached from that sworn gift.

Deuteronomy 11:10

10For the land, where you go in to possess isn’t like the land of Egypt that you came out of, where you sowed your seed and watered it with your foot, as a garden of herbs;

Arminian

The land they go in to possess “isn’t like the land of Egypt” that they came out of, where they sowed seed and watered it with the foot, as a garden of herbs.

Arminian readers hear a contrast of dependence. Egypt’s garden could be watered by human labor; the land ahead will not be managed in the same way.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same contrast. The verse does not pause to explain the exact method of watering with the foot, and no one reconstruction is treated as settled.

Both traditions take the point to be the difference between a land Israel worked like a garden and the land now being given, whose watering the next verses will ascribe to rain from God.

Deuteronomy 11:11

11but the land that you go over to possess is a land of hills and valleys which drinks water from the rain of the sky,

Arminian

The land they go over to possess is a land of hills and valleys which “drinks water from the rain of the sky,”

Arminian readers see a country that must receive what comes from above. Hills and valleys live by rain, and the people will live by trusting the Giver of that rain.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same dependence. The land drinks what the sky gives, not what Israel’s foot can pump as in Egypt.

Both traditions read the verse as preparation for verse 12, and for the later warning that the sky can be shut.

Deuteronomy 11:12

12a land which the LORD your God cares for. The LORD your God’s eyes are always on it, from the beginning of the year even to the end of the year.

Arminian

It is “a land which the LORD your God cares for.” His eyes are always on it, from the beginning of the year to the end of the year.

Arminian readers hear ongoing care, not a one-time gift then left alone. The year’s whole circuit is under the LORD’s watch, which is both comfort and a call to stay with him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the LORD’s constant regard. The land is not a neutral climate; it is watched by the God who gives it.

Both traditions take the verse as a promise of attentive care over the land of the oath, shared ground before the conditions of verses 13–17 are stated.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
