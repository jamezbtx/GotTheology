Passage

Deuteronomy 11

Verses 13–25 · WEB

Moses now states the land’s blessing in “if” form: if Israel will listen diligently to the commandments, love the LORD, and serve him with all the heart and soul, he will give the early rain and the latter rain, grain, wine, oil, and grass, and they will eat and be full. They must be careful not to let the heart be deceived and turn to other gods, or the LORD will shut the sky, the land will fail, and they will perish quickly from the good land. Therefore his words are to be laid up in heart and soul, bound as a sign, taught to the children, and written on door posts and gates, that days may be multiplied in the sworn land. If they diligently keep the commandments, love him, walk in his ways, and cling to him, he will drive out greater nations, and every place the sole of their foot treads will be theirs, from the wilderness and Lebanon to the Euphrates and the western sea. Both traditions read these as real terms for life in the land, given to a people already redeemed. The formal setting of blessing and curse as a choice before them is taken up in verses 26–32.

Deuteronomy 11:13

13It shall happen, if you shall listen diligently to my commandments which I command you today, to love the LORD your God, and to serve him with all your heart and with all your soul,

Arminian

The promise is introduced with a condition: “if you shall listen diligently to my commandments which I command you today, to love the LORD your God, and to serve him with all your heart and with all your soul,”

Arminian readers take the “if” as a real call to a whole-person love and service. Listening diligently is not a mere form; heart and soul are named.

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear a genuine call, set within the land-covenant terms Moses is announcing. Love and service with all the heart and soul recall the charge already given in Deuteronomy 6, and they remain the way of life in the land.

Both traditions hold this “if” together with the redemption already remembered in verses 1–7. The formal setting of blessing and curse as a choice before the people is taken up in verses 26–32, and the difference of emphasis between the traditions is discussed at Deuteronomy 30:19.

Deuteronomy 11:14

14that I will give the rain for your land in its season, the early rain and the latter rain, that you may gather in your grain, your new wine, and your oil.

Arminian

Then the LORD will give the rain for the land in its season, “the early rain and the latter rain,” so that they may gather grain, new wine, and oil.

Arminian readers see harvest as God’s gift in season, not as a mechanical yield. Early and latter rains name the year’s needed showers, and the fruit is grain, wine, and oil for a full life in the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the rain is given, in its season, by the LORD. The land of verses 11–12 drinks what he sends.

Both traditions read the agricultural blessing as national life in the land, without turning the two rains into a separate typological code.

Deuteronomy 11:15

15I will give grass in your fields for your livestock, and you shall eat and be full.

Arminian

He will also give grass in the fields for the livestock, and the people shall eat and be full.

Arminian readers hear provision that reaches the flocks and then the table. Fullness is a gift to be received with the listening love of verse 13.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see grass and a full meal as the LORD’s giving. Livestock and people are both under his care in the land he watches.

Both traditions take the verse as the close of the rain-and-harvest promise, on shared ground.

Deuteronomy 11:16

16Be careful, lest your heart be deceived, and you turn away to serve other gods and worship them;

Arminian

A warning follows: “Be careful, lest your heart be deceived, and you turn away to serve other gods and worship them;”

Arminian readers hear a real danger to the heart. Fullness can be the occasion of deception, and turning to other gods is a path they might actually take.

Reformed

Reformed readers also take the caution as earnest. The heart can be deceived, and idolatry is the named turn away from the LORD who gives the rain.

Both traditions see the warning as the necessary counterpart of verses 13–15, not as an empty threat.

Deuteronomy 11:17

17and the LORD’s anger be kindled against you, and he shut up the sky so that there is no rain, and the land doesn’t yield its fruit; and you perish quickly from off the good land which the LORD gives you.

Arminian

If they turn, the LORD’s anger will be kindled, he will shut up the sky so that there is no rain, the land will not yield its fruit, and they will perish quickly from off the good land which the LORD gives them.

Arminian readers hear the gift reversed: the sky of verse 11 can be shut, and the good land, still described as what the LORD gives, can be lost quickly by a people who turn away.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that anger, closed sky, and failed fruit are the LORD’s acts. Even in the warning, the land is “the good land which the LORD gives you.”

Both traditions read a covenant judgment on the nation’s life in the land, matching the drought-and-exile pattern also heard in Leviticus 26, without opening a new contrast here.

Deuteronomy 11:18

18Therefore you shall lay up these words of mine in your heart and in your soul. You shall bind them for a sign on your hand, and they shall be for frontlets between your eyes.

Arminian

Therefore they shall lay up these words in heart and soul, bind them for a sign on the hand, and have them “for frontlets between your eyes.”

Arminian readers see the words taking up residence in the inner life and in daily marks of remembrance. The aim is that the heart not be deceived as verse 16 warned.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same inward laying up and outward sign, language that also appears in Deuteronomy 6:8. Whether later tefillin practice is already in view is discussed; the verse itself commands a sign and frontlets and does not describe a later form in detail.

Both traditions take the command as a call to keep the words at the center of life, and they do not treat a single later ritual explanation as settled by this line alone.

Deuteronomy 11:19

19You shall teach them to your children, talking of them when you sit in your house, when you walk by the way, when you lie down, and when you rise up.

Arminian

They shall teach them to their children, talking of them when they sit in the house, walk by the way, lie down, and rise up.

Arminian readers hear a teaching that fills ordinary time. The words are not reserved for sacred moments only; children learn them in the day’s sitting, walking, sleeping, and rising.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the household as a place of catechesis along the way, in language close to Deuteronomy 6:7, though the wording here is not identical to that verse.

Both traditions take the command as the passing on of the same words, on shared ground.

Deuteronomy 11:20

20You shall write them on the door posts of your house and on your gates;

Arminian

They shall write them on the door posts of the house and on the gates.

Arminian readers see the words meeting the family at the door and the community at the gate. Coming and going are to pass under what God has said.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same public writing, again close to Deuteronomy 6:9. The house and the gate are marked by the commandment.

Both traditions read the verse as a visible reminder at the thresholds of ordinary life, without a doctrinal fork.

Deuteronomy 11:21

21that your days and your children’s days may be multiplied in the land which the LORD swore to your fathers to give them, as the days of the heavens above the earth.

Arminian

The purpose is that their days and their children’s days may be multiplied in the land which the LORD swore to the fathers to give them, “as the days of the heavens above the earth.”

Arminian readers hear a hope of long, multiplied life in the sworn land, pictured by the lasting days of the heavens. The gift is for the children too, if the words are kept and taught.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress again the oath to the fathers and the vast comparison with the heavens. Length of days in the land is covenant blessing under that oath.

Both traditions keep the promise tied to life in the land God swore to give, not as an abstract immortality detached from that setting.

Deuteronomy 11:22

22For if you shall diligently keep all these commandments which I command you—to do them, to love the LORD your God, to walk in all his ways, and to cling to him—

Arminian

A further “if” gathers the charge: if they diligently keep all these commandments, to do them, “to love the LORD your God, to walk in all his ways, and to cling to him”

Arminian readers hear love, walking, and clinging as a living attachment, not a bare checklist. The people are called to hold fast to the God who has held them.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see keeping, loving, walking, and clinging as the covenant way. The condition introduces the dispossession promised in the next verses.

Both traditions again treat the “if” as a real charge to a redeemed people, with the formal blessing-and-curse alternative stated in verses 26–32.

Deuteronomy 11:23

23then the LORD will drive out all these nations from before you, and you shall dispossess nations greater and mightier than yourselves.

Arminian

Then the LORD will drive out all these nations from before them, and they shall dispossess nations greater and mightier than themselves.

Arminian readers hear victory over stronger nations as the LORD’s doing, granted along the path of the clinging described in verse 22. Israel does not prevail by size.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the LORD drives the nations out. Israel’s dispossessing of greater peoples is the effect of his act, not of their might.

Both traditions take the verse as a land-conquest promise under the terms just named, on shared ground.

Deuteronomy 11:24

24Every place on which the sole of your foot treads shall be yours: from the wilderness and Lebanon, from the river, the river Euphrates, even to the western sea shall be your border.

Arminian

Every place on which the sole of their foot treads shall be theirs: from the wilderness and Lebanon, from the river Euphrates, even to the western sea shall be their border.

Arminian readers see a wide promised range, from south and north to the great river and the sea. The foot’s tread is under a gift already described, not a blank warrant for any later claim.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same borders. The wilderness, Lebanon, the Euphrates, and the western sea name the land in large terms, as God has spoken.

Both traditions read a territorial promise to this covenant people, without turning the map into a separate later debate between the traditions.

Deuteronomy 11:25

25No man will be able to stand before you. The LORD your God will lay the fear of you and the dread of you on all the land that you tread on, as he has spoken to you.

Arminian

No man will be able to stand before them. The LORD will lay the fear and dread of them on all the land they tread, as he has spoken to them.

Arminian readers hear security in the land as God’s act: fear and dread fall because he lays them there, as he has already spoken.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that no one can stand, because the LORD has spoken and will lay fear on the land. The promise is as reliable as his word.

Both traditions close this blessing section on shared ground, then turn with verses 26–32 to the blessing and curse formally set before the people.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
