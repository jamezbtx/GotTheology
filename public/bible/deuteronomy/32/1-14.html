Passage

Deuteronomy 32

Verses 1–14 · WEB

The Song of Moses opens by summoning heaven and earth, proclaiming the LORD as the Rock whose work is perfect and contrasting him with a corrupt generation. It recalls how God set the boundaries of the nations, chose Jacob as his portion, found Israel in the wilderness, carried them like an eagle, and lavished the riches of the land on them.

Deuteronomy 32:1

1Give ear, you heavens, and I will speak. Let the earth hear the words of my mouth.

Arminian

The Song opens by calling heaven and earth to listen. Arminian readers see creation summoned as witness to God’s dealings with his people. The whole created order is invited to listen.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the cosmic audience emphasizing the seriousness of the message. The opening echoes 31:28, where heaven and earth are named as witnesses.

Deuteronomy 32:2

2My doctrine will drop as the rain. My speech will condense as the dew, as the misty rain on the tender grass, as the showers on the herb.

Arminian

Moses’ teaching will fall like rain and dew on tender grass. Arminian readers see God’s word as nourishing and life-giving for those who receive it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the gentle, refreshing power of God’s word. The images of rain and dew suggest teaching that brings growth. Teaching that refreshes is still teaching that calls for response.

Deuteronomy 32:3

3For I will proclaim the LORD’s name. Ascribe greatness to our God!

Arminian

Moses will proclaim the LORD’s name and calls the people, “Ascribe greatness to our God!” Arminian readers see praise of God as the starting point of the Song.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Song centered on God’s greatness. The call to ascribe greatness invites the people to join in worship. The Song begins with God before it speaks about Israel.

Deuteronomy 32:4

4The Rock: his work is perfect, for all his ways are just. A God of faithfulness who does no wrong, just and right is he.

Arminian

God is the Rock, whose work is perfect, whose ways are just, faithful, and without wrong. Arminian readers see God’s justice and faithfulness as the standard against which Israel’s unfaithfulness will be measured.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s perfection and righteousness affirmed. The title Rock recurs throughout the Song, emphasizing God’s stability and reliability. Whatever follows about judgment rests on this confession of God’s justice and faithfulness.

Deuteronomy 32:5

5They have dealt corruptly with him. They are not his children, because of their defect. They are a perverse and crooked generation.

Arminian

Israel has dealt corruptly with God; they are “a perverse and crooked generation.” Arminian readers see the people’s defect as their own responsibility. Their corruption contrasts sharply with the Rock described in the verse before.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Israel’s corruption contrasted with God’s perfection. Philippians 2:15 uses similar language, describing believers living “in the middle of a crooked and perverse generation.”

Deuteronomy 32:6

6Is this the way you repay the LORD, foolish and unwise people? Isn’t he your father who has bought you? He has made you and established you.

Arminian

Moses asks, “Is this the way you repay the LORD, foolish and unwise people? Isn’t he your father who has bought you?” Arminian readers see ingratitude toward a loving Father and Redeemer.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God as Father, Maker, and Redeemer of Israel. The rhetorical questions expose how unreasonable Israel’s rebellion is. Ingratitude toward such a Father is shown to be foolish as well as wrong.

Deuteronomy 32:7

7Remember the days of old. Consider the years of many generations. Ask your father, and he will show you; your elders, and they will tell you.

Arminian

Israel must remember the days of old and ask fathers and elders. Arminian readers see memory and testimony passed down as a safeguard for faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the importance of recalling God’s past works. The verse draws on intergenerational teaching like that commanded in 31:13. Faith is nourished by remembering what God has done.

Deuteronomy 32:8

8When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance, when he separated the children of men, he set the bounds of the peoples according to the number of the children of Israel.

Arminian

When the Most High divided the nations, he set their boundaries according to the number of Israel’s children. Arminian readers see God’s rule over all nations, with Israel holding a special place.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s sovereignty in ordering the nations. Acts 17:26 speaks of God “having determined appointed seasons and the boundaries of their dwellings,” a related theme. Some ancient copies read “sons of God” rather than “children of Israel”; the exact wording is debated, and no conclusion here depends on the variant.

Deuteronomy 32:9

9For the LORD’s portion is his people. Jacob is the lot of his inheritance.

Arminian

The LORD’s portion is his people; Jacob is his inheritance. Arminian readers see God’s special relationship with Israel as his chosen people. The verse frames Israel’s identity in terms of belonging to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God claiming Israel as his own. The language of portion and inheritance expresses ownership and affection. Israel’s value lies in being claimed by God, not in its own size or strength.

Deuteronomy 32:10

10He found him in a desert land, in the waste howling wilderness. He surrounded him. He cared for him. He kept him as the apple of his eye.

Arminian

God found Israel in a howling wilderness and kept him “as the apple of his eye.” Arminian readers see tender, protective care for a vulnerable people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s initiative and protection. The image of the apple of the eye conveys how precious Israel is to God. Israel’s story begins with God finding them.

Deuteronomy 32:11

11As an eagle that stirs up her nest, that flutters over her young, he spread abroad his wings, he took them, he bore them on his feathers.

Arminian

God is like an eagle stirring its nest and bearing its young on its wings. Arminian readers see God training and carrying his people toward maturity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s care and strength in the eagle image. The picture recalls Exodus 19:4, where God speaks of bearing Israel on eagles’ wings.

Deuteronomy 32:12

12The LORD alone led him. There was no foreign god with him.

Arminian

The LORD alone led Israel, with no foreign god. Arminian readers see God’s exclusive role in Israel’s deliverance, which makes later idolatry all the more ungrateful.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God alone as Israel’s guide. The emphasis on no foreign god prepares for the condemnation of idolatry that follows. Credit for the journey belongs to God alone.

Deuteronomy 32:13

13He made him ride on the high places of the earth. He ate the increase of the field. He caused him to suck honey out of the rock, oil out of the flinty rock;

Arminian

God set Israel on the heights and gave honey and oil from the rock. Arminian readers see abundant provision as evidence of God’s generosity. Israel received what it did not produce.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s lavish blessing on his people. The images of honey and oil from rock stress provision beyond natural expectation. The gifts flow from the Rock himself.

Deuteronomy 32:14

14butter from the herd, and milk from the flock, with fat of lambs, rams of the breed of Bashan, and goats, with the finest of the wheat. From the blood of the grape, you drank wine.

Arminian

Butter, milk, fat, fine wheat, and wine describe the richness God gave. Arminian readers see blessing that should have produced gratitude. Every good thing on the list came from God’s hand rather than Israel’s effort.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s bounty in full detail. The richness of the list sets up the tragic turn in the next verse. Blessing is described at its fullest.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
