Passage

Deuteronomy 32

Verses 15–33 · WEB

Jeshurun grows fat and rejects the Rock of his salvation, turning to strange gods and demons. God resolves to hide his face, provoke Israel to jealousy with “those who are not a people,” and send fierce judgments, yet restrains total destruction lest enemies boast. The section laments Israel’s folly and contrasts the true Rock with the poisonous vine of the nations.

Deuteronomy 32:15

15But Jeshurun grew fat, and kicked. You have grown fat. You have grown thick. You have become sleek. Then he abandoned God who made him, and rejected the Rock of his salvation.

Arminian

Jeshurun grew fat and kicked, abandoning God and rejecting “the Rock of his salvation.” Arminian readers see prosperity leading to freely chosen rebellion. Abundance did not produce thankfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the heart’s ingratitude amid blessing. Jeshurun is a poetic name for Israel, often linked with the Hebrew word for upright, which sharpens the irony of the turning away. Rejecting God means rejecting salvation itself.

Deuteronomy 32:16

16They moved him to jealousy with strange gods. They provoked him to anger with abominations.

Arminian

Israel provoked God to jealousy with strange gods and abominations. Arminian readers see covenant betrayal portrayed in relational terms. The language of jealousy portrays God as a wronged husband or partner, deeply affected by unfaithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s jealousy as his rightful claim on his people’s worship. The verse names idolatry as the core offense. Worship belongs to God alone, and its misdirection provokes him.

Deuteronomy 32:17

17They sacrificed to demons, not God, to gods that they didn’t know, to new gods that came up recently, which your fathers didn’t dread.

Arminian

They sacrificed to demons, not God, to new gods their fathers did not dread. Arminian readers see idolatry exposed as service to evil spiritual powers.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see false worship as more than empty ritual. Paul uses similar language in 1 Corinthians 10:20: “they sacrifice to demons, and not to God.”

Deuteronomy 32:18

18Of the Rock who became your father, you are unmindful, and have forgotten God who gave you birth.

Arminian

Israel forgot the Rock who fathered them and the God who gave them birth. Arminian readers see forgetfulness as a moral failure of gratitude and loyalty.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the people’s ingratitude toward their Creator and Father. The birth imagery underscores God’s intimate relationship with Israel. Forgetting the giver of life is shown to be both unnatural and grievous.

Deuteronomy 32:19

19The LORD saw and abhorred, because of the provocation of his sons and his daughters.

Arminian

The LORD saw and abhorred because his sons and daughters provoked him. Arminian readers see God responding personally to his children’s betrayal. Rebellion wounds a relationship, not just a rule.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s holy displeasure at sin. The family language makes the offense more painful. Israel’s status as God’s children makes their provocation more serious, not less, and his response is just.

Deuteronomy 32:20

20He said, “I will hide my face from them. I will see what their end will be; for they are a very perverse generation, children in whom is no faithfulness.

Arminian

God says, “I will hide my face from them,” for they are a perverse generation without faithfulness. Arminian readers see God’s withdrawal as a response to Israel’s unfaithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see judgment fitting the people’s faithlessness. The verse echoes 31:17–18, where God speaks of hiding his face. The phrase “children in whom is no faithfulness” names the root problem.

Deuteronomy 32:21

21They have moved me to jealousy with that which is not God. They have provoked me to anger with their vanities. I will move them to jealousy with those who are not a people. I will provoke them to anger with a foolish nation.

Arminian

God will provoke Israel to jealousy with “those who are not a people” and a foolish nation. Arminian readers see a measure-for-measure judgment answering Israel’s idolatry.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God turning Israel’s sin back on them. Paul quotes this verse in Romans 10:19: “I will provoke you to jealousy with that which is no nation.”

Deuteronomy 32:22

22For a fire is kindled in my anger, that burns to the lowest Sheol, devours the earth with its increase, and sets the foundations of the mountains on fire.

Arminian

God’s anger burns like fire to the lowest Sheol and sets the mountains ablaze. Arminian readers see the intensity of divine judgment against persistent sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fearful reach of God’s wrath. The WEB footnote explains Sheol as the place of the dead. The fire imagery shows how far judgment can reach when God’s anger is kindled.

Deuteronomy 32:23

23“I will heap evils on them. I will spend my arrows on them.

Arminian

God will heap evils and spend his arrows on Israel. Arminian readers see covenant curses falling on a rebellious people as warned. Judgment is personal, coming from God himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s judgment executed fully. The arrows image pictures judgments sent by God himself. The warnings of Deuteronomy are not empty threats, and the Song presents them as coming from God himself.

Deuteronomy 32:24

24They shall be wasted with hunger, and devoured with burning heat and bitter destruction. I will send the teeth of animals on them, with the venom of vipers that glide in the dust.

Arminian

Hunger, plague, wild beasts, and venom will afflict them. Arminian readers see the covenant curses listed in Deuteronomy 28 coming into view. Each image intensifies the sense of distress.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the comprehensive nature of the judgment. The verse portrays devastation in vivid poetic terms. Nature itself becomes an instrument of judgment, so Israel has no safe place left when it abandons God.

Deuteronomy 32:25

25Outside the sword will bereave, and in the rooms, terror on both young man and virgin, the nursing infant with the gray-haired man.

Arminian

The sword outside and terror inside will strike young and old. Arminian readers see judgment reaching every part of the community. Neither youth nor age offers protection.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see no one exempt. The poetry emphasizes the totality of the disaster that faithlessness brings. The pairing of young and old, inside and outside, shows that judgment reaches everyone.

Deuteronomy 32:26

26I said that I would scatter them afar. I would make their memory to cease from among men;

Arminian

God says he would scatter them and erase their memory. Arminian readers see how severe the deserved judgment could be. The verse states what Israel’s sin deserved before showing restraint.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the extent of what Israel’s sin warranted. The next verse explains why God stops short of total destruction. God’s restraint, not Israel’s merit, preserves them.

Deuteronomy 32:27

27were it not that I feared the provocation of the enemy, lest their adversaries should judge wrongly, lest they should say, ‘Our hand is exalted; the LORD has not done all this.’ ”

Arminian

God refrains, lest the enemy say, “Our hand is exalted; the LORD has not done all this.” Arminian readers see God guarding his name from misunderstanding.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God acting for the sake of his own glory. The verse shows mercy restrained judgment for the sake of God’s reputation.

Deuteronomy 32:28

28For they are a nation void of counsel. There is no understanding in them.

Arminian

They are a nation without counsel or understanding. Arminian readers see this as a description of the enemy nations’ folly, or of Israel’s, depending on how the referent is read.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the ambiguity of the referent in the flow of the poem. Interpreters differ on whether the verse describes Israel or its enemies.

Deuteronomy 32:29

29Oh that they were wise, that they understood this, that they would consider their latter end!

Arminian

“Oh that they were wise, that they understood this, that they would consider their latter end!” Arminian readers see God’s longing for repentance and wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the lament over folly and failure to consider consequences. The wish expresses grief rather than indifference. The problem is a failure to think about where their path leads.

Deuteronomy 32:30

30How could one chase a thousand, and two put ten thousand to flight, unless their Rock had sold them, and the LORD had delivered them up?

Arminian

How could one chase a thousand unless their Rock had sold them and the LORD delivered them up? Arminian readers see defeat explained as God’s withdrawal of protection.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s hand behind military outcomes. The verse insists that victories and defeats are not merely matters of strength. Israel’s defeats are a sign that the Rock has withdrawn.

Deuteronomy 32:31

31For their rock is not as our Rock, even our enemies themselves concede.

Arminian

Their rock is not like our Rock, as even enemies concede. Arminian readers see the superiority of the LORD over false gods acknowledged even by outsiders.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the uniqueness of Israel’s God affirmed. The play on the word rock contrasts the true Rock with false refuges. Even outsiders can see the difference.

Deuteronomy 32:32

32For their vine is of the vine of Sodom, of the fields of Gomorrah. Their grapes are poison grapes. Their clusters are bitter.

Arminian

Their vine is from Sodom and Gomorrah, with poisonous grapes. Arminian readers see corruption likened to the most notorious examples of wickedness. The comparison is meant to shock.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see moral rot portrayed in vivid imagery. The reference to Sodom and Gomorrah recalls Genesis 19. Fruit reveals the nature of the vine, and the fruit here is poisonous and bitter.

Deuteronomy 32:33

33Their wine is the poison of serpents, the cruel venom of asps.

Arminian

Their wine is serpents’ poison and cruel venom. Arminian readers see the deadly consequences of corrupted ways. What seems like wine turns out to be poison, a fitting picture of sin’s deception.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see sin’s destructive effects. The vine imagery continues from the previous verse. The fruit of false worship harms those who drink it, however attractive it first appears.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
