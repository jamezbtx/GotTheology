Passage

Deuteronomy 32

Verses 34–43 · WEB

God declares, “Vengeance is mine, and recompense,” promising to judge his people yet have compassion on his servants when their strength is gone. He mocks the idols that cannot help and proclaims, “I kill and I make alive,” a verse commonly treated in both traditions as shared ground on divine sovereignty. The Song ends with the nations called to rejoice with his people as God avenges and makes atonement. The WEB footnote at 32:43 gives a longer Septuagint form; no doctrine here rests on that variant.

Deuteronomy 32:34

34“Isn’t this laid up in store with me, sealed up among my treasures?

Arminian

God says, “Isn’t this laid up in store with me, sealed up among my treasures?” Arminian readers see judgment reserved in God’s knowledge until its time.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s judgments stored according to his purpose. The image suggests certainty and timing under God’s control. Nothing is forgotten before God, and his judgments wait for their appointed time.

Deuteronomy 32:35

35Vengeance is mine, and recompense, at the time when their foot slides, for the day of their calamity is at hand. Their doom rushes at them.”

Arminian

God says, “Vengeance is mine, and recompense,” for their day of calamity is near. Arminian readers see judgment belonging to God, not to individuals. Personal revenge is left to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s exclusive right to repay. Paul cites this in Romans 12:19, “Vengeance belongs to me; I will repay, says the Lord.” and Hebrews 10:30 uses similar words.

Deuteronomy 32:36

36For the LORD will judge his people, and have compassion on his servants, when he sees that their power is gone, that there is no one remaining, shut up or left at large.

Arminian

The LORD will judge his people and have compassion on his servants when their strength is gone. Arminian readers see judgment and compassion together, with mercy shown when Israel is helpless.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s compassion toward his servants. Hebrews 10:30 has the words “The Lord will judge his people.” Compassion appears precisely when the people have nothing left to rely on.

Deuteronomy 32:37

37He will say, “Where are their gods, the rock in which they took refuge,

Arminian

God asks, “Where are their gods, the rock in which they took refuge?” Arminian readers see the failure of false gods exposed when they are most needed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the impotence of idols. The question mocks the refuges Israel chose instead of the true Rock. The gods Israel trusted are absent when crisis comes.

Deuteronomy 32:38

38which ate the fat of their sacrifices, and drank the wine of their drink offering? Let them rise up and help you! Let them be your protection.

Arminian

The idols that ate the sacrifices are challenged: “Let them rise up and help you!” Arminian readers see the emptiness of worship directed to false gods.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see idols unable to save. The taunt underscores that only the LORD can deliver. The idols consumed offerings but provide no help, and the challenge exposes their emptiness.

Deuteronomy 32:39

39“See now that I myself am he. There is no god with me. I kill and I make alive. I wound and I heal. There is no one who can deliver out of my hand.

Arminian

God declares, “I kill and I make alive. I wound and I heal. There is no one who can deliver out of my hand.” Arminian readers affirm God’s sovereign authority over life and death.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm God’s sovereign rule. Readers in both traditions commonly treat this verse as shared ground on God’s sovereignty; 1 Samuel 2:6 echoes it: “The LORD kills and makes alive.”

Deuteronomy 32:40

40For I lift up my hand to heaven and declare, as I live forever,

Arminian

God lifts his hand to heaven and swears, “as I live forever.” Arminian readers see God binding himself by oath to act. The oath assures the faithful.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God swearing by his own eternal life. The oath underscores the certainty of what follows. No higher guarantee can be given, since God swears by his own life.

Deuteronomy 32:41

41if I sharpen my glittering sword, my hand grasps it in judgment; I will take vengeance on my adversaries, and will repay those who hate me.

Arminian

God will sharpen his sword and take vengeance on his adversaries. Arminian readers see justice executed against those who oppose God and his people. God’s justice is not indifferent to evil.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God as righteous Judge. The imagery is poetic but conveys real judgment. His vengeance is the action of a just ruler, directed against adversaries who hate him.

Deuteronomy 32:42

42I will make my arrows drunk with blood. My sword shall devour flesh with the blood of the slain and the captives, from the head of the leaders of the enemy.”

Arminian

God’s arrows and sword will devour the enemy. Arminian readers see vivid warrior imagery expressing the completeness of God’s victory. The poem does not soften the cost of opposing God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see judgment pictured in martial terms. The poetry emphasizes God’s power over his foes. The Song uses strong images to show that evil will not stand before God.

Deuteronomy 32:43

43Rejoice, you nations, with his people, for he will avenge the blood of his servants. He will take vengeance on his adversaries, and will make atonement for his land and for his people.

Arminian

The nations are called to rejoice with his people, for God will avenge his servants and make atonement for his land and people. Arminian readers see judgment ending in restoration.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s vindication and atonement for his people. Romans 15:10 cites similar words: “Rejoice, you Gentiles, with his people.” The WEB footnote gives a longer Septuagint form; no doctrine should rest on the variant.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
