Passage

Deuteronomy 28

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Chapter 28 sets out the covenant sanctions, as Leviticus 26 does. This first unit lists the blessings promised if Israel listens to the LORD’s voice: exaltation, fruitfulness, victory, rain, and a holy standing that the nations will recognize.

Deuteronomy 28:1

1It shall happen, if you shall listen diligently to the LORD your God’s voice, to observe to do all his commandments which I command you today, that the LORD your God will set you high above all the nations of the earth.

Arminian

The blessings hinge on listening diligently. Arminian readers take the if as a genuine condition: Israel’s response truly affects whether these national blessings are enjoyed, though grace precedes the call.

Reformed

Being set high above the nations fulfills the promise to Abraham’s offspring. Reformed readers commonly read these blessings as the Sinai covenant’s terms for national life in the land, distinct from the ground of personal salvation.

Deuteronomy 28:2

2All these blessings will come upon you, and overtake you, if you listen to the LORD your God’s voice.

Arminian

The blessings will come upon you and overtake you, as if pursuing the obedient. Arminian readers find in the image God’s goodness running after those who walk with him.

Reformed

Blessings overtaking Israel picture God’s initiative in giving good things. Reformed readers note that even under conditions, the blessing is God’s gift and not wages earned.

Deuteronomy 28:3

3You shall be blessed in the city, and you shall be blessed in the field.

Arminian

City and field together cover the whole of life. Arminian readers see that faithfulness to God is meant to shape every sphere, from public business to farm work.

Reformed

The pairs in 28:3-6 are mirrored closely in 28:16-19. Reformed readers note the symmetry, which makes plain that both outcomes are under God’s hand. Neither outcome is chance.

Deuteronomy 28:4

4You shall be blessed in the fruit of your body, the fruit of your ground, the fruit of your animals, the increase of your livestock, and the young of your flock.

Arminian

Children, crops, and herds are blessed. Arminian readers note that God cares about ordinary needs and that material provision was part of Israel’s covenant hope in the land.

Reformed

Fruitfulness in womb and field recalls the creation blessing (Genesis 1:28). Reformed readers often see Israel’s prosperity as a picture of the fuller blessing found in Christ.

Deuteronomy 28:5

5Your basket and your kneading trough shall be blessed.

Arminian

Basket and kneading trough are kitchen items, showing blessing reaches daily bread. Arminian readers find encouragement that God’s care touches the smallest details. No corner of the household lies outside his care.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that even daily food depends on God’s providence, as the Lord’s Prayer later teaches with the request for daily bread. The trough holds tomorrow’s bread, so the blessing reaches forward into the future.

Deuteronomy 28:6

6You shall be blessed when you come in, and you shall be blessed when you go out.

Arminian

Coming in and going out covers all of a person’s activity. Arminian readers see a promise of God’s presence across every movement of a faithful life.

Reformed

The same pairing appears in Psalm 121:8. Reformed readers see God’s protecting providence surrounding his people in all their ways. Daily routines are covered by his watchful care.

Deuteronomy 28:7

7The LORD will cause your enemies who rise up against you to be struck before you. They will come out against you one way, and will flee before you seven ways.

Arminian

Enemies come one way and flee seven. Arminian readers stress that victory comes from the LORD and is tied to Israel’s continuing faithfulness. Faithfulness, not military strength, is the decisive factor here.

Reformed

The number seven suggests complete rout. Reformed readers note that God, not Israel’s strength, secures victory, a recurring theme since 3:22. The LORD causes the enemies to be struck; Israel’s role is secondary.

Deuteronomy 28:8

8The LORD will command the blessing on you in your barns, and in all that you put your hand to. He will bless you in the land which the LORD your God gives you.

Arminian

The LORD will command the blessing. Arminian readers see God’s active, personal involvement in providing for those who honor him. Barns and every undertaking alike fall within the reach of his favor.

Reformed

Commanding the blessing shows God’s sovereign word bringing prosperity. Reformed readers often connect this with Psalm 133:3, where the LORD commands blessing. Where God commands, the result is sure.

Deuteronomy 28:9

9The LORD will establish you for a holy people to himself, as he has sworn to you, if you shall keep the commandments of the LORD your God, and walk in his ways.

Arminian

The LORD will establish you as a holy people if you keep his commandments. Arminian readers note the condition attached to Israel’s established standing. Holiness is gift and calling at once.

Reformed

As he has sworn to you grounds the promise in God’s oath. Reformed readers see holiness as both God’s establishing act and Israel’s calling, echoing 7:6.

Deuteronomy 28:10

10All the peoples of the earth shall see that you are called by the LORD’s name, and they will be afraid of you.

Arminian

Called by the LORD’s name, Israel becomes a visible witness. Arminian readers stress that God’s purpose for Israel included the nations seeing and fearing him.

Reformed

Bearing God’s name marks Israel as his possession. Reformed readers see this identity as God’s gift, later applied to the church (compare James 2:7). The nations’ fear points beyond Israel to God.

Deuteronomy 28:11

11The LORD will grant you abundant prosperity in the fruit of your body, in the fruit of your livestock, and in the fruit of your ground, in the land which the LORD swore to your fathers to give you.

Arminian

Abundant prosperity comes in the land sworn to the fathers. Arminian readers note that God’s promises to the patriarchs are fulfilled as later generations walk in faith.

Reformed

The oath to the fathers stands behind the abundance. Reformed readers emphasize God’s covenant faithfulness across generations as the root of every blessing. The fathers’ oath outlasts the generations.

Deuteronomy 28:12

12The LORD will open to you his good treasure in the sky, to give the rain of your land in its season, and to bless all the work of your hand. You will lend to many nations, and you will not borrow.

Arminian

Rain from God’s good treasure in the sky. Arminian readers note that the land depends on rain from heaven (11:11-14), keeping Israel dependent on God.

Reformed

Lending to nations and not borrowing describes economic security. Reformed readers see in this God’s sovereign control over nature and nations. Opening his treasure is pictured as God’s free generosity, not a payment owed.

Deuteronomy 28:13

13The LORD will make you the head, and not the tail. You will be above only, and you will not be beneath, if you listen to the commandments of the LORD your God which I command you today, to observe and to do,

Arminian

Head and not tail, if you listen. Arminian readers stress that the condition is repeated, so blessing is tied to listening and doing. Position follows obedience, not birthright.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the image of head and tail reverses in 28:44, showing that God governs Israel’s place among the nations. Rank among nations is his to assign.

Deuteronomy 28:14

14and shall not turn away from any of the words which I command you today, to the right hand or to the left, to go after other gods to serve them.

Arminian

Turning neither right nor left, especially toward other gods. Arminian readers stress steady faithfulness as a daily choice against idolatry. Small deviations, left or right, are where drift toward other gods often begins.

Reformed

The prohibition of other gods sums up the condition. Reformed readers see loyalty to the LORD alone as the heart of covenant obedience. All other duties depend on this first loyalty.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
