Passage

Deuteronomy 28

Verses 15–35 · WEB

The curses begin by reversing the blessings of 28:3-6 point by point, then describe disease, drought, defeat, and oppression. The language is stark and cumulative, meant to impress on Israel the seriousness of forsaking the LORD.

Deuteronomy 28:15

15But it shall come to pass, if you will not listen to the LORD your God’s voice, to observe to do all his commandments and his statutes which I command you today, that all these curses will come on you and overtake you.

Arminian

The same voice that offered blessing now warns of curse. Arminian readers stress that the warning is real and conditional, addressed to a people who could choose either path.

Reformed

Reformed readers take the curse as fully real and note that Israel’s later history, as the prophets interpret it, shows these sanctions coming to pass.

Deuteronomy 28:16

16You will be cursed in the city, and you will be cursed in the field.

Arminian

City and field are now cursed, reversing 28:3. Arminian readers note that the same spheres can become places of blessing or loss depending on faithfulness.

Reformed

The mirrored structure underlines that God is the author of both blessing and curse. Reformed readers see his justice and goodness expressed in his covenant sanctions.

Deuteronomy 28:17

17Your basket and your kneading trough will be cursed.

Arminian

Even the kneading trough is cursed, reaching daily bread. Arminian readers see how unfaithfulness affects the most ordinary parts of life. Loss begins at the family table, where gratitude was meant to begin.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God who gives daily bread can withhold it, a reminder that provision is never automatic. Want and plenty both come from his hand.

Deuteronomy 28:18

18The fruit of your body, the fruit of your ground, the increase of your livestock, and the young of your flock will be cursed.

Arminian

Fruitfulness becomes barrenness. Arminian readers note that the blessings of family and land can be lost through turning from God. The curse touches the hope of the next generation as well as the present harvest.

Reformed

The reversal of the creation blessing pictures the covenant curse. Reformed readers see in this the disorder that sin brings into God’s world. Barrenness reverses Genesis 1:28.

Deuteronomy 28:19

19You will be cursed when you come in, and you will be cursed when you go out.

Arminian

Coming in and going out are cursed. Arminian readers note that every activity becomes burdened when God’s favor is forfeited. Nothing they set out to do will prosper while they remain estranged from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that no area of life remains untouched when God’s people break covenant. The verse closes the four mirrored curses that answer the four blessings in 28:3-6, completing the symmetry.

Deuteronomy 28:20

20The LORD will send on you cursing, confusion, and rebuke in all that you put your hand to do, until you are destroyed and until you perish quickly, because of the evil of your doings, by which you have forsaken me.

Arminian

Cursing, confusion, and rebuke follow because of the evil of your doings. Arminian readers stress that the judgment answers Israel’s own persistent choices, so the curse is never arbitrary but a just response to forsaking the LORD.

Reformed

The phrase by which you have forsaken me shows that the curse is rooted in a broken relationship, not merely broken rules. Reformed readers often see forsaking God as the essence of sin, from which particular transgressions flow.

Deuteronomy 28:21

21The LORD will make the pestilence cling to you, until he has consumed you from off the land where you go in to possess it.

Arminian

Pestilence clings until Israel is consumed from the land. Arminian readers see how a gift received by grace can be forfeited by a people who refuse to walk in it, a theme they find echoed in New Testament warnings.

Reformed

The land God gave can be lost through covenant breaking, yet the oath to the fathers remains. Reformed readers often distinguish the loss of national privilege in the land from God’s saving purpose, which the later chapters reaffirm (30:1-6).

Deuteronomy 28:22

22The LORD will strike you with consumption, with fever, with inflammation, with fiery heat, with the sword, with blight, and with mildew. They will pursue you until you perish.

Arminian

The list of fever, sword, blight, and mildew shows judgment reaching bodies, harvests, and security alike. Arminian readers note that such breadth makes the warning hard to ignore and urges early repentance.

Reformed

Amos 4:9 recalls blight and mildew as judgments Israel experienced without returning. Reformed readers often note how the prophets read Israel’s history through the sanctions of this chapter.

Deuteronomy 28:23

23Your sky that is over your head will be bronze, and the earth that is under you will be iron.

Arminian

A bronze sky and iron earth picture drought so severe that the ground will not yield. Arminian readers see creation itself joining in the covenant’s witness against a faithless people (compare 4:26).

Reformed

Leviticus 26:19 uses the same pair of metals in reverse order. Reformed readers see in the image God’s providential rule over weather, which he can turn toward discipline as readily as toward blessing.

Deuteronomy 28:24

24The LORD will make the rain of your land powder and dust. It will come down on you from the sky, until you are destroyed.

Arminian

Rain turns to powder and dust, reversing the good treasure of 28:12. Arminian readers draw from such reversals the warning that God’s gifts are meant to lead to gratitude, and that ingratitude puts them at risk.

Reformed

The drought in Elijah’s day (1 Kings 17:1) is often cited by Reformed readers as an instance of this sanction. They stress that the God who sends rain may justly withhold it from a rebellious people.

Deuteronomy 28:25

25The LORD will cause you to be struck before your enemies. You will go out one way against them, and will flee seven ways before them. You will be tossed back and forth among all the kingdoms of the earth.

Arminian

Fleeing seven ways reverses the victory promised in 28:7. Arminian readers note how deliberately the curses mirror the blessings, keeping both futures before Israel as real possibilities.

Reformed

Being tossed among all the kingdoms foreshadows dispersion. Reformed readers see the defeat as God’s own act, since the verse says the LORD will cause you to be struck before your enemies.

Deuteronomy 28:26

26Your dead bodies will be food to all birds of the sky, and to the animals of the earth; and there will be no one to frighten them away.

Arminian

Unburied bodies left to birds were a deep disgrace in the ancient world. Arminian readers see the severity of the image as a mercy of warning, given in advance so that it might never be needed.

Reformed

Jeremiah 7:33 applies this curse to Judah in his day. Reformed readers note how the prophets understood the covenant sanctions as God’s word being fulfilled in history.

Deuteronomy 28:27

27The LORD will strike you with the boils of Egypt, with the tumors, with the scurvy, and with the itch, of which you can not be healed.

Arminian

The boils of Egypt recall the plagues God sent on Pharaoh (Exodus 9:9-11). Arminian readers note the sobering point that God’s people are not exempt from the judgments once poured on their oppressors.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the first hint of a reversal of the exodus, a theme that climaxes in 28:68. A redeemed nation that rebels can be treated as Egypt was treated.

Deuteronomy 28:28

28The LORD will strike you with madness, with blindness, and with astonishment of heart.

Arminian

Madness, blindness, and astonishment of heart reach the mind and inner life. Arminian readers see that turning from God affects not only circumstances but the capacity to think and see clearly.

Reformed

Reformed readers often connect such judicial blindness with passages where God gives people over to confusion, such as Romans 1:28, while holding the people fully responsible.

Deuteronomy 28:29

29You will grope at noonday, as a blind person gropes in darkness, and you will not prosper in your ways. You will only be oppressed and robbed always, and there will be no one to save you.

Arminian

Groping at noonday is a vivid irony: darkness in full light. Arminian readers see a picture of those who have rejected the light of God’s word and lost their way.

Reformed

The repeated line no one to save points to helplessness. Reformed readers see in it the truth that only God can rescue, which makes forsaking him uniquely ruinous.

Deuteronomy 28:30

30You will betroth a wife, and another man shall lie with her. You will build a house, and you won’t dwell in it. You will plant a vineyard, and not use its fruit.

Arminian

Wife, house, and vineyard are the very things whose enjoyment 20:5-7 protected. Arminian readers note that the curse strips away the ordinary joys God had specially guarded.

Reformed

Reformed readers see futility as part of the curse: labor without fruit. They often link it with the frustration of work after Genesis 3:17-19. Hope deferred becomes judgment.

Deuteronomy 28:31

31Your ox will be slain before your eyes, and you will not eat any of it. Your donkey will be violently taken away from before your face, and will not be restored to you. Your sheep will be given to your enemies, and you will have no one to save you.

Arminian

Livestock seized before the owner’s eyes shows humiliating powerlessness. Arminian readers stress that forsaking God leaves people exposed to forces they cannot resist. Theft happens openly, unchecked.

Reformed

The repeated phrase no one to save you appears again (compare 28:29). Reformed readers hear in this refrain the absence of the Savior Israel had forsaken.

Deuteronomy 28:32

32Your sons and your daughters will be given to another people. Your eyes will look and fail with longing for them all day long. There will be no power in your hand.

Arminian

Children given to another people is perhaps the deepest grief named so far. Arminian readers see how sin’s consequences spread to the next generation, a sobering call to faithfulness for parents.

Reformed

There will be no power in your hand marks total helplessness. Reformed readers note that parents cannot secure their children’s future apart from God’s mercy.

Deuteronomy 28:33

33A nation which you don’t know will eat the fruit of your ground and all of your work. You will only be oppressed and crushed always,

Arminian

A nation you do not know eats your labor. Arminian readers note the reversal of 28:12, where Israel lent to nations; now foreigners consume Israel’s produce.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that oppressed and crushed always describes ongoing suffering, which the prophets later identify with foreign domination. The fruit of their labor goes to strangers, an outcome God had promised to prevent for the obedient.

Deuteronomy 28:34

34so that the sights that you see with your eyes will drive you mad.

Arminian

The sights drive people mad. Arminian readers see how suffering can overwhelm the mind, and they note that naming such distress beforehand was itself a summons to turn back.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the psychological dimension of judgment, linking it with 28:28 and 28:67, where fear and distress grip the heart. Judgment reaches the mind as well as the body.

Deuteronomy 28:35

35The LORD will strike you in the knees and in the legs with a sore boil, of which you cannot be healed, from the sole of your foot to the crown of your head.

Arminian

Incurable boils from sole to crown mark complete affliction. Arminian readers see how judgment can touch the whole person, body and spirit alike. Knees and legs are struck, so even standing and walking become painful.

Reformed

The phrasing matches Job 2:7, though Job suffered without covenant guilt. Reformed readers caution against reading every affliction as punishment, while recognizing this verse as covenant sanction.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
