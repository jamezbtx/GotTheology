Passage

Deuteronomy 28

Verses 36–57 · WEB

The curses move toward exile, foreign domination, and finally siege. Verses 53-57 describe the horror of starvation in a besieged city, where even tender people turn against their own children. Later Scripture records such scenes in sieges of Samaria and Jerusalem (2 Kings 6:28-29; Lamentations 4:10). The text describes judgment; it does not commend what it describes.

Deuteronomy 28:36

36The LORD will bring you, and your king whom you will set over yourselves, to a nation that you have not known, you nor your fathers. There you will serve other gods of wood and stone.

Arminian

A king set over yourselves goes into exile with the people. Arminian readers note that even institutions Israel itself chose offer no protection when the nation forsakes God.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see this warning fulfilled when kings of Israel and Judah were carried away (2 Kings 17; 2 Kings 24-25), and they stress God’s sovereignty over rulers and nations.

Deuteronomy 28:37

37You will become an astonishment, a proverb, and a byword among all the peoples where the LORD will lead you away.

Arminian

Israel becomes a proverb and byword. Arminian readers note that the people meant to display God’s wisdom (4:6) could become an object of mockery. Witness turns into warning.

Reformed

First Kings 9:7 repeats this warning to Solomon. Reformed readers note how covenant curses reach even the house of David. The verse also says the LORD leads them away.

Deuteronomy 28:38

38You will carry much seed out into the field, and will gather little in, for the locust will consume it.

Arminian

Much seed, little harvest. Arminian readers see how unfaithfulness can bring frustration in work that God would otherwise bless. The farmer’s hope at sowing is mocked at harvest, a picture of effort without blessing.

Reformed

Locusts consume the crop, an image the prophet Joel later develops (Joel 1:4). Reformed readers see God’s control over even small creatures. Locusts obey their Maker.

Deuteronomy 28:39

39You will plant vineyards and dress them, but you will neither drink of the wine, nor harvest, because worms will eat them.

Arminian

Vineyards planted and dressed but no wine. Arminian readers note the bitter waste of effort when God’s blessing is withdrawn. Diligent care cannot replace the blessing only God gives.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with the prophets’ use of vineyard imagery for Israel’s fruitlessness (Isaiah 5:1-7). Here worms, not locusts, destroy the vines, showing judgment can come through the smallest means.

Deuteronomy 28:40

40You will have olive trees throughout all your borders, but you won’t anoint yourself with the oil, for your olives will drop off.

Arminian

Olive trees everywhere but no oil. Arminian readers note that abundance of resources does not guarantee enjoyment without God’s favor. Anointing with oil was a sign of gladness; its loss marks a joyless life.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that the sequence of seed, vine, and olive covers Israel’s main crops, so no part of life is exempt. Each crop fails in turn.

Deuteronomy 28:41

41You will father sons and daughters, but they will not be yours, for they will go into captivity.

Arminian

Sons and daughters go into captivity. Arminian readers see the generational cost of national sin, a strong motive for faithful living. Children born in hope are lost to captors, reversing the blessing of 28:4.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that captivity of children fulfills the warning of exile, and that God’s mercy later promised return (30:3-4). Judgment is real, yet not final.

Deuteronomy 28:42

42Locusts will consume all of your trees and the fruit of your ground.

Arminian

Locusts return to finish what 28:38 began, now taking the trees as well. Arminian readers note that the repetition intensifies the warning, as if Moses pleads with Israel by piling up images of loss.

Reformed

The verse says the locusts will possess the trees and fruit. Reformed readers see even swarming insects as agents under God’s command, sent or restrained by his providence.

Deuteronomy 28:43

43The foreigner who is among you will mount up above you higher and higher, and you will come down lower and lower.

Arminian

The foreigner among you rises higher and higher while Israel sinks. Arminian readers note that the resident outsider, whom Israel was told to protect (27:19), now overtakes a people who abandoned covenant justice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God reversing the order he had established in 28:1, showing that Israel’s high position was always his gift and never a natural right.

Deuteronomy 28:44

44He will lend to you, and you won’t lend to him. He will be the head, and you will be the tail.

Arminian

He will lend to you mirrors 28:12-13 closely, with head and tail exchanged. Arminian readers see the two destinies placed side by side so Israel can see where each road leads.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God assigns the rank of nations, so Israel’s fall in standing is his judicial act rather than mere economic misfortune. Commerce follows covenant.

Deuteronomy 28:45

45All these curses will come on you, and will pursue you and overtake you, until you are destroyed, because you didn’t listen to the LORD your God’s voice, to keep his commandments and his statutes which he commanded you.

Arminian

All these curses will overtake you because you didn’t listen. Arminian readers stress the explicit cause-and-effect link between refusal and judgment, which preserves God’s justice and Israel’s responsibility.

Reformed

The verse summarizes the section in language matching 28:15. Reformed readers see the curse pursuing and overtaking as the mirror of the blessings in 28:2, both administered by God.

Deuteronomy 28:46

46They will be for a sign and for a wonder to you and to your offspring forever.

Arminian

The curses become a sign and a wonder to Israel and its offspring forever. Arminian readers see an ongoing warning meant to call later generations to repentance.

Reformed

Signs and wonders often describe God’s acts in the exodus (4:34). Reformed readers note the sober reversal: God’s mighty acts now testify against his people rather than for them.

Deuteronomy 28:47

47Because you didn’t serve the LORD your God with joyfulness and with gladness of heart, by reason of the abundance of all things;

Arminian

Israel did not serve the LORD with joyfulness and gladness amid abundance. Wesleyan readers highlight that God desires glad, wholehearted love, not grudging compliance, and that ingratitude in prosperity is itself a serious sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the heart’s posture matters: serving without joy in the midst of God’s gifts reveals a deeper failure. Many connect this with Psalm 100:2, serve the LORD with gladness.

Deuteronomy 28:48

48therefore you will serve your enemies whom the LORD sends against you, in hunger, in thirst, in nakedness, and in lack of all things. He will put an iron yoke on your neck until he has destroyed you.

Arminian

Those who would not serve God gladly will serve enemies in hunger and want. Arminian readers see a tragic exchange: freedom under God given up for bondage under others.

Reformed

The iron yoke recalls the iron furnace of Egypt (4:20). Reformed readers see the reversal of redemption, which only God’s renewed mercy can undo. Hunger and nakedness complete the picture.

Deuteronomy 28:49

49The LORD will bring a nation against you from far away, from the end of the earth, as the eagle flies: a nation whose language you will not understand,

Arminian

A nation from far away, swift as an eagle, with an unknown language. Arminian readers avoid fixing a single referent and note how the warning fits more than one later invader.

Reformed

Interpreters have connected this nation with Assyria, Babylon, or Rome; Habakkuk 1:8 uses eagle imagery for the Chaldeans. Reformed readers generally see God raising up nations as instruments of judgment, without pressing one identification.

Deuteronomy 28:50

50a nation of fierce facial expressions, that doesn’t respect the elderly, nor show favor to the young.

Arminian

A nation without respect for old or young shows judgment by a ruthless people. Arminian readers note the contrast with God’s law, which honored the elderly and protected the weak.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God permitting a merciless instrument while remaining just himself, a tension the prophet Habakkuk wrestles with (Habakkuk 1:12-13). God’s use of such a nation does not make its cruelty right.

Deuteronomy 28:51

51They will eat the fruit of your livestock and the fruit of your ground, until you are destroyed. They also won’t leave you grain, new wine, oil, the increase of your livestock, or the young of your flock, until they have caused you to perish.

Arminian

The invader eats grain, wine, oil, and herds until Israel perishes. Arminian readers note that these are the very gifts listed in 7:13 as blessings of covenant love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the withdrawal of 7:13’s blessings as the covenant working in reverse, a just outcome of Israel’s rebellion. The gifts of covenant love are withdrawn one by one.

Deuteronomy 28:52

52They will besiege you in all your gates until your high and fortified walls in which you trusted come down throughout all your land. They will besiege you in all your gates throughout all your land which the LORD your God has given you.

Arminian

Israel trusted in high, fortified walls. Arminian readers stress that misplaced trust in human defenses rather than in God is exposed when the walls come down.

Reformed

The repetition of in all your gates emphasizes a nationwide siege. Reformed readers see the land God gave becoming the place of judgment, underscoring the seriousness of covenant breaking.

Deuteronomy 28:53

53You will eat the fruit of your own body, the flesh of your sons and of your daughters, whom the LORD your God has given you, in the siege and in the distress with which your enemies will distress you.

Arminian

The verse describes the horror of starvation in siege, where parents eat their children. Arminian readers treat it soberly as a warning of how far human desperation sinks when God’s protection is withdrawn, not as a scene to dwell on.

Reformed

Scripture records such horrors in sieges of Samaria and Jerusalem (2 Kings 6:28-29; Lamentations 4:10; compare Jeremiah 19:9). Reformed readers see God’s word fulfilled while stressing that the text describes judgment and does not approve the acts.

Deuteronomy 28:54

54The man who is tender among you, and very delicate, his eye will be evil toward his brother, toward the wife whom he loves, and toward the remnant of his children whom he has remaining,

Arminian

Even the tender, refined man turns cold toward brother, wife, and remaining children. Arminian readers note how extreme suffering can erode natural affection, showing why Moses pleads for faithfulness now.

Reformed

His eye will be evil describes a grudging, hostile look. Reformed readers see in this the depth of human corruption exposed when restraint is removed, which many connect with the doctrine of total depravity.

Deuteronomy 28:55

55so that he will not give to any of them of the flesh of his children whom he will eat, because he has nothing left to him, in the siege and in the distress with which your enemy will distress you in all your gates.

Arminian

Refusing to share with his own family shows the breakdown of every bond. Arminian readers note that the curse destroys community as well as bodies, the opposite of the covenant meal of 27:7.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that the verse closes with the refrain in the siege and in the distress, emphasizing the enemy’s pressure and God’s judgment working through it.

Deuteronomy 28:56

56The tender and delicate woman among you, who would not venture to set the sole of her foot on the ground for delicateness and tenderness, her eye will be evil toward the husband that she loves, toward her son, toward her daughter,

Arminian

The delicate woman who would not set her foot on the ground is reduced to desperation. Arminian readers see how no class or comfort shields anyone when the nation turns from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the reversal: the most sheltered become the most desperate. Lamentations 4:10 grieves over compassionate women brought to such acts. The verse is a lament, not a spectacle.

Deuteronomy 28:57

57toward her young one who comes out from between her feet, and toward her children whom she bears; for she will eat them secretly for lack of all things in the siege and in the distress with which your enemy will distress you in your gates.

Arminian

Even the newborn is not spared in the secret desperation of famine. Arminian readers handle the verse with restraint, seeing in it the gravest warning of how sin and judgment destroy the most basic human bonds.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the verse soberly as the climax of the siege curse, a picture of the full horror of covenant judgment, and they point to God’s mercy promised beyond it (30:1-3).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
