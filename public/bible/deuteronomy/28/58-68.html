Passage

Deuteronomy 28

Verses 58–68 · WEB

The final unit restates the condition, fear of the LORD’s glorious name, and climaxes in scattering among the nations and an anxious life without rest. It ends with a reversal of the exodus itself: being taken back to Egypt, where no one will even buy them as slaves.

Deuteronomy 28:58

58If you will not observe to do all the words of this law that are written in this book, that you may fear this glorious and fearful name, THE LORD your God,

Arminian

The final section restates the condition: observing all the words written in this book. Arminian readers note that the warning keeps Israel’s choice central to the very end and treats the people as genuinely able, by grace, to heed it.

Reformed

The glorious and fearful name, printed THE LORD in the WEB, is the object of the reverence God seeks. Reformed readers see the fear of God’s name as the heart of true religion and the source from which obedience flows.

Deuteronomy 28:59

59then the LORD will make your plagues and the plagues of your offspring fearful, even great plagues, and of long duration, and severe sicknesses, and of long duration.

Arminian

Plagues of long duration describe judgment that does not quickly lift. Arminian readers note the long patience God showed beforehand, and the sober cost when a people persistently exhausts it.

Reformed

The verse makes the LORD the subject who renders the plagues fearful. Reformed readers see even prolonged sickness placed under God’s control, so that suffering is never outside his purpose.

Deuteronomy 28:60

60He will bring on you again all the diseases of Egypt, which you were afraid of; and they will cling to you.

Arminian

The diseases of Egypt that Israel dreaded will cling to them. Arminian readers note the direct reversal of 7:15, where God promised to keep these very diseases from an obedient people.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the return of Egypt’s diseases as another sign of the exodus undone, preparing for the final image of 28:68 and underscoring the gravity of covenant breaking.

Deuteronomy 28:61

61Also every sickness and every plague which is not written in the book of this law, the LORD will bring them on you until you are destroyed.

Arminian

Even sicknesses not written in this book will come. Arminian readers take the list as illustrative rather than exhaustive, so that no one can calculate the limits of judgment or presume on escaping it.

Reformed

The phrase the book of this law treats Moses’ words as a written, binding document. Reformed readers often note this early witness to covenant Scripture as an authoritative written standard.

Deuteronomy 28:62

62You will be left few in number, even though you were as the stars of the sky for multitude, because you didn’t listen to the LORD your God’s voice.

Arminian

Once as many as the stars, Israel will be left few. Arminian readers see the promise to Abraham (Genesis 15:5; Deuteronomy 10:22) shrinking in experience when a generation refuses to listen.

Reformed

Left few in number still implies a remnant. Reformed readers often stress that God’s promise to Abraham is not canceled by judgment, since a surviving people remains for restoration (30:1-5).

Deuteronomy 28:63

63It will happen that as the LORD rejoiced over you to do you good, and to multiply you, so the LORD will rejoice over you to cause you to perish and to destroy you. You will be plucked from the land that you are going in to possess.

Arminian

The LORD will rejoice over you to destroy you is a startling phrase. Arminian readers set it beside Ezekiel 33:11, where God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked, and commonly read it as God’s settled approval of his own just judgment rather than delight in suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm Ezekiel 33:11 and often explain the verse as God rejoicing in the vindication of his justice and holiness. Interpreters in both traditions acknowledge the tension and handle it with care.

Deuteronomy 28:64

64The LORD will scatter you among all peoples, from one end of the earth to the other end of the earth. There you will serve other gods which you have not known, you nor your fathers, even wood and stone.

Arminian

Scattering to the ends of the earth leads to serving gods of wood and stone. Arminian readers see a tragic irony: idolatry freely chosen in the land becomes idolatry imposed in exile.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the dispersion is God’s own act, the LORD will scatter you, and they note that his later promise to gather from the ends of heaven answers it (30:4).

Deuteronomy 28:65

65Among these nations you will find no ease, and there will be no rest for the sole of your foot; but the LORD will give you there a trembling heart, failing of eyes, and pining of soul.

Arminian

No ease and no rest for the sole of the foot reverses the rest promised in the land (12:9-10). Arminian readers see restlessness as the condition of a people who have turned from the source of rest.

Reformed

A trembling heart, failing eyes, and pining soul are said to be given by the LORD. Reformed readers see an inward judgment, God handing a rebellious people over to the anxiety that follows unbelief.

Deuteronomy 28:66

66Your life will hang in doubt before you. You will be afraid night and day, and will have no assurance of your life.

Arminian

Life hanging in doubt, with no assurance, describes existence without security. Arminian readers often contrast it with the confidence God offers those who trust and walk with him.

Reformed

Reformed readers often note that the absence of assurance here is national and temporal, a covenant curse on life in exile, distinct from the assurance of salvation discussed in the New Testament.

Deuteronomy 28:67

67In the morning you will say, “I wish it were evening!” and at evening you will say, “I wish it were morning!” for the fear of your heart which you will fear, and for the sights which your eyes will see.

Arminian

Wishing for evening in the morning and morning in the evening captures sleepless anguish. Arminian readers see the human cost of fear when every hour brings dread rather than hope.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the fear arises from the sights your eyes will see, linking it to 28:34, and they see God’s judgment reaching even the experience of time.

Deuteronomy 28:68

68The LORD will bring you into Egypt again with ships, by the way of which I told you that you would never see it again. There you will offer yourselves to your enemies for male and female slaves, and nobody will buy you.

Arminian

Being taken back to Egypt by ships undoes the exodus itself; there Israel will offer itself as slaves and no one will buy. Arminian readers see a final warning that redemption can be squandered by a people who refuse God, while later chapters still hold out return (30:1-10).

Reformed

The way I told you that you would never see again recalls 17:16. Some interpreters connect the verse with later events, such as the flight to Egypt in Jeremiah 42-44; Reformed readers commonly read it primarily as the full reversal of redemption, answered only by God’s renewed mercy.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
