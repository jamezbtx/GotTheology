Passage

Deuteronomy 21

Verses 18–23 · WEB

This section contains two difficult laws. In the first, parents who have repeatedly disciplined a stubborn, rebellious son, described as a glutton and a drunkard, bring him to the elders at the city gate, and the men of the city put him to death (verses 18–21). Even in Israel’s law, the decision is taken from the parents alone and placed with the public elders, and both parents must testify. Scripture records no instance of this law being carried out, though that silence cannot settle how it was applied. The second law requires that the body of an executed man hung on a tree be buried the same day, since one hanged is cursed by God and the land must not be defiled (verses 22–23). Paul applies this verse to Christ, who became a curse for his people (Galatians 3:13). Both traditions read these laws within Israel’s covenant setting, not as civil code for today, and both see the cross as the place where the curse is borne.

Deuteronomy 21:18

18If a man has a stubborn and rebellious son who will not obey the voice of his father or the voice of his mother, and though they chasten him, will not listen to them,

Arminian

A son is stubborn and rebellious, refusing to obey his father and mother even after discipline. The case assumes persistent rebellion, not a single offense.

Arminian readers note that the parents have already sought to correct him; his rebellion is his own continued choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law takes seriously the command to honor parents (Exodus 20:12), which Paul repeats as the first commandment with a promise (Ephesians 6:2).

Both traditions read the law as addressing an extreme case within Israel’s covenant life, not as a pattern for families today.

Deuteronomy 21:19

19then his father and his mother shall take hold of him and bring him out to the elders of his city and to the gate of his place.

Arminian

His father and mother bring him to the elders at the city gate. The decision is taken out of the parents’ hands alone.

Arminian readers note that the law requires both parents to act together and submits the matter to public judgment, restraining private anger.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law places the case under the community’s ordered justice rather than family vengeance. This was a limit on parental power.

Both traditions see wisdom in removing ultimate judgment from a household in crisis, while reading the law as part of Israel’s civil code.

Deuteronomy 21:20

20They shall tell the elders of his city, “This our son is stubborn and rebellious. He will not obey our voice. He is a glutton and a drunkard.”

Arminian

The parents testify that their son is stubborn and rebellious, a glutton and a drunkard. The charge describes a settled pattern of life.

Arminian readers note that the son’s character has been formed by repeated choices, and the law treats the matter as grave.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Proverbs warns against gluttony and drunkenness (compare Proverbs 23:20–21), and that Jesus himself was slandered with the charge of being a gluttonous man and a drunkard (Matthew 11:19).

Both traditions observe that the charge could be misused against the innocent, as it was against Christ.

Deuteronomy 21:21

21All the men of his city shall stone him to death with stones. So you shall remove the evil from among you. All Israel shall hear, and fear.

Arminian

The men of the city stone him, and evil is removed from Israel; all will hear and fear. The penalty is severe.

Arminian readers acknowledge the difficulty of this law and note that Scripture records no example of its being carried out, though that silence cannot prove how it was used.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the penalty within Israel’s unique covenant setting, where rebellion against parents was treated as rebellion against God’s order. They do not treat it as binding civil law today.

Both traditions see the gospel’s welcome of repentant rebels, as in the parable of the prodigal son (Luke 15:11–32), as part of how Christ fulfills the law.

Deuteronomy 21:22

22If a man has committed a sin worthy of death, and he is put to death, and you hang him on a tree,

Arminian

If someone guilty of a capital crime is executed and his body hung on a tree, the law continues with how the body must be treated. Hanging here follows execution.

Arminian readers note that even the condemned are treated with a measure of dignity under God’s law.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the public display of the body marked the seriousness of the crime, yet God limits it. Justice is not permitted to become cruelty.

Both traditions read this as Israel’s civil practice, while seeing in it principles of restraint and respect for the dead.

Deuteronomy 21:23

23his body shall not remain all night on the tree, but you shall surely bury him the same day; for he who is hanged is accursed of God. Don’t defile your land which the LORD your God gives you for an inheritance.

Arminian

The body must be buried the same day, for one who is hanged is cursed by God, and the land must not be defiled. Paul writes that “Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law, having become a curse for us” (Galatians 3:13), citing this verse.

Arminian readers see in Christ’s bearing of the curse God’s provision for all who will believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ bearing the curse in the place of his people, a central text for substitutionary atonement. John 19:31 records concern that the bodies not remain on the cross.

Both traditions confess that Christ became a curse to redeem sinners, and they agree on the heart of this text.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
