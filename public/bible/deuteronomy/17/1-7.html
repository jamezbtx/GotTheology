Passage

Deuteronomy 17

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Deuteronomy 16:18–18:22 gathers Moses’ instructions for the offices that would lead Israel in the land: judges and officers (16:18–17:13), the king (17:14–20), the Levitical priests (18:1–8) and the prophet (18:9–22). Deuteronomy 17:1–7 sits inside the section on justice “in all your gates” (16:18). Verse 1 closes the preceding laws on pure worship by banning defective sacrifices. Verses 2–7 then give the procedure for a case of covenant apostasy, the worship of other gods or the heavenly bodies. The law belongs to Israel’s life as a covenant nation under the LORD in the land, where turning to other gods was treated as treason against Israel’s divine King. It requires diligent inquiry, at least two or three witnesses, and the witnesses’ own accountability before any sentence is carried out.

Deuteronomy 17:1

1You shall not sacrifice to the LORD your God an ox or a sheep in which is a defect or anything evil; for that is an abomination to the LORD your God.

Arminian

This verse completes the run of worship laws in 16:21–17:1. An ox or sheep with “a defect or anything evil” is called “an abomination to the LORD your God.” To offer the LORD a damaged animal treated him as worth less than the best.

Arminian readers see here a call for wholehearted, sincere response. God’s covenant people answer his goodness by bringing him what is truly valuable, not leftovers.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God sets the terms of acceptable worship. What pleases him is defined by his word, not by the worshiper’s convenience or preference.

Shared ground: both traditions read the unblemished sacrifice as part of the Old Testament pattern fulfilled in Christ, who offered himself as a perfect sacrifice, and both see a call to give God our best in worship.

Deuteronomy 17:2

2If there is found among you, within any of your gates which the LORD your God gives you, a man or woman who does that which is evil in the LORD your God’s sight in transgressing his covenant,

Arminian

The case is framed as covenant law: a man or woman “in transgressing his covenant” within the towns “which the LORD your God gives you.” The land is the LORD’s gift, and life in it is bound to loyalty to him. Man and woman are treated alike.

Arminian readers note that the covenant assumes real responsibility. Israelites could keep it or break it, and the law addresses those who actually turned away.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the offense is defined first in relation to God: it is evil “in the LORD your God’s sight.” Apostasy is treason against Israel’s covenant King, not just a private opinion.

Shared ground: both traditions read this in Israel’s specific setting as a theocratic nation in the land. Neither tradition treats it as a pattern for civil penalties in the church age.

Deuteronomy 17:3

3and has gone and served other gods and worshiped them, or the sun, or the moon, or any of the stars of the sky, which I have not commanded,

Arminian

The offense named is serving and worshiping “other gods,” including “the sun, or the moon, or any of the stars of the sky.” Deuteronomy elsewhere warns against worshiping the heavenly bodies (4:19). The phrase “which I have not commanded” marks such worship as something the LORD never authorized.

Arminian readers see in this a defense of the first commandment, which guards Israel’s exclusive relationship with the God who redeemed them.

Reformed

Reformed readers often note that “which I have not commanded” expresses the principle that God alone determines how he is to be worshiped. Worship of creation in place of the Creator is the root of idolatry.

Shared ground: both traditions agree that the sun, moon and stars are created things. To worship them is to give creation the honor owed to God alone.

Deuteronomy 17:4

4and you are told, and you have heard of it, then you shall inquire diligently. Behold, if it is true, and the thing certain, that such abomination is done in Israel,

Arminian

Before any action, the community must “inquire diligently.” Only “if it is true, and the thing certain” may the case go forward. Rumor is not enough; the charge must be investigated and established.

Arminian readers value this as a picture of God’s justice: he does not condemn on hearsay, and his people must take care to protect the innocent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see in this verse the justice of God reflected in Israel’s courts. The law that demands loyalty also demands careful truth-finding, so zeal for God never licenses careless accusation.

Shared ground: both traditions see due process here as part of the law’s righteousness. The same concern for verified truth is present in 13:14 and 19:18.

Deuteronomy 17:5

5then you shall bring out that man or that woman who has done this evil thing to your gates, even that same man or woman; and you shall stone them to death with stones.

Arminian

Once the charge is proven, the offender is brought “to your gates,” the place of public judgment, and stoned. The penalty is severe because the offense struck at the covenant that constituted Israel as the LORD’s people.

Arminian readers read this within the covenant setting. The penalty shows how seriously God regarded idolatry within the nation he had set apart for himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the death penalty here as part of Israel’s judicial law, given to Israel as a nation in the land. They distinguish this from the moral law’s abiding call to worship God alone.

Shared ground: both traditions read the passage in its ancient covenant setting. Both see the New Testament church dealing with unrepentant sin through church discipline rather than the sword; Paul applies the refrain of 17:7 in this way: “Put away the wicked man from among yourselves” (1 Corinthians 5:13).

Deuteronomy 17:6

6At the mouth of two witnesses, or three witnesses, he who is to die shall be put to death. At the mouth of one witness he shall not be put to death.

Arminian

“At the mouth of one witness he shall not be put to death.” Two or three witnesses are required, a rule repeated for all cases in 19:15. It guards against false accusation and personal vendetta.

Arminian readers note that the rule protects the accused and reflects God’s concern for fairness toward every person.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the rule carries forward into the New Testament. Jesus applies it to church discipline (Matthew 18:16), and Paul writes, “At the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word be established” (2 Corinthians 13:1).

Shared ground: both traditions treat the two-or-three-witness rule as an enduring principle of just judgment, and both note Hebrews 10:28, which recalls that under Moses’ law a man died “on the word of two or three witnesses.”

Deuteronomy 17:7

7The hands of the witnesses shall be first on him to put him to death, and afterward the hands of all the people. So you shall remove the evil from among you.

Arminian

The witnesses must cast the first stones, then all the people. Those who testify bear the weight of the sentence they bring about, which discourages false testimony. The refrain “So you shall remove the evil from among you” recurs in Deuteronomy (compare 13:5).

Arminian readers see in the shared action the whole community’s responsibility for keeping covenant faithfulness in Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the community’s holiness is at stake. Israel, as the LORD’s people, must not tolerate open rebellion against him in its midst.

Shared ground: both traditions read the refrain as concern for the purity of God’s people. Both see Paul’s use of it in 1 Corinthians 5:13 as applying the principle to church discipline, not to civil execution.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
