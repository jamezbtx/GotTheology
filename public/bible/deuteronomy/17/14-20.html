Passage

Deuteronomy 17

Verses 14–20 · WEB

Deuteronomy 17:14–20 looks ahead to Israel’s life in the land and anticipates the people’s request for a king “like all the nations” (17:14; compare 1 Samuel 8:5, 20). Moses neither commands nor forbids kingship outright; he regulates it. The king must be chosen by the LORD and be an Israelite (17:15). He must not multiply horses, wives, or silver and gold (17:16–17), and he must write and read a copy of the law all his life (17:18–19). Israel’s king is to be a brother under the law, not a ruler above it (17:20). Later narratives, notably Solomon’s reign (1 Kings 10:26–11:4), are told against this standard.

Deuteronomy 17:14

14When you have come to the land which the LORD your God gives you, and possess it and dwell in it, and say, “I will set a king over me, like all the nations that are around me,”

Arminian

Moses anticipates the day when Israel will say, “I will set a king over me, like all the nations that are around me.” The wording is echoed when the people later demand a king “like all the nations” (1 Samuel 8:5, 20).

Arminian readers note that God anticipates a real human choice and makes provision for it. He regulates kingship rather than simply forbidding it.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that God foresees Israel’s request and governs it in advance. Even a desire that has mixed motives is brought under God’s rule and law.

Shared ground: both traditions read the law as permitting kingship while warning against modeling it on the nations. Both note that 1 Samuel 8:7 presents the people’s later demand as a rejection of the LORD as king over them.

Deuteronomy 17:15

15you shall surely set him whom the LORD your God chooses as king over yourselves. You shall set as king over you one from among your brothers. You may not put a foreigner over you, who is not your brother.

Arminian

The king must be one “whom the LORD your God chooses,” and he must come “from among your brothers,” not a foreigner. Israel’s king is a fellow member of the covenant people, under the same God.

Arminian readers see God’s choice working together with the people’s act: they “set” him as king, but only the one the LORD chooses.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the LORD’s choice comes first. The people set over themselves only the king God has chosen, as later with Saul and David.

Shared ground: both traditions affirm that the LORD chooses Israel’s king and that the people are to receive him. Here the difference over how divine choice relates to human action is not raised by the verse, which concerns a public office, not individual salvation.

Deuteronomy 17:16

16Only he shall not multiply horses to himself, nor cause the people to return to Egypt, to the end that he may multiply horses; because the LORD has said to you, “You shall not go back that way again.”

Arminian

The king must not “multiply horses,” or send the people back to Egypt to get them. Horses were chiefly military strength. Reliance on chariot forces could replace trust in the LORD. The quoted words, “You shall not go back that way again,” reflect the LORD’s purpose that Israel not return to Egypt (compare 28:68).

Arminian readers see a warning against trusting human power rather than God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same warning: the king must not build his security on military might or on reversing the exodus. Israel’s strength is the LORD who redeemed them.

Shared ground: both traditions note that 1 Kings 10:26–28 records Solomon’s many chariots and horsemen and that “The horses which Solomon had were brought out of Egypt,” which shows the narrative measuring the king by this law.

Deuteronomy 17:17

17He shall not multiply wives to himself, that his heart not turn away. He shall not greatly multiply to himself silver and gold.

Arminian

The king must not multiply wives, “that his heart not turn away,” nor greatly multiply silver and gold. Royal marriages often sealed foreign alliances, and wealth could breed pride and self-reliance.

Arminian readers stress the warning’s focus on the heart. A faithful king could still be led astray, so he must guard himself against what would draw him from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the heart and see the warning fulfilled in Solomon: “His wives turned his heart away” (1 Kings 11:3), “after other gods” (11:4).

Shared ground: both traditions read the verse as a warning that power, pleasure and wealth can turn even a gifted leader from wholehearted devotion to the LORD.

Deuteronomy 17:18

18It shall be, when he sits on the throne of his kingdom, that he shall write himself a copy of this law in a book, out of that which is before the Levitical priests.

Arminian

When the king takes the throne, he must write “a copy of this law in a book,” taken from the copy kept by the Levitical priests. The Greek translation (the Septuagint) rendered “copy of this law” with a phrase from which the English name Deuteronomy (meaning second law) comes.

Arminian readers see the king’s first duty as personal engagement with God’s word, not merely holding power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the king as placed under the written law from his first day. Scripture, kept by the priests, governs the throne; the throne does not govern Scripture.

Shared ground: both traditions value this verse as showing the central place of God’s written word in the life of leaders and people. Compare 1 Samuel 10:25, where Samuel wrote down “the regulations of the kingdom.”

Deuteronomy 17:19

19It shall be with him, and he shall read from it all the days of his life, that he may learn to fear the LORD his God, to keep all the words of this law and these statutes, to do them;

Arminian

The law must be with the king, and he must “read from it all the days of his life, that he may learn to fear the LORD his God.” Reading is meant to shape reverence and practical obedience.

Arminian readers see Scripture as a means by which God draws and strengthens a person who responds to his word in faith and obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Scripture as a means of grace by which God forms his servants. Lifelong reading is how the king learns to fear the LORD.

Shared ground: both traditions affirm that regular, lifelong reading of God’s word cultivates the fear of the LORD and faithful obedience, for rulers and ordinary believers alike.

Deuteronomy 17:20

20that his heart not be lifted up above his brothers, and that he not turn away from the commandment to the right hand, or to the left, to the end that he may prolong his days in his kingdom, he and his children, in the middle of Israel.

Arminian

The reading has two goals: “that his heart not be lifted up above his brothers,” and that he not turn from the commandment “to the right hand, or to the left.” The king remains a brother, under the same law as everyone. Faithfulness is tied to the continuance of his dynasty “in the middle of Israel.”

Arminian readers note that the promise of a long reign is conditioned on the king’s faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the conditional promise of dynasty and connect it with the later covenant with David. They note that Israel’s kings repeatedly failed this standard, which pointed to the need for a righteous King.

Shared ground: both traditions see the law as forbidding pride in rulers. Both see Jesus as the King who fulfills the ideal of a ruler wholly devoted to God’s word and humble toward his brothers.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
