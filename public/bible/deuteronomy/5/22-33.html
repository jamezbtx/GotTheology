Passage

Deuteronomy 5

Verses 22–33 · WEB

Moses recalls the people’s response at Horeb: overwhelmed by the fire, cloud and voice, they asked him to hear the rest of God’s words and relay them, promising to listen and obey. The LORD approves their request and expresses a longing that they would have such a heart to fear him and keep his commandments always, for their own good and their children’s. Moses then receives the further commandments to teach, and the section closes with a call to walk in all God’s ways so that the people may live and prosper in the land. Both traditions commonly read the passage as showing the goodness of God’s law, the need for a mediator (compare Hebrews 12:18–24), and the need for a changed heart. They differ mainly on how to understand God’s expressed longing in verse 29 alongside his later promise to give a new heart (Deuteronomy 30:6).

Deuteronomy 5:22

22The LORD spoke these words to all your assembly on the mountain out of the middle of the fire, of the cloud, and of the thick darkness, with a great voice. He added no more. He wrote them on two stone tablets, and gave them to me.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that God himself spoke these words to the whole assembly and wrote them on stone, adding no more. The Decalogue stands apart for its directness and permanence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the writing on stone tablets as marking the enduring character of these commandments (compare Exodus 32:16). Readers in both traditions commonly see the Ten Commandments as a summary of God’s moral will.

Deuteronomy 5:23

23When you heard the voice out of the middle of the darkness, while the mountain was burning with fire, you came near to me, even all the heads of your tribes, and your elders;

Arminian

Arminian readers see the leaders and elders coming to Moses as an honest response to overwhelming holiness. The people recognize their need for help.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fire and darkness as displaying God’s awesome majesty. Both traditions commonly read the verse as preparing for the people’s request for a mediator.

Deuteronomy 5:24

24and you said, “Behold, the LORD our God has shown us his glory and his greatness, and we have heard his voice out of the middle of the fire. We have seen today that God does speak with man, and he lives.

Arminian

Arminian readers note the people’s amazement that God speaks with humans and they live. God’s self-revelation is an act of grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the people acknowledging God’s glory and greatness in his word. Readers in both traditions commonly see this as a true insight: God reveals himself and preserves those to whom he speaks.

Deuteronomy 5:25

25Now therefore, why should we die? For this great fire will consume us. If we hear the LORD our God’s voice any more, then we shall die.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the people’s fear of death as a sober awareness of human frailty before God. They do not presume on his mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fear of being consumed as fitting for sinners before a holy God. Both traditions commonly see the need for mediation arising from this fear (compare Deuteronomy 18:16).

Deuteronomy 5:26

26For who is there of all flesh who has heard the voice of the living God speaking out of the middle of the fire, as we have, and lived?

Arminian

Arminian readers note the people’s sense of privilege at having heard the living God and survived. Their experience was unique among the nations.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the verse as stressing the wonder of God’s self-revelation to Israel. Readers in both traditions commonly read it as an expression of awe, not mere terror.

Deuteronomy 5:27

27Go near, and hear all that the LORD our God shall say, and tell us all that the LORD our God tells you; and we will hear it, and do it.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see the people’s promise to hear and do as a sincere commitment. Later events show how hard it is to keep such a promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the promise as well-intended but soon broken, revealing the weakness of human resolve. Both traditions commonly see here a lesson about the need for divine help to obey.

Deuteronomy 5:28

28The LORD heard the voice of your words when you spoke to me; and the LORD said to me, “I have heard the voice of the words of this people which they have spoken to you. They have well said all that they have spoken.

Arminian

Arminian readers find it striking that God approves of the people’s words. He takes their request seriously and responds graciously.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God affirming the rightness of their request for a mediator. Readers in both traditions commonly see in this approval a link to the promise of a prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15–18; compare Acts 3:22).

Deuteronomy 5:29

29Oh that there were such a heart in them that they would fear me and keep all my commandments always, that it might be well with them and with their children forever!

Arminian

Arminian readers hear in “Oh that there were such a heart in them” God’s sincere longing for his people’s obedience and good, a longing that people can disappoint. They commonly connect it with texts in which God desires that all turn and live (Ezekiel 18:23; 2 Peter 3:9).

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the same sincere longing and commonly read it alongside God’s promise to circumcise his people’s hearts (Deuteronomy 30:6) and the observation that he had not yet given them a heart to know (Deuteronomy 29:4). Many distinguish God’s revealed desire from his decision to grant renewing grace, while affirming that the people are responsible for their hearts.

Key contrast Both traditions generally affirm that God sincerely desires his people’s obedience and that their failure is truly their own. Arminian readers commonly see this longing as showing that God’s grace can be resisted, while Reformed readers commonly see it as pointing to the new heart God himself must give, distinguishing his revealed desire from his purpose to renew.

Deuteronomy 5:30

30“Go tell them, ‘Return to your tents.’

Arminian

Arminian readers see God sending the people back to their tents as granting their request. He meets them where they are.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the instruction as confirming Moses’ role as mediator. Both traditions commonly read the verse as a simple narrative step that underlines God’s accommodation to human frailty.

Deuteronomy 5:31

31But as for you, stand here by me, and I will tell you all the commandments, and the statutes, and the ordinances, which you shall teach them, that they may do them in the land which I give them to possess.”

Arminian

Arminian readers note that Moses stays with God to receive the commandments he will teach. Teaching follows from listening to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses as the appointed channel of God’s further law (compare John 1:17). Readers in both traditions commonly see the verse as establishing the authority of the teaching that fills Deuteronomy.

Deuteronomy 5:32

32You shall observe to do therefore as the LORD your God has commanded you. You shall not turn away to the right hand or to the left.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the call not to turn to the right or left as a call to steady, chosen faithfulness. The path is clear, and the people must keep to it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same call as describing a life shaped wholly by God’s word. Both traditions commonly read the image as a warning against both adding to and drifting from God’s ways.

Deuteronomy 5:33

33You shall walk in all the way which the LORD your God has commanded you, that you may live and that it may be well with you, and that you may prolong your days in the land which you shall possess.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the promise of life and blessing as an encouragement to walk in God’s ways. Obedience and flourishing belong together in the covenant.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the promises as gracious blessings attached to covenant faithfulness in the land. Readers in both traditions commonly read the verse as describing the fruit of obedience, not the ground of the relationship.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
