Passage

Deuteronomy 3

Verses 12–22 · WEB

Moses recounts how the conquered land east of the Jordan was allotted to Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh (compare Numbers 32), on condition that their fighting men cross over to help their brothers. He then charges Joshua not to fear.

Deuteronomy 3:12

12This land we took in possession at that time: from Aroer, which is by the valley of the Arnon, and half the hill country of Gilead with its cities, I gave to the Reubenites and to the Gadites;

Arminian

Moses’ allotment to Reuben and Gad answers their own request in Numbers 32. Arminian readers note that God works through the choices and desires of his people, shaping their requests into his larger purpose.

Reformed

Moses speaks of giving the land, yet he gives what God first gave Israel. Reformed readers see the human distribution of the inheritance as an outworking of God’s prior grant, administered by his appointed servant.

Deuteronomy 3:13

13and the rest of Gilead, and all Bashan, the kingdom of Og, I gave to the half-tribe of Manasseh—all the region of Argob, even all Bashan. (The same is called the land of Rephaim.

Arminian

The half-tribe of Manasseh receives Bashan, the former kingdom of the giant king. What once intimidated Israel now becomes its inheritance, a reversal that invites grateful obedience rather than pride.

Reformed

The note that Bashan is called the land of Rephaim recalls the fearsome former inhabitants and underlines that the inheritance came by God’s power. Reformed readers often stress that God’s gifts are given to the undeserving.

Deuteronomy 3:14

14Jair the son of Manasseh took all the region of Argob, to the border of the Geshurites and the Maacathites, and called them, even Bashan, after his own name, Havvoth Jair, to this day.)

Arminian

Jair’s naming of the towns after himself records genuine human initiative within God’s gift. Arminian readers appreciate that the narrative honors what particular people did with the opportunities God opened to them.

Reformed

The phrase to this day signals that the narrator writes for later readers who could still see the names. Reformed readers note that God’s acts leave lasting marks in history that call later generations to remember him.

Deuteronomy 3:15

15I gave Gilead to Machir.

Arminian

This brief verse completes the allotment by naming Machir’s clan. Even a short notice reminds readers that each family received a real portion and a real responsibility within the people of God.

Reformed

The terse record of Gilead given to Machir shows God’s care for specific clans within his people. Reformed readers often connect this with the covenant concern for families and generations.

Deuteronomy 3:16

16To the Reubenites and to the Gadites I gave from Gilead even to the valley of the Arnon, the middle of the valley, and its border, even to the river Jabbok, which is the border of the children of Ammon;

Arminian

The careful boundaries, down to the middle of the valley, protect each tribe’s inheritance and limit disputes. Arminian readers see in this God’s concern for justice and order within the community he forms.

Reformed

Precise borders reflect a God who orders his people’s life down to the details. Reformed readers often see such ordering as part of the law’s good use in structuring the life of the covenant community.

Deuteronomy 3:17

17the Arabah also, and the Jordan and its border, from Chinnereth even to the sea of the Arabah, the Salt Sea, under the slopes of Pisgah eastward.

Arminian

The Arabah and the Salt Sea mark the southern reach of the eastern allotment, under the slopes of Pisgah where Moses will later view the land. Arminian readers note the poignant setting for what follows.

Reformed

The mention of Pisgah prepares for Moses’ final view of the land (3:27; 34:1). Reformed readers note how the geography itself frames the coming account of God’s sovereign refusal and gracious provision.

Deuteronomy 3:18

18I commanded you at that time, saying, “The LORD your God has given you this land to possess it. All of you men of valor shall pass over armed before your brothers, the children of Israel.

Arminian

The eastern tribes have received their land, but they must still cross armed for their brothers. Arminian readers find here a picture of grace received that obligates continued faithfulness and solidarity rather than ease.

Reformed

God’s gift precedes the command: the land has been given, and therefore they must go. Reformed readers often see this order, gift then duty, as the grammar of the covenant and of the Christian life.

Deuteronomy 3:19

19But your wives, and your little ones, and your livestock, (I know that you have much livestock), shall live in your cities which I have given you,

Arminian

Moses’ aside about their much livestock is gentle realism; he knows why they wanted this land. Arminian readers note that God accommodates practical needs while holding people to their wider obligations.

Reformed

Families and flocks are settled in cities God has already provided. Reformed readers see God’s provision for the weak and dependent members of his people even while the men go to war.

Deuteronomy 3:20

20until the LORD gives rest to your brothers, as to you, and they also possess the land which the LORD your God gives them beyond the Jordan. Then you shall each return to his own possession, which I have given you.”

Arminian

Rest is promised to all the tribes, not just to those already settled. Arminian readers stress that Israel’s shared inheritance required each part of the people to keep its pledge, which could be broken.

Reformed

Rest given by the LORD becomes a key theme that later Scripture develops (Joshua 21:44; Hebrews 4). Reformed readers often trace this land rest as a type of the fuller rest God secures for his people.

Deuteronomy 3:21

21I commanded Joshua at that time, saying, “Your eyes have seen all that the LORD your God has done to these two kings. So shall the LORD do to all the kingdoms where you go over.

Arminian

Joshua is told to let what his eyes have seen shape his expectations. Arminian readers emphasize that remembered experience strengthens a faith that leaders must actively choose and renew.

Reformed

The promise that the LORD will do the same to all the kingdoms grounds Joshua’s confidence in God’s consistent character and purpose. Reformed readers see past acts as pledges of future faithfulness.

Deuteronomy 3:22

22You shall not fear them; for the LORD your God himself fights for you.”

Arminian

The command not to fear rests on God fighting for Israel. Arminian readers see courage as a responsive trust that Joshua must exercise, enabled by but not replaced by God’s presence.

Reformed

God himself fights for you is the heart of holy war theology in Deuteronomy. Reformed readers stress that victory depends on God’s action, so that his people’s confidence rests on him rather than on their strength.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
