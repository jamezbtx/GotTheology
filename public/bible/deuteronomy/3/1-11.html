Passage

Deuteronomy 3

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Moses continues his retrospective sermon on the plains of Moab, recalling the defeat of Og king of Bashan after Sihon (2:26-37). The victory east of the Jordan becomes a pledge that God will finish what he has begun west of it.

Deuteronomy 3:1

1Then we turned, and went up the way to Bashan. Og the king of Bashan came out against us, he and all his people, to battle at Edrei.

Arminian

Moses retells the battle so that a generation that did not see Egypt can trust God for what lies ahead. Remembered deliverance is meant to nourish a faith that Israel must freely and continually exercise as it advances.

Reformed

Moses recounts the march as God’s ordered history rather than a string of chance encounters. Og comes out to fight, but the narrative has already placed the outcome in God’s hand, as verse 2 makes explicit.

Deuteronomy 3:2

2The LORD said to me, “Don’t fear him; for I have delivered him, with all his people and his land, into your hand. You shall do to him as you did to Sihon king of the Amorites, who lived at Heshbon.”

Arminian

The command not to fear is addressed to people who could still fear and draw back, as the previous generation did at Kadesh (1:26-32). God’s promise invites trust but does not override Israel’s responsibility to answer it.

Reformed

God speaks of the victory as already given before the battle begins: I have delivered him. Reformed readers often see here the pattern in which God’s decree secures the outcome while his people act within it.

Deuteronomy 3:3

3So the LORD our God also delivered into our hand Og, the king of Bashan, and all his people. We struck him until no one was left to him remaining.

Arminian

The phrase the LORD our God also delivered keeps the credit with God while Israel genuinely fights. Arminian readers find in this a cooperative picture in which divine enabling and human action both truly occur.

Reformed

Victory is ascribed first to God, and only then is Israel the subject of the striking. The order of the sentence reflects a common Reformed emphasis that human agency operates within and under divine initiative.

Deuteronomy 3:4

4We took all his cities at that time. There was not a city which we didn’t take from them: sixty cities, all the region of Argob, the kingdom of Og in Bashan.

Arminian

The detailed tally of sixty cities underlines that God’s promise proved reliable in concrete, countable ways. Wesleyan preaching often presses such memories as reasons for present obedience rather than for complacency.

Reformed

The completeness of the conquest, with not a city left untaken, displays a God whose purposes are not partially accomplished. Reformed readers often connect such completeness with God’s faithfulness to finish his work.

Deuteronomy 3:5

5All these were cities fortified with high walls, gates, and bars, in addition to a great many villages without walls.

Arminian

Strong walls and bars did not determine the outcome, a direct answer to the earlier report that frightened Israel at Kadesh (1:28). Fear of human strength is shown to be unbelief that Israel could and should have resisted.

Reformed

The fortifications magnify the victory: what looked impregnable fell because God had given it. The verse reinforces that God’s people do not measure his promises by the strength of the obstacles in front of them.

Deuteronomy 3:6

6We utterly destroyed them, as we did to Sihon king of Heshbon, utterly destroying every inhabited city, with the women and the little ones.

Arminian

Arminian interpreters generally treat the total destruction as a unique and limited command tied to this moment in Israel’s history, not a model for later believers. Many also wrestle openly with how it fits God’s love for all peoples, and readings vary.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters often read the ban as God’s judgment on peoples whose iniquity had matured (compare Genesis 15:16), carried out under a specific divine command. They too treat it as unrepeatable, and they differ among themselves on how to explain its moral difficulty.

Deuteronomy 3:7

7But all the livestock, and the plunder of the cities, we took for plunder for ourselves.

Arminian

The livestock and goods taken as plunder show that the ban here was applied differently from Jericho, where all was devoted (Joshua 6). The variation reminds readers to attend to each command in its setting.

Reformed

God provides for his people out of the spoils of those he judges. Reformed readers sometimes note in this the theme of God transferring the wealth of the nations to his people as part of fulfilling his promise.

Deuteronomy 3:8

8We took the land at that time out of the hand of the two kings of the Amorites who were beyond the Jordan, from the valley of the Arnon to Mount Hermon.

Arminian

The geographic summary from Arnon to Hermon marks the extent of the gift God offered and Israel received. Arminian readers note that the land is both granted and taken, a gift that must be laid hold of in obedience.

Reformed

The two kings named together become a recurring reference point in Israel’s memory (4:47; Psalm 135:11; Psalm 136:19-20). Reformed readers see in such repeated remembrance the covenant habit of rehearsing God’s acts as grounds for trust.

Deuteronomy 3:9

9(The Sidonians call Hermon Sirion, and the Amorites call it Senir.)

Arminian

The parenthetical note on the names of Hermon shows a narrator explaining geography to later readers. It invites an Arminian emphasis that Scripture addresses real audiences in their own setting so they can understand and respond.

Reformed

The aside reflects the text’s concern for concrete places and peoples. Reformed readers often value such details as signs that God’s redemption unfolds in actual history, not in myth or abstraction.

Deuteronomy 3:10

10We took all the cities of the plain, and all Gilead, and all Bashan, to Salecah and Edrei, cities of the kingdom of Og in Bashan.

Arminian

Listing the plain, Gilead, and Bashan reinforces that the whole territory promised east of the Jordan came into Israel’s hand. The completeness urges Israel to trust for what remained across the river.

Reformed

The verse closes the conquest account by naming the boundaries of Og’s former kingdom. Reformed readers see the same God who gave this land as able to keep every remaining promise to his people.

Deuteronomy 3:11

11(For only Og king of Bashan remained of the remnant of the Rephaim. Behold, his bedstead was a bedstead of iron. Isn’t it in Rabbah of the children of Ammon? Nine cubits was its length, and four cubits its width, after the cubit of a man.)

Arminian

The iron bedstead, about thirteen by six feet by the cubit in the footnote, is mentioned to show how imposing Og seemed. Some interpreters suggest the term may mean a sarcophagus or ceremonial couch; the text does not settle this, and no claim about its identification is needed to grasp the point.

Reformed

Og is the last of the Rephaim, whose size had frightened Israel (compare 2:10-11, 20-21). The detail magnifies God’s victory over what seemed giant-sized. Reformed readers draw the point without relying on speculative identifications of the object or its location.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
