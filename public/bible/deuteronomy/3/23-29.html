Passage

Deuteronomy 3

Verses 23–29 · WEB

Moses recalls pleading to enter the land and God’s refusal, linked here to the people’s rebellion (compare Numbers 20:12). The leader who will not cross is told to see the land from Pisgah and to strengthen Joshua, who will lead Israel in.

Deuteronomy 3:23

23I begged GOD at that time, saying,

Arminian

Moses’ begging shows that even the greatest servant of God speaks to him with real requests. Arminian readers value this as evidence that prayer is genuine dialogue with a God who listens and responds.

Reformed

Moses prays as a servant before a sovereign Lord. Reformed readers note that prayer is fully appropriate even where God’s answer will be no, since prayer submits our desires to his will.

Deuteronomy 3:24

24“Lord GOD, you have begun to show your servant your greatness, and your strong hand. For what god is there in heaven or in earth that can do works like yours, and mighty acts like yours?

Arminian

Moses’ prayer opens with praise for what God has begun to show. Arminian readers note how he appeals to God’s character and works, inviting God to continue what he started in Moses’ lifetime.

Reformed

The rhetorical question, what god is there like you, confesses God’s incomparability, a theme chapter 4 will develop (4:35, 39). Reformed readers see such praise as the right foundation for every petition.

Deuteronomy 3:25

25Please let me go over and see the good land that is beyond the Jordan, that fine mountain, and Lebanon.”

Arminian

Moses’ longing to see the good land is deeply human and unhidden before God. Arminian readers find comfort that God’s servants may bring their heart’s desires openly, even desires God may decline.

Reformed

Moses asks for something God had already ruled out (Numbers 20:12). Reformed readers see a godly servant still bringing a request, while God’s prior word stands, and they find no contradiction between earnest prayer and divine decree.

Deuteronomy 3:26

26But the LORD was angry with me because of you, and didn’t listen to me. The LORD said to me, “That is enough! Speak no more to me of this matter.

Arminian

Moses says God was angry because of you, linking his exclusion to the people’s provocation as well as his own failure at Meribah. Arminian readers note how leaders bear consequences intertwined with the people they serve.

Reformed

God’s firm refusal, that is enough, shows a sovereign Lord whose decisions are not reversed by pressure. Reformed readers note that God’s no to Moses is not a withdrawal of his favor, as the rest of the passage shows.

Deuteronomy 3:27

27Go up to the top of Pisgah, and lift up your eyes westward, and northward, and southward, and eastward, and see with your eyes; for you shall not go over this Jordan.

Arminian

God denies entry but grants Moses a full view from Pisgah. Arminian readers see mercy within discipline: consequences remain real, yet God does not abandon his servant or withhold all comfort.

Reformed

Moses sees the land in every direction, recalling God’s word to Abraham to look north, south, east, and west (Genesis 13:14). Reformed readers note the covenant continuity, with the promise sure even when the individual does not enter.

Deuteronomy 3:28

28But commission Joshua, and encourage him, and strengthen him; for he shall go over before this people, and he shall cause them to inherit the land which you shall see.”

Arminian

Moses is to commission, encourage, and strengthen Joshua. Arminian readers see leadership as a call to empower others, and they note that Joshua’s courage must still be strengthened and chosen.

Reformed

God provides a successor before Moses dies, so his purpose does not depend on any one servant. Reformed readers often note that God’s work continues through his appointed means and people.

Deuteronomy 3:29

29So we stayed in the valley near Beth Peor.

Arminian

The quiet ending at Beth Peor sets up the warning about Baal Peor in 4:3. Arminian readers note that the very place where Israel once fell becomes the setting for renewed calls to faithfulness.

Reformed

The brief note marks a pause before Moses turns to exhortation. Reformed readers see the location’s associations with Israel’s sin (Numbers 25) as a backdrop that magnifies God’s patience with his people.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
