Passage

Deuteronomy 6

Verses 10–19 · WEB

Moses warns that prosperity in the promised land, with cities, houses, cisterns and vineyards Israel did not build or plant, could lead the people to forget the LORD who brought them out of slavery. Israel must fear and serve him alone, refuse other gods, avoid testing him as at Massah, and do what is right and good in his sight. Jesus answers the tempter in the wilderness with words from this passage (Matthew 4:7, 10). Readers in both traditions commonly find here shared wisdom about the danger of forgetting grace in times of plenty and about exclusive loyalty to God. The warnings are addressed to Israel’s covenant life in the land, and both traditions generally apply them to believers by analogy.

Deuteronomy 6:10

10It shall be, when the LORD your God brings you into the land which he swore to your fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, to give you–a land with great and good cities which you didn’t build,

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the land is a gift promised to the patriarchs, with cities Israel did not build. Blessing comes from God’s faithfulness, not Israel’s merit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the land as the fulfillment of God’s sworn promise to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. Readers in both traditions commonly read the verse as setting up a warning about receiving gifts gratefully.

Deuteronomy 6:11

11and houses full of all good things which you didn’t fill, and cisterns dug out which you didn’t dig, vineyards and olive trees which you didn’t plant, and you shall eat and be full;

Arminian

Arminian readers see the list of things Israel did not fill, dig or plant as emphasizing pure gift. Abundance is meant to lead to gratitude.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s generous provision as grace that calls for thankful remembrance. Both traditions commonly see a lesson for believers who enjoy blessings they did not earn (compare Deuteronomy 8:10).

Deuteronomy 6:12

12then beware lest you forget the LORD, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the warning against forgetting the LORD as recognizing a real danger: prosperity can lead people away from God. Remembering is an active choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the human heart’s tendency to forget grace when life is comfortable (compare Hosea 13:6). Readers in both traditions commonly see this verse as a timeless warning.

Deuteronomy 6:13

13You shall fear the LORD your God; and you shall serve him, and shall swear by his name.

Arminian

Arminian readers see fearing, serving and swearing by the LORD as signs of exclusive loyalty. Jesus quotes this verse in resisting temptation (Matthew 4:10).

Reformed

Reformed readers see reverent fear and service as the proper response to God’s grace. Both traditions commonly see the verse as calling for wholehearted devotion.

Deuteronomy 6:14

14You shall not go after other gods, of the gods of the peoples who are around you,

Arminian

Arminian readers see the ban on following other gods as guarding against the pull of the surrounding nations. Loyalty to God is tested in a pluralistic setting.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command as reaffirming the first commandment in the context of life in the land. Readers in both traditions commonly apply it to modern forms of idolatry.

Deuteronomy 6:15

15for the LORD your God among you is a jealous God, lest the anger of the LORD your God be kindled against you, and he destroy you from off the face of the earth.

Arminian

Arminian readers see God’s jealousy as the protective love of a covenant partner who will not share his people. The warning of judgment is real.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s jealousy as his rightful zeal for his glory and his people. Both traditions commonly see the warning as underlining the seriousness of idolatry.

Deuteronomy 6:16

16You shall not tempt the LORD your God, as you tempted him in Massah.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the warning not to tempt the LORD as recalling Massah, where the people demanded proof of God’s presence (Exodus 17:7). Jesus quotes it in the wilderness (Matthew 4:7).

Reformed

Reformed readers see testing God as distrust that demands he prove himself on human terms. Readers in both traditions commonly see the verse as a call to trust without demanding signs.

Deuteronomy 6:17

17You shall diligently keep the commandments of the LORD your God, and his testimonies, and his statutes, which he has commanded you.

Arminian

Arminian readers see diligent obedience as the fitting response to God’s grace. Care in keeping his word reflects love for him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the commandments, testimonies and statutes as God’s good gifts for ordering life. Both traditions commonly see this diligence as the fruit of a grateful heart (compare 1 John 5:3).

Deuteronomy 6:18

18You shall do that which is right and good in the LORD’s sight, that it may be well with you and that you may go in and possess the good land which the LORD swore to your fathers,

Arminian

Arminian readers see doing what is right and good in God’s sight as tied to blessing in the land. Faithfulness and flourishing go together in the covenant.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the blessings as God’s gracious fulfillment of his oath, enjoyed in the way of obedience. Readers in both traditions commonly read the verse as describing covenant life, not earning God’s favor.

Deuteronomy 6:19

19to thrust out all your enemies from before you, as the LORD has spoken.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the promise to thrust out enemies as God’s commitment to fulfill his word. The people are called to trust and obey.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the LORD’s spoken promise as certain. Both traditions commonly read the verse as part of Israel’s specific conquest setting, applied to believers only by analogy.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
