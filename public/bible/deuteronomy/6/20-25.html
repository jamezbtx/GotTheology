Passage

Deuteronomy 6

Verses 20–25 · WEB

When children ask what the commandments mean, parents are to tell the story of redemption: Israel was enslaved in Egypt, the LORD brought them out with signs and wonders, and he brought them out in order to bring them into the land he had promised. The commandments are given for Israel’s good and life, and faithful obedience “shall be righteousness to us” before the LORD. Arminian and Reformed readers generally share a Protestant reading of this passage: the law is given to a people already redeemed by grace, and obedience is their faithful response, not the ground of their acceptance (compare Ephesians 2:8–10). Readers within each tradition discuss how verse 25 relates to Paul’s teaching on the righteousness of faith (Romans 10:5; Philippians 3:9), but these discussions do not neatly divide Arminian from Reformed.

Deuteronomy 6:20

20When your son asks you in time to come, saying, “What do the testimonies, the statutes, and the ordinances, which the LORD our God has commanded you mean?”

Arminian

Arminian readers value the expectation that children will ask what the commandments mean. Faith grows through honest questions and thoughtful answers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the question as an opportunity for covenant instruction in the home (compare Exodus 12:26–27). Readers in both traditions commonly see this verse as a model for teaching the next generation.

Deuteronomy 6:21

21then you shall tell your son, “We were Pharaoh’s slaves in Egypt. The LORD brought us out of Egypt with a mighty hand;

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the answer begins with the story of slavery and deliverance, not with rules. The commandments are explained by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gospel-shaped order: redemption comes first, then the law. Both traditions commonly treat this as the key to understanding the commandments.

Deuteronomy 6:22

22and the LORD showed great and awesome signs and wonders on Egypt, on Pharaoh, and on all his house, before our eyes;

Arminian

Arminian readers see the signs and wonders as public evidence of God’s power and faithfulness. The family’s faith rests on what God has done.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the judgments on Egypt as God’s mighty acts on behalf of his people. Readers in both traditions commonly see testimony to God’s works as central to passing on faith.

Deuteronomy 6:23

23and he brought us out from there, that he might bring us in, to give us the land which he swore to our fathers.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the striking phrase “that he might bring us in” as showing that deliverance has a purpose. God redeems people for a future with him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s purpose to bring his people into the promised land as part of his faithful plan. Both traditions commonly see redemption and inheritance joined in this verse.

Deuteronomy 6:24

24The LORD commanded us to do all these statutes, to fear the LORD our God, for our good always, that he might preserve us alive, as we are today.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the commandments given “for our good always” and to preserve life. God’s law is a gift, not a burden.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law as a good gift that guides the redeemed into life and blessing (compare Romans 7:12). Readers in both traditions commonly see this verse as rebutting the idea that God’s commands are arbitrary.

Deuteronomy 6:25

25It shall be righteousness to us, if we observe to do all these commandments before the LORD our God, as he has commanded us.”

Arminian

Arminian readers read “it shall be righteousness to us” as describing the covenant faithfulness of a people already redeemed, not as earning salvation. They commonly see such obedience as made possible by grace and as the fitting response to God’s deliverance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the verse as describing the righteousness of covenant faithfulness that God approves in his redeemed people, while holding that acceptance before God rests on grace through faith (compare Genesis 15:6; Philippians 3:9). Readers within each tradition discuss how this verse relates to Paul’s use of the law, but the main reading is generally shared.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
