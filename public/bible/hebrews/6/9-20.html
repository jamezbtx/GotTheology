Passage

Hebrews 6

Verses 9–20 · WEB · Voices

After the severe warning of 6:4–8, the writer turns to confidence. He is persuaded of better things for his readers, things that belong to salvation, because God will not forget their love and service. He urges them to show the same diligence to the end and to imitate those who inherit the promises through faith and patience. Then he grounds their hope in God’s promise to Abraham, confirmed by an oath, so that by two unchangeable things the heirs of the promise have strong encouragement. This hope is a sure and steady anchor, fastened where Jesus has entered as forerunner. Both traditions read this section alongside the warning. They differ on whether the anchor guarantees the outcome or secures those who keep holding on.

Hebrews 6:9

9But, beloved, we are persuaded of better things for you, and things that accompany salvation, even though we speak like this.

Arminian

We are persuaded of better things for you, things that accompany salvation. Arminian readers see pastoral confidence that the readers are genuine and will continue, without cancelling the warning just given.

Reformed

Reformed readers see “things that accompany salvation” as distinguishing true believers from those described in verses 4–8. The writer is confident his readers are in the first group.

Key contrast Both see the writer’s confidence about his readers. Arminian readers see hope that they will continue, while falling away remains possible. Reformed readers see a contrast between true believers and those described in the warning, who never had the things that accompany salvation.

Hebrews 6:10

10For God is not unrighteous, so as to forget your work and the labor of love which you showed toward his name, in that you served the saints, and still do serve them.

Arminian

God is not unjust so as to overlook your work and love for his name. Arminian readers see God honoring the faithful service of believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the readers’ love as evidence of grace, which God, being just to his promise, will not forget.

Hebrews 6:11

11We desire that each one of you may show the same diligence to the fullness of hope even to the end,

Arminian

We want each of you to show the same earnestness to have full assurance of hope until the end. Arminian readers see assurance growing through diligent continuance in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see full assurance as something believers should seek, confirmed by the fruit of grace.

Hebrews 6:12

12that you won’t be sluggish, but imitators of those who through faith and perseverance inherited the promises.

Arminian

Do not be sluggish, but imitate those who through faith and patience inherit the promises. Arminian readers stress that faith and patience are the path to inheritance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faith and patience as the God-given means by which heirs reach their inheritance.

Hebrews 6:13

13For when God made a promise to Abraham, since he could swear by no one greater, he swore by himself,

Arminian

When God made a promise to Abraham, he swore by himself, since he had no one greater to swear by. Arminian readers see God’s faithfulness to keep his word.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God binding himself by oath, showing the unconditional certainty of his promise.

Hebrews 6:14

14saying, “Surely blessing I will bless you, and multiplying I will multiply you.”

Arminian

“Surely I will bless you and multiply you.” Arminian readers see a promise that Abraham received by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign covenant commitment to Abraham and his offspring.

Hebrews 6:15

15Thus, having patiently endured, he obtained the promise.

Arminian

Abraham, having patiently waited, obtained the promise. Arminian readers see Abraham’s patience as a model of the endurance believers must show.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God fulfilling his promise through the patience he gave Abraham.

Hebrews 6:16

16For men indeed swear by a greater one, and in every dispute of theirs the oath is final for confirmation.

Arminian

People swear by something greater, and an oath ends every dispute. Arminian readers see the oath as a human picture of certainty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God condescending to human custom to strengthen faith.

Hebrews 6:17

17In this way God, being determined to show more abundantly to the heirs of the promise the immutability of his counsel, interposed with an oath,

Arminian

God, wanting to show the heirs of the promise the unchangeable character of his purpose, guaranteed it with an oath. Arminian readers see the unchanging purpose as God’s settled plan to save those who believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s unchangeable purpose of election, guaranteed to the heirs by oath.

Key contrast Both affirm that God’s purpose is unchangeable. Arminian readers see it as his purpose to save all who believe in Christ. Reformed readers see it as his purpose to save particular heirs, whose salvation is therefore secure.

Hebrews 6:18

18that by two immutable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we may have a strong encouragement, who have fled for refuge to take hold of the hope set before us.

Arminian

By two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we who have fled for refuge have strong encouragement to hold fast the hope set before us. Arminian readers see strong encouragement for those who keep holding fast.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the promise and oath as two unshakable grounds of assurance for all who have fled to Christ.

Hebrews 6:19

19This hope we have as an anchor of the soul, a hope both sure and steadfast and entering into that which is within the veil,

Arminian

We have this hope as a sure and steadfast anchor of the soul. Arminian readers see the anchor holding firm as long as believers stay attached to it by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the anchor as sure because it is fixed in God’s promise and Christ’s presence, not in human strength.

Key contrast Both see hope as a sure anchor. Arminian readers stress that believers must stay attached to it by continuing faith. Reformed readers stress that it is fixed in God and Christ, so it holds those who belong to him.

Hebrews 6:20

20where as a forerunner Jesus entered for us, having become a high priest forever after the order of Melchizedek.

Arminian

Jesus has gone as a forerunner on our behalf, a high priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. Arminian readers see Jesus going ahead to prepare the way for all who follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a forerunner implying that others will certainly follow where he has gone.

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on better things that belong to salvation, God’s oath and immutable counsel, and hope as an anchor within the veil, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“But, beloved—in this one place he calls them so; he never uses this appellation but in exhorting. We are persuaded of you things that accompany salvation—we are persuaded you are now saved from your sins; and that ye have that faith, love, and holiness, which lead to final salvation.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Heb 6:9, 11)

Reformed Notable voices

“As the preceding sentences were like thunderbolts, it was needful to mitigate this severity. He testifies that he thus warned them because he had a good hope of them.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Heb 6:9, 17–18)
