Passage

Hebrews 6

Verses 1–8 · WEB · Voices

Hebrews 6 holds the letter’s hardest warning. The writer has just told his readers they are slow to learn, and now urges them past the basics toward maturity before describing people who were enlightened, tasted God’s gifts, shared in the Holy Spirit, and then fell away. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that the warning is severe and meant to be felt. They disagree about who is being described: believers who can finally fall, or people whose experience of grace never became saving faith.

Hebrews 6:1

1Therefore leaving the teaching of the first principles of Christ, let’s press on to perfection—not laying again a foundation of repentance from dead works, of faith toward God,

Arminian

The “therefore” ties this call to the rebuke at the end of chapter 5, where the readers still need milk. Leaving the first principles does not mean abandoning them. Wesley read it as saying no more about them for now, the way a builder stops pouring a foundation so the walls can go up. Repentance and faith stay underneath everything that follows.

Arminian readers see growth here as something grace makes possible and God sincerely requires. Lingering in spiritual infancy is not a harmless delay. The writer will soon argue that the way of maturity is also the way believers keep their footing, and that stalling leaves them exposed.

Reformed

Calvin made the same point about the foundation: it is laid once and then built on, not laid over and over. The basic teaching belongs to new converts, and the church should move on to what rests on it. Reformed readers add that the progress the writer calls for is itself God’s work in those he has begun to save.

The call to press on is a command, and it is also one of the means God uses to carry his people forward. Reformed readers therefore treat the warning that follows as part of the way perseverance happens, not as evidence that its outcome is in doubt.

Hebrews 6:2

2of the teaching of baptisms, of laying on of hands, of resurrection of the dead, and of eternal judgment.

Arminian

The list reads like an early church catechism: teaching about washings, the laying on of hands, the resurrection of the dead, and eternal judgment. Wesley connected these with baptism, the gift of the Spirit, and the final judgment. None of them is set aside, because they are the foundation the readers already have.

Arminian readers note the gap between knowing these truths and living by their power. A congregation can recite the basics and still be in danger, which is why the writer moves straight from this list to the warning of verses 4–6.

Reformed

Calvin took the list as the church’s shared catechism, taught once to those newly received. Going back to it as if starting over would be a step backward. The doctrines stay true, but a church that only circles them remains immature.

Reformed readers see the same gospel providing both milk and solid food. God means his people to grow under the word, and the list marks what a mature church should be able to take for granted.

Hebrews 6:3

3This will we do, if God permits.

Arminian

“If God permits” joins human resolve to divine permission. Wesley read the writer as committing himself to press on all the more diligently because of the danger he is about to describe. The effort is real, and God is the one who grants the advance.

Arminian readers do not take the condition as fatalism. It is a call to keep moving while admitting that every step forward is a gift. Neglecting that gift is the danger the next verses describe.

Reformed

Calvin saw the phrase as a reminder that progress in the Christian life is God’s gift and cannot be achieved whenever a person chooses. God gives the growth he commands. The severe words that follow are meant to shake the readers out of complacency.

Reformed readers hold comfort and fear together here. The elect do not make themselves mature, yet the warning is addressed to them so that they will stay watchful.

Hebrews 6:4

4For concerning those who were once enlightened and tasted of the heavenly gift, and were made partakers of the Holy Spirit,

Arminian

The descriptions are strong: once enlightened, having tasted the heavenly gift, made partakers of the Holy Spirit. Wesley understood them as the experience of real believers, including the light of God’s love in Christ, the sweetness of forgiveness, and the witness of the Spirit. Arminian readers resist reducing these phrases to outward contact with the church.

For classical Arminians, this is why the warning carries weight. People who have truly shared in these blessings can still turn away completely. Taking the privileges at face value is what makes the warning pastoral rather than theoretical.

Reformed

Calvin also took the language seriously and did not treat it as describing mere outsiders. He held, however, that God can give some people a measure of light and a real taste of his goodness that never becomes the regenerating work of the Spirit. Their faith is temporary and has no deep root, like the rocky soil in Jesus’ parable.

On this reading, those who finally fall away show that they were never sealed as God’s children, while the elect are kept. The warning remains terrifying, and Reformed readers believe it is meant to drive self-examination and diligence in those who hear it.

Key contrast Both traditions refuse to treat enlightenment and sharing in the Spirit as trivial. Arminian readers see renewing grace from which a person can willfully and totally fall. Reformed readers see a real but non-regenerating experience that some receive and later lose, while the elect cannot finally fall.

Hebrews 6:5

5and tasted the good word of God and the powers of the age to come,

Arminian

To taste the good word of God and the powers of the age to come is to have found Scripture sweet and to have felt something of the world to come. Wesley compared it to a newborn’s first experience of light and nourishment. This describes a living Christian experience, not a bare profession.

Arminian readers stress that those who have known this are exactly the people being warned. The tragedy in view is turning away after having tasted so much.

Reformed

Calvin described the gospel as a sweet testimony of God’s love, through which believers glimpse the kingdom. He recognized that this verse speaks of true knowledge of the gospel, yet he held that for some people even this knowledge can fade.

Reformed readers distinguish tasting from being kept as part of the Father’s gift to the Son (John 6:37–39). The verse makes the warning more severe without implying that everyone who tasted was elect. Greater privilege means greater guilt if the fall is final.

Hebrews 6:6

6and then fell away, it is impossible to renew them again to repentance; seeing they crucify the Son of God for themselves again, and put him to open shame.

Arminian

Wesley read “and then fell away” as a description of something that does happen, not a hypothetical case. Those who deliberately and completely cast off faith, hope, and love cannot be brought back to repentance, even though they were renewed once. By turning away, they side with those who crucified Christ and hold him up to contempt.

Classical Arminians therefore take the impossibility at face value and apply it to people who had believed. Final apostasy after sharing in the Spirit leaves no way back to the foundation. Arminian readers also distinguish this deliberate repudiation from the ordinary failures of struggling Christians, who are invited to repent.

Reformed

Calvin understood the falling away as total defection from the gospel, not ordinary sin or even serious moral failure. Such apostates do not come to true repentance again, because they have been left to a hardened mind. He added that if someone returns after a grievous fall, that return shows they had not committed this kind of apostasy.

Reformed readers usually apply the impossible renewal to hardened apostates who had only a temporary taste of grace. The elect cannot finally fall, and God uses this warning as a means to keep them fearful of sin, watchful, and enduring.

Key contrast Arminian readers see deliberate, total apostasy by someone who had been renewed, with no renewal to repentance afterward. Reformed readers see total defection by people whose experience of grace was temporary, with the elect preserved and any recovery proving that the fall was not this final apostasy. Both keep the warning severe, and both reject the harshness that would refuse repentant sinners.

Hebrews 6:7

7For the land which has drunk the rain that comes often on it and produces a crop suitable for them for whose sake it is also tilled, receives blessing from God;

Arminian

The writer turns to farming. Land that drinks frequent rain and produces a useful crop receives God’s blessing. The rain stands for gospel privileges given again and again, and the crop for the life those privileges are meant to produce.

Arminian readers note that grace is meant to bear fruit. The same rain that blesses one field will, in the next verse, leave another field near a curse if it produces only thorns.

Reformed

Calvin compared the hearers to soil that should let the seed of the word take root without delay. Those who receive it into their hearts and put out healthy shoots will grow to a ripe harvest by God’s blessing.

Reformed readers see the fruitfulness of the land as God’s work in soil he has made fertile. The image urges a glad welcome of the word and distinguishes kinds of ground by what grows, without denying that the rain truly fell on all of it.

Hebrews 6:8

8but if it bears thorns and thistles, it is rejected and near being cursed, whose end is to be burned.

Arminian

Land that produces only thorns and thistles after all that rain is rejected and near to being cursed, and its end is burning. Wesley noted that no more labor is spent on such ground and connected the image with the judgment that soon fell on Jerusalem.

Arminian readers see the outcome of the apostasy described in verses 4–6. Privilege without fruit ends in judgment, so the call is to press on to maturity while the rain is still falling.

Reformed

Calvin observed that a field yielding only thorns after cultivation offers no hope of harvest, and the farmer burns the weeds. Those who choke the seed of the gospel through indifference or corrupt desire show that they are not fruitful ground.

Reformed readers see the warning as a means that separates and preserves. Fruitless professors under abundant rain are near the curse, yet the writer immediately tells the readers he is confident of better things in their case (v. 9).

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on the impossibility of renewing apostates and the fork between falling away and temporary taste, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“It is impossible for those who were once enlightened—with the light of the glorious love of God in Christ. And have tasted the heavenly gift—remission of sins, sweeter than honey and the honeycomb.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Heb 6:4–6)

Reformed Notable voices

“The knot of the question is in the word, fall away. The Apostle speaks not here of theft, or murder, or adultery; but he refers to a total defection from the Gospel, when a sinner entirely renounces his grace.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Heb 6:4–6)
