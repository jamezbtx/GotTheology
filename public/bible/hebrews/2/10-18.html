Passage

Hebrews 2

Verses 10–18 · ESV · Voices

The pioneer of salvation is perfected through suffering as the Father brings many sons to glory: sanctifier and sanctified share one source, so he calls them brothers, trusts the Father, and presents the children given him. Sharing flesh and blood, he destroys the devil’s death-power, frees from lifelong fear, helps Abraham’s offspring—not angels—and becomes a merciful, faithful high priest who propitiates and helps the tempted.

Hebrews 2:10

10For it was fitting that he, for whom and by whom all things exist, in bringing many sons to glory, should make the founder of their salvation perfect through suffering.

Arminian

Wesley: It became him—it was suitable to all his attributes, justice, goodness, and wisdom. For whom—as their ultimate end—and by whom—as their first cause—are all things. In bringing many sons to glory—to perfect the captain of their salvation through sufferings. Captain: prince, leader, and author. Perfect/consummate: bring him to a full and glorious end of all his troubles. What is said of our Lord’s being made perfect through sufferings has no relation to our being saved or sanctified by sufferings as atonement; his atonement and his Spirit carrying on faith’s work alone can sanctify us.

Shared: the pioneer is perfected through suffering; many sons are brought to glory. Arminians gladly confess the fittingness of the Father’s plan without making our pains a second sacrifice.

Reformed

Calvin: that Christ’s humiliation appear glorious to the godly. The best cause is God’s good pleasure—for whom and by whom are all things. It was necessary that Christ, as the first-begotten, should by the cross be inaugurated into his supremacy, since that is the common lot of all; this is the conforming of the head with the members (Rom 8:29). By sorrows the faithful are sanctified for glory as Christ himself was—so they may kiss the cross rather than dread it. τελειῶσαι: consecrated/perfected as captain.

Reformed accent: the King who governs fits the Captain to the many sons by the same cruciform path he has decreed for his family—head and members.

Hebrews 2:11

11For he who sanctifies and those who are sanctified all have one source. That is why he is not ashamed to call them brothers,

Arminian

Wesley: He that sanctifieth—Christ—and they who are sanctified—brought to God—are all of one—partakers of one nature from one parent, Adam. Hence he is not ashamed to call them brethren.

Shared solidarity: sanctifier and sanctified share one human source. Classical Arminians hear genuine fraternity that grounds sympathy (vv. 17–18) without dissolving the distinction between Redeemer and redeemed.

Reformed

Calvin: the author of holiness and we who partake of it are of one nature—out of the same mass. He not only as God sanctifies us; there is also power of sanctifying in his human nature, God having poured upon it a perfect fullness of holiness. He is not ashamed to call them brethren—even when elevated after the resurrection into immortal glory.

Reformed accent: union and shared nature; holiness drawn from the Head. Fraternity is honor conferred by the exalted Christ.

Hebrews 2:12

12saying, “I will tell of your name to my brothers; in the midst of the congregation I will sing your praise.”

Arminian

Wesley: I will declare thy name to my brethren—Christ declares God as gracious and merciful to all who believe. In the midst of the church will I sing praise—as precentor of the choir; he still does this by his word and Spirit.

Shared: the singing Brother leads congregational praise. Psalm 22’s voice is Christ’s among his brethren.

Reformed

Calvin: from Ps 22—Christ is the speaker. Proclaiming God’s name began with the gospel and continues by pastors’ ministry; Christ is author of the gospel. He leads our songs—chief composer of our hymns.

Reformed accent: the King who governs still speaks through gospel ministry; Christ himself is the herald in his disciples.

Hebrews 2:13

13And again, “I will put my trust in him.” And again, “Behold, I and the children God has given me.”

Arminian

Wesley: I will put my trust in him—as one who has communion with his brethren in sufferings as well as nature. Behold I and the children which God hath given me—he presents all believers to God.

Arminian note: children given are believers he presents; trust amid suffering is the Brother’s pattern. Shared: incarnation solidarity.

Reformed

Calvin: trust in God proves he was man exposed to human wants; what is natural to our helplessness he willingly undertook. “Children God has given me”: primary cause of obedience is God’s adoption; Christ brings none to the Father but those given him—this donation depends on eternal election. “All that the Father gives me will come to me” (John 6:37).

Reformed accent: the children are the Father’s gift to the Son—election underwriting the trust and the family.

Key contrast Both hear the Son trusting the Father and naming “children.” Arminians typically emphasize believers presented as brethren who share his trust-path. Reformed readers (Calvin) typically stress that those children are given by the Father’s eternal election—Christ keeps and presents the gifted ones.

Hebrews 2:14

14Since therefore the children share in flesh and blood, he himself likewise partook of the same things, that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil,

Arminian

Wesley: The children partake of flesh and blood—human nature with its infirmities. He likewise took part of the same, that through his own death he might destroy the tyranny of him that had, by God’s permission, the power of death—the devil. Death is the devil’s sergeant delivering those seized in sin.

Shared: real incarnation, real death, devil’s death-power broken. Arminians underline “likewise”—full participation—so help to the tempted is no Docetism.

Reformed

Calvin: the Son of God became man to partake of our condition and by death redeem us from it. The devil’s destruction means he cannot prevail against us; his power to hurt is destroyed or restrained. Power of death is ascribed from the effect—destructive and bringing death. Tyranny abolished so that we may reckon him as though he were not.

Reformed accent: the King who governs dismantles the devil’s reign for his people through the Brother’s death.

Hebrews 2:15

15and deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery.

Arminian

Wesley: deliver them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage. Every man who fears death is subject to bondage; every man fears death who knows not Christ. He delivers all true believers from this bondage.

Arminian pastoral: deliverance is for true believers; lifelong slavery is the pre-Christ condition. The warning passages later still matter—true faith continues.

Reformed

Calvin: miserable are those who fear death apart from Christ—only curse appears, God’s wrath against sin. Christ, undergoing our curse, took away what is dreadful in death. We are not yet freed from dying, yet in life and death we have peace when Christ goes before us. Extreme fear of death is evidence of little proficiency in Christ’s faith.

Reformed accent: effectual deliverance from death’s bondage-fear for those in Christ—peace because the curse-bearer leads.

Hebrews 2:16

16For surely it is not angels that he helps, but he helps the offspring of Abraham.

Arminian

Wesley: He taketh not hold of angels—does not take their nature. He taketh hold of the seed of Abraham—takes human nature; Paul says Abraham’s seed rather than Adam’s because the promise was made to Abraham.

Shared: help is for Abraham’s offspring, not angels. Incarnation targets the promised line.

Reformed

Calvin: he enhances the benefit by comparison—he never did so much for angels. Preference for us was owing to our misery, not our excellency. This overthrows those who deny Christ to be real man of human seed. He names Abraham’s seed for greater credit from Scripture.

Reformed accent: particular help to Abraham’s seed—promise people—shows the King’s discriminating mercy.

Hebrews 2:17

17Therefore he had to be made like his brothers in every respect, so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest in the service of God, to make propitiation for the sins of the people.

Arminian

Wesley: It behoved him to be made like his brethren in all things pertaining to human nature and in all sufferings and temptations—that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest: merciful toward sinners, faithful toward God; to make reconciliation for the people’s sins.

Shared high-priest christology: likeness, mercy, faithfulness, propitiation. Arminians stress the fitness (“behoved”) of full likeness so that mercy is not theoretical.

Reformed

Calvin: real flesh and real human feelings; Christ was made subject to human affections that he might be a merciful and therefore faithful high priest. Mercy is especially required in a priest; experience produces sympathy. He had no need of experience to know mercy, but we could not be persuaded he is ready to help had he not become acquainted by experience with our miseries. He made propitiation for the people’s sins.

Reformed accent: propitiation for the people; the merciful priest is the King’s provision—sympathy grounded in real assumption of our frame.

Hebrews 2:18

18For because he himself has suffered when tempted, he is able to help those who are being tempted.

Arminian

Wesley: In that he hath suffered being tempted, he is able to succour them that are tempted—he has given a demonstrative proof that he is able so to do.

Arminian pastoral: ability to help the tempted is proven in his own suffering. Hearers under pressure (and under 2:1’s warning) have a Brother who can actually help—help to be sought, not presumed while drifting.

Reformed

Calvin: having been tried by our evils, he is ready to bring help. Temptation means experience or probation; to be able is to be fit or inclined. Whenever evils pass over us, nothing happens but what the Son of God has experienced that he might sympathize; he is present as though he suffered with us.

Reformed accent: the King who governs has fitted his priest by temptation’s school—sure help for those being tempted among his people.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the pioneer perfected through suffering, brotherhood, and the merciful high priest—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Hebrews 2:10–18.

Arminian Notable voices

“It became him—it was suitable to all his attributes, both to his justice, goodness, and wisdom. For whom—as their ultimate end—and by whom—as their first cause—are all things, in bringing many sons to glory, to make the captain of their salvation perfect through sufferings. Captain—prince, leader, and author of their salvation. To perfect or consummate implies the bringing him to a full and glorious end of all his troubles. He that sanctifieth and they who are sanctified are all of one—partakers of one nature. Since the children partake of flesh and blood, he also himself likewise took part of the same; that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil, and deliver them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage. He taketh not hold of angels, but of the seed of Abraham. It behoved him to be made like unto his brethren, that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest… to make reconciliation for the sins of the people. For in that he himself hath suffered being tempted, he is able to succour them that are tempted.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Heb 2:10–18)

Reformed Notable voices

“His object is to make Christ’s humiliation appear glorious to the godly. No better cause can be found than the good pleasure of God—for whom and by whom are all things. It was necessary that Christ, as the first-begotten, should by the cross be inaugurated into his supremacy, since that is the common lot of all; this is the conforming of the head with the members. He that sanctifieth and they who are sanctified are all of one—of one nature. He is not ashamed to call them brethren. “Behold, I and the children which God hath given me”: Christ brings none to the Father but those given him by the Father; this donation depends on eternal election. He put on our nature that by death he might redeem us from it; the devil’s power to hurt us is destroyed or restrained. He took the seed of Abraham, not angels. He was made like his brethren that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest… to make propitiation for the sins of the people. Having been tried by our evils, he is ready to bring us help.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Heb 2:10–18)
