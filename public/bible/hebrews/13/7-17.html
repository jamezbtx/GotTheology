Passage

Hebrews 13

Verses 7–17 · ESV · Voices

Remember leaders who spoke the word of God; consider their outcome and imitate their faith. Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever—so do not be led away by strange teachings; the heart is strengthened by grace, not by foods. We have an altar the tent-servants have no right to share; as sin-offerings were burned outside the camp, Jesus suffered outside the gate to sanctify the people by His blood. Therefore go to Him outside the camp, bear His reproach, seek the coming city, offer through Him the sacrifice of praise, do good and share, and obey leaders who watch for your souls.

Hebrews 13:7

7Remember your leaders, those who spoke to you the word of God. Consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith.

Arminian

Wesley: remember them who are now with God, considering the happy end of their conversation on earth; follow their faith.

Classical Arminians hear living memory that moves imitation. Leaders who spoke the word leave an outcome to weigh; faith is to be copied, not a personality cult. The pastoral tone is gratitude and emulation.

Reformed

Calvin: sets before them teachers for imitation—especially those who sealed doctrine with blood—yet generally all who persevered in true faith to the end. Those who begot us in Christ stand as fathers; their firmness in persecution should deeply move hearers.

Reformed accent: doctrine and life’s end together. Imitate faith proven durable; remembrance serves perseverance in sound teaching.

Hebrews 13:8

8Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.

Arminian

Wesley: men may die; but Jesus Christ—yea, and his gospel—is the same from everlasting to everlasting.

Classical Arminians confess with the Reformed the unchanging Christ and gospel. Leaders pass; the Lord does not. Stability for the church is His sameness, not novelty of teaching.

Reformed

Calvin: the only way to persevere in right faith is to hold the foundation and not depart from Christ—all treasures of wisdom are in Him. Yesterday comprehends the whole Old Testament; today and forever mean the knowledge of Him continues the same. He speaks of the knowledge the godly always had of the Mediator, the Church’s perpetual foundation—not a bare metaphysical aside.

Reformed confession: unchanging Christ anchors against every strange wind. Both camps stand here together on His constancy.

Hebrews 13:9

9Do not be led away by diverse and strange teachings, for it is good for the heart to be strengthened by grace, not by foods, which have not benefited those devoted to them.

Arminian

Wesley: be not carried about with various doctrines differing from that one faith in our one unchangeable Lord; strange to ears and hearts that abide in Him. Good that the heart be stablished with grace springing from faith in Christ—not with Jewish ceremonies that can never stablish the heart.

Classical Arminians hear grace as the heart’s real strength. Strange teachings pull from the same Christ; foods and rites cannot establish. Stay where grace has already begun to fortify.

Reformed

Calvin: conclude we ought not to fluctuate, since Christ’s truth remains fixed. Variety of errors arises when men abide not in Christ alone; apart from Him there is no stability. Grace here is spiritual worship and regeneration establishing the heart—not observance of meats, which profits nothing for those still occupied in them after the gospel’s light.

Reformed reading: grace versus foods is Christ versus ceremony. Strange doctrines are foreign to Him; cleave to Christ and you are guarded.

Hebrews 13:10

10We have an altar from which those who serve the tent have no right to eat.

Arminian

Wesley: we have an altar—the cross of Christ—whereof they have no right to eat (partake of the benefits) who serve the tabernacle—who adhere to the Mosaic law. On this verse the praise and beneficence of vv. 15–16 depend.

Classical Arminians hear exclusive gospel privilege at Christ’s cross. Tent-service and cross-benefits do not mix; come to the altar by faith, not by clinging to shadows.

Reformed

Calvin: beautiful adaptation of the sin-offering of which priests ate nothing. Christ was sacrificed on condition that tabernacle-servants should not feed on Him; to partake of Christ we must renounce the tabernacle’s ceremonies. Altar includes sacrifice and victim; tent, all external types.

Reformed accent: no dual membership at two altars. Serving the tent bars eating at Christ’s; the typology itself taught the break.

Hebrews 13:11

11For the bodies of those animals whose blood is brought into the holy places by the high priest as a sacrifice for sin are burned outside the camp.

Arminian

Wesley: according to their own law, sin-offerings were wholly consumed; no Jew ate thereof. But Christ was a sin-offering; therefore they cannot feed upon Him as we do who are freed from the Mosaic law.

Classical Arminians hear the type explaining the bar of v. 10. Outside-the-camp burning matches the gospel’s claim: the true sin-offering is not temple food for law-adherents.

Reformed

Calvin: the Levites brought the sin-offering without the camp to be burnt and ate nothing of it—so if we retain the ceremonies we shall not be partakers of the sacrifice Christ once offered, nor of the expiation made by His blood brought into the heavenly sanctuary.

Reformed reading: the law’s own rite preached exclusion. Outside the camp is where the antitype will be found.

Hebrews 13:12

12So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood.

Arminian

Wesley: Jesus exactly answering those typical sin-offerings suffered without the gate of Jerusalem—answering the old camp—that He might sanctify—reconcile and consecrate to God—the people by His own blood, not shadowy sacrifices now useless.

Classical Arminians glory with the Reformed that His own blood sanctifies outside the camp. Outside the gate is historical and theological: the true sin-offering is Christ Himself. Shared ground—not a camp fight over who the people are.

Reformed

Calvin: Jesus suffered without the gate to sanctify the people with His own blood—expiation once for all. His blood He brought into the heavenly sanctuary; the types cease for those who would share the antitype.

Reformed confession: sanctify here is cleansing by the once-offered blood. Shared with Wesley: tent-service cannot add to or replace that blood; the people are sanctified at the cross outside the gate.

Hebrews 13:13

13Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured.

Arminian

Wesley: let us go forth without the camp—out of the Jewish dispensation—bearing His reproach: all manner of shame, obloquy, and contempt for His sake.

Classical Arminians hear a costly summons the church may answer: leave the camp, take the shame. Identification with Jesus outside is voluntary discipleship under grace, not a spectator verse.

Reformed

Calvin: the allegory must not be frigid—connect an important duty: leave the tabernacle and follow Christ through exiles, flights, reproaches, and afflictions. This warfare unto blood opposes the shadowy practices ceremony-teachers boasted of.

Reformed accent: going outside is active discipleship under the cross. Reproach is expected; the camp’s safety is refused for Christ’s fellowship.

Hebrews 13:14

14For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come.

Arminian

Wesley: here on earth we have no continuing city—all things are but for a moment; Jerusalem itself was then on the point of destruction.

Classical Arminians hear pilgrim realism: seek the coming city. Earthly security, even religious, is temporary; hope looks forward while bearing reproach now.

Reformed

Calvin: extends the going forth—as strangers and wanderers we have no fixed residence but in heaven. When driven or changed, remember heaven is the inheritance; quiet earthly rest can foster sloth, so tossing trains eyes upward.

Reformed reading: no lasting city relativizes every camp. Seeking the coming city sustains those who have left the earthly one.

Hebrews 13:15

15Through him then let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name.

Arminian

Wesley: the altar was named (v. 10); now the sacrifices—praise and (v. 16) beneficence—with both God is well pleased. Continuously offer praise through Him.

Classical Arminians hear priestly privilege for the whole church: lips that confess His name are the fruit God wants. Through Jesus, not through renewed animal rites.

Reformed

Calvin: another kind of sacrifice remains—the calves of our lips (Hosea). Praise is the highest worship, preferred to external Law sacrifices; yet God is truly invoked only through Christ the Mediator, who sanctifies unclean lips and opens the way for prayer.

Reformed confession: continual praise through the Mediator replaces the old altar’s animals. Confession of the Name is the Christian sacrifice.

Hebrews 13:16

16Do not neglect to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God.

Arminian

Wesley pairs beneficence with praise as sacrifices with which God is well pleased.

Classical Arminians hear mercy and sharing as worship, not optional extras. Doing good and communicating goods please God as truly as lips of praise; neglect here is neglect of sacrifice.

Reformed

Calvin: all acts of love are so many sacrifices; God gave abundant opportunities to sacrifice without beasts. Benefits to men He considers done to Himself and honors as sacrifices. Communicate embraces mutual aid; with such—not the ancient animal offerings—God is well pleased.

Reformed accent: love’s deeds are sacred to God. Forgetting to do good and share robs both neighbor and the God who claims those works.

Hebrews 13:17

17Obey your leaders and submit to them, for they are keeping watch over your souls, as those who will have to give an account. Let them do this with joy and not with groaning, for that would be of no advantage to you.

Arminian

Wesley: obey them that lead or guide you in truth and holiness; submit—give up not conscience or judgment, but your own will in things purely indifferent. They watch over your souls with zeal against danger, as those who must give account to the great Shepherd. Let them watch with joy, not groans—unprofitable for you if pastors groan over you.

Classical Arminians hear pastoral obedience without blind surrender of conscience. Joyful oversight is mutual good; stubbornness harms the flock as well as the shepherd.

Reformed

Calvin: speaks of pastors who faithfully perform office—watching for souls—not mere titles or wolves. Obey and honor true watchers; heavier burden deserves honor; their account before God binds the people’s teachability. Causing pastors grief by ingratitude is unprofitable to the people’s own salvation.

Reformed reading: obedience is owed to faithful soul-watchers, not to empty hierarchy. Joyful oversight flourishes where the flock is teachable under the word.

Key contrast Both urge real obedience to leaders who watch for souls and must give account, and both refuse empty hierarchy. Arminians typically stress Wesley’s limit: give up not conscience or judgment, but your own will in things purely indifferent—so submission stays pastoral, not blind. Reformed readers typically stress Calvin’s accent: honor and teachability are owed to faithful watchers under the word; causing them grief by ingratitude harms the flock’s own good.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on remembering leaders, the unchanging Christ, grace versus strange teachings, the altar outside the camp, the sacrifice of praise, and obedience to watchful pastors—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Hebrews 13:7–17.

Arminian Notable voices

“Remember them which have the rule over you, who have spoken unto you the word of God: whose faith follow, considering the end of their conversation—those now with God, considering the happy end of their conversation on earth. Jesus Christ the same yesterday, and to day, and for ever—men may die; but Jesus Christ, yea and his gospel, is the same from everlasting to everlasting. Be not carried about with various and strange doctrines; it is good that the heart be stablished with grace springing from faith in Christ, not with meats. We have an altar—the cross of Christ—whereof they have no right to eat who serve the tabernacle. Jesus suffered without the gate that he might sanctify the people who believe in him by his own blood. Let us go forth unto him without the camp, bearing his reproach. Obey them that have the rule over you—who lead in truth and holiness—and submit yourselves: give up not your conscience or judgment, but your own will in things purely indifferent; for they watch for your souls as they that must give account.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Hebrews 13:7–17)

Reformed Notable voices

“Remember your teachers—especially those who sealed doctrine with their blood—considering the end of their conversation; imitate their faith. Jesus Christ the same yesterday, today, and forever: the only way to persevere is to hold the foundation and not depart from Christ, who reigned from the beginning and whose knowledge continues forever. Diverse and strange doctrines lead away from Him; the heart is established by grace, not by meats. We have an altar of which tabernacle-servants have no right to eat—renounce the ceremonies to partake of Christ. Jesus suffered without the gate to sanctify the people with His own blood; let us go forth to Him bearing His reproach, for here we have no continuing city. Offer through Him the sacrifice of praise—the fruit of lips—and do good and communicate; with such sacrifices God is well pleased. Obey faithful pastors who watch for your souls as those who must give account; honor is due to true watchers, not to mere titles.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Hebrews 13:7–17)
