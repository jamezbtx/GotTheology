Passage

Hebrews 13

Verses 18–25 · WEB

The writer asks for prayer, confident that he has a clear conscience, and urges them to pray that he may be restored to them soon. He then offers one of the great benedictions of the New Testament: may the God of peace, who brought again from the dead the great shepherd of the sheep by the blood of the eternal covenant, equip them with everything good to do his will, working in them what pleases him. He asks them to bear with his word of exhortation, shares news of Timothy, and sends greetings. Both traditions see the benediction joining God’s work and human obedience.

Hebrews 13:18

18Pray for us, for we are persuaded that we have a good conscience, desiring to live honorably in all things.

Arminian

Pray for us, for we are sure we have a clear conscience. Arminian readers see prayer as real participation in ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see prayer as a God-ordained means of blessing his servants.

Hebrews 13:19

19I strongly urge you to do this, that I may be restored to you sooner.

Arminian

Pray that I may be restored to you sooner. Arminian readers see prayer affecting what happens.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God answering prayer within his providence.

Hebrews 13:20

20Now may the God of peace, who brought again from the dead the great shepherd of the sheep with the blood of an eternal covenant, our Lord Jesus,

Arminian

The God of peace brought again from the dead the great shepherd of the sheep, by the blood of the eternal covenant. Arminian readers see Christ’s resurrection opening the eternal covenant to all who come.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the eternal covenant as God’s unbreakable commitment to his people, sealed by Christ’s blood.

Hebrews 13:21

21make you complete in every good work to do his will, working in you that which is well pleasing in his sight, through Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory forever and ever. Amen.

Arminian

May he equip you with everything good to do his will, working in us what is pleasing in his sight. Arminian readers see God enabling believers to do his will, which they freely carry out.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God working in his people what he commands, so that their obedience is the fruit of his grace (Philippians 2:13).

Key contrast Both see God working in believers to do his will. Arminian readers stress that God enables and believers freely cooperate. Reformed readers stress that God effectually produces the obedience he requires.

Hebrews 13:22

22But I exhort you, brothers, endure the word of exhortation, for I have written to you in few words.

Arminian

Bear with my word of exhortation, for I have written briefly. Arminian readers see the whole letter as an urgent appeal to persevere.

Reformed

Reformed readers see exhortation as God’s means of preserving his people.

Hebrews 13:23

23Know that our brother Timothy has been freed, with whom, if he comes shortly, I will see you.

Arminian

Our brother Timothy has been released. Arminian readers see the fellowship of the early church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the bonds of ministry and friendship in the church.

Hebrews 13:24

24Greet all of your leaders and all the saints. The Italians greet you.

Arminian

Greet all your leaders and all the saints. Arminian readers see the unity of the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the communion of saints.

Hebrews 13:25

25Grace be with you all. Amen.

Arminian

Grace be with all of you. Arminian readers see grace as the final word for all believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace as the beginning and end of the Christian life.
