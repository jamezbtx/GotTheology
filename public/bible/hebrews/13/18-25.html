Passage

Hebrews 13

Verses 18–25 · WEB · Voices

The letter closes with a request for prayer and a rich benediction: the God of peace, who raised Jesus the great shepherd through the blood of the eternal covenant, equip you to do his will. The writer asks the readers to bear with his ‘word of exhortation,’ shares news of Timothy’s release, and sends greetings.

Hebrews 13:18

18Pray for us, for we are persuaded that we have a good conscience, desiring to live honorably in all things.

Arminian

The writer seeks the church’s prayers, appealing to a clear conscience and a desire to live honorably in all things.

What comes through is mutual prayer as love’s duty. Leaders need the flock’s intercession; conscience and honorable aim commend the request without claiming sinless perfection.

Reformed

Calvin: after commending himself to their prayers, he declares a good conscience to move them more effectually. Prayers should embrace the world, yet we are peculiarly solicitous for godly men whose integrity is known. “We trust” shows modesty and confidence together.

Prayer for leaders is fitting love. Clear conscience is claimed under God, not as boast before men.

Hebrews 13:19

19I strongly urge you to do this, that I may be restored to you sooner.

Arminian

Earnest urging: pray so that restoration to them may come sooner. Prayer is treated as a means God uses in timing reunion.

Arminian interpreters hear sincere partnership—the church’s prayers matter for the writer’s return. Do not flatten the urge into fatalism that makes petition pointless.

Reformed

Calvin: adds that their prayers would profit them all as well as him—restoration would be the common good. He may have been detained by trouble or fear of persecution; yet even if free, man’s steps are in God’s hand.

Earnest prayer seeks sooner restoration under providence. Petition is how the church joins God’s ordering of the servant’s path.

Hebrews 13:20

20Now may the God of peace, who brought again from the dead the great shepherd of the sheep with the blood of an eternal covenant, our Lord Jesus,

Arminian

Wesley: the everlasting covenant—the Christian covenant, not temporary like the Jewish, but designed to remain forever. The God of peace raised the great Shepherd through the blood of that covenant.

Arminians hear shared Easter gospel: peace’s God brought Jesus up; the Shepherd’s blood establishes an everlasting covenant. Benediction rests on accomplished resurrection and covenant blood.

Reformed

Calvin: we pray aright for perfection when we acknowledge God’s power in Christ’s resurrection and Christ as our Pastor. He was raised that we might be renewed unto eternal life by the same power; He is great Shepherd to protect the sheep. He rose so that the blood shed once retains efficacy forever for ratifying the everlasting covenant.

God of peace, risen Shepherd, eternal-covenant blood—one gospel ground both camps bless.

Hebrews 13:21

21make you complete in every good work to do his will, working in you that which is well pleasing in his sight, through Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory forever and ever. Amen.

Arminian

Wesley: by the application of that blood by which this covenant was established, may He make you in every respect inwardly and outwardly holy.

Wesley’s heirs hear equipping as God’s making-holy that enables doing His will—cooperative grace in which God works and the church acts. Holiness inward and outward is the aim; through Jesus Christ glory is ascribed.

Reformed

Calvin: katartisai—confirm, fit, or perfect in every good work. We are by no means fit to do good until God forms us, and we shall not continue unless He strengthens us—perseverance is His peculiar gift. Good works are defined by God’s will; He works in us what is well pleasing. Through Christ come regeneration and graces, and only through His pardon are our imperfect works acceptable.

Equipping and working-in-you are God’s effectual forming and sustaining. The will done is what He works in us through Jesus.

Key contrast Both ascribe equipping and pleasing works to the God of peace through Jesus Christ and the blood of an eternal covenant, and both refuse a Pelagian self-holiness. Arminians hear God’s application of that blood making believers inwardly and outwardly holy—grace that equips so they may truly do His will. Reformed commentators hear God fitting and working in us what pleases Him—forming us for good and granting perseverance as His peculiar gift, so that even our works are acceptable only through Christ’s pardon.

Hebrews 13:22

22But I exhort you, brothers, endure the word of exhortation, for I have written to you in few words.

Arminian

Wesley: suffer the word of exhortation addressed in this letter—longer than usual letters, yet few words considering the subject’s fullness.

This is a pastoral plea: bear with exhortation. Brief relative to the theme; the church is asked to receive, not resent, urging toward endurance and holiness.

Reformed

Calvin: he wrote briefly so as not to lessen ordinary pastoral teaching. Scripture is not given to silence pastors; constant exhortations should sound from their mouths. Men prefer novelty to reminders and ill bear stimulation and reproof.

Bear with exhortation is the right hearing of a short letter that still presses hard. Preferring the new over the needed is a fault the Spirit already names.

Hebrews 13:23

23Know that our brother Timothy has been freed, with whom, if he comes shortly, I will see you.

Arminian

Wesley: if he come—to me—then the writer hopes to see them with Timothy.

Wesleyan Arminians hear fraternal news that ties the letter to names and hopes. Release and possible visit keep the close personal and hopeful.

Reformed

Calvin: prefers reading “Know ye” as information to Jews across the sea. If this is Paul’s companion Timothy, the author’s circumstances may fit Luke or Clement after Paul’s death; Timothy’s release is glad news bound to a hoped visit.

Concrete fellowship closes the theology. Released Timothy and a conditional visit root the exhortation in the church’s shared life.

Hebrews 13:24

24Greet all of your leaders and all the saints. The Italians greet you.

Arminian

Greetings embrace leaders and all the saints—the whole holy people—plus those from Italy.

Arminian interpreters hear catholic warmth: honor leaders and include every saint. Italian believers’ greeting widens the family’s peace.

Reformed

Calvin: saluting rulers particularly may conciliate them toward the doctrine; “all the saints” may mean Gentile believers or a charge to pass the letter on—cultivating unity.

Leaders and saints together receive peace. Cross-regional greeting displays the one church under the same Shepherd.

Hebrews 13:25

25Grace be with you all. Amen.

Arminian

Wesley: grace be with you all—St. Paul’s usual benediction. God apply it to our hearts!

One hears the letter end as it must: grace for all the addressed. Application to the heart is the fitting prayer after every exhortation.

Reformed

Calvin closes the commentaries noting the final grace upon them all—the apostolic wish that matches the God of peace who equips through Christ.

Grace with all of you is the last word. What the benediction asked, this farewell seals—favor from the raised Shepherd’s God.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What earlier interpreters on each side wrote about Hebrews 13:18–25.

Arminian Notable voices

“Now the God of peace, that brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus, that great shepherd of the sheep, through the blood of the everlasting covenant—the Christian covenant, not temporary like the Jewish, but designed to remain for ever. By the application of that blood by which this covenant was established, may he make you, in every respect, inwardly and outwardly holy! Suffer the word of exhortation—addressed to you in this letter, which though longer than my usual letters is yet contained in few words considering the copiousness of the subject. Grace be with you all—St. Paul’s usual benediction. God apply it to our hearts!”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Hebrews 13:20–22, 25)

Reformed Notable voices

“He ends with prayer that God would confirm, fit, or perfect them in every good work. We are by no means fit to do good until formed for the purpose by God, and we shall not continue long in doing good unless He strengthens us—perseverance is His peculiar gift. Look to Christ raised from the dead, the great Shepherd, that we may rightly trust God for help; He so rose that His shed blood retains efficacy forever for the everlasting covenant. Good works are defined by God’s will; He works in us what is well pleasing in His sight through Jesus Christ—regeneration and graces bestowed through Him, and our imperfect works made acceptable by His pardon. Bear with the word of exhortation; Scripture does not silence pastors’ constant appeals. Grace be with you all.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Hebrews 13:18–25)
