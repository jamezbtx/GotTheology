Passage

Hebrews 4

Verses 14–16 · WEB

After describing God’s word as able to search every heart, the writer turns to comfort. Believers have a great high priest, Jesus the Son of God, who has passed through the heavens, who sympathizes with human weakness, and who was tempted in every way without sinning. So they are to hold fast their confession and come boldly to the throne of grace. These verses are mostly common ground for Arminian and Reformed readers, with a difference in how they hear the command to hold fast.

Hebrews 4:14

14Having then a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus, the Son of God, let’s hold tightly to our confession.

Arminian

The high priest of Israel passed through the curtain into the most holy place once a year. Wesley drew the comparison: Jesus, the eternal Son, has passed through the heavens with his own blood into the very presence of God. Both traditions share this view of Christ, and the writer uses it as the reason to “hold tightly to our confession.”

Arminian readers hear the command as a real summons to the will. The priest has been given, and grace enables believers to keep their confession, but the call to hold fast assumes that letting go is a danger worth warning against. The letter’s repeated warnings support that reading.

Reformed

Calvin took “confession” as another word for faith, and saw the priesthood of Christ as the strongest reason not to waver. Since the Son of God himself stands behind the gospel, believers have every reason for confidence.

Reformed readers hear the command as one of the means God uses to keep his people. Those who belong to Christ will hold fast, and the exhortation is how that perseverance is worked out. A profession that does not endure was not the confession Christ keeps.

Key contrast Both traditions treat “hold tightly to our confession” as an earnest command grounded in the same great high priest. Arminian readers see a call that believers must answer, with real danger if they do not. Reformed readers see the call as the means by which God preserves those who truly belong to Christ.

Hebrews 4:15

15For we don’t have a high priest who can’t be touched with the feeling of our infirmities, but one who has been in all points tempted like we are, yet without sin.

Arminian

Jesus is not a priest who cannot sympathize with weakness. Wesley noted that he shares the feeling of our innocent infirmities, needs, and dangers, and because he was without sin, he is able to keep believers from sin in their temptations.

This is common ground. The Son took real human flesh and faced real temptation, yet never sinned. Arminian readers stress that this gives weak believers both a sympathetic priest and a model of holiness they are enabled to pursue.

Reformed

Calvin observed that if believers thought only of the majesty of the Son of God, their consciences would find no rest. Christ took our flesh and its feelings so that he could sympathize with us from experience, while his feelings remained always governed by righteousness.

Reformed readers likewise see this as shared ground. The Mediator is near to us as a brother and pure as the Son. Believers can bring their temptations to one who faced them and did not fall.

Hebrews 4:16

16Let’s therefore draw near with boldness to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy and may find grace for help in time of need.

Arminian

Because of this priest, believers are invited to come boldly to the throne of grace. Wesley described it as the throne of God their reconciled Father, where every blessing is given out of unearned favor. Arminian readers see an open invitation to the needy, who should not hang back in fear.

The promise is mercy and timely help. Grace is offered for each moment of need, and the believer is urged to keep drawing near to receive it.

Reformed

Calvin noted that access to God is open to all who come relying on Christ the Mediator. The throne is not displayed in a majesty that would terrify, but is named for grace. Those who feel their need of mercy are especially encouraged to come.

Reformed readers see here a pastoral comfort that both traditions share. Because the Mediator stands between the sinner and God’s majesty, believers can approach with confidence and find help when they need it.
