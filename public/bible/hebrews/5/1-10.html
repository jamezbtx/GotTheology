Passage

Hebrews 5

Verses 1–10 · ESV · Voices

The writer unfolds what a high priest is for—sympathy, sacrifice, and a call from God—and then shows Christ as Son and priest forever after Melchizedek’s order: in the days of His flesh He prayed with loud cries, learned obedience through suffering, and became the source of eternal salvation.

Hebrews 5:1

1For every high priest chosen from among men is appointed to act on behalf of men in relation to God, to offer gifts and sacrifices for sins.

Arminian

Every high priest is taken from among men and appointed for men before God—to offer gifts and sacrifices for sins. Wesley: till he is taken, of the same rank with them; appointed to bring God near to men, and men to God. The office is representative and sacrificial, not a private spirituality.

Shared christology: both camps hear here the pattern Christ will fulfill more eminently. Arminian pastoral note: mediation is real and needed; sinners do not stroll into God’s presence without a priest who offers. The Levitical sketch prepares the better priest already named in 4:14–16.

Reformed

Calvin: priests were taken from among men, acted for the whole people, and came furnished with sacrifices—without a sacrifice there is no peacemaker between God and man. That the Son shares our nature does not diminish His dignity; it fits Him to reconcile us.

Shared christology: Christ alone, having taken away sins by His own sacrifice, can reconcile God to us. Reformed pastoral weight: our intercourse with God hangs on a priest God appointed. Angels and apostles cannot fill this place; the appointed Man does.

Hebrews 5:2

2He can deal gently with the ignorant and wayward, since he himself is beset with weakness.

Arminian

Gentle dealing with the ignorant and wayward flows from shared weakness. Wesley: compassion in proportion to the offense; the priest himself is compassed with infirmity—and so needs the compassion he shows.

Arminian pastoral: sympathy is not soft on sin; it is fit help for those who err. In Christ the pattern is perfected without sinful infirmity: He can deal gently because He truly entered our frailty, and that gentleness meets real wanderers who must still return.

Reformed

Calvin: the priest ought to be kind and gentle to sinners because he partakes of their infirmities—yet Christ differed in this, that He had no necessity of offering for Himself. It is enough that He bore our infirmities, free from sin and undefiled.

Reformed comfort: Christ is brother not only by shared flesh but by experience of infirmity, so that He is formed to forbearance and kindness. Weakness in the Levitical priest explains mercy; sinlessness in Christ perfects it without removing sympathy.

Hebrews 5:3

3Because of this he is obligated to offer sacrifice for his own sins just as he does for those of the people.

Arminian

The earthly high priest must sacrifice for his own sins as for the people’s. The obligation exposes the limit of Aaron’s line: the helper himself needs atonement.

Shared contrast with Christ (already implied): whatever is wanting in those priests is in Him. Arminian note: the verse heightens gratitude for a priest who needs no offering for Himself—while still calling sinners to the sacrifice He does bring for them.

Reformed

Calvin marks the difference sharply: all said of Levitical priests does not apply equally to Christ; He was exempt from every contagion of sin and had no need to sacrifice for Himself.

Reformed accent: the type shows need; the antitype shows sufficiency. Christ’s priesthood is not improved Aaron—it is the reality toward which Aaron’s obligation pointed and in which Aaron’s self-offering is left behind.

Hebrews 5:4

4And no one takes this honor for himself, but only when called by God, just as Aaron was.

Arminian

No one takes the honor; God calls, as He called Aaron. Wesley: the sum is that whatever is excellent in the Levitical priesthood is in Christ more eminently, and whatever is wanting in those priests is in Him. Aaron and his line were called together; preaching was no part of the priestly office.

Arminian reading of vocation: lawful ministry waits on God’s call. The honor is gift, not grab—yet the called must still answer and serve. Christ’s call will be shown next as the Father’s word, not self-exaltation.

Reformed

Calvin: what makes an office lawful is the call of God; no one can rightly perform it without being made fit by God. Christ and Aaron shared divine calling; they differed in that Christ succeeded by a new and perpetual way.

Reformed accent: God alone rules His church and prescribes its administration. Self-made priesthood is spurious. The verse defends Christ’s right by locating its author in God—and limits every human claim to what God has actually granted.

Hebrews 5:5

5So also Christ did not exalt himself to be made a high priest, but was appointed by him who said to him, “You are my Son, today I have begotten you”;

Arminian

Christ did not exalt Himself; the Father who said “You are my Son” appointed Him. Wesley: He did not take this honor to Himself, but received it from Him who said, “Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee”—not at the same time as the priesthood’s public bestowal, for His generation was from eternity.

Shared christology: Sonship and priesthood meet in the Father’s appointment. Arminian pastoral: the pattern for every servant is the same humility—wait to be sent. Glory is received, not seized.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ did not honor or exalt Himself; He who said “Thou art my Son” did. The begetting named in the psalm is the Father’s testimony before men, and the Son manifested is Mediator—His begetting includes His priesthood.

Shared christology: appointment is from above. Reformed weight: the Father who made Him king also made Him priest. Our confidence rests not on Christ’s self-promotion but on the Father’s decree publicly attested.

Hebrews 5:6

6as he says also in another place, “You are a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek.”

Arminian

Priest forever after Melchizedek’s order—Psalm 110:4. Wesley simply points to the psalm; the Holy Ghost concealed Melchizedek’s biography so he might be the more eminent type of Christ: priest and king of Salem.

Shared christology: both camps agree the order is not Aaron’s temporary line but a forever priesthood typed in Melchizedek. Arminian hope: forever means a living priest who continues able to save those who come to God through Him—as the letter will press.

Reformed

Calvin: Melchizedek was God’s priest; the king at God’s right hand is a priest according to that order—royal dignity will not prevent priesthood, for a type was already presented. “According to the order” means according to the manner of that singular king-priest.

Shared christology: Messiah’s priesthood is what the psalm commends. Reformed accent: Aaron’s order was temporary by decree; this forever word opens the better priesthood the apostle will unfold in chapter 7.

Hebrews 5:7

7In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard because of his reverence.

Arminian

Days of His flesh: prayers, loud cries, tears, heard because of reverence. Wesley: those days when sufferings were at the height; thrice He offered prayers in the garden. What He most feared was the weight of infinite justice—being bruised by God’s hand—yet He longed to obey and lay down His life. He was heard not so that the cup should pass, but so that He drank it without fear.

Arminian pastoral: the Son’s real anguish invites real prayer. Grace does not make Gethsemane theatrical; it teaches sufferers to cry to the Father who is able, and to trust a hearing that may perfect obedience rather than remove the cup.

Reformed

Calvin: days of His flesh mean life in this world—liable to human sorrows He has now laid aside in heaven. He prayed with tears so no one would think He had an iron heart; deliverance is from God alone; He was heard from what He feared, coming forth conqueror though He still underwent death.

Reformed comfort: whenever evils press us, we recall the Son who labored under the same. God often hears when deliverance is not immediate. Christ’s reverence and anguish confirm both His true humanity and the Father’s saving help in the appointed path.

Hebrews 5:8

8Although he was a son, he learned obedience through what he suffered.

Arminian

Although Son, He learned obedience through suffering. Wesley: “learned” before “suffered” shows how willingly He learned—obedience in suffering and dying when He applied Himself to the cup. Sonship did not exempt Him from the school of the cross.

Arminian fork for disciples: the Son’s path is the pattern. Prevenient and cooperating grace enable believers to learn obedience in trial; the learning is real and can be resisted by sloth or embraced by faith. Suffering is not fate—it is the classroom where yielded hearts are trained.

Reformed

Calvin: not that Christ was driven by force or needed taming; He was willing. Yet by death He learned fully what it is to obey—renouncing His own will. We too must be taught by sorrows; we have a contumacious disposition until the Lord subdues us by such exercises to bear His yoke.

Reformed fork: the cross is God’s effectual school. Obedience in the elect is produced as the Father subdues the will—not as a bare example left to unaided choice. Christ’s learning is unique and vicarious in its saving end; ours is the Spirit’s work conforming us to Him.

Key contrast Both confess the Son truly learned obedience through suffering and that disciples are schooled by the cross. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled, resistible learning the believer must yield to in trial; Reformed readers typically stress that the Lord effectually subdues contumacious hearts by the sufferings He appoints.

Hebrews 5:9

9And being made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him,

Arminian

Made perfect, He became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey Him. Wesley: perfected by sufferings, brought through all to glory; author means procuring and efficient cause—to all that obey Him by doing and suffering His whole will.

Arminian accent: “all who obey” is a real condition of enjoying what the perfected Priest procured. Salvation’s source is Christ alone; the obedience of faith is sincerely required and graciously enabled. None who refuse Him may claim His eternal salvation while remaining disobedient.

Reformed

Calvin: suffering was Christ’s consecration into priesthood; by obedience He blotted out our transgressions and became the cause of salvation. “To all who obey Him” recommends faith—He and His blessings become ours as we receive Him; “all” shows no one teachable and obedient to the gospel is precluded.

Reformed accent: the perfected Priest effectually saves those given to Him; obedience is the fruit and evidence of a faith God works. The universal term invites all hearers, while perseverance in obeying is secured for the elect by the same grace that united them to the source.

Key contrast Both name Christ as the perfected source of eternal salvation and take “obey him” seriously. Arminians typically hear a genuine condition—salvation enjoyed by those who, enabled by grace, keep obeying; Reformed readers typically hear faith’s obedience as the Spirit’s effectual fruit in those the perfected Priest actually saves.

Hebrews 5:10

10being designated by God a high priest after the order of Melchizedek.

Arminian

Designated by God a high priest after Melchizedek’s order. Wesley: the Greek signifies surnamed; His name is the Son of God. Melchizedek’s identity is veiled so the type may shine—priest and king of Salem.

Shared close: the Father’s designation seals the argument of verses 1–10. Arminian pastoral: cling to the priest God named, not to self-made mediators. The digression that follows (11–14) will ask whether dull ears can yet hear more of this order.

Reformed

Calvin: named by God—necessary prelude to the fuller Melchizedek comparison. The apostle digresses while retaining his argument, stirring dull minds to attention.

Shared close: God’s designation, not human invention, establishes the forever priest. Reformed pastoral: rest in the Father’s appointment; the hardness ahead is in the hearers, not in the subject. Melchizedek’s order will repay the attention God now summons.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the Son who learned obedience in the days of His flesh and became the source of eternal salvation—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Hebrews 5:1–10.

Arminian Notable voices

“Though he were a Son—This is interposed, lest any should be offended at all these instances of human weakness. In the garden, how frequently did he call God his Father! And hence it most evidently appears that his being the Son of God did not arise merely from his resurrection. Yet learned he—The word learned, premised to the word suffered, elegantly shows how willingly he learned. He learned obedience, when he began to suffer; when he applied himself to drink that cup: obedience in suffering and dying. And being perfected—By sufferings; brought through all to glory. He became the author—The procuring and efficient cause. Of eternal salvation to all that obey him—By doing and suffering his whole will.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Heb 5:8–9)

Reformed Notable voices

“Yet learned he obedience. The proximate end of Christ’s sufferings was thus to habituate himself to obedience; not that he was driven to this by force, or that he had need of being thus exercised, as the case is with oxen or horses when their ferocity is to be tamed, for he was abundantly willing to render to his Father the obedience which he owed. But this was done from a regard to our benefit, that he might exhibit to us an instance and an example of subjection even to death itself. It may at the same time be truly said that Christ by his death learned fully what it was to obey God, since he was then led in a special manner to deny himself; for renouncing his own will, he so far gave himself up to his Father that of his own accord and willingly he underwent that death which he greatly dreaded.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Heb 5:8)
