Passage

Hebrews 5

Verses 11–14 · ESV · Voices

Before pressing deeper into Melchizedek, the writer turns on the readers: much remains to say, but they have become dull of hearing—needing milk again when they ought to be teachers, still unskilled when solid food belongs to the mature who discern good from evil by trained practice.

Hebrews 5:11

11About this we have much to say, and it is hard to explain, since you have become dull of hearing.

Arminian

Much to say about Melchizedek’s order—hard to explain because they have become dull of hearing. Wesley: the hardness is not chiefly in the subject but in their slothfulness in considering and dullness in apprehending the things of God. The digression reproves, admonishes, and exhorts.

Arminian pastoral: dullness is a real decline from earlier attentiveness—“have become” names a change. Grace that once opened ears can be neglected; the rebuke assumes responsibility to wake up and hear again before deeper truth is wasted.

Reformed

Calvin: difficulty is stated to stimulate attention, not to repel; the cause is in themselves, not in the subject. The Lord’s word is light, but its brightness is dimmed through our darkness—dullness, sloth, vanity, worldly cares, and fleshly lusts.

Reformed pastoral: God still speaks clearly; darkened reception is the crisis. The warning is a means to pierce sluggishness. Melchizedek remains the type of Christ—worth every sharpened ear the Spirit restores.

Hebrews 5:12

12For though by this time you ought to be teachers, you need someone to teach you again the basic principles of the oracles of God. You need milk, not solid food,

Arminian

By this time they ought to be teachers; instead they need the ABCs of God’s oracles again—milk, not solid food. Wesley: first principles of religion (enumerated in 6:1); milk is the first and plainest doctrines.

Arminian fork: time allotted for growth makes prolonged infancy culpable. Prevenient and cooperating grace enable progress toward teaching others; remaining on milk when solid food is due is resistible neglect, not a fixed decree that some must stay babes.

Reformed

Calvin: unreasonable and disgraceful to linger in elements when they ought to instruct others—not even disciples of ordinary truth, as if they did not know the alphabet. We must learn through life, yet so as not to remain always in first principles; every one ought to impart what he knows.

Reformed fork: progress corresponding to time is what God requires and, in His people, produces. Milk and solid food are both sound doctrine; prolonged childhood under much light exposes sloth God rebukes as He presses the elect toward maturity.

Key contrast Both shame lingering on milk when time called for teaching. Arminians typically describe growth as grace-enabled yet resistible advance believers must pursue; Reformed readers typically describe due progress as what God both demands and effectually works in those He keeps from settling into perpetual infancy.

Hebrews 5:13

13for everyone who lives on milk is unskilled in the word of righteousness, since he is a child.

Arminian

Living on milk means unskilled in the word of righteousness—still a child. Wesley: one who neither desires nor can digest anything else; unexperienced in the sublimer truths of the gospel. He warns those who desire and digest nothing but justification and imputed righteousness as if that were the whole diet.

Arminian note: childhood here is not precious simplicity but arrested development. The word of righteousness invites skill; refusing solid truth while clinging only to first comforts leaves the hearer fragile when harder Melchizedek teaching—and the warning of chapter 6—arrives.

Reformed

Calvin: babes who from weakness refuse solid doctrine; God is constrained even to prattle with them. He takes “word of righteousness” as completeness of knowledge leading to perfection, not the narrow question of justification alone—those who indulge ignorance preclude themselves from a real knowledge of Christ.

Reformed accent: infancy of understanding is culpable when time was given to grow. The gospel itself supplies milk and meat; remaining only on milk is a willful tenderness God names so that hearers will seek the skill His word gives.

Hebrews 5:14

14But solid food is for the mature, for those who have their powers of discernment trained by constant practice to distinguish good from evil.

Arminian

Solid food for the mature—powers of discernment trained by constant practice. Wesley: strong meat belongs to those of full age; habit means strength of spiritual understanding from maturity of spiritual age, so they exercise themselves with ease, readiness, cheerfulness, and profit.

Arminian close: maturity is practiced, not merely possessed. Grace enables the training; the believer must use senses repeatedly to tell good from evil. Dull hearing is reversed along the same path—attentive, habitual obedience that can still be neglected or pursued.

Reformed

Calvin: perfect means adults in Christ, spiritual men who by continual practice attain habit to discern good and evil. Faith that doubts, suspended between truth and falsehood, is near to nothing; all senses must be furnished by God’s word against Satan’s fallacies.

Reformed close: discernment is the Spirit’s fruit through constant use of the word. God arms His people for battle; maturity is not a human ladder into favor but the trained steadiness He works in those who are no longer tossed by every wind. Practice is commanded because grace makes it fruitful.

Key contrast Both tie solid food to practiced discernment of good and evil. Arminians typically stress habitual exercise believers must keep up under assisting grace that remains resistible; Reformed readers typically stress the Spirit’s effectual training through the word, producing stable discernment in the mature God is conforming to Christ.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on dullness of hearing, milk versus solid food, and senses trained to discern—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Hebrews 5:11–14.

Arminian Notable voices

“Concerning whom—The apostle here begins an important digression, wherein he reproves, admonishes, and exhorts the Hebrews. We—Preachers of the gospel. Have many things to say, and hard to be explained—Though not so much from the subject-matter, as from your slothfulness in considering, and dulness in apprehending, the things of God. Ye have need that one teach you again which are the first principles of religion. And have need of milk—The first and plainest doctrines. But strong meat—These sublimer truths relating to perfection. Belong to them of full age, who by habit—Habit here signifies strength of spiritual understanding, arising from maturity of spiritual age. By, or in consequence of, this habit they exercise themselves in these things with ease, readiness, cheerfulness, and profit.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Heb 5:11–14)

Reformed Notable voices

“He reminds them that they were hard or difficult things; not indeed to repel them, but to stimulate them to greater attention. He however states that the cause of the difficulty was not in the subject but in themselves. And indeed the Lord speaks to us so clearly and without any obscurity, that his word is rightly called our light; but its brightness become dim through our darkness. This happens partly through our dullness and partly through our sloth; for though we are very dull to understand the truth of God, yet there is to be added to this vice the depravity of our affections, for we apply our minds to vanity rather than to God’s truth. We are also continually impeded either by our perverseness, or by the cares of the world, or by the lusts of our flesh.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Heb 5:11)
