Passage

Hebrews 7

Verses 11–19 · ESV · Voices

If perfection had been attainable through the Levitical priesthood—under which the people received the law—another priest after Melchizedek's order would not have been needed. Change of priesthood entails change of law. The Lord arose from Judah, a tribe Moses never tied to altar service; another priest arises not by bodily descent but by the power of an indestructible life, as Psalm 110 witnesses. The former commandment is set aside for weakness and uselessness—the law made nothing perfect—and a better hope is introduced, through which we draw near to God.

Hebrews 7:11

11Now if perfection had been attainable through the Levitical priesthood (for under it the people received the law), what further need would there have been for another priest to arise after the order of Melchizedek, rather than one named after the order of Aaron?

Arminian

If perfection had been by the Levitical priesthood—if it answered all God's designs and man's wants—why another priest after Melchizedek? Wesley: under it the people received the law, whence some might infer perfection by that priesthood; from this single consideration priesthood and law must give way to a better priesthood and more excellent dispensation.

Shared: Levitical perfection was not attained; another order was needed. Classical Arminians hear "perfection" as what justifies and sanctifies—already pointing toward Wesley's later note that the law made nothing perfect. The Melchizedek priest is not optional decoration; Aaron's order must yield.

Reformed

Calvin: no covenant between God and man is ratified except it rests on a priesthood; the Law was introduced under the Levitical priesthood. If the ministry under Aaron were perfect, nothing could be changed; it follows the Law's ministry was not perfect, for David's new order was introduced.

Shared: the "if" proves imperfection. Reformed accent: priesthood and covenant stand together; another order means the old ministry was not the ultimate end. Perfection was never by Levi; the Son's order was always the goal the Psalm announced.

Hebrews 7:12

12For when there is a change in the priesthood, there is necessarily a change in the law as well.

Arminian

Change of priesthood necessarily changes the law. Wesley: one of these cannot be changed without the other.

Shared axiom. Arminian hearing: the Mosaic economy tied to Aaron cannot stay when Melchizedek's priest arrives. Better priesthood than Levi means a more excellent dispensation—not lawlessness, but the gospel's order. Hear the necessity; do not keep the shadows as if the priest had not changed.

Reformed

Calvin: as the authority of Law and priesthood is the same, Christ became not only priest but Lawgiver; Moses' ministry was no less temporary than Aaron's; both were annulled by Christ's coming. By Law he means what peculiarly belonged to Moses regarding the ancient priesthood—not the rule of life and promises of mercy that Christ and Moses share.

Shared: priesthood change entails law change. Reformed precision: ceremonies and the old priestly ministration pass; faith, fear of God, and the holy life's rule are not abolished as doctrine. The accessory falls with its principal.

Hebrews 7:13

13For the one of whom these things are spoken belonged to another tribe, from which no one has ever served at the altar.

Arminian

The one spoken of belonged to another tribe—none of which ever served at the altar. Wesley: the priesthood is manifestly changed from one order to another and from one tribe to another. He of whom these things are spoken—namely Jesus—pertains to Judah; no man of that tribe was suffered to minister at the altar.

Shared: tribal transfer proves the change. Classical Arminians take the historical fact as decisive for Hebrew hearers who knew Jesus' lineage. The altar's old monopoly is broken by the Melchizedek priest from Judah.

Reformed

Calvin: speaking to those who confessed Jesus the Son of Mary to be the Christ, he proves an end was put to the ancient priesthood because the new Priest was of another tribe, not Levi. The honor of priesthood was to continue in Levi by special privilege; Christ of Judah ends that exclusive claim.

Shared: another tribe, no altar service from it under Moses. Reformed reading: if he is the Christ, he is David's son; if David's son, Judah; if Judah, the Levitical order cannot contain him. The law's tribal fence yields to Psalm 110's priest.

Hebrews 7:14

14For it is evident that our Lord was descended from Judah, and in connection with that tribe Moses said nothing about priests.

Arminian

It is evident our Lord was descended from Judah; Moses said nothing about priests from that tribe. Wesley: whatever difficulties have arisen since, it was then clear beyond dispute that our Lord sprang out of Judah.

Shared historical premise for both camps. Arminian note: "our Lord" is the Melchizedek priest; Moses' silence about Judah-priests is itself evidence that a new order, not Aaron's, is in view. Hear the evidence; let the law's silence speak.

Reformed

Calvin: it was then a fact commonly known that Christ was born of Judah. Those who acknowledged him as the Christ must be persuaded he was the son of David; the promised one could derive his origin from no other.

Shared: Judah, not Levi; Moses silent on priests there. Reformed accent: the confession of Jesus as Christ already carries Judah and David—and therefore a priesthood Moses never legislated for that tribe. The change is evident, not speculative.

Hebrews 7:15

15This becomes even more evident when another priest arises in the likeness of Melchizedek,

Arminian

Still more evident when another priest arises in Melchizedek's likeness. Wesley: both priesthood and law are changed, because the priest now raised up is not only of another tribe, but of a quite different order.

Shared: likeness to Melchizedek multiplies the proof. Classical Arminians hear "arises" as God's raising of the Son's priesthood—different order, not a patched Aaron. The resemblance of chapter 7:1–10 now drives the legal consequence.

Reformed

Calvin: he proves by another argument that the Law is abolished—now as to the nature of the priesthood. The ancient priesthood had to do with external rites; in Christ's priesthood there is nothing but what is spiritual. The former was evanescent; the latter perpetual.

Shared: another priest in Melchizedek's likeness makes the change clearer still. Reformed accent: order and nature differ—spiritual, endless priesthood versus carnal ordinance. The likeness is the Psalm's forever-priest, not a second Levi.

Hebrews 7:16

16who has become a priest, not on the basis of a legal requirement concerning bodily descent, but by the power of an indestructible life.

Arminian

Priest not by legal requirement of bodily descent, but by the power of an indestructible life. Wesley: not after the law of a carnal commandment—Mosaic commandments carnal compared with the gospel's spirituality—but after the power of an endless life, which he has in himself as the eternal Son of God.

Shared: descent-law versus endless life. Arminian hearing: the Son's own indestructible life is the basis—able Savior, better priest than Levi. Bodily genealogy cannot found this order; the eternal Son can.

Reformed

Calvin: carnal commandment refers to external rites—ointment, vestments, blood, earthly ceremonies. Christ was consecrated not by Moses a mortal man with weak elements, but by the Holy Spirit with celestial power, set in opposition to those elements. Hence the eternity of his priesthood.

Shared: not bodily descent; power of indestructible life. Reformed accent: appointment and power are spiritual and effectual. The forever-priest is made by divine power, not by Levitical pedigree. Indestructible life secures a priesthood that cannot pass to a successor.

Hebrews 7:17

17For it is witnessed of him, "You are a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek."

Arminian

Witnessed of him: "You are a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek." The Psalm is the court of appeal. Wesley has already tied endless life to this forever-priesthood.

Shared: Scripture's witness settles the order. Both camps hear forever as more than one age of shadow—Christ's priesthood does not expire with a mortal. Historic hearing: David's oath-word (soon in vv. 20–21) already stands behind this citation. The Son remains priest; Aaron's line does not.

Reformed

Calvin lays stress on the single word forever: Christ differs from the whole race of Levi because he is made a priest forever. Applied to Christ, forever must be understood as eternity; by his coming a renovation of the world was effected, and no end can be found to his age.

Shared: the quotation is decisive. Reformed reading: Mosaic "forevers" for sacrifices were unto Christ; Christ's forever has no such terminus. The witness of Psalm 110 is the legal charter of the new order.

Hebrews 7:18

18For on the one hand, a former commandment is set aside because of its weakness and uselessness

Arminian

A former commandment is set aside because of its weakness and uselessness. Wesley: implied in this new and everlasting priesthood is a disannulling of the preceding commandment—an abrogation of the Mosaic law—for its insufficiency either to justify or to sanctify.

Shared: weakness, not divine mistake. Classical Arminians stress justify and sanctify: the law could not give the righteousness or holiness the gospel brings. The commandment is set aside as priestly-legal economy, so that a better hope may be introduced. Hear the verdict of insufficiency.

Reformed

Calvin: the Law together with the priesthood had come to an end because it was weak and unprofitable—speaking of ceremonies that had nothing substantial in themselves. Types derived all virtue from Christ; separated from him, nothing is left but weakness. The commandment going before is annulled by the later word of the Psalm.

Shared: set aside for weakness and uselessness. Reformed accent: ceremonies and the old priesthood, not the moral will of God, are in view as unprofitable when set against Christ. Former law yields to the forever-priest.

Hebrews 7:19

19(for the law made nothing perfect); but on the other hand, a better hope is introduced, through which we draw near to God.

Arminian

The law made nothing perfect; a better hope is introduced, through which we draw near to God. Wesley: the law taken by itself, separate from the gospel, could not perfect its votaries either in faith or love, in happiness or holiness. The better hope is the gospel dispensation, which gives a better ground of confidence. By which we draw nigh to God—yea, so nigh as to be one spirit with him. And this is true perfection.

Shared: law imperfect; better hope; drawing near. Arminian pastoral: true perfection is gospel nearness—faith and love made possible by the better hope—not Levitical ritual finished. The able Savior brings us nigh; the former commandment could not.

Reformed

Calvin: the Law was only a beginning; something more perfect had to follow. By a better hope he means the condition of the faithful under Christ's reign—nearer access than the fathers', who stood afar in the court while only the high priest entered within. He who still holds the shadows puts God at a great distance and deprives us of the liberty the Gospel grants.

Shared: nothing perfected by the law; better hope; we draw near. Reformed accent: the contrast is covenantal access and the form of revelation—fuller approach by the Mediator—more than a program of progressive holiness language.

Key contrast Arminian readings (Wesley) typically frame the law's failure as insufficiency to justify or sanctify, and the better hope as the gospel by which we draw nigh—even to be one spirit with him—as true perfection. Reformed readings (Calvin) typically frame the same verses as the old priestly economy's weakness and the new covenant's nearer access: the Law a beginning, Christ bringing a better hope and familiar approach that the sanctuary veils denied; perfection was never by the Levitical order.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the Levitical order yielding—change of priesthood and law, a priest from Judah by indestructible life, the former commandment set aside for weakness, and a better hope by which we draw near—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Hebrews 7:11–19.

Arminian Notable voices

“If now perfection were by the Levitical priesthood—if this perfectly answered all God's designs and man's wants—what farther need was there that another priest of a new order should be set up? From this single consideration it is plain that both the priesthood and the law, inseparably connected, were now to give way to a better priesthood and more excellent dispensation. Our Lord sprang out of Judah. Who is made a priest not after the law of a carnal commandment, but after the power of an endless life—which he has in himself as the eternal Son of God. There is a disannulling of the preceding commandment—an abrogation of the Mosaic law—for its insufficiency either to justify or to sanctify. For the law made nothing perfect; but the bringing in of a better hope—of the gospel dispensation—does. By which we draw nigh to God—yea, so nigh as to be one spirit with him. And this is true perfection.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Heb 7:11–19)

Reformed Notable voices

“If the ministry of the Church was perfect under the order of Aaron, why was it necessary to return to another order? It follows that the ministry of the Law was not perfect. As the authority of the Law and the priesthood is the same, when the priesthood is changed there is necessarily a change of the law as well—that part which regarded the ancient priesthood. Christ is of Judah; he is made priest after the power of an endless life, not by carnal commandment. There is a disannulling of the former commandment because of its weakness and unprofitableness; the Law made nothing perfect. But a better hope is introduced, through which we draw near to God—a fuller, nearer access than the fathers had under the veils of the old sanctuary.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Heb 7:11–19)
