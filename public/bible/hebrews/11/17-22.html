Passage

Hebrews 11

Verses 17–22 · ESV · Voices

Faith’s catalog presses into Abraham’s severest trial: offering Isaac, the son of promise, while holding that God was able to raise him from the dead. Isaac then blesses Jacob and Esau concerning things to come; dying Jacob blesses Joseph’s sons and worships; Joseph, at the end, speaks of the exodus and commands concerning his bones—promise trusted beyond the grave.

Hebrews 11:17

17By faith Abraham, when he was tested, offered up Isaac, and he who had received the promises was in the act of offering up his only son,

Arminian

Wesley: when God made that glorious trial, Abraham offered up Isaac—the will accepted as if he had actually done it. He that had received the promises, particularly “In Isaac shall thy seed be called,” offered up this very son, the only one by Sarah.

Classical Arminians hear a real trial answered by real obedience of faith. The promises are not discarded; they are trusted in the dark. Pastoral force: faith can be tested severely and still offer what God asks.

Reformed

Calvin: this trial surpassed every other; God is said to have then in an especial manner tried him. The act flowed from faith; Abraham had nothing more excellent than faith, which brought forth such extraordinary fruit. The resolution to obey was as though he had actually sacrificed his son.

Reformed accent: testing proves what faith God has wrought. Offering the only son of promise is faith’s fruit, not a gamble that may fail God’s election.

Hebrews 11:18

18of whom it was said, “Through Isaac shall your offspring be named.”

Arminian

Wesley: in Isaac shall thy seed be called—from him shall the blessed seed spring. The word of promise stands in the very sentence that seems to undo it.

Classical Arminians hear the tension every believer may face: God’s command and God’s promise both true. Faith holds both without inventing a third way of human cleverness.

Reformed

Calvin: all the promises were founded on this declaration; when this foundation was taken away, no hope of blessing or grace remained. Nothing earthly was at issue, but eternal salvation—of Abraham, yea of the whole world.

Reformed reading: the named offspring is the hinge of grace’s hope. Faith does not edit the promise; it trusts the God of the promise when obedience seems to cancel it.

Hebrews 11:19

19He considered that God was able even to raise him from the dead, from which, figuratively speaking, he did receive him back.

Arminian

Wesley: accounting that God was able even to raise him from the dead—though there had not been any instance of this in the world. From whence, figuratively, he received him afterwards, snatched from the jaws of death.

Classical Arminians hear resurrection-faith as the way sincere trust survives apparent contradiction: God is able. The figurative receiving back encourages hearers to reckon on God’s power beyond visible outcomes.

Reformed

Calvin: he did not renounce the promise, but extended its power beyond Isaac’s life; he bound not God’s power to Isaac’s life, but felt persuaded it would be efficacious in his ashes no less than while alive. Hope did not disappoint; it was a sort of resurrection when his son was suddenly delivered.

Reformed confession: faith keeps the promise by ascribing raising-power to God. This is not open-ended uncertainty; it is confidence that God’s word cannot fail.

Key contrast Both glory that Abraham held the promise by reckoning God able to raise Isaac, and both refuse a faith that discards God’s word. Arminians typically hear this as the pattern for tested believers who must actively account God able when obedience and promise seem to clash. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s Abraham who did not renounce but extended the promise—faith emerging invincible because God’s power and word held him, not a faith that might as well have let the promise go.

Hebrews 11:20

20By faith Isaac invoked future blessings on Jacob and Esau.

Arminian

Wesley: blessed—prophetically foretold the particular blessings they should partake of—preferring, in the event, according to the oracle.

Classical Arminians hear faith speaking future things God has shown. Blessing is not empty wish; it is trust that God will do what He said concerning things to come.

Reformed

Calvin: to bless as to future things was the work of faith; when the thing itself does not exist and the word only appears, faith must bear rule. Isaac had nothing he could bestow but the word of God. Faith mainly shone when he distinguished the twins, giving first place to the younger, following God’s oracle.

Reformed accent: the blessing depends on faith in God’s word, including God’s choice regarding Jacob and Esau.

Hebrews 11:21

21By faith Jacob, when dying, blessed each of the sons of Joseph, bowing in worship over the head of his staff.

Arminian

Wesley: Jacob when dying—when near death—bowing down on the top of his staff as he sat on the side of his bed, blessed both sons of Joseph and worshipped.

Classical Arminians hear faith active at the edge of death: blessing and worship together. Dying believers still speak promise and adore.

Reformed

Calvin: nothing here agreeable to reason—Ephraim the younger preferred, eyes dim, two portions assigned as though lord of the land famine had driven him from—but faith ruled supreme. He worshipped, evidence of gratitude, led by faith to submit himself.

Reformed reading: dying faith blesses against sight and worships. Reason is not the master; God’s word is.

Hebrews 11:22

22By faith Joseph, at the end of his life, made mention of the exodus of the Israelites and gave directions concerning his bones.

Arminian

Wesley: concerning his bones—to be carried into the land of promise. At the end, Joseph’s faith still pointed Israel forward.

Classical Arminians hear end-of-life faith that mentions exodus and orders bones home. Promise outlasts Egyptian honor. Hearers are called to the same long memory of redemption.

Reformed

Calvin: wealth, luxuries, and honors made not the holy man forget the promise nor detained him in Egypt—evidence of no small faith. He ordered bones exported not for a sweeter grave, but to sharpen the nation’s desire for redemption and strengthen their faith that they would be delivered.

Reformed accent: faith ascends above what is elevated in the world. Joseph’s last word is gospel for a people who must wait.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Abraham’s testing with Isaac, resurrection-faith, and the dying blessings of Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Hebrews 11:17–22.

Arminian Notable voices

“By faith Abraham—when God made that glorious trial of him—offered up Isaac. The will being accepted as if he had actually done it. Yea, he that had received the promises—particularly that grand promise, “In Isaac shall thy seed be called”—offered up this very son. Accounting that God was able even to raise him from the dead—though there had not been any instance of this in the world. From whence also, to speak in a figurative way, he did receive him afterwards, snatched from the jaws of death. By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau concerning things to come. By faith Jacob, when dying, blessed both the sons of Joseph, and worshipped. By faith Joseph, when he died, made mention of the departing of the children of Israel, and gave commandment concerning his bones—to be carried into the land of promise.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Hebrews 11:17–22)

Reformed Notable voices

“The sacrificing of Isaac is to be estimated according to the purpose of the heart: it was not owing to Abraham that he did not actually perform what he was commanded. All the promises were founded on this declaration, “In Isaac shall thy seed be called”; when this foundation was taken away, no hope of blessing or of grace remained. Here nothing earthly was at issue, but the eternal salvation of Abraham, yea, of the whole world. Yet by faith he emerged above all these thoughts. Abraham ascribed this honor to God, that he was able to raise his son again from the dead. He then did not renounce the promise, but extended its power and its truth beyond the life of his son; he bound not God’s power to Isaac’s life, but felt persuaded it would be efficacious in his ashes when dead no less than in him while alive. By faith Isaac blessed as to future things; by faith Jacob preferred the younger; by faith Joseph, amid wealth and honors, forgot not the promise nor was detained in Egypt—ordering his bones to be exported to sharpen the nation’s desire for redemption.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Hebrews 11:17–22)
