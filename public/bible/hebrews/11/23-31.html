Passage

Hebrews 11

Verses 23–31 · ESV · Voices

Faith’s examples move through Moses: hidden by parents who did not fear the king’s edict; refusing Pharaoh’s household; choosing mistreatment with God’s people; considering Christ’s reproach greater wealth than Egypt’s treasures; enduring as seeing the Invisible; keeping the Passover. Then the people cross the Red Sea, Jericho’s walls fall, and Rahab does not perish with the disobedient.

Hebrews 11:23

23By faith Moses, when he was born, was hidden for three months by his parents, because they saw that the child was beautiful, and they were not afraid of the king's edict.

Arminian

Wesley: they saw—doubtless with a divine presage of things to come—and were not afraid of the king’s commandment.

Classical Arminians hear parental faith that fears God more than the edict. Beauty here is read with promise-sense, not mere sentiment. Courage to hide is faith’s fruit for the child’s life.

Reformed

Calvin: induced to save him because God had promised a deliverer under oppression; they relied on that promise. Some mark of future excellency inspired hope of approaching deliverance. Their faith, though weak, was so approved as to secure Moses’ life, on which the Church’s deliverance depended.

Reformed accent: promise-reliant courage, not fearlessness as temperament. God approved even weak faith that preferred the infant’s safety.

Hebrews 11:24

24By faith Moses, when he was grown up, refused to be called the son of Pharaoh's daughter,

Arminian

Wesley: refused to be called—any longer—the son of Pharaoh’s daughter. Grown-up faith renounces false belonging.

Classical Arminians hear a deliberate refusal: identity with God’s people over palace title. Hearers likewise must refuse names that deny their calling.

Reformed

Calvin: when of mature age, not by boyish levity, he disregarded the adoption. By visiting and relieving his brethren he proved he preferred his own nation. He could not have done so without being persuaded of the blessing promised to Abraham’s race—whose only witness was God’s promise.

Reformed reading: mature faith renounces Egypt’s name because it has seen the promise afar.

Hebrews 11:25

25choosing rather to be mistreated with the people of God than to enjoy the fleeting pleasures of sin.

Arminian

Wesley does not pause here with a long gloss; the choice is plain in the text he elsewhere frames by looking off to the reward (v. 26).

Classical Arminians hear a real alternative set before Moses and before us: mistreatment with God’s people, or fleeting sinful pleasure. Faith chooses the people and the hardship. Do not invent a third path of comfortable neutrality.

Reformed

Calvin: shun as deadly poison whatever cannot be enjoyed without offending God; pleasures of sin are allurements that draw us from God and our calling. Temporary pleasures vanish with life itself. He chose affliction with the people of God—otherwise he could not avow himself one of God’s people.

Reformed accent: choosing mistreatment is faith’s separation to the Church; fleeting pleasure is unbelief’s bait.

Hebrews 11:26

26He considered the reproach of Christ greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt, for he was looking to the reward.

Arminian

Wesley: the reproach of Christ—that which he bore for believing in the Messiah to come, and acting accordingly. He looked off from perishing treasures unto the recompence of reward—not Canaan, which he never attained, but what his believing ancestors looked for: a future state of happiness in heaven.

Classical Arminians hear looking to the reward as holy motive for enduring reproach. Greater wealth is Christ’s reproach now; the recompense is heavenly. Faith’s eyes train off Egypt toward promised joy.

Reformed

Calvin: those God has chosen He has also foreordained to be conformed to the Son’s image—not all by the same cross, but none may decline the cross in common with Christ. He had eyes fixed on God’s promise. Faith as to righteousness before God does not look on reward but on God’s gratuitous goodness and Christ alone; yet faith, embracing every word of God, has respect to the reward promised—without this having place in free justification.

Reformed confession: reproach of Christ is wealth; reward-hope is faith’s embrace of promise, not merit that purchases justification.

Key contrast Both honor Moses for counting Christ’s reproach better than Egypt and for looking to God’s promised reward, and both refuse treasure-religion. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s look-off to heavenly recompense as the believer’s sincere motive that sustains costly obedience. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s careful fork: justification-faith rests on free mercy in Christ alone, while faith more broadly may eye the promised reward—never as wages that buy righteousness.

Hebrews 11:27

27By faith he left Egypt, not being afraid of the anger of the king, for he endured as seeing him who is invisible.

Arminian

Wesley: by faith he left Egypt, taking the Israelites; not then fearing the wrath of the king—as he did many years before. He endured as seeing Him who is invisible.

Classical Arminians hear endurance fueled by sight of the Invisible. Fear of the king yields when God is seen. Pastoral call: endure by that sight.

Reformed

Calvin: wonderful strength of faith to bring out an untrained multitude and hope God would open a way. True faith sets God always before our eyes; beholds higher things in God than senses perceive; a view of God alone strengthens weakness to withstand Satan’s assaults. The weaker and less resolute, the less faith.

Reformed accent: seeing the Invisible is faith’s office; endurance is its fruit, not stoic grit.

Hebrews 11:28

28By faith he kept the Passover and sprinkled the blood, so that the Destroyer of the firstborn might not touch them.

Arminian

Wesley: the pouring out of the blood of the paschal lamb, sprinkled on the door-posts, lest the destroying angel should touch the Israelites.

Classical Arminians hear Passover faith: blood sprinkled because God said so, before the Destroyer’s pass was seen. Shelter under the blood is for those who believe and keep what God appointed.

Reformed

Calvin: kept by faith not merely because the lamb typified Christ, but because its benefit did not yet appear when doorposts were sprinkled. Satisfied with God’s word alone that the people would be exempt, Moses did not hesitate. Faith acquiesces in the word when the effect is hid.

Reformed reading: sprinkling is obedience of faith to a not-yet-seen deliverance. The Destroyer is stayed by God’s appointed blood, received by faith.

Hebrews 11:29

29By faith the people crossed the Red Sea as on dry land, but the Egyptians, when they attempted to do the same, were drowned.

Arminian

Wesley: they—Moses, Aaron, and the Israelites—passed the Red Sea. Faith crosses where presumption drowns.

Classical Arminians hear the same path taken two ways: by faith unto life, by unbelieving assay unto death. The sea is not magic; trust in God’s word makes the difference.

Reformed

Calvin: many in that multitude were unbelieving; yet the Lord granted to the faith of a few that the whole multitude should pass. Faith’s crossing condemns Egypt’s copy without faith.

Reformed accent: God honors faith; unbelieving imitation of the form perishes. Deliverance is the Lord’s through faith.

Hebrews 11:30

30By faith the walls of Jericho fell down after they had been encircled for seven days.

Arminian

Wesley: by the faith of Joshua. Seven days of obedient circling precede the fall.

Classical Arminians hear patient, odd obedience that faith sustains until walls fall. Methods that look foolish are wise when God has spoken.

Reformed

Calvin ascribes Jericho’s fall to faith—walls down after the word-commanded circuit. Faith waits God’s timing through seven days of apparent futility.

Reformed reading: the fall is God’s act meeting faith’s obedience. Do not credit the march as technique; credit the God who spoke.

Hebrews 11:31

31By faith Rahab the prostitute did not perish with those who were disobedient, because she had given a friendly welcome to the spies.

Arminian

Wesley: Rahab—though formerly one not of the fairest character—perished not with them that believed not, when she received the spies with peace.

Classical Arminians hear mercy to a scandalous past where faith welcomes God’s messengers. She did not perish with the disobedient; belief and hospitality mark the difference. Hope for unlikely hearers.

Reformed

Calvin: by faith Rahab perished not with the unbelieving when she received the spies with peace. Faith separated her from the disobedient city.

Reformed accent: she is saved with God’s people by faith, not by prior respectability. Disobedience perishes; faith receives and lives.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Moses’ choice, the reproach of Christ, Passover faith, the Red Sea, Jericho, and Rahab—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Hebrews 11:23–31.

Arminian Notable voices

“By faith Moses, when he was born, was hid three months of his parents, because they saw he was a proper child; and they were not afraid of the king’s commandment. By faith Moses, when he was come to years, refused to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter. Esteeming the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures in Egypt: for he looked off from all those perishing treasures unto the recompence of reward—not to an inheritance in Canaan, but what his believing ancestors looked for, a future state of happiness in heaven. By faith he forsook Egypt, not fearing the wrath of the king: for he endured, as seeing him who is invisible. Through faith he kept the passover, and the sprinkling of blood. By faith they passed through the Red sea. By faith the walls of Jericho fell down. By faith the harlot Rahab perished not with them that believed not, when she had received the spies with peace.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Hebrews 11:23–31)

Reformed Notable voices

“The parents of Moses relied confidently on God’s promise of a deliverer and preferred the infant’s safety to their own. Moses, when of mature age, disregarded the adoption of Pharaoh’s daughter; he preferred to return to his own nation. He reckons among the reproaches of Christ all the ignominious trials the faithful endure; he had his eyes fixed on the promise of God. If any concludes his faith did not recumb on God’s mercy alone because he had respect to the reward—the question here is not respecting righteousness or the cause of salvation; faith as to righteousness before God does not look on reward, but on the gratuitous goodness of God, not on our works but on Christ alone; but faith, apart from justification, since it extends to every word of God, has respect to the reward that is promised. By faith he forsook Egypt, not fearing the wrath of the king; he endured as seeing him who is invisible. Through faith he kept the Passover, acquiescing in God’s word alone when the effect did not appear. By faith they passed the Red Sea; by faith Jericho fell; by faith Rahab perished not with the unbelieving.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Hebrews 11:23–31)
