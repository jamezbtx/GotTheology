Passage

Hebrews 11

Verses 32–40 · ESV · Voices

The writer admits time would fail to tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel, and the prophets—who through faith conquered, obtained promises, and were made strong out of weakness. Others suffered torture, mockery, and death, seeking a better resurrection. All these, though commended through faith, did not receive what was promised, since God provided something better for us, that apart from us they should not be made perfect.

Hebrews 11:32

32And what more shall I say? For time would fail me to tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, of David and Samuel and the prophets—

Arminian

Wesley: after Samuel, the prophets are properly mentioned. David also was a prophet; but he was a king too. The prophets—Elijah, Elisha, etc.—including likewise the believers who lived with them.

Classical Arminians hear an open-ended catalog: time fails because faith’s examples overflow. Named judges and prophets invite imitation, not mere applause.

Reformed

Calvin: he anticipates the fear that praises of faith are confined to a few; what he said of a few extended to the whole Church of God. Under David’s name he includes pious kings; with Samuel and the prophets he teaches that Judah’s kingdom was founded and stood by faith.

Reformed accent: the Church in every age is preserved by God’s hand through faith—no other way to know His kindness.

Hebrews 11:33

33who through faith conquered kingdoms, enforced justice, obtained promises, stopped the mouths of lions,

Arminian

Wesley: David in particular subdued kingdoms; Samuel wrought righteousness; the prophets obtained promises, stopped mouths of lions as Daniel.

Classical Arminians hear faith’s public fruits: conquest, justice, promises obtained, lions stopped. God answers those who trust Him in history’s crises.

Reformed

Calvin: all that was praiseworthy in them is ascribed to faith. That clause ought especially to be noticed—they obtained the promises by faith; though God continues faithful were we all unbelieving, yet our unbelief makes the promises void to us—ineffectual to us.

Reformed reading: promises are obtained by faith; unbelief empties them for the unbeliever without making God false.

Hebrews 11:34

34quenched the power of fire, escaped the edge of the sword, were made strong out of weakness, became mighty in war, put foreign armies to flight.

Arminian

Wesley: quenched the violence of fire as Shadrach’s companions; Jephthah escaped the sword; Samson out of weakness was made strong; Barak valiant; Gideon put aliens to flight. Faith overcomes impediments and inverts the course of nature.

Classical Arminians hear strength out of weakness as hope for frail believers who trust God in war and fire. Faith animates heroic enterprises without denying human frailty.

Reformed

Calvin: the Lord by His hand raised His saints when cast down and brought help to their weakness so as to endue them with full strength. By faith David returned conqueror; Daniel came safe from lions; friends walked in the furnace.

Reformed accent: made strong out of weakness is God’s work through faith, not native heroism. Goodness and bounty are communicated by faith.

Hebrews 11:35

35Women received back their dead by resurrection. Some were tortured, refusing to accept release, so that they might rise again to a better life.

Arminian

Wesley: women—naturally weak—received their dead children. Others were tortured, not accepting deliverance on sinful terms, that they might obtain a better resurrection—an higher reward, the greater their sufferings the greater their glory.

Classical Arminians hear both gift-resurrections now and refusal of shameful release for a better rising. Faith suffers rather than deny God. Pastoral courage for the persecuted.

Reformed

Calvin: saints reduced to extreme miseries struggled by faith to persevere invincible even to death. Victory of faith appears more splendid in contempt of death than if life were extended to the fifth generation. They must have purchased a short lease of life by denying God—a shameful price; that they might live forever in heaven, they rejected life on earth. Except minds sustained by hope of blessed resurrection, they must have immediately failed.

Reformed confession: better life/resurrection hope keeps faith from accepting release by apostasy. Patience under the cross is faith’s power.

Hebrews 11:36

36Others suffered mocking and flogging, and even chains and imprisonment.

Arminian

Wesley: the apostle seems here to pass on to recent examples—mockings, scourgings, bonds, imprisonment.

Classical Arminians hear ordinary brutalities inside faith’s catalog. Not only miracles; also chains. Hearers under scorn are not outside the story.

Reformed

Calvin: reproaches, want, and extreme troubles borne with resignation are a more glorious evidence of faith than miraculous recovery. Fortitude of the saints in all ages was the work of faith; we are not capable of overcoming evils except faith sustains us.

Reformed accent: mocking and chains are faith’s arena. Fainting under persecution proves unbelief; faith’s nature is the same now as then.

Hebrews 11:37

37They were stoned, they were sawn in two, they were killed with the sword. They went about in skins of sheep and goats, destitute, afflicted, mistreated—

Arminian

Wesley: sawn asunder—as, according to Jewish tradition, Isaiah was by Manasseh. Torments and death mentioned alternately; tempted every way by threatenings, tortures, promises, and allurements.

Classical Arminians hear the cost catalog without romance: stones, saws, swords, goatskins, destitution. Faith does not always win publicly in this age.

Reformed

Calvin: points to persecutions such as Antiochus carried on and those that followed. Mean and rough clothing of saints wandering in deserts. We ought not to refuse the Lord’s favor of being connected with so many holy men exercised by many sufferings—common persecutions of the Church, not of a few only.

Reformed reading: mistreatment is not God’s absence; it is the shared lot of the faithful whom God cared for before us.

Hebrews 11:38

38of whom the world was not worthy—wandering about in deserts and mountains, and in dens and caves of the earth.

Arminian

Wesley: of whom the world was not worthy—it did not deserve so great a blessing. They wandered, being driven out from men.

Classical Arminians hear inverted verdict: the world is unworthy of the saints it drives out. Exile is not proof God forgot them.

Reformed

Calvin: the Apostle inverts the sentiment—the world was not worthy of them. Wherever God’s servants come, they bring His blessing. Whenever the righteous are taken away, such events are presages of evil; we are unworthy of having them with us. Let us boldly despise the world; were it to cast us out, God provides for our safety.

Reformed accent: caves and dens may be safer than hostile temples. God’s servants are the world’s unrecognized blessing.

Hebrews 11:39

39And all these, though commended through their faith, did not receive what was promised,

Arminian

Wesley: all these, though they obtained a good testimony, yet did not receive the great promise, the heavenly inheritance.

Classical Arminians hear commendation without full possession yet—faith’s “not yet” that still finishes well. The promise remains ahead for them and for us who believe.

Reformed

Calvin: argument from the less to the greater—if they on whom grace’s light had not so brightly shone displayed such constancy, what ought the Gospel’s full brightness to produce in us? A small spark led them to heaven; when the sun of righteousness shines, how can we excuse cleaving to earth? “Received not the promise” means its ultimate fulfillment in Christ.

Reformed accent: commendation through faith without yet receiving the ultimate promise presses us who have clearer light to endure.

Hebrews 11:40

40since God had provided something better for us, that apart from us they should not be made perfect.

Arminian

Wesley: that they might not be perfected without us—that is, that we might all be perfected together in heaven.

Classical Arminians hear corporate hope: something better for us, and perfection together—not a lonely finish. The fathers wait with the church; hearers are summoned into that one perfected company by persevering faith.

Reformed

Calvin: God intended to unite us all into one body, and distributed a small portion of grace to them that He might defer full perfection to our time, even to the coming of Christ. Singular evidence of benevolence: in all He bestowed on Abraham, Moses, David, and the prophets, He had regard for us, that we might be united with them in Christ’s body. Doubly ungrateful if less faith appears in us under Christ’s kingdom than the fathers showed under the Law.

Reformed confession: something better is gospel fullness in Christ; they are not perfected apart from us because God ordered one body and one perfection in His time.

Key contrast Both confess one people of God perfected together, and both refuse a faith that stops short of endurance. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s perfected-together-in-heaven as the shared hope that summons every hearer to finish with the fathers. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s greater gospel light and electing provision of “something better”—one body ordered by God so that halting yet approved faith then, and clearer grace now, both press persevering constancy rather than a faith that may quietly fail.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the judges and prophets, suffering faith, the better resurrection, and perfection not apart from us—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Hebrews 11:32–40.

Arminian Notable voices

“And what shall I more say? for the time would fail me to tell of Gedeon, and of Barak, and of Samson, and of Jephthae; of David also, and Samuel, and of the prophets. Faith animates to the most heroic enterprises; faith overcomes all impediments. Women received their dead raised to life again: and others were tortured, not accepting deliverance; that they might obtain a better resurrection—an higher reward, seeing the greater their sufferings the greater would be their glory. And all these, though they obtained a good testimony, yet did not receive the great promise, the heavenly inheritance. God having provided some better thing for us, that they without us should not be made perfect—that is, that we might all be perfected together in heaven.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Hebrews 11:32–35, 39–40)

Reformed Notable voices

“As it was to be feared that by referring to a few examples he should appear to confine the praises of faith to a few, he says there would be no end if he dwelt on every instance. There was not one of them whose faith did not halt—Gideon hesitated, Barak trembled, Samson betrayed, Jephthah marred victory by a foolish vow—yet faith, though halting and imperfect, is still approved by God. Saints reduced to extreme miseries struggled by faith so as to persevere invincible even to death; the victory of faith appears more splendid in the contempt of death than if life were extended to the fifth generation. If they on whom the light of grace had not as yet so brightly shone displayed so great a constancy, what ought the full brightness of the Gospel to produce in us? God intended to unite us all into one body, and distributed a small portion of grace to them, that he might defer its full perfection to our time, even to the coming of Christ. By “they received not the promise” is to be understood its ultimate fulfillment, which took place in Christ.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Hebrews 11:32–40)
