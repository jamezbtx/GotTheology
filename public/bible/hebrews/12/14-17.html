Passage

Hebrews 12

Verses 14–17 · ESV · Voices

After the call to strengthen weak knees, the writer presses peace with everyone and the holiness without which no one will see the Lord. The church must watch that no one fails to obtain grace, that no root of bitterness springs up to defile many, and that no one becomes sexually immoral or unholy like Esau, who sold his birthright for a meal and afterward found no chance to repent though he sought the blessing with tears.

Hebrews 12:14

14Strive for peace with everyone, and for the holiness without which no one will see the Lord.

Arminian

Wesley: follow peace with all—this branch concerns neighbors; holiness concerns God. Not following after all holiness is the direct way to fall into sin of every kind.

Classical Arminians hear a double pursuit: peace and holiness, both to be followed. Without holiness none see the Lord—a real warning for hearers who must pursue sanctification, not a bare slogan over the already secure.

Reformed

Calvin: men naturally shun peace by studying their own interest; strive with all care, forgetting offenses. Yet peace with the ungodly must not approve vice—holiness together with peace. Without holiness none see the Lord; we see God with eyes renewed after His image.

Reformed accent: peace is pursued; holiness is the bond of union with God. Seeing the Lord requires the holiness He works in His people.

Hebrews 12:15

15See to it that no one fails to obtain the grace of God; that no "root of bitterness" springs up and causes trouble, and by it many become defiled;

Arminian

Wesley: looking diligently lest any—if he do not lift drooping hands—fall from the grace of God. Root of bitterness—envy, anger, suspicion—destroys peace; any corruption in doctrine or practice may pollute many.

Classical Arminians hear a congregational watch: grace can be failed of; bitterness can spring and defile. The warning is pastoral and real for those still in the race—see to it, lest any fall.

Reformed

Calvin: attention is required because as soon as Satan sees us secure he circumvents us. Under grace he includes whole vocation. Inferring freewill cooperation from the precept is frivolous; the Lord who warns also moves hearts. Cut down bitter roots lest they choke good seed; hypocrites mixed with the good must not be allowed to grow unchecked.

Reformed reading: failing of grace is a real church danger under vigilance language; diligence itself is God’s gift. Bitterness defiles many—discipline the pest.

Key contrast Both urge diligent watch so that none miss God’s grace and that bitterness not defile many, and both refuse a careless church. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s “fall from the grace of God” as a real danger for those who grow slack—Esau-like failure is possible for professors who do not endure. Reformed readers typically hear the same vigilance while insisting with Calvin that required diligence is God’s gift and that those who finally fail never truly possessed saving grace—Esau as the profane who never valued the birthright.

Hebrews 12:16

16that no one is sexually immoral or unholy like Esau, who sold his birthright for a single meal.

Arminian

Wesley: Esau was profane for so slighting the blessing along with the birthright. One morsel traded away a sacred privilege.

Classical Arminians hear Esau as a warning example for hearers who might trade holiness for appetite. Sexual immorality and profaneness are the opposite of the holiness of v. 14. Use the present time; do not sell what God has set before you.

Reformed

Calvin: fornicator names a particular defilement; profane is the general contrary of holiness. Esau valued one meal above the birthright and lost the blessing. Profane are those in whom worldly love so reigns that they forget heaven—ambition, money, gluttony, pleasures crowding out Christ’s kingdom.

Reformed accent: birthright language evokes the honor of the firstborn called to eternal life. Selling it for a meal exposes a heart that never prized God’s gift.

Hebrews 12:17

17For you know that afterward, when he desired to inherit the blessing, he was rejected, for he found no chance to repent, though he sought it with tears.

Arminian

Wesley: he was rejected—could not obtain it. No place for repentance—no room for such repentance as would regain what he lost. He sought the blessing diligently with tears—too late. Let us use the present time.

Classical Arminians hear a sober urgency: afterward may be too late. Tears without timely turning do not reopen what was sold. The pastoral point is present obedience, not despair of all who weep.

Reformed

Calvin: he laughed when full, then too late found his loss. Rejected means denied his request. No place of repentance—he gained nothing by late repentance; these tears were not sincere conversion to God but terror of a man past hope seeking the blessing, not knocking at God’s door by faith. The Apostle warns despisers of grace to take heed lest the same befall them.

Reformed confession: Esau’s tears prove that remorse without faith does not regain the birthright. The warning stands; God’s severity on profane mockers is real.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on peace and holiness, failing of grace, the root of bitterness, and Esau’s profane trade—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Hebrews 12:14–17.

Arminian Notable voices

“Follow peace with all men, and holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord. Looking diligently lest any man fall from the grace of God; lest any root of bitterness—of envy, anger, suspicion—springing up destroy the sweet peace; lest any, not following after holiness, fall into fornication or profaneness. Esau was profane for so slighting the blessing which went along with the birthright. He was rejected—he could not obtain it. He found no place for repentance—there was no room for any such repentance as would regain what he had lost. He sought the blessing diligently with tears—he sought too late. Let us use the present time.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Hebrews 12:14–17)

Reformed Notable voices

“Follow peace with all, yet with holiness; without holiness no man shall see the Lord. Looking diligently lest any fail of the grace of God—under grace he includes our whole vocation. That attention is required shows how easily we fall away if secure; yet to infer that grace is not efficacious unless we cooperate of ourselves is frivolous—the diligence required is God’s gift. Lest any root of bitterness corrupt many. Esau set more value on one meal than on his birthright and lost the blessing; profane are they in whom love of the world so prevails that they forget heaven. He found no place of repentance—he gained nothing by late repentance; his tears were not those of sincere conversion but of a man past hope who sought the blessing, not God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Hebrews 12:14–17)
