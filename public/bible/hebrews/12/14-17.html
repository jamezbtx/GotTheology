Passage

Hebrews 12

Verses 14–17 · WEB · Voices

The writer urges the readers to pursue peace with everyone and the holiness without which no one will see the Lord. They must see to it that no one fails to obtain the grace of God, that no bitter root springs up, and that no one is sexually immoral or godless like Esau, who sold his birthright for a single meal and afterward could not reverse it. Verse 14 is a key text in Wesleyan teaching on holiness, and verse 15 raises the question of falling short of grace.

Hebrews 12:14

14Follow after peace with all men, and the sanctification without which no man will see the Lord,

Arminian

Strive for peace with everyone, and for the holiness without which no one will see the Lord. Wesleyan readers see a real holiness of heart and life that God requires and grace makes possible. Wesley often cited this verse in teaching sanctification.

Reformed

Reformed readers see holiness as the necessary fruit of justification, not its ground. Everyone who is justified will also be sanctified.

Key contrast Both affirm that holiness is necessary to see the Lord. Wesleyan readers stress a holiness grace makes attainable and believers must pursue. Reformed readers stress that holiness always follows justification as its fruit.

Hebrews 12:15

15looking carefully lest there be any man who falls short of the grace of God, lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you and many be defiled by it,

Arminian

See to it that no one fails to obtain the grace of God, and that no root of bitterness springs up. Arminian readers see the real possibility of falling short of grace, which the community must guard against.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a warning to the visible church, where some may profess faith without having grace.

Key contrast Both take the warning seriously. Arminian readers see a real danger that someone could fall away from grace once received. Reformed readers see it describing those in the church who never truly received grace.

Hebrews 12:16

16lest there be any sexually immoral person or profane person, like Esau, who sold his birthright for one meal.

Arminian

Let no one be sexually immoral or godless like Esau, who sold his birthright for a meal. Arminian readers see Esau as an example of trading eternal blessing for momentary pleasure.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Esau as an example of a profane person who despised spiritual things, and some connect him with Romans 9:13.

Hebrews 12:17

17For you know that even when he afterward desired to inherit the blessing, he was rejected, for he found no place for a change of mind though he sought it diligently with tears.

Arminian

Afterward he wanted the blessing but was rejected; he found no chance to repent, though he sought it with tears. Arminian readers see a warning that a time can come when it is too late, after persistent rejection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Esau’s tears as regret over lost blessing, not true repentance. Some read “no place for repentance” as no way to change Isaac’s decision.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What older interpreters in each camp said about peace and holiness, failing of grace, the root of bitterness, and Esau’s profane trade.

Arminian Notable voices

“Follow peace with all men, and holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord. Looking diligently lest any man fall from the grace of God; lest any root of bitterness—of envy, anger, suspicion—springing up destroy the sweet peace; lest any, not following after holiness, fall into fornication or profaneness.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Hebrews 12:14–17)

Reformed Notable voices

“Follow peace with all, yet with holiness; without holiness no man shall see the Lord. Looking diligently lest any fail of the grace of God—under grace he includes our whole vocation.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Hebrews 12:14–17)
