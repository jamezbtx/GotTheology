Passage

Hebrews 12

Verses 18–29 · WEB

The writer contrasts two mountains. Believers have not come to Sinai, with its fire, darkness, and terrifying voice, but to Mount Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, with innumerable angels, the assembly of the firstborn enrolled in heaven, God the judge, the spirits of the righteous made perfect, and Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant. They must not refuse the one who speaks from heaven. God will shake earth and heaven once more, leaving only what cannot be shaken. Receiving an unshakable kingdom, believers should worship God with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire. Both traditions see the glory of the new covenant here. They differ on the force of the closing warning.

Hebrews 12:18

18For you have not come to a mountain that might be touched and that burned with fire, and to blackness, darkness, storm,

Arminian

You have not come to what may be touched, a blazing fire, darkness, gloom, and storm. Arminian readers see the terrors of the law contrasted with the grace of the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Sinai as the covenant of the law, which revealed God’s holiness and human guilt.

Hebrews 12:19

19the sound of a trumpet, and the voice of words; which those who heard it begged that not one more word should be spoken to them,

Arminian

The sound of a trumpet and a voice whose words made the hearers beg that no more be spoken. Arminian readers see the fear the law produces in sinners.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law’s voice driving sinners to seek a mediator.

Hebrews 12:20

20for they could not stand that which was commanded, “If even an animal touches the mountain, it shall be stoned”.

Arminian

They could not endure the command that even a beast touching the mountain must be stoned. Arminian readers see the distance the law put between God and sinners.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s holiness making direct approach impossible without a mediator.

Hebrews 12:21

21So fearful was the appearance that Moses said, “I am terrified and trembling.”

Arminian

Even Moses said, “I tremble with fear.” Arminian readers see that even the greatest servant trembled before God’s holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fear of the law, contrasted with the confidence of the gospel.

Hebrews 12:22

22But you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to innumerable multitudes of angels,

Arminian

You have come to Mount Zion, the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and innumerable angels. Arminian readers see believers already sharing in the heavenly assembly by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church’s present union with the heavenly city.

Hebrews 12:23

23to the festal gathering and assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven, to God the Judge of all, to the spirits of just men made perfect,

Arminian

To the assembly of the firstborn enrolled in heaven, to God the judge of all, and to the spirits of the righteous made perfect. Arminian readers see the enrolled as the faithful people of God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see names “enrolled in heaven” as a picture of election, recorded by God.

Hebrews 12:24

24to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the blood of sprinkling that speaks better than that of Abel.

Arminian

To Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the sprinkled blood that speaks better than Abel’s. Arminian readers see Christ’s blood speaking mercy to all who come.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s blood speaking forgiveness for his people, while Abel’s blood cried for vengeance.

Hebrews 12:25

25See that you don’t refuse him who speaks. For if they didn’t escape when they refused him who warned on the earth, how much more will we not escape who turn away from him who warns from heaven,

Arminian

See that you do not refuse him who is speaking. If they did not escape, how much less will we. Arminian readers see a real warning to believers about turning away.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a warning God uses to keep his people listening.

Key contrast Both read the verse as a serious warning. Arminian readers see a real danger that someone who has come to Zion could turn away. Reformed readers see the warning as God’s means of keeping his people from doing so.

Hebrews 12:26

26whose voice shook the earth then, but now he has promised, saying, “Yet once more I will shake not only the earth, but also the heavens.”

Arminian

“Yet once more I will shake not only the earth but also the heavens.” Arminian readers see the coming judgment as the final shaking of creation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s final judgment and renewal of all things.

Hebrews 12:27

27This phrase, “Yet once more” signifies the removing of those things that are shaken, as of things that have been made, that those things which are not shaken may remain.

Arminian

“Yet once more” indicates the removal of what is shaken, so that what cannot be shaken may remain. Arminian readers see only what is founded on Christ surviving.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the permanence of God’s kingdom and his people.

Hebrews 12:28

28Therefore, receiving a Kingdom that can’t be shaken, let’s have grace, through which we serve God acceptably, with reverence and awe,

Arminian

Receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, let us be grateful and offer acceptable worship with reverence and awe. Arminian readers see gratitude and reverence as the fitting response to grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an unshakable kingdom as a gift that grounds assurance and grateful worship.

Hebrews 12:29

29for our God is a consuming fire.

Arminian

Our God is a consuming fire. Arminian readers see God’s holiness as a reason for serious faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s holiness as unchanged in the new covenant, calling for reverent worship.
