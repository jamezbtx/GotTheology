Passage

Hebrews 12

Verses 1–13 · WEB · Voices

Surrounded by the cloud of witnesses from chapter 11, the church is urged to lay aside every weight and clinging sin and run with perseverance the race set before us, looking to Jesus, the author and perfecter of faith. Considering his cross keeps faint hearts from wearying. Affliction is then reframed as the Lord’s loving discipline of sons—painful now, later yielding the peaceful fruit of righteousness—so drooping hands and weak knees may be lifted and lame feet healed.

Hebrews 12:1

1Therefore let’s also, seeing we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, lay aside every weight and the sin which so easily entangles us, and let’s run with perseverance the race that is set before us,

Arminian

Wesley: encompassed with a cloud—a great multitude tending upward with holy swiftness—witnesses of the power of faith. Lay aside every weight that damps the soul’s vigour, and the sin which easily besets—of constitution, education, profession—and run with patience the race set before us.

The accent falls on a sincere summons to endurance: the cloud cheers runners who may yet finish. Weights and clinging sin are to be thrown off by grace-enabled effort.

Reformed

Calvin: the dense throng of chapter 11 should stimulate imitation; faith is sufficiently proved by their testimony. Lay aside burdens that impede the course—worldly loves and cares—and the fountain of sin, concupiscence that snares on every side. The metaphor is a running contest calling forth greatest exertions; Christ invites to the prize.

Witnesses are associates who have shown the way, not rivals. Endurance is faith’s patience seeking the invisible kingdom. The race is set before those God surrounds with examples.

Hebrews 12:2

2looking to Jesus, the author and perfecter of faith, who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising its shame, and has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God.

Arminian

Wesley: looking from all other things to Jesus—as the wounded to the brazen serpent. Author and finisher—who begins faith in us, carries it on, and perfects it. For the joy set before him he patiently endured the cross and is set down where there is fulness of joy.

Arminians glory that Jesus begins, carries, and perfects faith in those who look to Him. The cross is the model for runners who may yet endure. Looking is the living act of trust, not a private decree announced after the fact.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ underwent bitter, ignominious death though He might have exempted Himself; joy set before Him may mean instead of earthly enjoyment, or the blessed issue. He endured death and despised shame; the glorious end teaches that evils end in salvation if we follow Christ. He is not only umpire but supplies strength and leads to the race’s end.

Founder and perfecter names Christ’s work from start to finish. Seated at God’s right hand is the pledge that sufferers who belong to Him will share His outcome.

Hebrews 12:3

3For consider him who has endured such contradiction of sinners against himself, that you don’t grow weary, fainting in your souls.

Arminian

Wesley: consider—draw the comparison. The Lord bore all this; shall His servants bear nothing? Such enmity of every kind—lest ye be weary, dull and languid, and faint in your course.

What comes through is pastoral comparison that strengthens runners against fainting. Hostility is expected; considering Jesus keeps the mind from quitting mid-race.

Reformed

Calvin: if the Son of God willingly underwent such conflicts, who dares refuse the same? Knowing we are associates of the Son who came down to our condition conquers temptation and gathers courage that would otherwise melt into despair.

Consideration of Christ is the Spirit’s means against weariness. Faintheartedness is met by His example, not by self-steel.

Hebrews 12:4

4You have not yet resisted to blood, striving against sin.

Arminian

Wesley: unto blood—unto wounds and death. They have not yet reached martyrdom’s extremity.

Arminians find a sober scale: present struggle is not denied, yet short of blood. The warning is to keep striving, not to despise lesser trials or claim they have already finished.

Reformed

Calvin: even persecutions for the gospel are a contest against sin—remedies that destroy sin, keep flesh from wantonness, and sometimes chastise. He addresses those who had already suffered loss yet were fainting halfway; Christ will have no discharged soldiers but those who have conquered death itself.

Not yet unto blood exposes sloth mid-contest. Struggle against sin includes resisting apostasy; God uses affliction as discipline for His people’s good.

Hebrews 12:5

5You have forgotten the exhortation which reasons with you as with children, “My son, don’t take lightly the chastening of the Lord, nor faint when you are reproved by him;

Arminian

Wesley: ye seem already to have forgotten the exhortation wherein God speaks with utmost tenderness. Despise not chastening—do not slight it or impute affliction to chance; see and revere God’s hand. Neither faint when rebuked—endure patiently and fruitfully.

Arminian readers hear as sons as an address calling for response: neither slight nor faint under the Father’s hand. Discipline is for those God speaks to as children who may yet endure fruitfully.

Reformed

Calvin: reads as a question—have ye forgotten?—intimating it is not yet time to forget. Solomon’s word “My son” should allure hearts. Two parts: do not reject the Lord’s correction; the reason is He loves those He chastises.

The exhortation addresses the church as sons. Forgetting it is spiritual amnesia under the cross; paternal speech binds hearers to endure as children.

Hebrews 12:6

6for whom the Lord loves, he disciplines, and chastises every son whom he receives.”

Arminian

Wesley: for—all springs from love; therefore neither despise nor faint. Love is the fountain of the rod.

Classical Arminians hear discipline as the Father’s love toward sons He receives—those under His hand who are called to endure. The rod is not rejection; it is love’s training so that sons may share holiness (v. 10).

Reformed

Calvin: though God visits elect and reprobate, as to the elect He has no other end but their salvation—paternal love. The reprobate often miss God’s hand as chance; the godly, persuaded they are chastised by God, are led to know they are loved. Judgment begins at God’s house; domestics feel peculiar care.

Every son whom He receives is chastened. Love and reception ground the rod; those kept as sons taste Fatherly scourges for salvation.

Key contrast Both confess that discipline springs from the Lord’s love and that hearers must neither despise nor faint under it, and both refuse a works-earned sonship. Arminians hear the Father disciplining sons He receives—those addressed and trained who may yet endure and share His holiness. Reformed readers hear chastisement as paternal love toward the elect He receives and keeps—Fatherly scourges that promote their salvation, not chance blows on those outside His house.

Hebrews 12:7

7It is for discipline that you endure. God deals with you as with children, for what son is there whom his father doesn’t discipline?

Arminian

Wesley: whom his father chastens not—when he offends. Enduring chastening is how God deals with you as sons.

Wesleyan Arminians hear endurance under discipline as the path of sonship’s dealings. The common-sense analogy presses: fathers discipline; so does God. Treat the hardship as training, not as proof you are cast off.

Reformed

Calvin: if no prudent father exempts children from correction, how much less will God neglect so necessary a remedy? As long as we live we are children before God; the rod remains. Those who seek exemption from the cross withdraw from the number of His children.

God treating you as sons is adoption’s evidence in the cross. Endurance is the mark of true children under the Father’s hand.

Hebrews 12:8

8But if you are without discipline, of which all have been made partakers, then you are illegitimate, and not children.

Arminian

Wesley: of which all sons are partakers—more or less. Absence of chastening would mark illegitimacy, not privilege.

Here the reader meets a pastoral warning: craving an undisciplined life is craving bastardy. All true sons share the Father’s training to some degree. Stay under the hand that proves belonging.

Reformed

Calvin: he calls refusants bastards rather than aliens because he addresses church members—children of God by profession. Withdrawing from the Father’s discipline would make the profession of Christ false; they would be bastards, no more children.

All sons participate; exemption claims expose spurious sonship. Discipline is the family mark of those truly in the house.

Hebrews 12:9

9Furthermore, we had the fathers of our flesh to chasten us, and we paid them respect. Shall we not much rather be in subjection to the Father of spirits and live?

Arminian

Wesley: we neither despised nor fainted under earthly correction. Much rather submit with reverence to the Father of spirits—that we may live with Him forever.

Arminian interpreters hear life as the end of subjection: submit and live. The a fortiori from fleshly fathers presses willing meekness under God. Life with Him is the promised outcome for those who yield.

Reformed

Calvin: if we reverenced harsh earthly fathers, much more honor is due our spiritual Father. By “and live” he points the end—that we may live; nothing is more ruinous than refusing to surrender to God.

Father of spirits names God’s peculiar claim. Subjection is just concession to His Fatherly authority; life hangs on that surrender.

Hebrews 12:10

10For they indeed for a few days disciplined us as seemed good to them, but he for our profit, that we may be partakers of his holiness.

Arminian

Wesley: earthly fathers for a few days chastened as they thought good—often erring by indulgence or severity. He always, unquestionably, for our profit, that we may be partakers of His holiness—of Himself and His glorious image.

Wesley’s tradition hears holiness-sharing as the clear aim of divine discipline. God’s rod is wiser than human parenting; the goal is participation in His character for those trained by it.

Reformed

Calvin: men’s discipline serves the present life; God’s looks to prepare us for eternal life. Chastisements help sanctify—helps to mortify the flesh—not as though they make us holy of themselves, but as the Lord exercises us in mortification.

For our good unto sharing His holiness is God’s perfect wisdom. The fruit is spiritual and perpetual, far above civil training.

Hebrews 12:11

11All chastening seems for the present to be not joyous but grievous; yet afterward it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it.

Arminian

Wesley: all chastening is for the present grievous, yet yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness—holiness and happiness—to them exercised thereby, who receive it as from God and improve it according to His will.

This is “trained by it” as cooperation under grace: receive and improve. Pain now, peaceful fruit later, for those who do not waste the exercise.

Reformed

Calvin: do not measure chastisements by present feelings; we are like children who dread the rod. Fix eyes on the end and receive the peaceable fruit of righteousness—fear of the Lord and a godly life of which the cross is teacher. Peaceable, because adversity alarms with impatient noise until a resigned mind sees the profit.

Later fruit belongs to those exercised by it. The cross teaches righteousness; present pain is not the verdict.

Hebrews 12:12

12Therefore lift up the hands that hang down and the feeble knees,

Arminian

Wesley: lift up the hands—your own or your brethren’s—that hang down, unable to continue the combat; and feeble knees unable to continue the race.

Those in Wesley’s line hear mutual and personal strengthening after the discipline lesson. Therefore—because the Father loves and trains—lift what hangs down. The race and combat continue by renewed vigor.

Reformed

Calvin: nothing weakens more than tasting no grace in adversity; knowing God is present even when He afflicts raises us. Not only bear afflictions; do not be slothful in duty. Fear of the cross folds hands; remove that fear and members are ready for God’s will.

Consolation that God solicits our welfare in the cross puts new vigor in hands and knees for obedience.

Hebrews 12:13

13and make straight paths for your feet, so what is lame may not be dislocated, but rather be healed.

Arminian

Wesley: make straight paths for your own and their feet—remove every hindrance and offence. The lame—weak, scarce able to walk—must not be turned out of the way of faith and holiness, but healed.

Arminians hear communal care: straighten paths so the weak are healed, not dislocated. Endurance includes clearing stumbling blocks for others still in the race.

Reformed

Calvin: walk prudently in a straight course; fear finds byways. Prepare to endure evils and go straight where the Lord calls. Halting—fluctuating, not sincerely devoted—if unchecked turns far from God; remove it in due time lest alienation follow.

Straight paths are obedience according to God’s will without fear’s detours. Healing the lame is better than letting weakness wander into labyrinth.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on the race of faith, looking to Jesus, and the Father’s discipline of sons, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“Let us lay aside every weight—whatever weighs us down or damps the vigour of our soul—and the sin which easily besetteth us, and run with patience the race set before us. Looking unto Jesus—the author and finisher of our faith—who begins it in us, carries it on, and perfects it; who for the joy set before him patiently endured the cross. Despise not the chastening of the Lord; neither faint when rebuked of him. For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth: all springs from love. If ye endure chastening, God dealeth with you as with sons. He disciplines us for our profit, that we may be partakers of his holiness. Afterward chastening yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness to them that are exercised thereby—that receive this exercise as from God and improve it according to his will.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Hebrews 12:1–2, 5–6, 10–11)

Reformed Notable voices

“This conclusion is an epilogue to the former chapter: the dense throng of witnesses should stimulate us to imitate them. Lay aside every burden that delays our spiritual course—love of this life, lusts, cares, riches—and the sin which so easily besets us, even concupiscence that holds us on every side. Run with patience the contest; Christ is not only umpire but extends his hand and supplies strength to the end. Whom the Lord loveth he chastens: as to the elect he has no other end but to promote their salvation—a demonstration of paternal love. God is offered as a Father to all who endure correction; those who kick like restive horses do not belong to this class. Bastards rather than aliens—he addresses members of the Church who would become false professors if they withdrew from the Father’s discipline.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Hebrews 12:1–2, 6–8)
