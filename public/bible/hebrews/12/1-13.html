Passage

Hebrews 12

Verses 1–13 · WEB · Voices

Surrounded by the great cloud of faithful witnesses from chapter 11, believers are to lay aside every weight and sin and run their race with endurance, looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of faith, who endured the cross. The readers’ hardships are God’s fatherly discipline, a sign that they are true children. Discipline is painful for a time, but it produces the peaceful fruit of righteousness. So they should strengthen weak hands and make straight paths. Both traditions draw deep comfort here. They differ on the meaning of “founder and perfecter of faith.”

Hebrews 12:1

1Therefore let’s also, seeing we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, lay aside every weight and the sin which so easily entangles us, and let’s run with perseverance the race that is set before us,

Arminian

Surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, let us lay aside every weight and sin, and run with endurance. Arminian readers see the race as a real contest that must be finished faithfully.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the race as one that all true believers will finish, through the endurance God supplies.

Hebrews 12:2

2looking to Jesus, the author and perfecter of faith, who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising its shame, and has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God.

Arminian

Looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith, who for the joy set before him endured the cross. Arminian readers see Jesus as the pioneer who blazed the trail of faith and brings it to completion in those who follow him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus as the author who begins faith in his people and the finisher who brings it to completion. Faith is his work from start to finish.

Key contrast Both see Jesus as the source and goal of faith. Arminian readers stress him as pioneer and model whom believers follow. Reformed readers stress him as the one who creates faith in his people and ensures it is completed.

Hebrews 12:3

3For consider him who has endured such contradiction of sinners against himself, that you don’t grow weary, fainting in your souls.

Arminian

Consider him who endured hostility from sinners, so that you may not grow weary. Arminian readers see Christ’s example strengthening believers to continue.

Reformed

Reformed readers see meditation on Christ as the means God uses to renew strength.

Hebrews 12:4

4You have not yet resisted to blood, striving against sin.

Arminian

You have not yet resisted to the point of shedding blood. Arminian readers see a call to greater perseverance in the struggle against sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the struggle against sin as part of sanctification.

Hebrews 12:5

5You have forgotten the exhortation which reasons with you as with children, “My son, don’t take lightly the chastening of the Lord, nor faint when you are reproved by him;

Arminian

“Do not despise the Lord’s discipline.” Arminian readers see trials as purposeful, meant to strengthen faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see discipline as God’s fatherly care, never punishment for believers in the judicial sense.

Hebrews 12:6

6for whom the Lord loves, he disciplines, and chastises every son whom he receives.”

Arminian

The Lord disciplines the one he loves. Arminian readers see discipline as an expression of love that calls for a response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see discipline flowing from God’s electing love for his children.

Hebrews 12:7

7It is for discipline that you endure. God deals with you as with children, for what son is there whom his father doesn’t discipline?

Arminian

God is treating you as sons. Arminian readers see hardship as evidence of belonging to God’s family.

Reformed

Reformed readers see adoption as the reason for discipline.

Hebrews 12:8

8But if you are without discipline, of which all have been made partakers, then you are illegitimate, and not children.

Arminian

If you are left without discipline, you are illegitimate, not sons. Arminian readers see a warning to examine whether one’s life shows God’s fatherly hand.

Reformed

Reformed readers see discipline as a mark of genuine sonship, distinguishing true believers from false professors.

Hebrews 12:9

9Furthermore, we had the fathers of our flesh to chasten us, and we paid them respect. Shall we not much rather be in subjection to the Father of spirits and live?

Arminian

We respected earthly fathers; shall we not much more submit to the Father of spirits and live? Arminian readers see submission as a real response believers must give.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the Father whose discipline gives life.

Hebrews 12:10

10For they indeed for a few days disciplined us as seemed good to them, but he for our profit, that we may be partakers of his holiness.

Arminian

God disciplines us for our good, that we may share his holiness. Wesleyan readers see holiness as the goal God pursues in his children.

Reformed

Reformed readers see discipline as one of God’s means of sanctification.

Hebrews 12:11

11All chastening seems for the present to be not joyous but grievous; yet afterward it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it.

Arminian

Discipline seems painful, but later yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those trained by it. Arminian readers note “to those trained by it”: the fruit comes as believers respond rightly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God producing righteousness through trials, which he uses for his people’s good (Romans 8:28).

Hebrews 12:12

12Therefore lift up the hands that hang down and the feeble knees,

Arminian

Lift your drooping hands and strengthen your weak knees. Arminian readers see a call to renewed effort in the life of faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see encouragement for the weary, grounded in God’s fatherly purpose.

Hebrews 12:13

13and make straight paths for your feet, so what is lame may not be dislocated, but rather be healed.

Arminian

Make straight paths, so that what is lame may be healed rather than put out of joint. Arminian readers see concern for weaker members who might fall away.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church’s care for the weak as a means God uses to restore them.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on the race of faith, looking to Jesus, and the Father’s discipline of sons, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“Let us lay aside every weight—whatever weighs us down or damps the vigour of our soul—and the sin which easily besetteth us, and run with patience the race set before us. Looking unto Jesus—the author and finisher of our faith—who begins it in us, carries it on, and perfects it; who for the joy set before him patiently endured the cross.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Hebrews 12:1–2, 5–6, 10–11)

Reformed Notable voices

“This conclusion is an epilogue to the former chapter: the dense throng of witnesses should stimulate us to imitate them. Lay aside every burden that delays our spiritual course—love of this life, lusts, cares, riches—and the sin which so easily besets us, even concupiscence that holds us on every side.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Hebrews 12:1–2, 6–8)
