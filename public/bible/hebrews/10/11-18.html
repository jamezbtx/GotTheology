Passage

Hebrews 10

Verses 11–18 · ESV · Voices

Priests stand daily offering the same sacrifices that can never take away sins; Christ offered a single sacrifice for sins and sat down at God's right hand. By that single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified, and the Spirit witnesses from Jeremiah: God will remember their sins and lawless deeds no more—so where there is forgiveness, there is no longer any offering for sin.

Hebrews 10:11

11And every priest stands daily at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins.

Arminian

Every priest stands daily, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices that can never take away sins. Wesley: every priest standeth as a servant in an humble posture.

Both camps: standing is unfinished work. The daily service is not diligence praised but inability exposed. Classical Arminians and Reformed together refuse to treat repetition as a path to perfection; only Christ's single offering takes away sins.

Reformed

Calvin: the conclusion of the whole argument—daily sacrificing is inconsistent with and wholly foreign to the priesthood of Christ; after his coming the Levitical priests were deposed from their office.

Reformed accent: when Christ's priesthood is set up, the other falls. The same sacrifices that can never take away sins are not a second gospel; they are the shadow's last word about its own limit.

Hebrews 10:12

12But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, he sat down at the right hand of God,

Arminian

But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, he sat down at the right hand of God. Wesley: the virtue of whose one sacrifice remains for ever; he sat down as a son, in majesty and honour.

Shared: single sacrifice; session at the right hand. Sitting means the priestly work of offering is finished. Arminian pastoral: the finished sacrifice is the ground of a sincere gospel; we do not re-offer Christ, we receive him by faith.

Reformed

Calvin: lest men imagine Christ idle in heaven, he sat down at God's right hand—his dominion and power. He will not suffer the efficacy of his death to be destroyed or buried; he lives that by his power he may fill heaven and earth.

Reformed confession: one sacrifice for all time, then session. The cross is not incomplete; the throne is not unemployed. Intercession and reign continue; expiatory offering does not.

Hebrews 10:13

13waiting from that time until his enemies should be made a footstool for his feet.

Arminian

Waiting until his enemies are made a footstool—Psalm 110. The finished offering does not cancel the coming victory; it secures it.

Both camps urge patience under the session of Christ. Enemies remain for a time; the seated Priest-King will have them underfoot. Faith waits without inventing another sacrifice.

Reformed

Calvin: this state continues until Christ lays prostrate all his enemies. If our faith seeks Christ sitting on God's right hand, we shall enjoy the fruit of his victory when Satan, sin, death, and the world are vanquished.

Reformed pastoral: waiting is not uncertainty about the offering; it is certainty about the reign. The footstool promise keeps hope forward-looking after the once-for-all cross.

Hebrews 10:14

14For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified.

Arminian

By a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified. Wesley: he hath perfected them for ever—has done all that was needful in order to their full reconciliation with God.

Shared: single offering; perfected for all time; ongoing "being sanctified." Do not invent limited-versus-general fights that ignore the cultus point. Classical Arminians hear perfection as full reconciliation accomplished in the cross for those who believe, while sanctification continues as they abide; the offering is not repeated.

Reformed

Calvin prefers "consecrated" or "perfected": all the saints find a full consecration in the one offering of Christ. "Them who are sanctified" includes all the children of God; the grace of sanctification is sought elsewhere in vain.

Reformed soteriology: perfected for all time is definitive for those in Christ; "being sanctified" is the ongoing work in the same people. The single offering actually perfects; it does not merely make perfection possible and then wait on an independent will.

Key contrast Both confess that by a single offering Christ has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified—cultus fulfillment, not a second altar. Arminians typically hear the perfected reconciliation as sincerely accomplished in Christ and applied to those who believe and continue in him. Reformed readers typically hear a definitive consecration of the children of God by that one offering, with ongoing sanctification in the same elect people.

Hebrews 10:15

15And the Holy Spirit also bears witness to us; for after saying,

Arminian

The Holy Spirit also bears witness to us. Wesley: in this and the following verses the apostle winds up his argument; he repeats Jeremiah, describing the new covenant as completely ratified and all its blessings secured by the one offering of Christ.

Both camps: the Spirit's witness in Scripture seals the argument. The new covenant is not a human upgrade; it is God's sworn mercy proved by the finished sacrifice.

Reformed

Calvin: this testimony from Jeremiah is not adduced superfluously. He now stands on the remission words to conclude there is no more need of a sacrifice since sins are blotted out.

Reformed accent: Spirit-breathed Scripture interprets the cross. The witness is to us—the church that reads Jeremiah fulfilled in Christ's blood.

Hebrews 10:16

16"This is the covenant that I will make with them after those days, declares the Lord: I will put my laws on their hearts, and write them on their minds,"

Arminian

This is the covenant: laws on hearts, written on minds. Interior writing, not only exterior tablets.

Arminian pastoral: the new covenant renews the people so they love God's will. Both camps refuse a grace that leaves the heart untouched. The writing is God's act; the response is living obedience enabled by that grace.

Reformed

Calvin notes the Jeremiah quotation returns with the heart-and-mind writing, then presses the remission clause as the hinge for "no more offering."

Reformed confession: God writes his laws within. The covenant people are remade. This interior work accompanies the once-for-all forgiveness that ends sacrificial repetition.

Hebrews 10:17

17then he adds, "I will remember their sins and their lawless deeds no more."

Arminian

I will remember their sins and their lawless deeds no more. Wesley: all the blessings of the new covenant secured by the one offering, rendering other expiatory sacrifices and any repetition of his own utterly needless.

Shared: real forgiveness, not divine amnesia as carelessness. God chooses not to recall against us what the blood has covered. Classical Arminians hear this mercy held out in the gospel to all who repent and believe.

Reformed

Calvin: God does not now remember sins, because an expiation has been made once for all; otherwise the Prophet's promise of the New Testament benefit would be to no purpose.

Reformed comfort: non-remembrance rests on a finished price. Sins are remitted in a way different from the old shadows—not a different mercy, but a different and final price.

Hebrews 10:18

18Where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer any offering for sin.

Arminian

Where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer any offering for sin. The logic is airtight: forgiven people do not need another victim.

Both camps shut the door on repeated propitiatory offerings. Arminian pastoral: flee to the one sacrifice, not back to the altar; hold the confession; the warning and the endurance that follow assume this finished forgiveness.

Reformed

Calvin: where remission of these is, there is no more offering for sin. The sacrifices of the Law are proved abolished, and any repeated expiatory mass is a vain fiction against the Apostle's argument.

Reformed pastoral: forgiveness ends offering. The church's worship is thanksgiving and communion, not a new Calvary. From this settled forgiveness the letter turns to confidence, assembly, warning, and endurance.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the priest who stands daily, the Christ who sat down, the single offering that perfects those being sanctified, and the new-covenant promise that God remembers sins no more—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Hebrews 10:11–18.

Arminian Notable voices

“Every priest standeth—as a servant in an humble posture. But he—the virtue of whose one sacrifice remains for ever—sat down as a son, in majesty and honour. He hath perfected them for ever—that is, has done all that was needful in order to their full reconciliation with God. The apostle winds up his argument concerning the excellency and perfection of the priesthood and sacrifice of Christ, describing the new covenant as now completely ratified, and all the blessings of it secured to us by the one offering of Christ, which renders all other expiatory sacrifices, and any repetition of his own, utterly needless.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Hebrews 10:11–18)

Reformed Notable voices

“The practice of daily sacrificing is inconsistent with and wholly foreign to the priesthood of Christ. By one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified; he includes all the children of God, and reminds us that the grace of sanctification is sought elsewhere in vain. He takes his stand on these words: Their iniquities will I remember no more; and hence he concludes that there is no more need of a sacrifice since sins are blotted out. God does not now remember sins, because an expiation has been made once for all.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Hebrews 10:11–18)
