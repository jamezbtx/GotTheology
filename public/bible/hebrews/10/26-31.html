Passage

Hebrews 10

Verses 26–31 · ESV · Voices

A severe warning kept intact: if we go on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins, but a fearful expectation of judgment. Trampling the Son of God, profaning the covenant blood, and outraging the Spirit of grace deserve worse punishment than Moses' law—and it is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.

Hebrews 10:26

26For if we go on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins,

Arminian

If we go on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins. Wesley: when we—any of us Christians—sin wilfully by total apostasy from God, termed drawing back; after experimental knowledge of the gospel truth, there remaineth no more sacrifice—none but that which we obstinately reject.

Do not soften. Classical Arminians take this as a terrifying pastoral warning about real apostasy from real grace: those who had received the truth may willfully reject the only sacrifice. Both camps refuse to shrink this into a cartoon or a minor lapse; it is deliberate persistence in forsaking Christ.

Reformed

Calvin: those who sin here are not such as offend in any way, but such as forsake the Church and wholly alienate themselves from Christ—not particular falls but complete defection. If we knowingly and willingly renounce the grace obtained, there remains no more sacrifice for sins. Christ offers himself daily to sinners who fall otherwise; he denies sacrifice to those who renounce his death by total renunciation of the faith.

Do not soften. Reformed readers take the warning as terrifying and as a means God uses; those who finally depart show they were not truly of the persevering people—yet the text must still land as dread, not as a paper threat.

Key contrast Both take deliberate apostasy after receiving the knowledge of the truth as a terrifying warning: no sacrifice remains but fearful judgment. Arminians typically hear the real possibility of falling away from real grace—apostasy from a justification once enjoyed. Reformed readers typically hear a genuine warning as God's means to preserve the elect, while those who finally abandon Christ were not truly of those who endure (cf. "not of us"), without turning the passage into a soft scare.

Hebrews 10:27

27but a fearful expectation of judgment, and a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries.

Arminian

But a fearful expectation of judgment, and a fury of fire that will consume the adversaries. The alternative to the rejected sacrifice is not neutrality; it is dread.

Arminian pastoral: keep the fear. Those who draw back become adversaries under fire. Both camps must preach this without smirking assurance that "real Christians" need not tremble at the words.

Reformed

Calvin: a fearful looking for—the torment of an evil conscience in those who know that having tasted grace they have lost it forever through their own fault. Fiery indignation will devour the adversaries—destroy but not consume; it will be inextinguishable. Those who refuse their place among the faithful are counted enemies of Christ.

Reformed pastoral: the expectation is fearful. Warning passages are not theater; they are how God keeps and how he unveils the reprobate who abandon the assembly and the Son.

Hebrews 10:28

28Anyone who has set aside the law of Moses dies without mercy on the evidence of two or three witnesses.

Arminian

Anyone who set aside Moses' law dies without mercy under two or three witnesses. Wesley: he that in capital cases despised—presumptuously transgressed—the law of Moses died without delay or mitigation.

Both camps: lesser-to-greater. If covenant apostasy under Moses meant death without mercy, gospel apostasy is not lighter. Fair reading: the issue is setting aside the covenant, not every stumble.

Reformed

Calvin: argument from the less to the greater. If violating Moses' law was capital, how much heavier the rejection of the gospel. He speaks of the entire denial of Christ—apostasy—referring to Deuteronomy on renouncing God's covenant.

Reformed accent: without mercy under witnesses showed the old covenant's severity; the comparison elevates the gospel's dignity and the apostate's guilt.

Hebrews 10:29

29How much worse punishment, do you think, will be deserved by the one who has trampled underfoot the Son of God, and has profaned the blood of the covenant by which he was sanctified, and has outraged the Spirit of grace?

Arminian

How much worse punishment for the one who has trampled underfoot the Son of God, profaned the blood of the covenant by which he was sanctified, and outraged the Spirit of grace? Wesley: by wilful, total apostasy; the blood counted unholy; by which he was sanctified—therefore Christ died for him also, and he was at least justified once; despite to the Spirit by rejecting all his motions.

Arminian reading (fair, not straw): sanctified here can describe a real consecration and justification once enjoyed, then spurned. The warning stays serious for both camps: this is worse than Moses, and the Spirit outraged is not a metaphor to shrug off.

Reformed

Calvin: apostates under Law and Gospel both perish without mercy, but the kind of death differs—eternal perdition. Three marks: Son trodden underfoot, blood counted unholy, despite to the Spirit of grace. The blood of the covenant is that by which sanctification is effected; to count it unholy is done by departing from the faith. The Spirit of grace enlightens, seals adoption, regenerates, grafts into Christ—to scorn him is extremely wicked.

Reformed reading (fair, not straw): covenant privilege and sanctification language can describe those enlightened and associated with the people who then apostasize; worse punishment is real; God remains inexorable toward those who tread underfoot the Mediator.

Key contrast Both hear worse punishment for trampling the Son, profaning covenant blood, and outraging the Spirit—kept terrifying. Arminians typically take "by which he was sanctified" as evidence of real grace once received and then abandoned. Reformed readers typically take the sanctification language as covenant/congregation privilege (or temporal consecration) in those who later prove apostate, while still insisting the warning is a true and fearful means, not empty rhetoric.

Hebrews 10:30

30For we know him who said, "Vengeance is mine; I will repay." And again, "The Lord will judge his people."

Arminian

We know him who said, Vengeance is mine; I will repay; and, The Lord will judge his people. Wesley: the Lord will judge his people—far more rigorously than the heathens if they rebel against him.

Both camps: God avenges; God judges his people. Covenant membership does not cancel judgment; it intensifies accountability. Do not soften into tribal comfort.

Reformed

Calvin: God will not be mocked with impunity; it is his office to render to the ungodly what they deserve. "The Lord shall judge his people" means he governs his church by purifying it—dreadful to hypocrites who usurp a place among the faithful.

Reformed pastoral: judgment begins with the household. The quote comforts the oppressed and terrifies the apostate. Vengeance belongs to God, not to private rage.

Hebrews 10:31

31It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.

Arminian

It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God. Wesley: to fall into the hands of his avenging justice.

Do not soften—for either camp. Arminian pastoral: living faith fears this and flees to the only sacrifice still offered to those who have not finally rejected him; those who go on deliberately in apostasy have nowhere else to go. Pair with 10:32–39: recall, endure, do not shrink back.

Reformed

Calvin: a mortal man's vengeance stops at death; God's power is not so bounded. Whoever considers he has to do with God must tremble. Whenever the flesh allures us, this admonition alone should arouse us: it is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God; his wrath is furnished with dreadful punishments forever. The Apostle speaks of wrath dreadful to the reprobate who have closed the door of grace.

Reformed pastoral: keep the fear. Then immediately, with the next unit, call them to remember former endurance and to live by faith—not of those who shrink back, but of those who preserve their souls.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on deliberate apostasy after receiving the knowledge of the truth, the fearful expectation of judgment, and falling into the hands of the living God—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Hebrews 10:26–31.

Arminian Notable voices

“For when we—any of us Christians—sin wilfully by total apostasy from God, termed drawing back, after having received the experimental knowledge of the gospel truth, there remaineth no more sacrifice for sins—none but that which we obstinately reject. Of how much sorer punishment is he worthy who by wilful, total apostasy hath trodden underfoot the Son of God, and counted the blood wherewith the better covenant was established an unholy thing—by which he hath been sanctified; therefore Christ died for him also, and he was at least justified once—and done despite to the Spirit of grace. It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God—of his avenging justice.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Hebrews 10:26–31)

Reformed Notable voices

“Those who sin, mentioned by the Apostle, are not such as offend in any way, but such as forsake the Church and wholly alienate themselves from Christ. If we knowingly and willingly renounce the grace which we had obtained, there remains no more sacrifice for sins. He who has trodden under foot the Son of God, counted the blood of the covenant an unholy thing, and done despite unto the Spirit of grace, deserves a sorer punishment. It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God; for his wrath is furnished with dreadful punishments which are to be forever.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Hebrews (on Hebrews 10:26–31)
