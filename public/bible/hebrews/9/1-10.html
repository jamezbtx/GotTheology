Passage

Hebrews 9

Verses 1–10 · WEB

The writer describes the earthly tabernacle of the first covenant: the holy place with its lampstand and table, and the Most Holy Place with the ark and mercy seat. Priests entered the first room regularly, but only the high priest entered the second, once a year, and never without blood. The Holy Spirit signified by this that the way into God’s presence had not yet been opened while the first tent stood. Its gifts and sacrifices could not perfect the worshiper’s conscience. Both traditions read the tabernacle as a picture fulfilled in Christ, and they largely agree on this passage.

Hebrews 9:1

1Now indeed even the first covenant had ordinances of divine service and an earthly sanctuary.

Arminian

The first covenant had regulations for worship and an earthly sanctuary. Arminian readers see God providing real means of approach under the old covenant, preparing for Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the old covenant’s ordinances as part of one covenant of grace, administered through types and shadows.

Hebrews 9:2

2For a tabernacle was prepared. In the first part were the lamp stand, the table, and the show bread, which is called the Holy Place.

Arminian

A tent was prepared, with the lampstand, table, and bread of the Presence in the holy place. Arminian readers see the furnishings as pictures of God’s provision for his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see each furnishing as a type fulfilled in Christ, who is the light of the world and the bread of life.

Hebrews 9:3

3After the second veil was the tabernacle which is called the Holy of Holies,

Arminian

Behind the second curtain was the Most Holy Place. Arminian readers see the barrier as a sign that sinners could not freely approach God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the curtain as a picture of separation from God, removed when Christ’s flesh was torn (10:20).

Hebrews 9:4

4having a golden altar of incense and the ark of the covenant overlaid on all sides with gold, in which was a golden pot holding the manna, Aaron’s rod that budded, and the tablets of the covenant;

Arminian

It had the golden altar of incense and the ark of the covenant, containing the manna, Aaron’s staff, and the tablets. Arminian readers see reminders of God’s faithfulness and provision.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the tablets the law at the heart of the covenant, covered by the mercy seat.

Hebrews 9:5

5and above it cherubim of glory overshadowing the mercy seat, of which things we can’t speak now in detail.

Arminian

Above it were the cherubim overshadowing the mercy seat. Arminian readers see the mercy seat as the place where God met his people in mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the mercy seat as the place of propitiation, fulfilled in Christ (Romans 3:25).

Hebrews 9:6

6Now these things having been thus prepared, the priests go in continually into the first tabernacle, accomplishing the services,

Arminian

The priests regularly entered the first section. Arminian readers see daily ministry that could not complete the work of reconciliation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repetition as a sign that the work was unfinished.

Hebrews 9:7

7but into the second the high priest alone, once in the year, not without blood, which he offers for himself and for the errors of the people.

Arminian

Only the high priest entered the second, once a year, with blood for himself and the people’s unintentional sins. Arminian readers see the need for atonement before approaching God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Day of Atonement as a type of Christ’s once-for-all entry with his own blood.

Hebrews 9:8

8The Holy Spirit is indicating this, that the way into the Holy Place wasn’t yet revealed while the first tabernacle was still standing.

Arminian

The Holy Spirit indicates that the way into the holy places was not yet opened while the first tent stood. Arminian readers see the Spirit teaching through Scripture’s structure.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit as the author of the types, pointing to Christ.

Hebrews 9:9

9This is a symbol of the present age, where gifts and sacrifices are offered that are incapable, concerning the conscience, of making the worshiper perfect,

Arminian

This is a symbol for the present age; its gifts cannot perfect the conscience of the worshiper. Arminian readers see the need for an inward cleansing that only Christ provides.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the old sacrifices as unable to cleanse the conscience, which Christ’s blood alone does (9:14).

Hebrews 9:10

10being only (with foods and drinks and various washings) fleshly ordinances, imposed until a time of reformation.

Arminian

They deal only with food, drink, and washings, regulations for the body until the time of reformation. Arminian readers see the old ceremonies as temporary.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the ceremonial law fulfilled and abolished in Christ, while the moral law remains.
