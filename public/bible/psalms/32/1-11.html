Passage

Psalms 32

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

A Maskil of David. The psalmist pronounces blessed the one whose transgression is forgiven and whose sin is covered; blesses the man against whom the LORD counts no iniquity and in whose spirit there is no deceit; recalls silence that wasted the bones through all-day groaning; names the LORD's hand heavy day and night and strength dried as by summer heat; then acknowledges sin, refuses to cover iniquity, resolves to confess transgressions, and receives forgiveness of the iniquity of his sin; therefore urges everyone godly to offer prayer when the LORD may be found, so that in the rush of great waters they shall not reach him; confesses God as hiding place who preserves from trouble and surrounds with shouts of deliverance; hears instruction and teaching in the way he should go, counsel with God's eye upon him; warns not to be like horse or mule without understanding, curbed with bit and bridle; contrasts many sorrows of the wicked with steadfast love surrounding the one who trusts in the LORD; and summons the righteous to be glad in the LORD and rejoice, and all the upright in heart to shout for joy.

Psalms 32:1

1Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered.

Arminian

Blessed is the one whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered. The beatitude opens the penitential psalm: forgiveness and covering name gospel mercy, not a small mop-up for the almost righteous. Paul later cites this blessing in Romans 4 beside Abraham’s counted righteousness—shared witness that the blessed are the forgiven.

Arminian pastoral: shared—preach pardon as real relief for lawless deeds. Do not require a prior self-made covering before the LORD covers sin. Let the blessing stand as wide as the mercy it names.

Reformed

Calvin: this exclamation springs from serious consideration of God’s judgment and mercy. Those only are blessed to whom God is reconciled; with Paul (Romans 4) this is the definition of the righteousness of faith—men blessed when freely reconciled and counted righteous. The blessedness utterly destroys the righteousness of works; free remission is the gate of eternal salvation.

Reformed pastoral: shared—forgiven and covered are God’s acts. Hold Paul’s reading: David sings gift-righteousness, not wages. Refuse satisfactions that would purchase what God freely remits.

Psalms 32:2

2Blessed is the man against whom the LORD counts no iniquity, and in whose spirit there is no deceit.

Arminian

Blessed is the man against whom the LORD counts no iniquity, and in whose spirit there is no deceit. Wesley: Imputeth—Whom God doth not charge with the guilt of his sins, but graciously pardons and accepts him in Christ. No guile—Who freely confesses all his sins, and turns from sin to God with all his heart. Non-imputation and honest turning belong together in the same blessing Paul hears in Romans 4.

Arminian pastoral: shared—the LORD refuses to count the believer’s sin as the last word, and accepts in Christ. Let no deceit mean open confession and heart-turning, not a polished pose of innocence.

Reformed

Calvin: Jehovah imputeth no sin; thrice-repeated forgiveness (forgiven, covered, not imputed) confirms anxious consciences. No guile distinguishes believers from hypocrites and senseless despisers who soothe guilt with subterfuge and never taste forgiveness. Shared with Romans 4: non-imputation is God’s counting, the asylum of the destitute of works.

Reformed pastoral: shared—blessed is the one God will not charge. Cleanse the heart of guile that hides from his bar; take non-imputation as free verdict, not as leave to remain unexamined.

Key contrast Both camps own the Romans 4 gospel here: blessedness is forgiveness, covering, and the LORD’s not counting iniquity—apart from works. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s pastoral pair: God pardons and accepts in Christ, and no guile means free confession with whole-heart turning from sin. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s twin stress: free remission that destroys works-righteousness, and no guile as the opposite of hypocrisy that will not come sincerely for pardon.

Psalms 32:3

3For when I kept silent, my bones wasted away through my groaning all day long.

Arminian

For when I kept silent, my bones wasted away through my groaning all day long. Wesley: Silence—From a full and open confession of my sins. Old—My spirit failed, and the strength of my body decayed. Roaring—Because of the continual horrors of my conscience, and sense of God’s wrath. Unconfessed sin wastes body and spirit under conscience’s roar.

Arminian pastoral: shared—do not call silence wisdom when it is refusal to confess. Bring wasted bones and all-day groaning to open acknowledgment; horror of wrath is meant to drive you to mercy, not to mute endurance.

Reformed

Calvin: David confirms by experience that nothing is so miserable as to be deprived of God’s favor. Whether silent or heightening grief by crying, his bones waxed old—whole strength withered. Silence here is not hope or obedience; it brought no alleviation. The sinner is not lightened until restored to God’s favor.

Reformed pastoral: shared—silence about sin is not patience. Let bone-wasting teach how terrible divine anger feels; hasten to reconciliation rather than gnaw the bit in private.

Psalms 32:4

4For day and night your hand was heavy upon me; my strength was dried up as by the heat of summer.

Arminian

For day and night your hand was heavy upon me; my strength was dried up as by the heat of summer. Wesley: Hand—Thy afflicting hand. My moisture—Was dried up. Day-and-night weight and summer drought name how thoroughly the afflicting hand pressed him.

Arminian pastoral: shared—confess the heaviness without blaming God of cruelty for calling you to repent. Drought of strength is a summons to the forgiveness the next verse finds.

Reformed

Calvin: heavy grief arose because he felt God’s hand sore against him—greatest of afflictions when the sinner has to do with a Judge whose indignation involves many deaths. Moisture dried up not from mere meditation on trouble but from discovering its cause. The hand ceased not day and night until he was subdued and made meek.

Reformed pastoral: shared—take the heavy hand as medicine that humbles fierce pride. Do not waste drought on sullen silence; let it prepare frank confession.

Psalms 32:5

5I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity; I said, “I will confess my transgressions to the LORD,” and you forgave the iniquity of my sin.

Arminian

I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not cover my iniquity; I said, “I will confess my transgressions to the LORD,” and you forgave the iniquity of my sin. Wesley: The iniquity—The guilt of my sin. Resolve to confess meets immediate pardon of guilt; covering is refused on the sinner’s side so God may cover.

Arminian pastoral: shared—acknowledge, do not hide, and confess to the LORD. Expect forgiveness of guilt as the psalm narrates; do not keep covering what mercy stands ready to lift.

Reformed

Calvin: the only relief was unfeignedly to condemn himself before God and flee for forgiveness—not mere remembrance such as Cain and Judas knew without profit. He came deliberately (I said) assured of pardon through mercy. Confession intervenes, yet faith, opening heart and tongue, really obtains pardon; confession is the manner of reconciliation, not a meritorious cause. As often as the sinner presents himself with ingenuous confession, he finds reconciliation awaiting.

Reformed pastoral: shared—pour out the heart without disguise. Seek pardon as a suppliant under faith’s guidance; refuse both stubborn silence and any dream that the bare act of confessing purchases grace.

Key contrast Both treat open acknowledgment as the hinge from wasting silence to forgiveness of guilt. Arminians typically keep Wesley’s plain pastoral sequence: confess the guilt of sin and receive the LORD’s forgiveness as the verse states. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s careful fork: confession is necessary manner, yet not the meritorious cause—faith fleeing to mercy really obtains the pardon hypocrites never find.

Psalms 32:6

6Therefore let everyone who is godly offer prayer to you at a time when you may be found; surely in the rush of great waters, they shall not reach him.

Arminian

Therefore let everyone who is godly offer prayer to you at a time when you may be found; surely in the rush of great waters, they shall not reach him. Wesley: For this—Upon the encouragement of my example. Found—In an acceptable and seasonable time, while God continues to offer grace and mercy. Waters—In the time of great calamities. Not come—So as to overwhelm him. One pardoned sinner becomes encouragement for every godly prayer while mercy is offered.

Arminian pastoral: shared—pray while he may be found. Let David’s pardon embolden your ask; trust that floodwaters will not overwhelm the one who seeks him in season.

Reformed

Calvin: whatever was set forth in David’s person belongs to all God’s children. Forgiveness came by faith and prayer, not by bare confession as some speak; he bids believers betake themselves to prayer, the true sacrifice of faith. Time of finding compares with Isaiah 55:6—critical seasons when need stimulates recourse to God, who is always willing to meet us though our sloth delays us. In the flood of many waters the godly remain safe if they betake themselves to the sanctuary of grace.

Reformed pastoral: shared—let one mercy catechize the whole church to pray. Seek him while he may be found; do not wait for the flood to teach what pardon already invites.

Key contrast Both urge the godly to pray on the strength of this forgiveness, safe from overwhelming waters. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s accent: example encourages prayer in the acceptable time while God continues to offer grace and mercy. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s: prayer and faith (not confession alone) appropriate the example, and the time of finding marks need-awakened seasons akin to Isaiah 55:6.

Psalms 32:7

7You are a hiding place for me; you preserve me from trouble; you surround me with shouts of deliverance.

Arminian

You are a hiding place for me; you preserve me from trouble; you surround me with shouts of deliverance. After pardon, God himself is refuge: preservation and surrounding songs answer the former drought and silence.

Arminian pastoral: shared—hide in him, not in renewed cover-ups of iniquity. Receive shouts of deliverance as gift; let trouble meet a Preserver, not only a memory of guilt.

Reformed

Calvin: thanksgiving in few words with much force. He denies any haven of safety but in God; persuades himself God will be faithful keeper; whatever adversity befalls, God will be deliverer. Compass means manifold deliverance—on every side matter for praise; songs of deliverance stand for help received.

Reformed pastoral: shared—God alone is hiding place. Expect surrounding songs after surrounding waters; offer gratitude rather than a mute return to silence.

Psalms 32:8

8I will instruct you and teach you in the way you should go; I will counsel you with my eye upon you.

Arminian

I will instruct you and teach you in the way you should go; I will counsel you with my eye upon you. Wesley: I will—This and the next verse seems to be the words of God, whom David brings in as returning this answer to his prayers. Mine eye—So Christ did St. Peter, when he turned and looked upon him. Divine counsel answers confession with guided walking under a watching eye.

Arminian pastoral: shared—take instruction as mercy’s next gift after pardon. Stay under the eye that counsels; remember Peter’s look as pastoral emblem of restoring gaze, without forcing foreign disputes onto the line.

Reformed

Calvin: that the exhortation may have force, the speaker addresses each person; David, care for others marked by the eye, turns his own benefit into guidance so brethren may share the same mercy. Those solicitous for our welfare are appointed as guides, showing God’s paternal care. (Calvin reads David as the instructor here, teaching sinners the way.)

Reformed pastoral: shared—walk the taught way under watchful counsel. Let forgiven people become guides; refuse a pardon that never learns the path.

Key contrast Both hear post-pardon instruction aimed at a guided way under a careful eye. Arminians typically follow Wesley: these words are God’s answer to prayer, the eye like Christ’s look on Peter. Reformed readers typically follow Calvin’s reading: David speaks, instructing others so they share the benefit—guides appointed in God’s paternal care.

Psalms 32:9

9Be not like a horse or a mule, without understanding, which must be curbed with bit and bridle, or it will not stay near you.

Arminian

Be not like a horse or a mule, without understanding, which must be curbed with bit and bridle, or it will not stay near you. Wesley: Will not—Unless they be forced to it by a bit or bridle. And so all the ancient translators understand it. Willing nearness beats stubborn force; understanding answers the eye of verse 8.

Arminian pastoral: shared—do not make God drag you to mercy with bit and bridle. Stay near by understanding; let confession be voluntary, not only pain-forced.

Reformed

Calvin: learn with quietness; lay aside stubbornness; put on meekness. If we were attentive to corrections, we would hasten to seek God’s favor. The refractory are likened to brute beasts—men know how to tame horses with bits; what then will God do with the intractable who kick against the pricks?

Reformed pastoral: shared—refuse mule-like hardness after pardon invited you near. Take counsel willingly; do not wait for heavier curb.

Psalms 32:10

10Many are the sorrows of the wicked, but steadfast love surrounds the one who trusts in the LORD.

Arminian

Many are the sorrows of the wicked, but steadfast love surrounds the one who trusts in the LORD. Wesley: Sorrows—This is an argument to enforce the foregoing admonition. Two circles: sorrows hemming the wicked; steadfast love surrounding the trusting.

Arminian pastoral: shared—trust the LORD and live inside surrounding love. Let the contrast enforce soft obedience; do not envy a wicked peace that the psalm says is not coming.

Reformed

Calvin: without figure he declares the rebellious condition—no end of miseries until consumed. God has innumerable plagues against the wicked and infinite goodness to succor his own. Remedy for afflictions: humble yourself under God’s hand and found salvation on his mercy alone; those who rely on God are shielded on every side.

Reformed pastoral: shared—sorrows multiply for the stiff-necked; mercy compasses those who hope in Jehovah. Found safety on mercy, not on stubbornness that still needs the bit.

Psalms 32:11

11Be glad in the LORD, and rejoice, O righteous, and shout for joy, all you upright in heart!

Arminian

Be glad in the LORD, and rejoice, O righteous, and shout for joy, all you upright in heart! The forgiven are summoned to gladness: righteousness and uprightness of heart answer the blessing of verses 1–2, not a claim of sinless perfection.

Arminian pastoral: shared—rejoice in the LORD who forgave. Shout with the upright in heart; let pardon produce praise, not a return to silent cover-up.

Reformed

Calvin: after teaching how accessible true happiness is, he exhorts to gladness in Jehovah—nothing prevents assuring themselves of God’s favor, who so kindly offers reconciliation. Holy rejoicing follows lively faith. He addresses the righteous, upright in heart, because external show does not avail. How call them righteous whose happiness is free non-imputation? Those received are dissatisfied with their sins and repent with whole heart—not that repentance merits pardon, but faith is never separated from the spirit of regeneration; God accepts upright disposition by free grace as if pure.

Reformed pastoral: shared—be glad in the LORD who remits. Let uprightness mark sincere hearts under pardon, not a rival righteousness of works; shout because mercy surrounds you.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the blessedness of forgiven transgression and covered sin, the LORD who counts no iniquity, silence that wastes the bones under a heavy hand, open acknowledgment that finds pardon, prayer when he may be found, God as hiding place with shouts of deliverance, willing instruction versus bit and bridle, sorrows of the wicked against steadfast love around the trusting, and glad rejoicing for the upright in heart—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 32:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered. Imputeth—Whom God doth not charge with the guilt of his sins, but graciously pardons and accepts him in Christ. No guile—Who freely confesses all his sins, and turns from sin to God with all his heart. Silence—From a full and open confession of my sins. Old—My spirit failed, and the strength of my body decayed. Roaring—Because of the continual horrors of my conscience, and sense of God’s wrath. Hand—Thy afflicting hand. My moisture—Was dried up. The iniquity—The guilt of my sin. For this—Upon the encouragement of my example. Found—In an acceptable and seasonable time, while God continues to offer grace and mercy. Waters—In the time of great calamities. Not come—So as to overwhelm him. I will—This and the next verse seems to be the words of God, whom David brings in as returning this answer to his prayers. Mine eye—So Christ did St. Peter, when he turned and looked upon him. Will not—Unless they be forced to it by a bit or bridle. And so all the ancient translators understand it. Sorrows—This is an argument to enforce the foregoing admonition.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament, Psalm 32

Reformed Notable voices

“Blessed are they whose iniquity is forgiven, and whose transgression is covered; blessed the man to whom Jehovah imputeth no sin, and in whose spirit there is no guile. This blessedness destroys the righteousness of works: men are then only blessed when freely reconciled to God and counted righteous by him—Paul interpreting the psalm as the definition of the righteousness of faith (Romans 4). Remission does not agree with satisfactions; God freely pardons in taking away, covering, and not imputing. No guile distinguishes believers from hypocrites and despisers who will not taste forgiveness. When I kept silence, my bones wasted away: nothing so miserable as to be deprived of God’s favor; day and night his hand was heavy until confession. I acknowledged my sin: not mere remembrance such as Cain and Judas knew, but unfeigned coming to God for pardon; confession intervenes, yet it is faith that really obtains pardon—confession is manner, not meritorious cause. Therefore every meek one shall pray in the time of finding; God is the only hiding-place and will compass with songs of deliverance. I will instruct thee: David turns his own mercy into counsel for others—be not like horse or mule. Many sorrows to the wicked; mercy surrounds the one who hopes in Jehovah. Be glad in Jehovah, ye righteous and upright in heart: holy rejoicing follows lively faith; those received are the dissatisfied with their sins who repent, for faith is never separated from the spirit of regeneration.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms, Psalm 32
