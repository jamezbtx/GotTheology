Passage

Psalms 55

Verses 1–23 · ESV · Voices

To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments. A Maskil of David. Betrayed by a familiar friend and oppressed in a city full of violence, David pours out anguish, longs for dove’s wings, prays that God divide the tongues of the wicked, casts his burden on the LORD, and ends in trust while blood and treachery head for the pit.

Psalms 55:1

1Give ear to my prayer, O God, and hide not yourself from my plea for mercy!

Arminian

Give ear; hide not yourself from my plea for mercy. Wesley’s outline opens with complaint that must be heard—prayer is the first move when distress is heavy. The plea assumes God can hide; faith begs him not to.

Arminian pastoral: mercy is sought by real petition. Grace invites the cry; the hearer must still pray rather than nurse silent despair.

Reformed

Calvin: from the opening we conclude David was under heavy distress—no ordinary amount produced such an effect upon a saint of distinguished courage. He is anxious to convey wretchedness, constrained to cry out aloud.

Reformed accent: the saint’s courage does not cancel need of an open ear in heaven. Shared: hide-not is earnest pleading before God.

Psalms 55:2

2Attend to me, and answer me; I am restless in my complaint and I moan,

Arminian

Attend; answer; restless in complaint and moan. The noise of grief is not irreverence—Wesley’s later notes treat the complaint as prayer that God hears at stated times.

Arminian note: restless moaning is brought to God, not suppressed as unworthy. Grace opens a path for honest anguish that still must be voiced.

Reformed

Calvin connects the moan with the enemy’s voice: threatenings and unjust violence cast upon him. He will wail in his address and make a noise—not boast fortitude, but show distress.

Reformed pastoral: complaint before God is permitted; it is not the barren comfort of endless self-pity. Shared: attend and answer are begged.

Psalms 55:3

3because of the noise of the enemy, because of the oppression of the wicked. For they drop trouble upon me, and in anger they bear a grudge against me.

Arminian

Wesley: Voice—their clamours and threats, and slanders. Cast—they lay many crimes to my charge. Oppression of the wicked drops trouble and nurses angry grudge.

Arminian hearing: false accusation and wrathful hatred are named so the sufferer may pray specifically. The wicked’s noise does not silence the plea; it fuels it.

Reformed

Calvin: voice may allude to Saul’s threatenings; casting iniquity = discharging unjust violence, or deceitful treachery paired with open wrath. Applying wicked to enemies implicitly asserts innocence.

Reformed comfort: conscious rectitude under persecution springs hope of the Lord’s help—he is shield of the distressed. Shared: trouble dropped in anger is real.

Psalms 55:4

4My heart is in anguish within me; the terrors of death have fallen upon me.

Arminian

Wesley: The terrors—deadly terrors; such as seize upon men in the agonies of death. Heart in anguish; death’s terrors fallen.

Arminian pastoral: even a believing king may feel death’s dread. The confession invites prayer, not stoic denial; grace meets anguish that is owned.

Reformed

Calvin: additional evidence of extremity—no soft person, yet overwhelmed with terrors, heart not insensible. Fortitude of the greatest servants fails in severe trial; Satan suggests God has withdrawn Spirit’s supports.

Reformed note: terrors of death show he was on the eve of sinking unless divine grace interposed. Shared: anguish is not unbelief by definition.

Psalms 55:5

5Fear and trembling come upon me, and horror overwhelms me.

Arminian

Fear, trembling, horror overwhelm. Wesley leaves the verse terse under the deadly-terrors theme of v. 4—horror that floods the whole person.

Arminian reading: overwhelm names a real spiritual and bodily crisis. Faith does not pretend calm; it brings horror to the God who still hears.

Reformed

Calvin: fearfulness and trembling come; horror overwhelms—inward fears join outward calamities. We are good soldiers while things go well; close combat shows weakness.

Reformed accent: the throne of God is where the sore conflict of spirit is brought. Shared: horror is confessed, not curated away.

Psalms 55:6

6And I say, "Oh, that I had wings like a dove! I would fly away and be at rest;

Arminian

Oh for dove’s wings—fly away and be at rest. The wish is escape, not revenge: rest from betrayal and city strife.

Arminian pastoral: longing for flight can be honest prayer-language. Grace does not mock the desire for rest; it also trains the heart to cast the burden rather than only flee.

Reformed

Calvin: more than no escape—exile coveted, and such as the dove’s flight to a deserted hiding-place. He could escape only by miracle; common banishment denied. Dove singled as timid and defenseless before the hawk.

Reformed note: great straits made him almost forget the kingdom promise in agitation. Shared: dove’s rest is the cry of a hunted soul.

Psalms 55:7

7yes, I would wander far away; I would lodge in the wilderness; Selah

Arminian

Wander far; lodge in the wilderness. Selah. Wesley’s tempest note (v. 8) frames this as flight from enemies’ fury into lonely quiet.

Arminian hearing: wilderness lodging pictures desperate need of distance from corrupt company. The pause (Selah) invites the heart to feel the cost of betrayal.

Reformed

Calvin: willing to prolong flight and repose in the wilderness—concession to adversaries’ fury, not a false truce to buy time. Words of a man driven near desperation.

Reformed pastoral: even contemplated disgraceful flight shows how deep the pit felt. Shared: far wilderness is preferred to near treachery.

Psalms 55:8

8I would hurry to find a shelter from the raging wind and tempest."

Arminian

Wesley: Tempest—from the force and fury of mine enemies. Hurry to shelter from raging wind.

Arminian note: shelter is sought from God-ward prayer as much as from geography. The haste is faith under pressure, not random panic.

Reformed

Calvin: hasten a deliverance from the wind raised by the whirlwind—a violent storm that compels the traveler to fly to the nearest covert.

Reformed accent: the image is providential peril, not mere weather. Shared: tempest names enemies’ rage.

Psalms 55:9

9Destroy, O Lord, divide their tongues; for I see violence and strife in the city.

Arminian

Wesley: Destroy—destroy them by dividing. Tongues—their speech, as at Babel (Gen 11:9), their votes, opinions, counsels—eminently among Absalom’s followers (2 Sam 17). Strife—injustice and fraud instead of the justice and peace David established. City—Jerusalem, in Absalom’s time a sink of all sins.

Arminian reading: prayer asks God to fracture wicked confederacy. Judgment that divides tongues is begged so violence in the city may not stand unchallenged.

Reformed

Calvin: having composed his mind, he resumes prayer—not endless barren complaint. Divide their tongue alludes to Babel: break criminal confederacies, distract impious counsels. God still weakens church enemies by factions and disagreements.

Reformed comfort: unbridled license signals judgments not far off—God visits the proud, gives grace to the humble. Shared: divide tongues is holy petition.

Psalms 55:10

10Day and night they go around it on its walls, and iniquity and trouble are within it;

Arminian

Wesley: They—violence and strife. Go about—encompass as a garrison. Walls—outward parts and very midst; all parts horribly corrupted. Iniquity and trouble within.

Arminian pastoral: civic rot day and night is named so intercession can be specific. The righteous may still pray when walls themselves seem manned by strife.

Reformed

Calvin: crimes go about the walls keeping sentry—order inverted; city beset with strife and oppression instead of protected. City may mean open public prevalence of crime under Saul’s confused government.

Reformed accent: inward corruption feeds outward violence. Shared: day-and-night circuit of evil is seen and mourned.

Psalms 55:11

11ruin is in its midst; oppression and fraud do not depart from its marketplace.

Arminian

Wesley: Streets—places of buying and selling and public commerce. Ruin in the midst; oppression and fraud do not depart from the marketplace.

Arminian note: marketplace fraud is moral, not only economic. Prayer against city sin includes the square where deals are struck.

Reformed

Calvin: wickedness in the midst; deceit and guile in the streets—source of prevailing crimes; the inwardly corrupt indulge violence against the defenseless. A nation of true religion with cities as dens of robbers faces portentous crisis.

Reformed pastoral: Spirit’s judgment stands against such cities. Shared: fraud in the market is ruin in the midst.

Psalms 55:12

12For it is not an enemy who taunts me—then I could bear it; it is not an adversary who deals insolently with me—then I could hide from him.

Arminian

Wesley: Hated—with a manifest or old hatred. Not an enemy’s taunt—then bearable; not an insolent adversary—then hideable.

Arminian hearing: open enmity can be met; familiar treachery blindsides. The wound of false friendship is named so healing prayer can be honest.

Reformed

Calvin: not that he could patiently bear an open enemy’s reproach, but that he could meet and parry it; against a known foe we watch, but a friend’s unsuspected stroke surprises. Self-defense impossible against secret treachery.

Reformed note: church history knows sword and also domestic fraud. Shared: the hurt is sharper because it was not an enemy.

Psalms 55:13

13But it is you, a man, my equal, my companion, my familiar friend.

Arminian

Wesley: Equal—not in power, but in reputation for wisdom and influence. Guide—whose counsel I highly prized and constantly followed. All which agrees to Achitophel.

Arminian pastoral: betrayal by a prized counselor is the psalm’s knife-point. Friendship and guidance were real; their violation is not minimized.

Reformed

Calvin: man of his own order—common bond of fraternity violated; leader and acquaintance; various bonds of relationship and office ought to be held sacred. Possibly a great man at court, or more than one sacrificing friendship for royal favor.

Reformed accent: Satan raises domestic enemies with secret weapons. Shared: equal, companion, familiar friend—named and grieved.

Psalms 55:14

14We used to take sweet counsel together; within God's house we walked in the throng.

Arminian

Sweet counsel together; walked in God’s house in the throng. Shared worship deepened the bond—and the betrayal.

Arminian reading: religious fellowship made the friendship sweeter and the wound worse. Walking in God’s house together was not theater; its memory still teaches reverence for holy ties.

Reformed

Calvin: sweet interchange of secret counsel; frequented religious assemblies in company—leader even in matters religious. Bond of religious fellowship is most sacred; such connections are providential design, not chance.

Reformed pastoral: treachery inside worship’s throng aggravates guilt. Shared: sweet counsel in God’s house is holy trust.

Psalms 55:15

15Let death steal over them; let them go down to Sheol alive; for evil is in their dwelling place and in their heart.

Arminian

Wesley: Them—all such as pretend to religion and have manifestly apostatized from profession and practice. Grave—cut off by sudden and violent death. Among them—Heb. in their inwards; wickedness deeply rooted in their hearts. Go down to Sheol alive.

Arminian note: imprecation targets apostate pretenders, not a vague vent. Evil in dwelling and heart is the stated ground.

Reformed

Calvin: denounces the faction that persecuted him—not personal bad feeling but speaking in God’s cause under the Spirit. Alludes to Korah, Dathan, Abiram; proud persuasion they would escape death. Wickedness in their dwelling and in the midst of them—inseparable companion.

Reformed warning: regulated fervor of holy judgment differs from petty revenge. Shared: Sheol-alive language is judicial, not casual curse.

Psalms 55:16

16But I call to God, and the LORD will save me.

Arminian

But I call to God, and the LORD will save me. Turn from the traitor’s circle to petition and promise.

Arminian pastoral: calling is the believer’s act under pressure. Salvation is expected from the LORD; the call must still be made evening, morning, and noon (v. 17).

Reformed

Calvin: future tense retained—he excites himself to prayer, hope, and confidence. No apparent escape; on the brink of destruction, yet resolves to continue in prayer assured it will succeed.

Reformed accent: the turn is faith stirring itself, not stoic grit. Shared: call and save stand together.

Key contrast Both turn from treachery to calling on God for salvation. Arminians typically stress the believer’s persevering act of prayer that must still be made; Reformed readers typically stress faith exciting itself to assured prayer when no escape appears—God’s certainty undergirding the call.

Psalms 55:17

17Evening and morning and at noon I utter my complaint and moan, and he hears my voice.

Arminian

Wesley: Evening, &c.—the three stated times of prayer among the Jews. Complaint and moan uttered; he hears my voice.

Arminian hearing: fixed hours help weak hearts keep praying. Grace enables; the saint still shows up morning, noon, and night with real moans God hears.

Reformed

Calvin: not content with bare praying—many do that perfunctorily and weary. Assiduity and vehemency; stated hours respect our indisposition. Cry aloud denotes vehemency under grief.

Reformed pastoral: restrict yourself to a rule or remissness grows. Shared: he hears the thrice-daily voice.

Psalms 55:18

18He redeems my soul in safety from the battle that I wage, for many are arrayed against me.

Arminian

Wesley: He hath—speaks of future deliverance as done because of certainty. Restored to former peace. For—more with me than against me; even holy angels employed to defend.

Arminian hope: redemption into safety is trusted as sure. Angelic help and answered prayer are gifts received, not self-secured.

Reformed

Calvin: redeemed into peace—strong phrase for danger and near-miraculous deliverance. Many arrayed against him magnifies the rescue (or, some hear angels with him).

Reformed accent: thankfulness names the mercy already as good as done. Shared: safety from the battle is God’s redeeming.

Psalms 55:19

19God will give ear and humble them, he who is enthroned from of old, Selah because they do not change and do not fear God.

Arminian

Wesley: Hear—my prayers. Eternity—who is eternal, therefore unchangeable and almighty. Because—they meet no crosses. Therefore—success makes them go on securely without regard to God or dread of judgments. They do not change and do not fear God.

Arminian warning: unbroken prosperity can harden. Prayer asks the eternal God to humble the fearless.

Reformed

Calvin: God shall hear and afflict them—he who sits from ancient time. Elevating thoughts to God’s eternity checks impatience. No changes → therefore they fear not God: indulgence hardens; pride forgets we are men.

Reformed note: compare short human life with God’s eternity; do not measure God by feeble apprehensions. Shared: changeless ease without fear of God invites humbling.

Psalms 55:20

20My companion stretched out his hand against his friends; he violated his covenant.

Arminian

Wesley: He—they, the persons last mentioned. Companion stretched hand against friends; violated covenant.

Arminian pastoral: covenant-breaking friendship is sin with a name. Peaceable bonds were real; the stretched hand is treachery, not misunderstanding.

Reformed

Calvin: leader of the wicked conspiracy—war in the midst of peace, breach of faith; attack unexpected and treacherous.

Reformed accent: Holy Spirit marks reprobation on crafty seduction. Shared: violated covenant is the charge.

Psalms 55:21

21His speech was smooth as butter, yet war was in his heart; his words were softer than oil, yet they were drawn swords.

Arminian

Speech smooth as butter; war in the heart; words softer than oil, yet drawn swords.

Arminian reading: flattery that kills is exposed so the simple may watch. Soft lips do not prove soft hearts; discernment is part of walking wisely under grace.

Reformed

Calvin: butter and oil on lips, war in heart, words as darts—poison besmeared with honey. Saul’s fair promises and courtiers’ arts. Special trial: crafty attempts to seduce the Lord’s people.

Reformed exhortation: cultivate simplicity of intention against treacherous flatteries. Shared: smooth speech can be a drawn sword.

Psalms 55:22

22Cast your burden on the LORD, and he will sustain you; he will never permit the righteous to be moved.

Arminian

Wesley: Burden—all thy crosses, cares, and fears, lay them upon the Almighty by faith and prayer. Directs this speech to his own soul and to all good men in like circumstances. Suffer—as he doth wicked men. Though he may for a season suffer them to be shaken, yet not to be overwhelmed. Cast your burden; he will sustain; never permit the righteous to be moved.

Arminian pastoral: casting is an act of faith and prayer the believer must take up. Sustain and unmoved are promised to those who actually lay the burden down.

Reformed

Calvin: cast thy giving/gift upon Jehovah—resign into God’s hands the care of what concerns our advantage; not clamorous dictation. He shall feed thee as shepherd; will not suffer the righteous always to stagger—eventually freed from harassing cares.

Reformed accent: absolute submission to the divine will checks impatient prayer. Shared: burden cast, righteous upheld.

Key contrast Both cast the burden on the LORD who sustains and will not let the righteous be finally moved. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s call to lay crosses on God by faith and prayer as a duty the soul must take up; Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s resigning of our ‘giving’ to providence—submission that refuses to dictate terms to God.

Psalms 55:23

23But you, O God, will cast them down into the pit of destruction; men of blood and treachery shall not live out half their days. But I will trust in you.

Arminian

Wesley: Them—the wicked. Not live—cut off by untimely and violent death. Trust in thee—in this confidence quietly wait for deliverance. Pit of destruction; blood and treachery.

Arminian hope: trust is the saint’s settled choice while judgment on bloody men is expected. Waiting is active confidence, not fatalism.

Reformed

Calvin: different end for oppressors—pit of corruption/everlasting destruction; bloody and deceitful. Life under God’s curse is death-like; career of the wicked short. Closes: I will hope in thee—judgment must be waited for in faith and patience.

Reformed comfort: fraud and violence do not rewrite the end. Shared: trust in God while the pit waits for treachery.

Key contrast Both expect the pit for bloody, deceitful men and end in trust. Arminians typically stress quiet waiting in confidence while judgment is expected; Reformed readers typically stress faith and patience waiting for a destruction that is God’s sure vengeance, not a short worldly balance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on complaint under treachery, burden cast on the LORD, and trust while God judges deceit—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 55.

Arminian Notable voices

“Burden—All thy crosses, and cares, and fears, lay them upon the Almighty, by faith and prayer. He directs this speech to his own soul, and to all good men in like circumstances. Suffer—As he doth wicked men. Tho' he may for a season suffer them to be shaken, yet not to be overwhelmed. Them—The wicked. Not live—But shall be cut off by an untimely and violent death. Trust in thee—And in this confidence I will quietly wait for deliverance.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 55:22–23)

Reformed Notable voices

“Cast thy giving upon Jehovah… resign into the hands of God the care of those things which may concern our advantage… He shall feed thee… he will not suffer the righteous always to stagger. Thou, O God! shalt cast them into the pit of corruption: bloody and deceitful men shall not live out half their days: but I will hope in thee. The only reflection which comforts the righteous… is that they can confidently look for a peaceful issue… while… they can discern by faith the certain destruction which impends the wicked.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 55:22–23)
