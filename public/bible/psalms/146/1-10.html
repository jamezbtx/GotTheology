Psalms 146

Verses 1–10 · ESV · Voices

First of the final Hallelujah doxologies that close the Psalter: Praise the LORD! Praise the LORD, O my soul—while life and being last. Put not your trust in princes, in a son of man with no salvation; when breath departs, he returns to earth and plans perish. Blessed is he whose help is the God of Jacob, whose hope is in the LORD his God—Maker of heaven, earth, sea, and all in them, who keeps faith forever; who executes justice for the oppressed, feeds the hungry, sets prisoners free, opens blind eyes, lifts the bowed down, loves the righteous, watches sojourners, upholds widow and fatherless, and ruins the wicked’s way. The LORD reigns forever, Zion’s God to all generations. Praise the LORD!

Psalms 146:1

1Praise the LORD! Praise the LORD, O my soul!

Arminian

Praise the LORD! Praise the LORD, O my soul! Clarke: first of the five closing Hallelujah Psalms.

Shared doxology: both camps begin where the finale begins—Hallelujah from the soul. Classical Arminians answer the summons with willing praise.

Reformed

Calvin’s concern is that God receive the whole praise due—false trusts steal doxology; Hallelujah restores it.

Reformed accent: praise undivided. Shared: Praise the LORD; O my soul.

Psalms 146:2

2I will praise the LORD as long as I live; I will sing praises to my God while I have my being.

Arminian

Praise as long as I live; sing while I have being. Clarke: heart overpowered with goodness; being as continuance.

Arminian vow: lifelong praise is sincerity’s length. Shared: while I live; while I have being.

Reformed

Calvin: praise to the end of the course—so God is not defrauded of ongoing thanks.

Reformed note: perseverance in praise. Shared doxology across camps.

Psalms 146:3

3Put not your trust in princes, in a son of man, in whom there is no salvation.

Arminian

Put not trust in princes or a son of man—no salvation there. Clarke: vanity of confidence in men; necessity of trusting God.

Classical Arminians warn patrons and politics alike. Shared: do not trust princes for salvation.

Reformed

Calvin: princes attract dangerous patronage; even the powerful are but a son of man—wrong to trust frail mortals as demigods.

Reformed accent: clear false stays so praise returns to God. Shared warning and shared cure: trust God, not princes.

Psalms 146:4

4When his breath departs, he returns to the earth; on that very day his plans perish.

Arminian

Breath departs; returns to earth; plans perish that day. Wesley: as soon as dead; thoughts—designs for self or others perish.

Arminian realism: human schemes end with breath. Shared: breath; earth; plans perish.

Reformed

Calvin: trust in men depends on a fleeting breath; at that day thoughts flow away—ambitious plans vanish.

Reformed comfort: mortality unmasks false hope. Shared anthropology: princes die; plans perish.

Psalms 146:5

5Blessed is he whose help is the God of Jacob, whose hope is in the LORD his God,

Arminian

Blessed—help is the God of Jacob; hope in the LORD his God. Clarke: trust in man miserable; trust in God happy.

Both camps: this is the beatitude of the doxology. Classical Arminians plant hope in the LORD Himself.

Reformed

Calvin: hopes stable only when entirely upon God; God of Jacob marks the true God versus false gods; rest by faith of adoption.

Reformed accent: blessed helper is Jacob’s God. Shared praise: blessed is he whose hope is in the LORD.

Psalms 146:6

6who made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that is in them, who keeps faith forever;

Arminian

Maker of heaven, earth, sea, and all in them; keeps faith forever. Wesley: forever—He lives forever to fulfil promises and is eternally faithful.

Arminian trust: Creator plus forever faithfulness grounds hope. Shared: Maker; keeps faith forever.

Reformed

Calvin’s blessed helper is no tribal mascot—He is Creator whose word stands. Forever faith underwrites praise.

Reformed note: creation and fidelity together. Shared doxology: keeps faith forever.

Psalms 146:7

7who executes justice for the oppressed, who gives food to the hungry. The LORD sets the prisoners free;

Arminian

Justice for oppressed; food for hungry; sets prisoners free. Clarke: judgment for those who suffer violence or calumny; food; loosing captives.

Shared mercy agenda: both camps praise the LORD who rights, feeds, and frees. Classical Arminians urge hope here without camp-splitting.

Reformed

Calvin clears false trusts so this God’s just, feeding, freeing character receives praise.

Reformed accent: praise for concrete mercies. Shared: justice; food; prisoners free.

Psalms 146:8

8the LORD opens the eyes of the blind. The LORD lifts up those who are bowed down; the LORD loves the righteous.

Arminian

Opens blind eyes; lifts bowed down; loves the righteous. Clarke: opens eyes to wretchedness and salvation; raises the guilt-bowed; loves those in His image.

Arminian pastoral: sight, lifting, and love meet the needy and the righteous. Shared: opens; lifts; loves.

Reformed

Calvin’s God of Jacob does what princes cannot sustain—sight, lifting, love of the righteous.

Reformed comfort: the LORD’s own verbs. Shared doxology across camps.

Psalms 146:9

9The LORD watches over the sojourners; he upholds the widow and the fatherless, but the way of the wicked he brings to ruin.

Arminian

Watches sojourners; upholds widow and fatherless; ruins the wicked’s way. Clarke: preserves strangers; provides for widow/orphan; turns the wicked’s way upside down.

Both camps: tender toward vulnerable; opposed to wicked paths. Shared praise includes both sides of the verse.

Reformed

Calvin’s overthrow of false stays makes room to praise this holy care and holy ruin of wicked ways.

Reformed accent: watchfulness and judgment together. Shared: sojourners; widow; fatherless; wicked ruined.

Psalms 146:10

10The LORD will reign forever, your God, O Zion, to all generations. Praise the LORD!

Arminian

The LORD reigns forever—Zion’s God to all generations. Praise the LORD! Clarke: He can never fail; trust Him and bless the Lord.

Shared finale: everlasting reign and Hallelujah. Classical Arminians and Reformed believers stand together in this closing doxology—no fork required.

Reformed

Calvin began by clearing rivals to praise; the psalm ends with forever reign and Hallelujah restored to God alone.

Reformed climax: Zion’s God forever. Shared: The LORD will reign forever. Praise the LORD!

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the first closing Hallelujah, lifelong praise, the vanity of trust in princes, blessed hope in the God of Jacob, and the LORD who keeps faith and reigns forever—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 146:1–10.

Arminian Notable voices

“This is the first of the Psalms called Hallelujah Psalms, of which there are five, and which conclude the book. While I live will I praise—The true feeling of a heart overpowered with a sense of God’s goodness. While I have any being—in my continuance, in my progression, my eternal existence. Put not your trust in princes—God permitted change in a king’s disposition to teach the vanity of confidence in men, and the necessity of trusting in himself. His breath goeth forth—when he ceases to respire he ceases to live; all his thoughts, purposes, and projects come to nought and perish. Happy is he that hath the God of Jacob for his help, whose hope is in the LORD his God—while he that trusts in man is miserable, he that trusts in God is happy. He made heaven and earth; he keeps his truth for ever; therefore his promises must be fulfilled to them that trust in him. The LORD shall reign for ever—therefore he can never fail; and he is thy God, O Zion. Therefore trust in him and bless the Lord.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 146)

Reformed Notable voices

“Trust not in princes: this admonition is appropriately inserted, for one means by which men blind themselves is involving their minds through inventions and being prevented from engaging in the praises of God. That God may have the whole praise due to him, David exposes and overthrows those false stays on which we would otherwise be too much disposed to trust. He names princes, from whom more is to be feared than common men—the great and wealthy have a dangerous attraction, suggesting that we take shelter under their patronage. The most powerful of the world’s princes is but a son of man—enough to rebuke our folly in worshipping them as a kind of demigods. Though princes are furnished with power, money, troops, and other resources, it is wrong to place our trust in frail mortal man. As soon as man has ceased to breathe, his corpse is subject to putrefaction; those who put their trust in men depend upon a fleeting breath; in that day all his thoughts perish. Blessed is he whose help is the Lord: the hopes of men are only stable and well-founded when they rest entirely upon God. He calls him the God of Jacob, to distinguish him from the multitude of false gods; it is only by an assured faith of adoption that any of us can rest upon him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 146)
