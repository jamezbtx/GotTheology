Psalms 116

Verses 1–19 · ESV · Voices

Egyptian Hallel thanksgiving: love for the Lord because He heard pleas for mercy; death's snares and Sheol's pangs drive a cry for deliverance. The Lord is gracious, righteous, merciful; He preserves the simple and deals bountifully. Delivered from death, tears, and stumbling, the psalmist walks before the Lord, renders the cup of salvation, pays vows among the people, confesses that precious in the Lord's sight is the death of His saints, and offers thanksgiving in Jerusalem's courts.

Psalms 116:1

1I love the Lord, because he has heard my voice and my pleas for mercy.

Arminian

I love the Lord, because He has heard my voice and my pleas for mercy. Wesley: I love the Lord, because He hath heard my voice. Love answers heard prayer.

Classical Arminians: mercy sincerely pleaded for is sincerely heard; love is the freed heart's response, not a coerced motion only.

Reformed

Calvin: the prophet testifies his love to God as the fruit of experienced deliverance. Hearing is God's prior kindness; love follows grace.

Reformed accent: we love because He first dealt mercifully. Shared: I love the Lord, because He has heard.

Psalms 116:2

2Because he inclined his ear to me, therefore I will call on him as long as I live.

Arminian

Because He inclined His ear to me, therefore I will call on Him as long as I live. Wesley: inclined His ear—therefore will I call as long as I live. Past hearing funds lifelong prayer.

Arminian pastoral: the ear of God invites continued calling; prayer is not exhausted by one answer.

Reformed

Calvin: God inclined His ear—an act of fatherly condescension. Lifelong calling is the fit return.

Shared: inclined His ear; call as long as I live.

Psalms 116:3

3The snares of death encompassed me; the pangs of Sheol laid hold on me; I suffered distress and anguish.

Arminian

Snares of death, pangs of Sheol, distress and anguish. The psalm does not minimize peril.

Both camps: real mortal danger drives the cry. Shared honesty about anguish.

Reformed

Calvin: when compassed by the snares of death, the need of prayer is felt. Sheol's grip is not poetic fluff.

Shared: snares of death encompassed me.

Psalms 116:4

4Then I called on the name of the Lord: “O Lord, I pray, deliver my soul!”

Arminian

Then I called on the name of the Lord: "O Lord, I pray, deliver my soul!" Wesley: then called I upon the name of the Lord. "Then" marks prayer as the turning.

Classical Arminians: calling is a real act under assisting grace—the soul truly asks, and God truly delivers those who call.

Reformed

Calvin: he fled to prayer—the name of the Lord as refuge. Deliverance of the soul is requested from the only Helper.

Reformed accent: prayer itself arises as God draws the distressed. Shared: O Lord, deliver my soul!

Key contrast Both confess that deliverance is sought by calling on the Lord's name. Arminians typically hear a graciously enabled, resistible cry that God sincerely answers. Reformed readers typically hear prayer as the Spirit-wrought flight of the saints, effectual in those God delivers according to His purpose.

Psalms 116:5

5Gracious is the Lord, and righteous; our God is merciful.

Arminian

Gracious is the Lord, and righteous; our God is merciful. Wesley: gracious, righteous, merciful—attributes held together.

Both camps: grace does not cancel righteousness; mercy does not deny justice.

Reformed

Calvin: God is gracious, just, and merciful—names that steady the delivered soul.

Shared: gracious, righteous, merciful.

Psalms 116:6

6The Lord preserves the simple; when I was brought low, he saved me.

Arminian

The Lord preserves the simple; when I was brought low, He saved me. Simple means undoing self-wisdom—lowliness that receives help.

Arminian comfort: brought low is not disqualifying; it is where saving help meets us.

Reformed

Calvin: He preserves the simple who distrust their own wisdom. Being brought low precedes being saved.

Shared: preserves the simple; He saved me.

Psalms 116:7

7Return, O my soul, to your rest; for the Lord has dealt bountifully with you.

Arminian

Return, O my soul, to your rest; for the Lord has dealt bountifully with you. Wesley: Return unto thy rest, O my soul. Rest is commanded self-talk under bounty.

Both camps: rest is found in God's dealing, not in stoic numbness.

Reformed

Calvin: the soul is invited back to rest because God has dealt bountifully. Rest is theological, not merely emotional.

Shared: Return to your rest; dealt bountifully.

Psalms 116:8

8For you have delivered my soul from death, my eyes from tears, my feet from stumbling;

Arminian

Delivered soul from death, eyes from tears, feet from stumbling. Triple mercy—life, comfort, steadiness.

Both camps: full-orbed deliverance. Shared thanksgiving.

Reformed

Calvin traces benefits from death to tears to stumbling—complete rescue by God's hand.

Shared: delivered from death, tears, stumbling.

Psalms 116:9

9I will walk before the Lord in the land of the living.

Arminian

I will walk before the Lord in the land of the living. Deliverance aims at lived communion, not mere survival.

Arminian pastoral: walking before the Lord is willing obedience in public life.

Reformed

Calvin: the delivered walk before God in the land of the living—consecrated existence under His eye.

Shared: walk before the Lord in the land of the living.

Psalms 116:10

10I believed, even when I spoke: “I am greatly afflicted”;

Arminian

I believed, even when I spoke: "I am greatly afflicted." Faith and honest affliction speech are not enemies.

Both camps: believing can confess pain without unbelief's final word.

Reformed

Calvin allows that faith speaks affliction plainly while still believing. Complaint is not always apostasy.

Shared: I believed, even when afflicted.

Psalms 116:11

11I said in my alarm, “All mankind are liars.”

Arminian

I said in my alarm, "All mankind are liars." Panic can overspeak; yet human help truly fails compared with God.

Arminian pastoral: alarm needs correcting by return to trust (vv. 7–9), not denial that people disappoint.

Reformed

Calvin: in alarm he pronounced all men liars—hyperbole of distress that still teaches not to make flesh our trust.

Shared: in alarm; all mankind are liars.

Psalms 116:12

12What shall I render to the Lord for all his benefits to me?

Arminian

What shall I render to the Lord for all His benefits to me? Wesley: What shall I render for all His benefits? Gratitude asks for a fitting return.

Both camps: benefits cannot be paid as wage; they can be answered with worship.

Reformed

Calvin: the question of rendering drives to vowed praise, not to merit. Benefits silence boasting.

Shared: What shall I render for all His benefits?

Psalms 116:13

13I will lift up the cup of salvation and call on the name of the Lord,

Arminian

I will lift up the cup of salvation and call on the name of the Lord. Wesley: I will take the cup of salvation. Thanksgiving drink-offering posture—receiving while calling.

Both camps: salvation's cup is lifted in dependence, not self-congratulation.

Reformed

Calvin: the cup of salvation and calling on the name answer benefits received. Praise is the return.

Shared: cup of salvation; call on the name.

Psalms 116:14

14I will pay my vows to the Lord in the presence of all his people.

Arminian

I will pay my vows to the Lord in the presence of all His people. Public fidelity—private mercy becomes congregational witness.

Arminian pastoral: vows paid are free devotion kept, not bargains that purchase grace.

Reformed

Calvin stresses public vows among the people—thanksgiving that edifies the Church.

Shared: pay my vows in the presence of all His people.

Psalms 116:15

15Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints.

Arminian

Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of His saints. Wesley: Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of His saints. Saints are not disposable to God.

Classical Arminians: every believer's death is dear to the Father who sincerely loved them in life and receives them in death.

Reformed

Calvin: the death of the saints is precious to God—He does not cast them off as worthless. Their end is watched with care.

Reformed comfort: preciousness secured in covenant election. Shared: precious is the death of His saints.

Key contrast Both confess that the death of the Lord's saints is precious in His sight. Arminians typically hear the Father's sincere care for every believer who dies in the Lord. Reformed readers typically hear that preciousness as covenant certainty for the elect saints whose times are appointed in God's counsel.

Psalms 116:16

16O Lord, I am your servant; I am your servant, the son of your maidservant. You have loosed my bonds.

Arminian

O Lord, I am Your servant, son of Your maidservant; You have loosed my bonds. Wesley: truly I am Thy servant; Thou hast loosed my bonds. Freedom serves.

Arminian reading: loosed bonds enable willing service—liberty for obedience, not license.

Reformed

Calvin: loosed bonds become lifelong service. Servant identity is gift and claim together.

Shared: I am Your servant; You have loosed my bonds.

Psalms 116:17

17I will offer to you the sacrifice of thanksgiving and call on the name of the Lord.

Arminian

I will offer the sacrifice of thanksgiving and call on the name of the Lord. Thanks is sacrifice—costly praise.

Both camps: thanksgiving and calling remain paired.

Reformed

Calvin: thanksgiving sacrifice and the Name belong together in the restored worshiper's life.

Shared: sacrifice of thanksgiving; call on the name.

Psalms 116:18

18I will pay my vows to the Lord in the presence of all his people,

Arminian

I will pay my vows to the Lord in the presence of all His people—refrain reinforcing public gratitude.

Both camps: repetition is zeal, not filler.

Reformed

Calvin's public-vow theme returns: the congregation is the theater of paid promises.

Shared: pay my vows in the presence of all His people.

Psalms 116:19

19in the courts of the house of the Lord, in your midst, O Jerusalem. Praise the Lord!

Arminian

In the courts of the house of the Lord, in Your midst, O Jerusalem. Praise the Lord! Located praise—Zion's courts, then Hallelujah.

Both camps: deliverance ends in corporate worship. Classical Arminians hear a willing choir in Jerusalem's midst.

Reformed

Calvin closes in the courts of the Lord's house—praise localized where God set His name, and Hallelujah as the fit seal.

Shared: courts of the house of the Lord; Praise the Lord!

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on love that answers heard prayer, deliverance from death and tears, and vows paid in the courts of the Lord—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 116:1–19.

Arminian Notable voices

“I love the Lord, because he hath heard my voice. Because he hath inclined his ear—therefore will I call upon him as long as I live. The sorrows of death compassed me—then called I upon the name of the Lord. Gracious is the Lord, and righteous; yea, our God is merciful. Return unto thy rest, O my soul. What shall I render unto the Lord for all his benefits? I will take the cup of salvation. Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints. O Lord, truly I am thy servant; thou hast loosed my bonds.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 116)

Reformed Notable voices

“The prophet testifies his love to God as the fruit of experienced deliverance. God inclined His ear—an act of fatherly condescension. When compassed by the snares of death, he fled to prayer. God is gracious, just, and merciful; He preserves the simple who distrust their own wisdom. The soul is invited back to rest because God has dealt bountifully. The cup of salvation and public vows answer benefits received. The death of the saints is precious to God—He does not cast them off as worthless. Loosed bonds become lifelong service and thanksgiving in the courts of His house.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Psalm 116)
