Passage

Psalms 135

Verses 1–21 · ESV · Voices

Servants standing in the LORD’s house are summoned to praise His name: He is good; He has chosen Jacob as His possession; He does whatever He pleases in heaven, earth, and deeps; He struck Egypt, kings, and Canaan and gave the land as heritage. His name endures; He will vindicate His people. Idols of silver and gold cannot speak, see, or hear—and their makers become like them. Houses of Israel, Aaron, and Levi, and all who fear the LORD, bless Him from Zion.

Psalms 135:1

1Praise the LORD! Praise the name of the LORD, give praise, O servants of the LORD,

Arminian

Praise the LORD; praise the name of the LORD; give praise, O servants of the LORD. Clarke frames the whole as exhortation for goodness, greatness, wonders in nature and Egypt, then the vanity of idols. Servants are named as the choir, not as spectators.

Arminian hearing: Hallelujah is a sincere summons to willing praise under grace. The name is praised because it is worthy; servants answer freely, not as mute instruments.

Reformed

Calvin: exhortation to praise for goodness to the chosen and for power apparent in the world. Servants and name come first so gratitude is not skipped for invented rites.

Reformed accent: praise reiterated because we undervalue it and are tardy. Shared: Praise the LORD; servants of the LORD.

Psalms 135:2

2who stand in the house of the LORD, in the courts of the house of our God!

Arminian

Who stand in the house of the LORD, in the courts of the house of our God. Wesley: ye—ye priests and Levites. Clarke: priests and Levites. Courts widen the call beyond the inner house alone.

Arminian pastoral: ordered servants lead, yet the courts include the people. Praise is public liturgy, not private mood.

Reformed

Calvin: not Levites only—reasons apply to all God’s children; priests lead, people in the courts respond (plural courts: priestly and common).

Reformed pastoral: ordered worship that includes the whole congregation. Shared: house and courts.

Psalms 135:3

3Praise the LORD, for the LORD is good; sing to his name, for it is pleasant!

Arminian

Praise the LORD, for the LORD is good; sing to His name, for it is pleasant. Clarke: first reason—He is good; second—it is becoming and truly comfortable to an upright mind to acknowledge this infinite Being.

Arminian accent: goodness sincerely invites song. Pleasant praise is fitting response, not flattery of a reluctant deity.

Reformed

Calvin: no sacrifice delights God more than thanksgiving; reiteration censures our tardiness. “Pleasant” may mean the name is sweet, or singing praises is comely.

Reformed accent: gratitude as chief worship. Shared: the LORD is good; pleasant to sing His name.

Psalms 135:4

4For the LORD has chosen Jacob for himself, Israel as his own possession.

Arminian

For the LORD has chosen Jacob for Himself, Israel as His own possession. Clarke: He has taken the Israelites for His peculiar people, His peculiar treasure. Covenant belonging is the third reason for praise.

Arminian reading: election here names treasured peoplehood that stirs gratitude and vocation—Jacob chosen for the LORD, not abandoned to idols.

Reformed

Calvin: chosen as peculiar treasure while the Gentile world was passed by—incomparable mercy. Praise of election given to God proves no excellency of their own, but free mercy.

Reformed confession: particular adoption of Jacob. Shared with Clarke: Israel as the LORD’s own possession.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD has chosen Jacob for Himself and Israel as His own possession. Arminians, with Clarke, typically hear covenant treasure and peculiar belonging that stirs praise and responsibility. Reformed readers, with Calvin, typically hear free mercy that adopted this people while the Gentile world was passed by—distinction owed to God, not Israel’s excellency.

Psalms 135:5

5For I know that the LORD is great, and that our Lord is above all gods.

Arminian

For I know that the LORD is great, and that our Lord is above all gods. Clarke: unlimited in power; Fountain of existence above every class of being, whether idolized or not. “I know” is confessed experience.

Both camps: greatness over rival gods. Classical Arminians preach a living God who eclipses idols without denying the hearer’s duty to know and confess.

Reformed

Calvin: from his own knowing he stirs others—immensity incomprehensible, yet glory manifested enough to leave ignorance without excuse. The covenant God is Creator; departure from His law is renunciation of the true God.

Reformed accent: retain Israel in the one true God. Shared: the LORD is great, above all gods.

Psalms 135:6

6Whatever the LORD pleases, he does, in heaven and on earth, in the seas and all deeps.

Arminian

Whatever the LORD pleases, He does, in heaven and on earth, in the seas and all deeps. Clarke: all He has done is right and therefore pleasing in His sight; all realms are under His control.

Arminian hearing: sovereignty that is wise and righteous—not random force. What He pleases is what is right; creation is not orphaned from His will.

Reformed

Calvin: not idle in heaven resigning reins to chance—real practical power; special providence; nothing by chance; will implies counsel; nothing without divine will and decree; mysterious will still fountain of justice.

Reformed confession: whatever He pleases, He does—against fortune and bare “general providence.” Shared: heaven, earth, seas, deeps.

Key contrast Both confess that whatever the LORD pleases He does in heaven, earth, seas, and deeps. Arminians, with Clarke, stress that all He has done is right and under His wise control—sovereignty without moral caprice. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress special providence against chance: nothing without divine will and decree; what seems fortuitous is ordered by secret wisdom.

Psalms 135:7

7He it is who makes the clouds rise at the end of the earth, who makes lightnings for the rain and brings forth the wind from his storehouses.

Arminian

He makes the clouds rise at the end of the earth, lightnings for the rain, and wind from His storehouses. Wesley: rain—an eminent instance of His good providence; from all parts of the earth.

Arminian pastoral: ordinary weather is praise-material. Good providence in clouds and wind is meant to be noticed and sung, not treated as blind nature only.

Reformed

Calvin: examples that nothing happens of itself—vapors, lightning mixed with rain, winds. Not philosophers’ bare order only: when it rains, by divine commission, not blind instinct of nature.

Reformed accent: particular providence in weather. Shared with Wesley: rain as providence.

Psalms 135:8

8He it was who struck down the firstborn of Egypt, both of man and of beast;

Arminian

He struck down the firstborn of Egypt, both of man and of beast. Deliverance history enters the hymn: judgment that opened Israel’s way out.

Shared: real historical stroke. Classical Arminians refuse a God who cannot act; praise names mercy to Israel through Egypt’s loss.

Reformed

Calvin: reverts to special benefits that obliged the Church—God opposed great kingdoms for His people’s sake. Firstborn struck as memorable proof of regard for their safety.

Reformed accent: Egypt’s blow as fatherly love to the chosen. Shared: firstborn of Egypt struck.

Psalms 135:9

9who in your midst, O Egypt, sent signs and wonders against Pharaoh and all his servants;

Arminian

Who in your midst, O Egypt, sent signs and wonders against Pharaoh and all his servants. Egypt is addressed as the theater of signs—Pharaoh’s court is not a rival providence.

Both camps: signs and wonders against Pharaoh. The fork is not whether God sent them.

Reformed

Calvin’s frame: signs and wonders in Egypt abundantly testify power and mercy in delivering the people.

Reformed pastoral: Pharaoh’s servants under the same hand. Shared: signs against Pharaoh.

Psalms 135:10

10who struck down many nations and killed mighty kings,

Arminian

Who struck down many nations and killed mighty kings. Conquest follows exodus in the praise list—many nations, not one night only.

Arminian reading: the LORD’s hand in history is moral and purposeful; kings fall under judgment that serves the heritage of His people.

Reformed

Calvin: He did not lead them out to wander aimlessly but to settle them in the promised inheritance—continued power after Egypt.

Reformed accent: unwearied kindness unto possession. Shared: nations and kings struck down.

Psalms 135:11

11Sihon, king of the Amorites, and Og, king of Bashan, and all the kingdoms of Canaan,

Arminian

Sihon, king of the Amorites, and Og, king of Bashan, and all the kingdoms of Canaan. Named foes keep memory concrete—anonymous “nations” become faces at the border.

Shared: particular kings under the LORD’s strike. Praise refuses vague spirituality detached from the story.

Reformed

Calvin: Sihon and Og first formidable at the entrance; people not yet habituated to war. Preference for a multitude of inconsiderable persons over mighty kings.

Reformed accent: divine goodness in the preference. Shared: Sihon, Og, kingdoms of Canaan.

Psalms 135:12

12and gave their land as a heritage, a heritage to his people Israel.

Arminian

And gave their land as a heritage, a heritage to His people Israel. Double “heritage” stresses gift—land as covenant grant, not mere spoil.

Arminian comfort: gift-language invites gratitude and faithful living in the land, not boast in Israel’s sword alone.

Reformed

Calvin: crowning goodness—firm possession; keeping no less achievement than acquiring, with deadly enemies around.

Reformed comfort: heritage held by the LORD’s hand. Shared: land as heritage to Israel.

Psalms 135:13

13Your name, O LORD, endures forever, your renown, O LORD, throughout all ages.

Arminian

Your name, O LORD, endures forever; Your renown, O LORD, throughout all ages. Name and memorial outlast empires and the idols about to be mocked.

Both camps: forever name. Classical Arminians hear enduring fame that still calls living servants to bless Him now.

Reformed

Calvin: everlasting praise due for preserving Church and people; the whole world is theater of divine attributes, but the Church is the orchestra—most conspicuous part.

Reformed accent: name forever tied to Church preservation. Shared: name and renown forever.

Psalms 135:14

14For the LORD will vindicate his people and have compassion on his servants.

Arminian

For the LORD will vindicate His people and have compassion on His servants. Wesley: Judge—will in due time plead their cause; Repent—He will recall that severe sentence which for their sins He had passed upon them.

Arminian pastoral: vindication and compassion arrive in history’s due time. Severe sentence for sin is real; so is recall in mercy toward servants who remain His.

Reformed

Calvin: judging = just government; God watches and preserves; under affliction sustain hope—help at proper season when sufficiently humbled; temporary displeasure; mercy in wrath; He cannot bear to be left childless of His people.

Reformed comfort: compassion as fatherly restoration. Shared with Wesley: vindicate and show compassion.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD will vindicate His people and have compassion on His servants. Arminians, with Wesley, typically hear timely pleading of their cause and recall of severe sentence after sin. Reformed readers, with Calvin, typically hear sustained hope under delay: temporary displeasure, mercy remembered in wrath, fatherly refusal to leave His people finally destroyed.

Psalms 135:15

15The idols of the nations are silver and gold, the work of human hands.

Arminian

The idols of the nations are silver and gold, the work of human hands. Clarke notes the near-parallel to Psalm 115. Handmade gods are demoted by their metal and their makers.

Both camps: idols are human workmanship. Classical Arminians and Reformed unite against trust in crafted deities.

Reformed

Calvin: heathen stupidity seeking nearness by idols; Israel reminded of signal mercy to abide in pure worship.

Reformed warning: superstition vs simplicity. Shared: idols of silver and gold, human hands.

Psalms 135:16

16They have mouths, but do not speak; they have eyes, but do not see;

Arminian

They have mouths, but do not speak; they have eyes, but do not see. Sensory organs without life—parody of the living God who speaks and sees.

Shared: dead images cannot answer. The contrast with the speaking, seeing LORD needs no genre detour.

Reformed

Calvin’s contrast (with Ps 115): vain show of divinity against the God who proved Himself by clear proofs—so the people praise and submit more cheerfully.

Shared: mouths that do not speak; eyes that do not see.

Psalms 135:17

17they have ears, but do not hear, nor is there any breath in their mouths.

Arminian

They have ears, but do not hear, nor is there any breath in their mouths. Breathlessness is the punchline—no life-breath, no answer.

Arminian warning: do not trust what cannot hear your cry. The God of vv. 1–14 breathes wind from storehouses; idols have none.

Reformed

No breath in their mouths—Calvin’s larger point: lying vanities cannot claim deity beside the LORD who governs deeps and wind.

Reformed accent: breathless idols vs the living God. Shared: ears that do not hear.

Psalms 135:18

18Those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them.

Arminian

Those who make them become like them; so do all who trust in them. Trust reshapes the trustor—makers and trusters go numb like their craft.

Arminian pastoral: imitation runs both ways. Trust the living LORD and become responsive; trust silver silence and grow like it.

Reformed

Calvin: the more zealous for idols, the heavier the judgment; aim is to deter those under the Word from following their example.

Reformed warning: makers and trusters become like their trust. Shared: become like them.

Psalms 135:19

19O house of Israel, bless the LORD! O house of Aaron, bless the LORD!

Arminian

O house of Israel, bless the LORD! O house of Aaron, bless the LORD! People and priestly house are named to bless—liturgy for the whole covenant family.

Both camps: blessing commanded. Classical Arminians hear sincere corporate duty under grace.

Reformed

Calvin: here bless (as Ps 115 says trust); house of Aaron with Israel—priestly lead in blessing.

Reformed pastoral: blessing as the people’s work. Shared: Israel and Aaron bless the LORD.

Psalms 135:20

20O house of Levi, bless the LORD! You who fear the LORD, bless the LORD!

Arminian

O house of Levi, bless the LORD! You who fear the LORD, bless the LORD! Levi added; then all who fear—so the circle is not bloodline alone.

Arminian accent: fear of the LORD widens the choir. The summons is real toward all who fear His name.

Reformed

Calvin: Levites added—two orders of priesthood; and those who fear Jehovah. Everything else matches the twin psalm’s close.

Reformed accent: fearers included with Levi. Shared: Levi and all who fear, bless.

Psalms 135:21

21Blessed be the LORD from Zion, he who dwells in Jerusalem! Praise the LORD!

Arminian

Blessed be the LORD from Zion, He who dwells in Jerusalem! Praise the LORD! Blessing is located where He pledged presence, then sealed with Hallelujah.

Arminian close: Zion-praise is not escape from obedience elsewhere—it is the fountain-place of blessing for servants who stand in His courts.

Reformed

Calvin: blesses out of Zion because God promised to hear and display favor there; “dwells in Jerusalem” is visible manifestation of favor, not cramped localism—majesty fills heaven and earth.

Reformed close: Zion as pledged meeting-place. Shared: Blessed be the LORD from Zion; Praise the LORD!

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on praise in the courts, Jacob chosen as the LORD’s own possession, the God who does whatever He pleases, empty idols, and blessing from Zion—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 135:1–21.

Arminian Notable voices

“An exhortation to praise God for his goodness and greatness, for his wonders in nature, his wonders done in Egypt and in the wilderness, and for his goodness to his people; then the vanity of idols; then Israel, with its priests and Levites, exhorted to praise the Lord. The Lord is good—here is the first reason why he should be praised; and a second: for it is pleasant—it is becoming to acknowledge this infinite Being and our dependence on him, and truly comfortable to an upright mind to be thus employed. For the Lord hath chosen Jacob—he has taken the Israelites for his peculiar people, his peculiar treasure. The Lord is great—unlimited in his power; above all gods—every class of being, whether idolized or not, because he is the Fountain of existence. Whatsoever the Lord pleased—all that he has done is right, and therefore pleasing in his sight; angels, men, spirits, the heavens, the earth, and all their contents were made by him and are under his control. The Lord will judge his people—he will do them justice against their enemies.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 135)

Reformed Notable voices

“An exhortation to praise God, both for his goodness specially shown to his chosen people, and for his power and glory apparent in the world at large. For God hath chosen Jacob unto himself, and Israel for his peculiar treasure: the mercy was surely one of incomparable value—adopted into favor while the whole Gentile world was passed by. The praise of their election is given to God—a clear proof that they owed the distinction not to any excellency of their own, but to the free mercy of God. Whatsoever doth please him, Jehovah does in heaven and in earth: he not only founded heaven and earth at first, but governs all things according to his power. Scripture teaches a real practical power by which he governs the whole world according to his will; nothing takes place by chance or without determination. There is a special providence in the government of the various parts of the world; what appears most fortuitous is ordered by his secret wisdom. Nothing happens without the divine will and decree. His will may be mysterious, but it is to be regarded with reverence as the fountain of all justice and rectitude.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 135)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English editions of the sources named.
