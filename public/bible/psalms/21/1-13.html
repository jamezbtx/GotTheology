Passage

Psalms 21

Verses 1–13 · ESV · Voices

Psalm 21 answers Psalm 20’s prayer with thanksgiving for the LORD’s anointed: the king rejoices in God’s strength and salvation; his heart’s desire and lip’s request are granted; rich blessings and a gold crown meet him; he asked life and received length of days forever and ever; glory, splendor, majesty, and gladness in God’s presence mark him; trusting in the LORD and upheld by the Most High’s steadfast love, he shall not be moved. Then the psalm turns outward: the LORD’s hand finds enemies, makes them a blazing oven in wrath, cuts off their offspring, frustrates their mischief, and puts them to flight—until the congregation closes, Be exalted, O LORD, in your strength! We will sing and praise your power.

Psalms 21:1

1O LORD, in your strength the king rejoices, and in your salvation how greatly he exults!

Arminian

Wesley frames the psalm as thanksgiving answering Psalm 20’s prayers—praises for blessings received concerning the king, with David looking beyond himself to Christ.

Arminian hearing: shared—joy is in the LORD’s strength and salvation, not in the king’s self-made triumph. The anointed exults because God has saved; the congregation learns to rejoice where strength truly sits. Grace enables that rejoicing; the people still must ascribe salvation to the LORD and refuse to steal the song for themselves.

Reformed

Calvin: David could have thanked God privately, but intends to testify that God elevated him and that blessings on him redound to the public good. Believing Israelites express persuasion that God, who created David king, has undertaken to defend him. Strength and salvation mean strong succor—God’s power preserving the king against all dangers.

Reformed accent: shared—rejoicing in God’s strength. The king’s face is a mirror of God’s reconciled countenance; safety of the head is safety of the body under the Head God appointed.

Psalms 21:2

2You have given him his heart’s desire and have not withheld the request of his lips. Selah

Arminian

Wesley ties this psalm to answered prayer: what was asked in Psalm 20 is here praised as given. Desire and request of the lips are real petitions God did not withhold.

Arminian reading: shared—God hears and grants. The king asked; God gave. Grace enables right desire and open request; the hearer still must bring petitions to the LORD rather than treat desire as self-fulfilling. Selah invites pause over answered prayer, not over fate without asking.

Reformed

Calvin: the cause of joy is that God heard the king and liberally granted what he desired. David did not give loose reins to fleshly appetite but bridled affections to desire what was good and lawful; the Spirit presided over his reign. By prophecy he looks to Christ, who desires our salvation—so the Church need not fear that God will reject prayers offered after the King’s example.

Reformed accent: shared—desire granted by God. The request God does not withhold is desire schooled by his Spirit and, finally, the intercession of the heavenly King.

Key contrast Both confess that God has given the king his heart’s desire and not withheld the request of his lips, and both refuse to read the verse as license for unchecked appetite. Arminian readers stress answered petition: grace-enabled desire and spoken request that God truly hears and grants. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress Spirit-bridled desire and Christ’s interceding example—so the gift confirms a lawful, God-shaped asking, not a bare will that finally decides what heaven must yield.

Psalms 21:3

3For you meet him with rich blessings; you set a crown of fine gold upon his head.

Arminian

Wesley (on “prevent”): crowning him with manifold blessings, both more and sooner than he expected—with excellent blessings. The crown of fine gold marks royal favor exceeding the king’s timetable.

Arminian pastoral: shared—God meets the anointed with rich good and sets the crown. Blessing can outrun expectation without erasing the reality of prior asking. Receive the crown as gift; do not invent a god who never surprises with kindness, nor one who crowns without covenant faithfulness.

Reformed

Calvin: “Thou wilt prevent him”—such will be God’s liberality and promptitude in spontaneously bestowing blessings that he not only grants what is asked but anticipates requests and loads the king with good far beyond expectation. The crown is emblem of royalty; God is guardian of the king he created. The doctrine of Christ’s everlasting kingdom is established: he was set on the throne not by men’s suffrages but by God, who from heaven set the crown on his head.

Reformed accent: shared—rich blessings and God-set crown. Prevenient liberality is the Creator-King’s spontaneous favor toward the head he appointed, crowning a kingdom whose duration runs to Christ.

Key contrast Both confess that God meets the king with rich blessings and sets the gold crown on his head, and both gladly allow blessing beyond bare desert. Arminian readers, with Wesley, hear manifold good “more and sooner than expected”—favor that still answers the story of prayer and hope. Reformed readers, with Calvin, hear spontaneous liberality that anticipates requests: God crowns the king he created, so the meeting is the Guardian’s initiative toward his own appointed head, not surprise that finally hangs on how quickly the king learned to expect it.

Psalms 21:4

4He asked life of you; you gave it to him, length of days forever and ever.

Arminian

Wesley: For ever—Thou gavest him a long life and reign here, and after that didst translate him to live with thee for ever. But this was more eminently fulfilled in Christ, who asked of his Father life, or to be saved from death, Heb. 5:7, though with submission to his will: yet the Father gave him perfect possession of everlasting life in soul and body at his right hand.

Arminian hearing: shared royal/messianic confession—David’s length of days points beyond himself to Christ’s everlasting life. Asking is real and submitted; the gift is life forever. Follow the Son’s “not my will” even while you ask for life.

Reformed

Calvin: this length of days exceeds any one man’s span; David comprehends the Eternal King. From weak beginnings fraught with peril comes glory almost above the heavens. The same is exemplified in Christ, who from contempt, ignominy, death, the grave, and despair was raised by the Father to the sovereignty of heaven, to sit at the Father’s right hand forever.

Reformed accent: shared—asked life, given forever in the Anointed. The gift runs through the kingdom promise; resurrection glory answers the ask that death seemed to deny.

Psalms 21:5

5His glory is great through your salvation; splendor and majesty you bestow on him.

Arminian

Wesley: Glory—his fame or renown. Salvation—by reason of those great and glorious deliverances which thou hast wrought both for him, and by him.

Arminian pastoral: shared—glory through God’s salvation, not through self-made renown. Splendor and majesty are bestowed. Praise the LORD for deliverances done for the king and through the king; refuse to treat royal glory as a human manufacture.

Reformed

Calvin: the people intimate that their king, through God’s protection and deliverances, would become more renowned than if he had reigned by human wealth and strength. They leave heathen kings to ennoble themselves by their own achievements; they set more value on God’s favor toward their king, and promise such assistance as will adorn him with majesty and honor.

Reformed accent: shared—glory through salvation God works. Bestowed splendor marks divine favor, not autonomous valor.

Psalms 21:6

6For you make him most blessed forever; you make him glad with the joy of your presence.

Arminian

Wesley: Countenance—smiling upon him, by thy grace and favour. Blessedness forever and gladness from God’s smiling presence crown the thanksgiving section.

Arminian reading: shared—God makes the king blessed and glad in his presence. Joy is relational: grace and favor known in the LORD’s countenance. Seek that presence; do not settle for gifts without the Giver’s smile.

Reformed

Calvin: the king is set to be blessings forever—an illustrious pattern of divine liberality, or a name used in prayer as an example of desired blessing. Joy before God’s countenance points to the proper cause: knowledge of being the object of divine favor. True happiness is to experience God so favorable that we dwell as it were in his presence.

Reformed accent: shared—blessed forever and glad in God’s presence. The light of the reconciled countenance is the solid joy; bare provision without that favor is not the verse’s gladness.

Psalms 21:7

7For the king trusts in the LORD, and through the steadfast love of the Most High he shall not be moved.

Arminian

Wesley’s outline marks verses 7–13 as confidence in God. The king trusts; through the Most High’s steadfast love he shall not be moved.

Arminian hearing: shared—trust in the LORD and hesed that steadies. The anointed must actually trust; grace enables that reliance, and the hearer still turns from self-security to the LORD. Unmoved standing is promised where trust meets steadfast love—not where presumption replaces trust.

Reformed

Calvin: the king, as he places by faith his dependence on God and his goodness, will not be subject to the disasters that overthrow worldly kingdoms. What is attributed to the king belongs to the whole Church—a promise to keep the faithful tranquil amid the world’s wheel of sudden falls. Only those who betake themselves to God’s bosom by assured faith have this firmness; the ground is that God mercifully cherishes those he has received into his favor.

Reformed accent: shared—trust and steadfast love. Stability is covenant hesed toward the kingdom typed in David and perfected in Christ; the unmoved stand is God’s cherishing, not a precarious balance the king maintains alone.

Key contrast Both confess that the king trusts in the LORD and that through the steadfast love of the Most High he shall not be moved. Arminian readers stress the required act of trust grace enables and the hearer must take up—hesed meets genuine reliance, not presumption. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress that firmness rests on God’s merciful cherishing of those received into his favor—so the unmoved kingdom is covenant stability under the Most High, not a stand that finally hangs on the strength of the king’s trusting alone.

Psalms 21:8

8Your hand will find out all your enemies; your right hand will find out those who hate you.

Arminian

Confidence turns to the enemies of the king and of God. The hand and right hand will find them out—none escape detection.

Arminian pastoral: shared—God’s hand finds enemies. Hatred of the anointed does not hide from the LORD. Take warning: opposing God’s king is not a private feud God overlooks.

Reformed

Calvin: after internal happiness comes invincible strength against enemies. This especially applies to Christ’s kingdom, never without foes; Satan’s ministers never cease to plot its overthrow. It is well that our King, who lifts his hand as a shield, is stronger than all. The hand finds—enemies shall not escape destruction.

Reformed accent: shared—right hand finds haters. Christ’s stronger hand shields the Church; malice does not outrun his reach.

Psalms 21:9

9You will make them as a blazing oven when you appear. The LORD will swallow them up in his wrath, and fire will consume them.

Arminian

Wesley: Oven—like wood, which when it is cast in there, is quickly consumed. Appearance, wrath, and fire picture swift, total judgment.

Arminian warning: shared—real wrath and consuming fire when God appears. The image is moral and pastoral: do not rage against the LORD’s anointed as if judgment were theater. Flee the oven while mercy still calls.

Reformed

Calvin: a dreadful vengeance against malicious despisers who rise against the only begotten Son. The king is called avenger; the office is God’s; execution is by fire—judgment committed to Christ. “In the time of thy wrath” teaches patience under the cross until the appointed day; God laughs at mad presumption now, yet the day of vengeance will come. The similitude evokes Sodom—an image of the eternal fire prepared for the reprobate.

Reformed accent: shared—blazing oven, wrath, and fire. Severity matches the crime against the Son; timing is God’s, comfort for the righteous who wait.

Key contrast Both confess that God will make enemies as a blazing oven when he appears, swallow them in wrath, and consume them with fire. Arminian readers stress the urgent moral warning: wrath is real and swift like wood in an oven—so flee hostility against the anointed while time remains. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress judgment committed to Christ against those who assault the Son, executed in the appointed time of wrath—so patience waits God’s day, and severity reveals reprobate hatred, not a scare whose outcome finally hangs on human timing alone.

Psalms 21:10

10You will destroy their descendants from the earth, and their offspring from among the children of man.

Arminian

Wesley: Fruit—their children. God will take away both root and branch, the parents and all that wicked race.

Arminian hearing: shared—judgment reaches the wicked line that persists in the parents’ war against God. The verse is not casual cruelty; it is the end of a race set against the LORD’s anointed. Do not enlist your house in that war.

Reformed

Calvin: wrath extends even to the children of the wicked—a doctrine common in Scripture—yet God does not indiscriminately crush the innocent with the guilty. The seed of the ungodly withheld from grace remain children of wrath; severity toward children as toward fathers shows how dear Christ’s kingdom is—mercy to a thousand generations of the righteous, wrath to the third and fourth of the reprobate.

Reformed accent: shared—descendants cut off. Covenant seriousness for the kingdom explains both long mercy and extended judgment.

Psalms 21:11

11Though they plan evil against you, though they devise mischief, they will not succeed.

Arminian

Wesley: Thee—against God, not directly, but by consequence, because it was against David, whom God had anointed, and against the LORD’s people, whose injuries God takes as done to himself.

Arminian pastoral: shared—evil plans against God’s king are against God, and they fail. Mischief is devised; success is denied. Stop plotting; the scheme cannot perform what pride imagines.

Reformed

Calvin: the ungodly deserved ruin because they molested not only man but rushed in pride to war against God. Violence against the kingdom consecrated in David is foul war against God; much more, attack on Christ’s kingdom violates God’s majesty, who wills to reign by his Son. They devised a stratagem that fails—foolish arrogance undertaking the impossible.

Reformed accent: shared—plans fail. Assault on the anointed is assault on God; vanity of the plot is theological, not luck.

Psalms 21:12

12For you will put them to flight; you will aim at their faces with your bows.

Arminian

Flight and aimed bows picture enemies turned and targeted—no escape by clever retreat.

Arminian hearing: shared—God puts enemies to flight and aims true. Confidence in verses 7–13 rests on this active opposition to mischief. Stand with the LORD’s aim, not against it.

Reformed

Calvin: God will make them a butt for his arrows—shooting directly at their faces. The great object is to teach patience until God, at the fit time, brings the ungodly to their end.

Reformed accent: shared—flight under God’s bow. Aimed judgment steadies the faithful to wait the fit time rather than seize vengeance.

Psalms 21:13

13Be exalted, O LORD, in your strength! We will sing and praise your power.

Arminian

Wesley: Exalted—by thy own power, or by the manifestation thereof. The psalm closes with the people’s vow to sing and praise.

Arminian close: shared—exalt the LORD in his strength; we will sing. Thanksgiving that began with the king’s joy ends in congregational praise of God’s power. Keep the vow: sing what the crown and the oven have taught—salvation and judgment both magnify the LORD.

Reformed

Calvin: the psalm concludes with prayer confirming that the kingdom is so connected with God’s glory that his power is reflected from it. True of David’s kingdom; fully accomplished in Christ, appointed King and God manifest in the flesh. His divine power terrifies the wicked and comforts the Church with joy and songs of praise.

Reformed accent: shared—be exalted; we will sing. Christ’s power is the Church’s song; exaltation of the LORD is the last word over both crown and conquest.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the king’s joy in the LORD’s strength, answered desire, crown and everlasting life, trust under the Most High’s steadfast love, and judgment on enemies—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 21:1–13.

Arminian Notable voices

“A thanksgiving for blessings received, ver. 1–6. An expression of confidence in God, ver. 7–13. Of David—The subject of this psalm is the same with the former, both being made for the people’s use, concerning the king. Only the prayers there used are here turned into praises, for the blessings received in answer to their prayers. And as David was an illustrious type of Christ, so in many of these expressions he looks beyond himself to Christ, in whom they are properly, and fully accomplished. Prevent—crowning him with manifold blessings, both more and sooner than he expected. For ever—Thou gavest him a long life and reign here, and after that didst translate him to live with thee for ever. But this was more eminently fulfilled in Christ, who asked of his Father life, or to be saved from death, Heb. 5:7, though with submission to his will: yet the Father gave him the perfect possession of everlasting life at his right hand. Glory—his fame or renown; Salvation—by reason of those great and glorious deliverances wrought for him and by him. Countenance—smiling upon him by thy grace and favour. Oven—like wood quickly consumed. Fruit—their children; God will take away both root and branch. Thee—against God, not directly, but by consequence, because against David whom God had anointed, and against the LORD’s people, whose injuries God takes as done to himself. Exalted—by thy own power, or by the manifestation thereof.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 21)

Reformed Notable voices

“This psalm contains a public and solemn thanksgiving for the prosperous and happy condition of the king. Its subject is almost the same with that of the preceding: there a common prayer excited concern for the head; here the safety and prosperity of the king ought to produce general rejoicing, inasmuch as God by this means intended to preserve the whole body. Above all, the Holy Spirit directs the minds of the faithful to Christ, the end and perfection of this kingdom, teaching that they could not be saved except under the head God himself appointed. David testifies that God elevated him and that blessings on him redounded to the public good; he did not give loose reins to fleshly desire but desired what was good and lawful, looking by prophecy to Christ, who reigns for our salvation. Thou wilt prevent him: such will be God’s liberality that he anticipates requests and loads the king with good far beyond expectation; the crown marks God as guardian of the king he created, and the everlasting duration of Christ’s kingdom is established. He asked life—David comprehends the Eternal King; from contempt, death, and despair Christ was raised to sit at the Father’s right hand forever. The king trusteth: as he depends on God and his goodness, he will not be subject to the disasters that overthrow worldly kingdoms. Thy hand shall find enemies; judgment is committed to Christ, who casts adversaries into everlasting fire—the judgment of God, in the time of his wrath. Raise thyself, O Jehovah—the kingdom is so connected with God’s glory that his power is reflected from it, fully in Christ, God manifest in the flesh.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 21)
