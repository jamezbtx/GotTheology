Passage

Psalms 9

Verses 1–20 · ESV · Voices

Psalm 9 is David’s thanksgiving and prayer: he gives whole-heart thanks and recounts the LORD’s wonderful deeds, glad and exulting in the Most High as enemies stumble before his presence. God has maintained his just cause from the throne of righteous judgment, rebuked nations, and blotted out the wicked—while the LORD sits enthroned forever for justice, a stronghold for the oppressed. Those who know his name trust him; he does not forsake seekers. Praise rises from Zion and deeds are told among the peoples, for he who avenges blood does not forget the afflicted. David then pleads for grace from the gates of death, that he may rejoice in salvation at Zion’s gates. Nations sink in their own pit; the LORD is known by judgment; the wicked return to Sheol, yet the needy are not always forgotten. He asks God to arise, put the nations in fear, and make them know they are but men.

Psalms 9:1

1I will give thanks to the LORD with my whole heart; I will recount all of your wonderful deeds.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame for the psalm: David praises God for victory over enemies (vv. 1–6), triumphs in confidence that God will judge the world and protect his people (vv. 7–10), calls others to praise (vv. 11–12), and prays for help (vv. 13–20).

Arminian hearing: whole-heart thanksgiving is a real, sincere act—recounting deeds already done so the heart stays grateful and open. Grace enables praise; the pray-er must still give thanks with the whole heart, not half-lips and half-boast.

Reformed

Calvin: David begins thus to induce God to succor him in present calamities. Past mercies should inspire confidence that God will be gracious in what comes. The whole heart means an upright, sincere heart opposed to a double heart—distinguishing him from hypocrites who praise with lips while hearts are unaffected. Whatever commendable in him proceeded entirely from pure grace. Worldly victors steal glory back to their own valor; David will not.

Reformed accent: whole-heart praise is grace-wrought sincerity that refuses to split glory with self. Remembered marvels open the gate of prayer.

Psalms 9:2

2I will be glad and exult in you; I will sing praise to your name, O Most High.

Arminian

Wesley’s summary places gladness and song beside thanksgiving as the posture of the delivered.

Arminian pastoral: exultation in God alone is practiced joy—gladness and song the hearer takes up toward the Most High. Praise is not a mood that happens to us; it is the glad response grace enables and obedience keeps.

Reformed

Calvin: the faithful praise without hypocrisy when they do not rest on themselves for happiness and are not intoxicated with carnal presumption, but rejoice in God alone—seeking joy from his favor and nowhere else. “Exult” means overflowing abundance of joy in God, so not even the smallest drop need be sought elsewhere. He who begins prayer by affirming God as the great object of his joy fortifies confidence before the hearer of prayer.

Reformed accent: joy is relocated—from self to God—before petitions rise.

Key contrast Both confess glad, singing praise to the Most High as the right answer to his deeds. Arminian readers stress practiced gladness and song the delivered must take up and keep. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress that joy is sought in God alone—overflowing favor that renounces self-sourced happiness—so praise becomes confidence before petition, not a rival claim on glory.

Psalms 9:3

3When my enemies turn back, they stumble and perish before your presence.

Arminian

Wesley: Turned back—put to flight. Enemies fall and perish at the LORD’s presence.

Arminian hearing: flight and fall are moral and historical—God’s presence undoes the pursuer. The delivered recount this so trust stays tethered to what God actually did, not to private nerve.

Reformed

Calvin: David assigns the reason he sings—frequent victories achieved not by his own power or his soldiers’, but by God’s free favor. Enemies turn back; faith alone can add that this was not chance or human strength but God fighting for him. They stumble and perish before his presence. Lift eyes from the visible rout to the secret aid of God, who guides the simple, amazes the crafty, strengthens the faint, and weakens the bold as he pleases.

Reformed accent: the battlefield outcome is providence, not luck or muscle.

Psalms 9:4

4For you have maintained my just cause; you have sat on the throne, giving righteous judgment.

Arminian

Wesley’s outline ties this verse to praise for maintained right and cause under God’s righteous throne.

Arminian reading: God maintains a just cause that was truly just. The pray-er may plead real integrity in the matter at hand; righteous judgment answers lived right, not a fiction.

Reformed

Calvin: God stretched forth his hand because David was unrighteously afflicted. If we desire God’s assistance, we ought to fight under his standard. God has acted according to his ordinary manner—undertaking the defense of good causes. He sits as righteous judge gone up to execute his office. David glories in having law and right on his side; God is maintainer of his right and cause.

Reformed accent: vindication is the Judge taking his seat for a cause God owns—not self-justification inventing a throne.

Key contrast Both confess that God maintained David’s just cause from the throne of righteous judgment. Arminian readers stress a real, lived just cause God may try and vindicate as the pray-er actually walked. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress that God undertakes the defense of good causes according to his ordinary manner—the Judge seated for righteousness, so aid is sought under his standard, not claimed as private triumph.

Psalms 9:5

5You have rebuked the nations; you have made the wicked perish; you have blotted out their name forever and ever.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: rebuke of nations and blotting of the wicked’s name belong to the victory-praise section.

Arminian warning: nations and the wicked meet real rebuke and lasting erasure of name. Judgment is public and moral; forgetfulness of God is not a safe anonymity.

Reformed

Calvin: when David beholds enemies overthrown, he does not rejoice in destruction simply considered, but in condemning them for unrighteousness—they received deserved punishment. “Nations” means not a few ungodly but great armies risen against him; God’s goodness shines in that, for favor to one servant, he spared not whole nations. Blotting out their name forever means destruction without hope of rising, devoted to everlasting shame. God buries the name of the ungodly; the memory of the righteous shall be forever blessed.

Reformed accent: judgment is desert under the Judge, not bloodlust—and God’s favor to his servant can overturn nations.

Psalms 9:6

6The enemy came to an end in everlasting ruins; their cities you rooted out; the very memory of them has perished.

Arminian

Wesley on v. 7’s contrast prepares this: cities and people may perish. Here the enemy ends in everlasting ruins; memory itself perishes.

Arminian pastoral: ruin and forgotten memory warn against trusting walls and fame. The church’s comfort is not that enemies never rise, but that their end is real when God roots them out.

Reformed

Calvin reads the verse in several ways; his preferred contrast sets the enemy’s devastating ambition over against God seated as judge. The ungodly set no limit but utter destruction; when confusion peaked, God seasonably appeared. However high violence carries, men cannot drag God from his seat. He has prepared his throne for judgment—not for idle majesty alone but to govern the world in righteousness.

Reformed comfort: when only desolation fills the eye, lift eyes to the heavenly throne; God still sits Judge.

Psalms 9:7

7But the LORD sits enthroned forever; he has established his throne for justice,

Arminian

Wesley: But—though cities and people may perish, yet the Lord abides for ever. Which is sufficient for the terror of his enemies, and the comfort of his church.

Arminian hearing: the church’s comfort and the enemy’s terror share one ground—the LORD’s enduring enthronement. Hold that forever when cities fall.

Reformed

Calvin: God sitteth forever—however high men’s violence, they cannot drag him from his seat; he cannot abdicate the office of judge. He has prepared his throne for judgment: he reigns not only for surpassing majesty but to govern the world in righteousness.

Reformed accent: permanence of the throne is judicial, not decorative—providence still on duty when earth is chaos.

Psalms 9:8

8and he judges the world with righteousness; he judges the peoples with uprightness.

Arminian

Wesley’s outline: confidence that God would judge the world and protect his people.

Arminian reading: worldwide righteous judgment is real hope for the oppressed and real warning for the oppressor. Upright judging of peoples means moral order is not finally optional—live toward that court.

Reformed

Calvin: God’s power is not inactive pleasure in heaven; it constantly preserves his authority and governs in righteousness. The use of the doctrine: power is not shut up in heaven but manifests itself in succoring men. Do not imagine with Epicurus a God devoted to ease; place him on the throne of power and equity. Though he does not immediately succor the oppressed, there is not a moment he ceases to take deep interest. Delay exercises patience; wait the issue with tranquillity. No one can deprive God of judging the world—or his own people.

Reformed accent: righteous world-judgment grounds present trust under delayed help.

Psalms 9:9

9The LORD is a stronghold for the oppressed, a stronghold in times of trouble.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame ties the stronghold to confidence that God protects his people.

Arminian pastoral: the oppressed have a stronghold in trouble—grace invites them to take refuge, not to despair when times are hard. Seek the stronghold; do not invent a private fortress.

Reformed

Calvin: a remedy when the weak see themselves abandoned to the ungodly while God keeps silence. God delays aid and to outward appearance forsakes his faithful, in order at length to succor them seasonably according to the greatness of their necessity. He does not cease his office though he suffer the good to extreme poverty and weeping—he lights a lamp so they see his judgments more clearly. Give providence time to manifest in the season of need. Do not feel so uneasy at being accounted poor before the world; afflictions call him to our aid.

Reformed accent: stronghold-in-trouble includes seasonable delay—fatherly timing, not absence.

Psalms 9:10

10And those who know your name put their trust in you, for you, O LORD, have not forsaken those who seek you.

Arminian

Wesley: Thy name—thy infinite power and wisdom, and faithfulness and goodness. The name of God is frequently put for God. Put their trust—the experience of thy faithfulness to thy people in all ages is a just ground for their confidence.

Arminian reading: knowing the name grounds trust; seeking is real recourse the afflicted must take. Experience of faithfulness in all ages invites present confidence—seek, and do not treat forsaking as the last word.

Reformed

Calvin: when the Lord delivers the righteous, they and the rest of the righteous acquire increasing confidence in his grace. Without persuasion that God cares for human affairs, constant disquietude follows. Advantage is restricted to the faithful—where there is no godliness, no sense of God’s works. Name means more than bare deity: God’s character as he has been pleased to make it known. Ground of trust: he does not forsake those who seek him. Seeking is both prayer and upright life, inseparably joined; here especially, betaking ourselves to him for help in danger.

Reformed accent: trust grows from known character and proven non-forsaking—revelation and deliverance, not anonymous optimism.

Key contrast Both confess that those who know the LORD’s name put their trust in him because he has not forsaken those who seek him. Arminian readers, with Wesley, stress experienced faithfulness across ages as just ground for confidence—and seeking as the real recourse the afflicted must take. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress that name means God’s made-known character, that trust’s fruit accrues to the faithful who sense his works, and that seeking is betaking ourselves to him for help—confidence deepened by deliverance under his care, not self-generated assurance.

Psalms 9:11

11Sing praises to the LORD, who sits enthroned in Zion! Tell among the peoples his deeds!

Arminian

Wesley: Zion—whose special and gracious presence is there. People—to the heathen nations.

Arminian hearing: praise in Zion and telling among the peoples are public duties. Declare deeds beyond the circle of the already convinced—grace that saved you is news for the nations.

Reformed

Calvin: David, not content with individual thanks, exhorts the faithful to unite in praising God—deliverances worthy of public celebration. Proclaim among the nations: Judea is too narrow for Jehovah’s infinite praises. “Who dwells in Zion” distinguishes the covenant God from false gods. It is not enough to honor some deity at random; yield to the only living God the worship he commands. External symbols were ladders to ascend to heaven, not chains for the senses. Wherever the faithful worship according to his word, he is graciously present.

Reformed accent: public praise and right worship of the covenant God—Zion as appointed rest, not superstition’s excuse.

Psalms 9:12

12For he who avenges blood is mindful of them; he does not forget the cry of the afflicted.

Arminian

Wesley: Blood—the bloodshed of his innocent and holy ones; though he may not seem to regard for a season, yet he will certainly call the authors to a severe account. Them—the humble, or the oppressed (v. 9), that trust in him and seek to him (v. 10), whom he seemed to have forgotten.

Arminian pastoral: delay is not final forgetfulness. Cry as the afflicted; trust that inquisition for blood will come. Humility and seeking remain the path while waiting.

Reformed

Calvin: select among God’s works his delivering the poor from death. He sometimes leaves them persecuted, but at length takes vengeance. He does not always stop injuries so speedily as we wish—important, lest we measure help by senses and fall into despair. Consolation: he will show how precious our blood was. Delay is for our humbling under the cross; vengeance after outrage does not mean he was unwilling, but that the time for manifesting grace was not yet. Care after death evidences fatherly love and blessed immortality. Crying encourages all who would find him deliverer to direct wishes and prayers to him.

Reformed accent: remembered blood and unforgotten cry are providence on a cross-shaped timetable—preciousness proven, sometimes after the grave.

Key contrast Both confess that God avenges innocent blood and does not finally forget the cry of the afflicted, though he may seem to disregard for a season. Arminian readers, with Wesley, stress certain severe account for shedders of holy blood and the call for the humble oppressed to keep trusting and seeking while they wait. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress precious blood under delayed help that humbles under the cross, care that reaches even the dead, and crying that directs the afflicted to the deliverer—timing belonging to grace’s manifestation, not to divine neglect.

Psalms 9:13

13Be gracious to me, O LORD! See my affliction from those who hate me, O you who lift me up from the gates of death,

Arminian

Wesley’s outline: prayer for help begins here. Affliction from haters; the LORD lifts from death’s gates.

Arminian hearing: ask for grace and for God to see affliction. The title “you who lift me up from the gates of death” invites present petition grounded in known mercy—pray again when enemies regroup.

Reformed

Calvin: second part of the psalm—thanksgiving made way for prayer; he implores present help from the God who had often delivered. Enemies may gather new courage and rush with greater rage. Greatest fear seized him; he would not call God to witness affliction for a small matter. Humbly betaking himself to mercy, he bears the cross patiently. Mark the title: lifter up from the gates of death—strengthen faith from past experience, and assure deliverance even in the jaws of death. Gates of death: utmost perils that lay the grave open. The office is not ascribed in vain.

Reformed accent: past lifting funds present prayer under new assault—mercy and patience together.

Psalms 9:14

14that I may recount all your praises, that in the gates of the daughter of Zion I may rejoice in your salvation.

Arminian

Wesley: Gates—in the great assemblies; elegantly opposed to the gates of death. Daughter of Zion—the people who live or meet in Zion; cities as mothers, people as daughters.

Arminian reading: deliverance aims at public praise and joy in salvation. Live to recount praises in the assembly—salvation received becomes salvation declared.

Reformed

Calvin: celebrate praises wherever the greatest concourse is (assemblies at city gates), with allusion to the gates of death—delivered from the grave, bear public testimony. Praise must proceed from the heart; he desires to live for no other purpose than to rejoice in having been preserved by God’s grace. “Daughter” names people or city from its principal part, Zion.

Reformed accent: purpose of rescue is heartfelt public joy in grace-wrought salvation—not private survival alone.

Psalms 9:15

15The nations have sunk in the pit that they made; in the net that they hid, their own foot has been caught.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame returns to confidence: nations sink in their own pit and net.

Arminian warning: schemes against the righteous boomerang. Do not dig pits; the moral order God upholds catches the foot that hid the net.

Reformed

Calvin: raised to holy confidence, David triumphs—enemies taken in their own craftiness. Same truth without figure: snared in their own wickedness. Not by chance—God’s work and striking proof of his judgment. The wicked imitate their father Satan with deceits; expect nets laid for God’s children. Comfort: the issue is not in their power; God will surprise them in their devices and make resources of mischief fall on their own heads.

Reformed accent: poetic justice is judicial providence, not karma.

Psalms 9:16

16The LORD has made himself known; he has executed judgment; the wicked are snared in the work of their own hands. Higgaion. Selah

Arminian

Wesley: Higgaion—either a musical term or a note of attention, intimating that the matter deserves deep meditation or consideration, as the word signifies.

Arminian pastoral: the LORD known by executed judgment; the wicked snared by their own hands—pause and meditate. Let judgment teach; do not rush past Selah.

Reformed

Calvin: “The known Lord has done judgment”—best sense: it is obvious to all that God executes the office of judge when he ensnares the wicked in their maliciousness. When God turns schemes back on their authors, judgment is so evident it cannot be ascribed to nature or fortune. Open eyes; confirm faith by judgments on the Church’s enemies. Higgaion (meditation): David would fix the godly in meditating on God’s judgments; Selah likewise tunes singing to the weight of the words.

Reformed accent: self-snaring of the wicked is public proof of the Judge—meditate, do not mythologize chance.

Psalms 9:17

17The wicked shall return to Sheol, all the nations that forget God.

Arminian

Wesley: Forget—that do not regard God, nor his precepts, nor his threatenings and judgments.

Arminian warning: Sheol awaits the wicked and nations that forget God—forgetfulness as lived disregard of precepts and warnings. Remember God while mercy invites; forgetting is a chosen path with an end.

Reformed

Calvin: not merely optative—David confirms the godly for the future: whatever the wicked attempt ends disastrously for them. “Turned” marks issue contrary to their imagination—tacit contrast between height of presumption and depth of fall. Without fear of God they exalt above the clouds and make a covenant with death; madness carries them headlong into the grave they thought far off. “Hell” (sheol) means more than common death—otherwise nothing unique is said of the wicked. Under the grave metaphor, ungodly perish; presumption that tramples righteousness brings ruin. The faithful also descend to the grave, but not with violence that plunges without hope—even shut in the grave they dwell in heaven by hope.

Reformed accent: forgetting God ends in judicial ruin; the faithful’s death is not that plunge.

Key contrast Both confess that the wicked and God-forgetting nations face Sheol as real judgment, not a poetic shrug. Arminian readers, with Wesley, stress forgetting as disregard of God, his precepts, threatenings, and judgments—a lived neglect the nations must turn from. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress the sudden reversal of proud presumption into ruin deeper than common death, while the faithful, though they enter the grave, are not plunged without hope but already dwell in heaven by hope.

Psalms 9:18

18For the needy shall not always be forgotten, and the hope of the poor shall not perish forever.

Arminian

Wesley’s outline: confidence that the needy are not finally forgotten and the poor’s hope does not perish.

Arminian pastoral: “not always” and “not forever” teach patient hope. Keep hoping; temporary forgetfulness is not the last divine word over the poor.

Reformed

Calvin: confirmation—God will not forsake the poor forever; they may seem forsaken for a time. Assistance is promised not by preventing all affliction but by succumbing us after we have been long subdued under the cross. He speaks of hope or expectation to encourage prayer. God seems not to notice so that we awaken him by prayer; when he hears, as if then mindful, he stretches out his hand. Delay is repeated so we persevere when expectation is not instantly gratified.

Reformed accent: hope of the poor is prayer-trained endurance under the cross—succor after subduing, not immunity from need.

Psalms 9:19

19Arise, O LORD! Let not man prevail; let the nations be judged before you!

Arminian

Wesley’s closing prayer: Arise—let not man prevail; let nations be judged before God.

Arminian hearing: ask God to arise so mortal arrogance does not win. Judgment of nations before his face is a right petition when man would prevail—pray against oppression’s swagger.

Reformed

Calvin: “Arise” refers to appearance to our senses—we perceive God as deliverer when he appears as on the judgment-seat. Reason: that man may not prevail. When God arises, fierceness of the ungodly falls. Whence their insolence if not because God is still and gives loose rein? His nod breaks their strength. Enemies are in God’s hand; he can render endeavors vain. He calls them enosh—mortal, frail man. Judge the nations before his face: compel them to appear; unbelievers turn their backs until dragged into his presence.

Reformed accent: prayer against prevailing man rests on God’s irresistible arising—frailty named, backs turned until judged.

Psalms 9:20

20Put them in fear, O LORD! Let the nations know that they are but men! Selah

Arminian

Wesley’s close matches the plea that nations know they are but men.

Arminian reading: fear that humbles nations into knowing their mortality is mercy’s warning as well as judgment. Learn you are but men—drop the god-like pose before harder rods fall.

Reformed

Calvin: morah as fear/dread, not “lawgiver.” God commonly subdues even the chosen by fear, yet moderates rigor and softens hearts so they submit willingly—he does not merely compel them. With the reprobate he takes another way: inflexible obduracy is broken by force; acknowledgement of weakness is extorted though they do not truly reform. Fear comprehends plagues that hammer rebellion. Point: nations know they are mortal men. Ungodly run to excess as if nothing hindered them—ignorance of their condition. Strike terror that they experience strength unequal to presumption; lying abased is much gained even without heart-repentance. The chosen should profit otherwise: humbled, divest vain confidence. Augustine: whole humility consists in knowledge of oneself. Pride being natural, God strikes terror so his people learn humility and the wicked are put to shame.

Reformed accent: fear teaches mortality—softening the elect, exposing the reprobate’s ridiculous confidence.

Key contrast Both confess the plea that God put the nations in fear so they know they are but men. Arminian readers stress fear as a warning that can humble mortals into dropping god-like presumption—learn frailty while mercy still teaches. Reformed readers, with Calvin, distinguish God’s way with the chosen (fear that softens into willing humility and self-knowledge) from his way with the reprobate (force that extorts acknowledgement of weakness without true reform)—pride answered so the church is humbled and arrogant confidence is shamed.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on thanksgiving for deliverance, the Judge’s throne, trust in the known name, and prayer that nations learn they are but men—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 9:1–20.

Arminian Notable voices

“David praises God for giving him victory over his enemies, ver. 1–6. Triumphs in confidence that God would judge the world and protect his people, ver. 7–10. Calls others to praise him, ver. 11, 12. Prays for help, and praises God, ver. 13–20. Turned back—put to flight. But—though cities and people may perish, yet the Lord abides for ever: which is sufficient for the terror of his enemies, and the comfort of his church. Thy name—thy infinite power and wisdom, and faithfulness and goodness. Put their trust—the experience of thy faithfulness to thy people in all ages is a just ground for their confidence. Zion—whose special and gracious presence is there. Blood—the bloodshed of his innocent and holy ones; though he may not seem to regard for a season, yet he will certainly call the authors of it to a severe account. Them—the humble, or the oppressed, that trust in him and seek to him, whom he seemed to have forgotten. Gates—in the great assemblies; elegantly opposed to the gates of death. Higgaion—a musical term, or a note of attention, intimating that the matter deserves deep meditation. Forget—that do not regard God, nor his precepts, nor his threatenings and judgments.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 9)

Reformed Notable voices

“David, after recounting former victories and exalting the grace and power of God, when he sees new enemies rising, implores the protection of the same God and beseeches him to overthrow their pride. The whole ought to be considered as a prayer in which, elevating his mind to confidence, he recalls wonderful displays of power by which he had been delivered; it is a mistake to limit this thanksgiving to one victory. He praises with his whole heart—an upright heart opposed to a double heart—and will not, like worldly victors, steal glory back to his own valor. He rejoices and exults in God alone. Enemies turn back and perish at God’s presence, not by chance or human strength. God maintains his just cause as righteous judge; he blots out the wicked’s name. Though desolations rage, Jehovah sits forever and has prepared his throne for judgment; he judges the world in righteousness. He is a refuge for the poor in seasonable times of trouble—delay is not desertion. Those who know his name trust him; he forsakes not those who seek him. He requires blood and does not forget the cry of the afflicted. David prays to be lifted from the gates of death to recount praises in Zion’s gates. The wicked are snared in their own works; they shall be turned into hell, yet the poor shall not always be forgotten. Arise—let not frail man prevail; put them in fear, that the nations may know they are but men.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 9)
