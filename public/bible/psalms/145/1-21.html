Passage

Psalms 145

Verses 1–21 · WEB · Voices

A Song of Praise. Of David—an alphabetic (acrostic) hymn: he extols God his King, blessing the name forever, day by day. Great is the LORD; His greatness unsearchable. Generations commend works and mighty acts; he meditates on majesty and wondrous works; they speak of awesome deeds and abundant goodness. The LORD is gracious, merciful, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love—good to all, mercy over all He has made. Works give thanks; saints bless; they tell kingdom glory and power. His kingdom is everlasting; [He is faithful in all his words, and loving in all his deeds.He upholds the falling, raises the bowed down, opens His hand to satisfy the desire of every living thing, is righteous and kind, near to those who call in truth, grants the desire of those who fear Him, preserves those who love Him, and destroys the wicked. Mouth and all flesh bless His holy name forever.

Psalms 145:1

1I will exalt you, my God, the King. I will praise your name forever and ever.

Arminian

I will exalt you, my God, the King. I will praise your name forever. Clarke: raise Thee on high; bless the name for ever and onward.

Classical Arminians take the vow of praise as sincere duty under grace. Kingship language puts God above every rival loyalty.

Reformed

Calvin: stirs others to offer praises due; calling God his king keeps earthly princes in place so no distinction steals God’s glory.

Praise owed, princes relativized. Extol; my God and King; bless forever.

Psalms 145:2

2Every day I will praise you. I will extol your name forever and ever.

Arminian

Every day bless; praise the name forever. Daily perseverance matches forever speech.

Daily blessing is habit grace enables and requires.

Reformed

Calvin: daily denotes perseverance; constant mercies make fainting in praise improper.

New mercies each day call for praise each day. Both agree: every day I will praise you.

Psalms 145:3

3Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised! His greatness is unsearchable.

Arminian

Great is the LORD; greatly to be praised; greatness unsearchable. Clarke: to His mightinesses no investigation—unlimited and eternal.

Arminians refuse a small God. Unsearchable greatness fuels worship, not speculation that neglects praise.

Reformed

Calvin: praise aright when overwhelmed with admiration of immensity; capacity-measured praise from that fountain.

Ecstatic admiration, not bare flattery. Agreed: great; greatly praised; unsearchable.

Psalms 145:4

4One generation will commend your works to another, and will declare your mighty acts.

Arminian

Generation to generation commend works and declare mighty acts. Clarke: creating/redeeming acts in the Word; providential dealings handed down.

Teach the next generation deliberately. One generation commends God’s works to the next.

Reformed

Calvin: all are made and preserved to devote themselves to God’s praise; God renews remembrance of His works day by day.

Life’s end is praise across generations. Praise is handed down from one generation to another.

Psalms 145:5

5I will meditate on the glorious majesty of your honor, on your wondrous works.

Arminian

Meditate on majesty’s splendor and wondrous works. Praise includes pondering, not only singing.

Arminians urge meditation that leads to speech. Meditate on majesty and wonders.

Reformed

Calvin: meditation (or speech) on beauty of excellency and wonderful works—true religion wants practical knowledge of works, not idle speculation on essence alone.

His meditation fixes on God’s works. Both hear: I will meditate.

Psalms 145:6

6Men will speak of the might of your awesome acts. I will declare your greatness.

Arminian

They speak of awesome deeds’ might; I declare Your greatness. Corporate and personal praise join.

If others speak, I still declare. Both see: speak; declare greatness.

Reformed

Calvin: always some to declare righteousness, goodness, wisdom; if others desist, he will still set forth God’s praises.

Refuse silent defrauding of God’s honor. Men will speak of God’s awesome deeds, and the psalmist will declare his greatness.

Psalms 145:7

7They will utter the memory of your great goodness, and will sing of your righteousness.

Arminian

Pour forth fame of abundant goodness; sing aloud of righteousness. Overflowing praise matches overflowing goodness.

Classical Arminians love loud goodness-songs. Undisputed: abundant goodness; righteousness sung.

Reformed

Calvin: gushing utterance like water from a fountain; memory of goodness we have experienced allures cheerful praise.

Experienced fatherly mercy fuels burst of praise. No dispute here: pour forth; sing aloud.

Psalms 145:8

8The LORD is gracious, merciful, slow to anger, and of great loving kindness.

Arminian

Gracious, merciful, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love. Clarke: nature disposed to favor; eye affects heart at misery; slow even under great provocation; abundant mercy.

This fourfold goodness is the Divine nature on display—worthy of trust and praise.

Reformed

Calvin opens the goodness already named: inclined to mercy, compassionate, slow to anger, great in mercy.

God’s own character, not a mood. Both affirm: gracious; merciful; slow to anger; steadfast love.

Psalms 145:9

9The LORD is good to all. His tender mercies are over all his works.

Arminian

Good to all; mercy over all He has made. Clarke stresses universal merciful regards over all His works.

The passage carries wide benevolence that underwrites sincere gospel offers and creaturely care—without turning the verse into a denial of judgment later in the psalm.

Reformed

Calvin: Jehovah is good to all. His mercies above all His works—goodness celebrated in providence toward the world He made, then special favor to His people in the psalm’s movement.

Creaturely mercy is true; it does not erase particular covenant care or the wicked’s end in v. 20. Where they meet: good to all; mercy over all He made.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD is good to all and that His tender mercies are over all He has made. Arminians, with Clarke, typically emphasize wide, sincere benevolence toward His intelligent offspring and all His works. Calvin reads the line within providence’s praise—creaturely mercies that still sit beside special care for the saints and the psalm’s later distinction regarding the wicked.

Psalms 145:10

10All your works will give thanks to you, LORD. Your saints will extol you.

Arminian

All works give thanks; all saints bless. Clarke: works praise the God good to all; saints (merciful ones) bless.

Creation and redeemed people share the choir in different keys. Works thank; saints bless.

Reformed

Calvin: all works praise; merciful ones bless—God’s people voice what creation displays.

Saint-blessing answers revealed mercy. Give thanks; bless You.

Psalms 145:11

11They will speak of the glory of your kingdom, and talk about your power,

Arminian

Speak kingdom glory; tell power. Royal speech continues the praise.

Classical Arminians love kingdom proclamation. Glory of Your kingdom; Your power.

Reformed

Calvin: they speak glory of the kingdom and talk of power—so dominion is confessed, not privatized.

The kingdom is to be spoken of publicly. Both traditions hear: speak; tell.

Psalms 145:12

12to make known to the sons of men his mighty acts, the glory of the majesty of his kingdom.

Arminian

Make known to humankind mighty deeds and kingdom splendor. Clarke: they delight to recommend their God and Father to others.

Praise turns outward to the sons of men. Make known mighty deeds.

Reformed

Calvin: make known His powers to the sons of men and the beauty of His kingdom—praise as witness.

Revelation for the world to hear. Common reading: children of man; glorious splendor.

Psalms 145:13

13Your kingdom is an everlasting kingdom. Your dominion endures throughout all generations. The LORD is faithful in all his words, and loving in all his deeds.

Arminian

The WEB includes the nun line: faithful in all his words, loving in all his deeds. Clarke notes the acrostic nun verse preserved in ancient Versions.

Classical Arminians rest on a kingdom that does not age out—and on faithfulness and kindness in speech and works.

Reformed

Calvin: kingdom of all ages; dominion to all successive generations—God’s rule outlasts human renown.

Undying dominion. Shared praise of the forever King in this acrostic song.

Psalms 145:14

14The LORD upholds all who fall, and raises up all those who are bowed down.

Arminian

Upholds the falling; raises the bowed down. Wesley: all that look up to Him for help. Clarke: those not able to keep their feet.

Look up—falling saints are upheld. Upholds; raises.

Reformed

Calvin’s praise of providence includes care for the distressed. Falling and bowed down meet holding and raising.

The King’s hand under the weak. Either reading: all who fall; all bowed down.

Psalms 145:15

15The eyes of all wait for you. You give them their food in due season.

Arminian

Eyes of all look; You give food in due season. Wesley: all living creatures; wait—expect supplies wholly from Thy bounty (even ascribed figuratively to brutes).

Arminian readers thank common provision. Both sides agree: eyes look; food in due season.

Reformed

Calvin: goodness reaches creatures who wait on God for food—providence praised before special nearness is named.

God gives every creature its food in due season. Look to You; You give.

Psalms 145:16

16You open your hand, and satisfy the desire of every living thing.

Arminian

Open hand; satisfy every living thing’s desire. Generosity pictured as an open palm.

Open-handed satisfaction of living desire. Open hand; satisfy.

Reformed

Calvin’s goodness theme continues—open hand as emblem of liberal providence.

Desire met by God’s hand. You open; You satisfy.

Psalms 145:17

17The LORD is righteous in all his ways, and gracious in all his works.

Arminian

Righteous in all ways; kind in all works. Character matches kingdom rule.

Both traditions see: no cruel randomness in His ways. Classical Arminians rest in kind righteousness.

Reformed

Calvin pairs righteousness and kindness across works—providence is not raw power without goodness.

All ways; all works. Righteous; kind.

Psalms 145:18

18The LORD is near to all those who call on him, to all who call on him in truth.

Arminian

Near to all those who call on him in truth. Wesley: nigh to answer prayers; in truth—with an upright heart. Clarke: near to answer; upright heart.

Sincere calling meets nearness—truthful prayer is invited and heard.

Reformed

Calvin’s movement from general goodness to special favor lands in nearness to callers. Truth marks approach.

Nearness to those who truly call—not a blank cheque to empty lips. Both note: near; call in truth.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD is near to all those who call on him in truth. Arminians, with Wesley and Clarke, typically stress sincere, upright calling that God answers. Reformed readers press that nearness is covenant reality for true callers—special favor within the psalm’s praise—not nearness redefined as indifference to whether the call is in truth.

Psalms 145:19

19He will fulfill the desire of those who fear him. He also will hear their cry, and will save them.

Arminian

Fulfills desire of those who fear Him; hears cry; saves. Fear and cry belong together.

Arminians urge reverent desire and crying out. Fear Him; He hears; He saves.

Reformed

Calvin: merciful ones who fear God find desire fulfilled and salvation in answer to cry.

Fear is not frozen silence—it cries and is saved. Fulfills; hears; saves.

Psalms 145:20

20The LORD preserves all those who love him, but he will destroy all the wicked.

Arminian

Preserves all those who love him; destroys all the wicked. Love and wickedness diverge in outcome.

Love Him—preservation answers attachment; wickedness meets destruction. Both sides of the verse stand.

Reformed

Calvin’s celebration of goodness never cancels judgment on the wicked. Preserve lovers; destroy the wicked.

Two ends under one Lord. Preserves who love; destroys the wicked.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD preserves those who love Him and destroys the wicked. Arminians underline love as the living attachment grace enables and requires. Calvinists typically underline God’s preserving of His own and certain destruction of the wicked as the King’s holy dominion—not a sentimental goodness that never judges.

Psalms 145:21

21My mouth will speak the praise of the LORD. Let all flesh bless his holy name forever and ever.

Arminian

My mouth will speak praise; let all flesh bless His holy name forever. Clarke’s psalm ends in universal summons.

Classical Arminians join personal vow to cosmic invitation.

Reformed

Calvin began by urging all to praise; the psalm ends with mouth and all flesh blessing the holy name forever.

Praise is owed to God without end. Speak praise; all flesh bless forever.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic Arminian and Reformed comment on Psalm 145:1–21, in the writers’ own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“This is the last of the acrostic Psalms; it is an incomparable Psalm of praise. I will extol thee—I will raise thee on high. I will bless thy name—for ever and onward, in this and the coming world. His greatness is unsearchable—To his mightinesses there is no investigation; all in God is unlimited and eternal. One generation—Thy creating and redeeming acts are recorded in thy word; but thy wondrous providential dealings with mankind must be handed down from generation to generation. The Lord is gracious—His holy nature is ever disposed to show favor. Full of compassion—Wherever he sees misery, his eye affects his heart. Slow to anger—When there is even the greatest provocation. Of great mercy—Great in his abundant mercy. The Lord is good to all—and his tender mercies are over all his works. All thy works shall praise thee; and thy saints shall bless thee. The Lord upholdeth all that fall—those not able to keep their feet; and raiseth up all who are bowed down. The Lord is nigh unto all that call upon him in truth—to answer their prayers, with an upright heart.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 145)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Praise of David—throughout a continued celebration of the perfections and doings of God. I will extol thee, my God and my king: David does not so much tell what he would do himself, as stir up and urge all others to this religious service of offering to God the praises due to his name. He insists upon persevering in the exercise; as God is constant in extending mercies, it would be highly improper in us to faint in his praises. In calling God his king he gives both himself and other earthly princes their proper place, and does not allow any earthly distinctions to interfere with the glory due to God. In speaking of the greatness of God as unmeasurable, he intimates that we only praise God aright when we are filled and overwhelmed with an ecstatic admiration of the immensity of his power. Generation to generation will praise thy works: all men were made and are preserved in life for this end, that they may devote themselves to the praise of God. Jehovah is gracious and compassionate, slow to anger, and great in mercy; Jehovah is good to all, and his mercies above all his works. All thy works shall praise thee, and thy merciful ones shall bless thee. Thy kingdom is a kingdom of all ages, and thy dominion unto all successive generations.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 145)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English editions of the sources named.
