Passage

Psalms 145

Verses 1–21 · ESV · Voices

A Song of Praise. Of David—an alphabetic (acrostic) hymn: he extols God his King, blessing the name forever, day by day. Great is the LORD; His greatness unsearchable. Generations commend works and mighty acts; he meditates on majesty and wondrous works; they speak of awesome deeds and abundant goodness. The LORD is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love—good to all, mercy over all He has made. Works give thanks; saints bless; they tell kingdom glory and power. His kingdom is everlasting; [He is faithful in all His words and kind in all His works.] He upholds the falling, raises the bowed down, opens His hand to satisfy living desire, is righteous and kind, near to those who call in truth, fulfills the desire of those who fear Him, preserves those who love Him, and destroys the wicked. Mouth and all flesh bless His holy name forever.

Psalms 145:1

1I will extol you, my God and King, and bless your name forever and ever.

Arminian

I will extol You, my God and King, and bless Your name forever. Clarke: raise Thee on high; bless the name for ever and onward.

Classical Arminians take the vow of praise as sincere duty under grace. Kingship language puts God above every rival loyalty.

Reformed

Calvin: stirs others to offer praises due; calling God his king keeps earthly princes in place so no distinction steals God’s glory.

Reformed accent: praise owed, princes relativized. Shared: extol; my God and King; bless forever.

Psalms 145:2

2Every day I will bless you and praise your name forever and ever.

Arminian

Every day bless; praise the name forever. Daily perseverance matches forever speech.

Arminian practice: daily blessing is habit grace enables and requires. Shared: every day; forever and ever.

Reformed

Calvin: daily denotes perseverance; constant mercies make fainting in praise improper.

Reformed note: new mercies, ongoing praise. Shared: every day I will bless You.

Psalms 145:3

3Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised, and his greatness is unsearchable.

Arminian

Great is the LORD; greatly to be praised; greatness unsearchable. Clarke: to His mightinesses no investigation—unlimited and eternal.

Classical Arminians refuse a small God. Unsearchable greatness fuels worship, not speculation that neglects praise.

Reformed

Calvin: praise aright when overwhelmed with admiration of immensity; capacity-measured praise from that fountain.

Reformed accent: ecstatic admiration, not bare flattery. Shared: great; greatly praised; unsearchable.

Psalms 145:4

4One generation shall commend your works to another, and shall declare your mighty acts.

Arminian

Generation to generation commend works and declare mighty acts. Clarke: creating/redeeming acts in the Word; providential dealings handed down.

Arminian pastoral: teach the next generation deliberately. Shared: generational commendation of works.

Reformed

Calvin: all are made and preserved to devote themselves to God’s praise; God renews remembrance of His works day by day.

Reformed accent: life’s end is praise across generations. Shared: one generation to another.

Psalms 145:5

5On the glorious splendor of your majesty, and on your wondrous works, I will meditate.

Arminian

Meditate on majesty’s splendor and wondrous works. Praise includes pondering, not only singing.

Classical Arminians urge meditation that leads to speech. Shared: meditate on majesty and wonders.

Reformed

Calvin: meditation (or speech) on beauty of excellency and wonderful works—true religion wants practical knowledge of works, not idle speculation on essence alone.

Reformed note: works-focused meditation. Shared: I will meditate.

Psalms 145:6

6They shall speak of the might of your awesome deeds, and I will declare your greatness.

Arminian

They speak of awesome deeds’ might; I declare Your greatness. Corporate and personal praise join.

Arminian hearing: if others speak, I still declare. Shared: speak; declare greatness.

Reformed

Calvin: always some to declare righteousness, goodness, wisdom; if others desist, he will still set forth God’s praises.

Reformed accent: refuse silent defrauding of God’s honor. Shared: awesome deeds; declare greatness.

Psalms 145:7

7They shall pour forth the fame of your abundant goodness and shall sing aloud of your righteousness.

Arminian

Pour forth fame of abundant goodness; sing aloud of righteousness. Overflowing praise matches overflowing goodness.

Classical Arminians love loud goodness-songs. Shared: abundant goodness; righteousness sung.

Reformed

Calvin: gushing utterance like water from a fountain; memory of goodness we have experienced allures cheerful praise.

Reformed comfort: experienced fatherly mercy fuels burst of praise. Shared: pour forth; sing aloud.

Psalms 145:8

8The LORD is gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love.

Arminian

Gracious, merciful, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love. Clarke: nature disposed to favor; eye affects heart at misery; slow even under great provocation; abundant mercy.

Arminian confession: this fourfold goodness is the Divine nature on display—worthy of trust and praise.

Reformed

Calvin opens the goodness already named: inclined to mercy, compassionate, slow to anger, great in mercy.

Reformed accent: God’s own character, not a mood. Shared: gracious; merciful; slow to anger; steadfast love.

Psalms 145:9

9The LORD is good to all, and his mercy is over all that he has made.

Arminian

Good to all; mercy over all He has made. Clarke stresses universal merciful regards over all His works.

Classical Arminians hear wide benevolence that underwrites sincere gospel offers and creaturely care—without turning the verse into a denial of judgment later in the psalm.

Reformed

Calvin: Jehovah is good to all, and His mercies above all His works—goodness celebrated in providence toward the world He made, then special favor to His people in the psalm’s movement.

Reformed accent: creaturely mercy is real; it does not erase particular covenant care or the wicked’s end in v. 20. Shared: good to all; mercy over all He made.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD is good to all and that His mercy is over all He has made. Arminians, with Clarke, typically emphasize wide, sincere benevolence toward His intelligent offspring and all His works. Reformed readers, with Calvin, typically read the line within providence’s praise—real creaturely mercies that still sit beside special care for the saints and the psalm’s later distinction regarding the wicked.

Psalms 145:10

10All your works shall give thanks to you, O LORD, and all your saints shall bless you!

Arminian

All works give thanks; all saints bless. Clarke: works praise the God good to all; saints (merciful ones) bless.

Arminian hearing: creation and redeemed people share the choir in different keys. Shared: works thank; saints bless.

Reformed

Calvin: all works praise; merciful ones bless—God’s people voice what creation displays.

Reformed note: saint-blessing answers revealed mercy. Shared: give thanks; bless You.

Psalms 145:11

11They shall speak of the glory of your kingdom and tell of your power,

Arminian

Speak kingdom glory; tell power. Royal speech continues the praise.

Classical Arminians love kingdom proclamation. Shared: glory of Your kingdom; Your power.

Reformed

Calvin: they speak glory of the kingdom and talk of power—so dominion is confessed, not privatized.

Reformed accent: public kingdom speech. Shared: speak; tell.

Psalms 145:12

12to make known to the children of man your mighty deeds, and the glorious splendor of your kingdom.

Arminian

Make known to humankind mighty deeds and kingdom splendor. Clarke: they delight to recommend their God and Father to others.

Arminian mission note: praise turns outward to the children of man. Shared: make known mighty deeds.

Reformed

Calvin: make known His powers to the sons of men and the beauty of His kingdom—praise as witness.

Reformed accent: revelation for the world to hear. Shared: children of man; glorious splendor.

Psalms 145:13

13Your kingdom is an everlasting kingdom, and your dominion endures throughout all generations. [The LORD is faithful in all his words and kind in all his works.]

Arminian

Everlasting kingdom; dominion through all generations. ESV includes the bracketed nun line: faithful in all words, kind in all works. Clarke notes the acrostic nun verse preserved in ancient Versions.

Classical Arminians rest on a kingdom that does not age out—and on faithfulness and kindness in speech and works. Shared: everlasting kingdom; faithful; kind.

Reformed

Calvin: kingdom of all ages; dominion to all successive generations—God’s rule outlasts human renown.

Reformed comfort: undying dominion. Shared praise of the forever King in this acrostic song.

Psalms 145:14

14The LORD upholds all who are falling and raises up all who are bowed down.

Arminian

Upholds the falling; raises the bowed down. Wesley: all that look up to Him for help. Clarke: those not able to keep their feet.

Arminian pastoral: look up—falling saints are upheld. Shared: upholds; raises.

Reformed

Calvin’s praise of providence includes care for the distressed. Falling and bowed down meet holding and raising.

Reformed accent: the King’s hand under the weak. Shared: all who fall; all bowed down.

Psalms 145:15

15The eyes of all look to you, and you give them their food in due season.

Arminian

Eyes of all look; You give food in due season. Wesley: all living creatures; wait—expect supplies wholly from Thy bounty (even ascribed figuratively to brutes).

Classical Arminians thank common provision. Shared: eyes look; food in due season.

Reformed

Calvin: goodness reaches creatures who wait on God for food—providence praised before special nearness is named.

Reformed note: due-season giving. Shared: look to You; You give.

Psalms 145:16

16You open your hand; you satisfy the desire of every living thing.

Arminian

Open hand; satisfy every living thing’s desire. Generosity pictured as an open palm.

Arminian wonder: open-handed satisfaction of living desire. Shared: open hand; satisfy.

Reformed

Calvin’s goodness theme continues—open hand as emblem of liberal providence.

Reformed comfort: desire met by God’s hand. Shared: You open; You satisfy.

Psalms 145:17

17The LORD is righteous in all his ways and kind in all his works.

Arminian

Righteous in all ways; kind in all works. Character matches kingdom rule.

Both camps: no cruel randomness in His ways. Classical Arminians rest in kind righteousness.

Reformed

Calvin pairs righteousness and kindness across works—providence is not raw power without goodness.

Reformed accent: all ways; all works. Shared: righteous; kind.

Psalms 145:18

18The LORD is near to all who call on him, to all who call on him in truth.

Arminian

Near to all who call—on Him in truth. Wesley: nigh to answer prayers; in truth—with an upright heart. Clarke: near to answer; upright heart.

Arminian emphasis: sincere calling meets real nearness—truthful prayer is invited and heard.

Reformed

Calvin’s movement from general goodness to special favor lands in nearness to callers. Truth marks genuine approach.

Reformed accent: nearness to those who truly call—not a blank cheque to empty lips. Shared: near; call in truth.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD is near to all who call on Him in truth. Arminians, with Wesley and Clarke, typically stress sincere, upright calling that God answers. Reformed readers typically stress that nearness is covenant reality for true callers—special favor within the psalm’s praise—not nearness redefined as indifference to whether the call is in truth.

Psalms 145:19

19He fulfills the desire of those who fear him; he also hears their cry and saves them.

Arminian

Fulfills desire of those who fear Him; hears cry; saves. Fear and cry belong together.

Classical Arminians urge reverent desire and crying out. Shared: fear Him; He hears; He saves.

Reformed

Calvin: merciful ones who fear God find desire fulfilled and salvation in answer to cry.

Reformed comfort: fear is not frozen silence—it cries and is saved. Shared: fulfills; hears; saves.

Psalms 145:20

20The LORD preserves all who love him, but all the wicked he will destroy.

Arminian

Preserves all who love Him; destroys all the wicked. Love and wickedness diverge in outcome.

Arminian hearing: love Him—preservation answers real attachment; wickedness meets destruction. Both sides of the verse stand.

Reformed

Calvin’s celebration of goodness never cancels judgment on the wicked. Preserve lovers; destroy the wicked.

Reformed accent: two ends under one Lord. Shared: preserves who love; destroys the wicked.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD preserves those who love Him and destroys the wicked. Arminians typically stress love as the living attachment grace enables and requires. Reformed readers typically stress God’s preserving of His own and certain destruction of the wicked as the King’s holy dominion—not a sentimental goodness that never judges.

Psalms 145:21

21My mouth will speak the praise of the LORD, and let all flesh bless his holy name forever and ever.

Arminian

My mouth will speak praise; let all flesh bless His holy name forever. Clarke’s psalm ends in universal summons.

Classical Arminians join personal vow to cosmic invitation. Shared praise: mouth and all flesh forever.

Reformed

Calvin began by urging all to praise; the psalm ends with mouth and all flesh blessing the holy name forever.

Reformed climax: owed praise without end. Shared: speak praise; all flesh bless forever.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on acrostic praise of the King, unsearchable greatness, goodness and mercy over all His works, an everlasting kingdom, and nearness to those who call in truth—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 145:1–21.

Arminian Notable voices

“This is the last of the acrostic Psalms; it is an incomparable Psalm of praise. I will extol thee—I will raise thee on high. I will bless thy name—for ever and onward, in this and the coming world. His greatness is unsearchable—To his mightinesses there is no investigation; all in God is unlimited and eternal. One generation—Thy creating and redeeming acts are recorded in thy word; but thy wondrous providential dealings with mankind must be handed down from generation to generation. The Lord is gracious—His holy nature is ever disposed to show favor. Full of compassion—Wherever he sees misery, his eye affects his heart. Slow to anger—When there is even the greatest provocation. Of great mercy—Great in his abundant mercy. The Lord is good to all—and his tender mercies are over all his works. All thy works shall praise thee; and thy saints shall bless thee. The Lord upholdeth all that fall—those not able to keep their feet; and raiseth up all who are bowed down. The Lord is nigh unto all that call upon him in truth—to answer their prayers, with an upright heart.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 145)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Praise of David—throughout a continued celebration of the perfections and doings of God. I will extol thee, my God and my king: David does not so much tell what he would do himself, as stir up and urge all others to this religious service of offering to God the praises due to his name. He insists upon persevering in the exercise; as God is constant in extending mercies, it would be highly improper in us to faint in his praises. In calling God his king he gives both himself and other earthly princes their proper place, and does not allow any earthly distinctions to interfere with the glory due to God. In speaking of the greatness of God as unmeasurable, he intimates that we only praise God aright when we are filled and overwhelmed with an ecstatic admiration of the immensity of his power. Generation to generation will praise thy works: all men were made and are preserved in life for this end, that they may devote themselves to the praise of God. Jehovah is gracious and compassionate, slow to anger, and great in mercy; Jehovah is good to all, and his mercies above all his works. All thy works shall praise thee, and thy merciful ones shall bless thee. Thy kingdom is a kingdom of all ages, and thy dominion unto all successive generations.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 145)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English editions of the sources named.
