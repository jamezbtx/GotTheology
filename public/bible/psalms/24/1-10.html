Passage

Psalms 24

Verses 1–10 · ESV · Voices

A Psalm of David. The earth is the LORD’s and the fullness thereof—founded upon the seas—then the question: who shall ascend the hill of the LORD and stand in his holy place? Clean hands and a pure heart, no false soul-lifting and no deceitful oath, receive blessing and righteousness from the God of salvation; such is the generation that seeks his face. The gates must lift their heads for the King of glory: the LORD strong and mighty in battle, the LORD of hosts.

Psalms 24:1

1The earth is the LORD’s and the fullness thereof, the world and those who dwell therein,

Arminian

Wesley’s opening frame: God’s dominion over all mankind is asserted before the ascent question. The earth and its fullness, the world and its dwellers, belong to the LORD—not to idols, empires, or private claim.

Arminian hearing: shared confession—creation ownership is total. Every dweller already stands under the Creator’s right. Grace does not invent a smaller god; the same LORD who owns the earth will ask who may ascend his hill.

Reformed

Calvin: the earth is Jehovah’s so that even by the law of nature all are bound to serve him. Fullness includes the riches of the earth and, principally, men themselves—the earth’s chief ornament. Paul cites this for meats (1 Cor 10:26): the earth is the Lord’s and the fullness thereof.

Reformed accent: shared—universal ownership and fatherly care. Special favor to Abraham’s seed shines brighter against this backdrop: nothing in the Jews entitled nearer approach than the Gentiles; election is free grace, not ethnic entitlement.

Psalms 24:2

2for he has founded it upon the seas and established it upon the rivers.

Arminian

Wesley: Seas—the whole collection of waters, as well as the sea and the rivers running into it, as that great abyss of waters which is contained in the bowels of the earth.

Arminian pastoral: shared—foundation language grounds the LORD’s claim. The earth is habitable because God established it; dominion follows creation. Praise the Founder before you ask for access to his hill.

Reformed

Calvin: men are rightfully under God’s authority in all places; providence is reflected in the face of the earth. That dry land appears above the waters is God’s purposeful preparation of a habitation for men—fatherly care before we existed.

Reformed accent: shared—creation order underwrites the King’s right. From that universal care, the prerogative of Israel proceeded only from free and sovereign choice.

Psalms 24:3

3Who shall ascend the hill of the LORD? And who shall stand in his holy place?

Arminian

Wesley: The hill—Zion or Moriah, the place of God’s sanctuary and special presence. Having asserted God’s dominion over all mankind, he now proposes an important question, by whom God will be served, and his blessing enjoyed? Stand—to minister before him; standing is the posture of ministers or servants. Who shall serve God, with God’s acceptation. Holy place—the place he hath sanctified for his service.

Arminian hearing: the question is pastoral and live—access is not a slogan. Grace opens the way; the hearer must still become the kind of servant the next verse names. Ascent and standing name accepted ministry, not bare curiosity about the sanctuary.

Reformed

Calvin: it was of pure grace that God erected his sanctuary; David distinguishes true Israelites from the false and bastards. All whom God receives into his flock he calls to holiness by his adoption. Hypocrites take the holy name with outside distinctions; the ungodly may resort to the tabernacle, yet David treats of those who may lawfully enter. External profession without truth in the inward man is vain.

Reformed accent: free grace built the hill’s house; the question unmasks counterfeit membership. Sanctuary nearness belongs to the sincere whom adoption obliges to holiness—not to title-bearers who pollute the courts.

Key contrast Both confess that ascending the LORD’s hill and standing in his holy place are not for empty profession, and both refuse a works-ladder that buys God or an antinomian shrug that ignores holiness. Arminian readers, with Wesley, hear a pastoral question—by whom will God be served and his blessing enjoyed?—so grace-enabled integrity must actually be walked for accepted ministry. Reformed readers, with Calvin, hear pure-grace election that still distinguishes true Israelites from hypocrites: God calls his flock to holiness by adoption, and lawful entrance is not the same as crowding the courts.

Psalms 24:4

4He who has clean hands and a pure heart, who does not lift up his soul to what is false and does not swear deceitfully.

Arminian

Wesley: He—whose actions and conversation are holy and unblameable. Pure heart—careful to approve itself to God, as well as to men; ordering a man’s very thoughts and affections according to God’s word. Vanity—who doth not value or desire the vain things of this life, such as honours, riches, pleasures; but makes God his portion.

Arminian pastoral: shared—hands and heart together; refuse false soul-lifting and deceitful oaths. Grace cleanses and enables; the hearer must still keep conversation holy and affections ordered by the word. God as portion displaces vanity’s claim.

Reformed

Calvin: under purity of hands and heart, and reverence of God’s name, he comprehends all religion and a well ordered life. True purity seats in the heart and shows fruit in the hands—boasting a sound heart without fruit is ridiculous; framing outward members without heart purity will not suffice. Fidelity in oaths stands for integrity in all of life; second-table uprightness marks true fear of God.

Reformed accent: shared—clean hands with pure heart, no falsehood, no deceitful swearing. These are fruits discovering genuine piety, not a substitute for faith before God.

Psalms 24:5

5He will receive blessing from the LORD and righteousness from the God of his salvation.

Arminian

Wesley: The blessing—grace and glory, and all other good things.

Arminian reading: the clean-handed, pure-hearted seeker truly receives—grace and glory from the God of his salvation. Shared confession that blessing is gift, not wages that put God in debt. Salvation’s God gives righteousness with blessing; the hearer who walks v. 4 may expect this gift without theatrical doubt.

Reformed

Calvin: nothing is more desirable than to be numbered among God’s flock. Blessing is not for those who boast the name only, but for those who answer their calling with the whole heart without hypocrisy. God has in reserve a blessing that cannot fail them; it is his peculiar office to give evidence of his righteousness by doing his true worshippers good—and hypocrites who profane sacred worship should not expect the reward of righteousness.

Reformed accent: shared—blessing and righteousness come from the God of salvation. The gift confirms God’s faithfulness to genuine servants; name-only badges do not obligate him.

Key contrast Both confess that blessing and righteousness come from the God of salvation to the clean-handed and pure-hearted, not to hollow profession. Arminian readers, with Wesley, hear grace and glory and all good things as the gift the seeker who walks this way truly receives. Reformed readers, with Calvin, hear God’s unfailing blessing reserved for those who answer their calling without hypocrisy—his righteousness shown in keeping promise to true worshippers, while counterfeit badges secure nothing.

Psalms 24:6

6Such is the generation of those who seek him, who seek the face of the God of Jacob. Selah

Arminian

Wesley: The generation—the true progeny which God regards. Face—his grace and favour, which is often called God’s face.

Arminian pastoral: shared—the true generation is defined by seeking, not by bare ancestry or ceremony. Seek the face—pursue the LORD’s favor in earnest. Selah invites pause: holiness and seeking belong together before the gates open for the King.

Reformed

Calvin: the demonstrative this erases counterfeit Israelites who trust circumcision and sacrifices yet will not offer themselves to God. All sprung from Abraham according to the flesh are not thereby legitimate children; God is not sought in truth unless holiness and righteousness go before. “Behold an Israelite indeed, in whom is no guile!” (John 1:47).

Reformed accent: shared—true progeny seeks the face. Ceremonial nearness without zealous cultivation of holiness is false seeking that God’s judgment will expose.

Psalms 24:7

7Lift up your heads, O gates! And be lifted up, O ancient doors, that the King of glory may come in.

Arminian

Wesley: Lift up—he speaks of the gates and doors of the temple, which by faith and the spirit of prophecy he beheld as already built, whose doors he calls everlasting in opposition to those of the tabernacle. As the temple was a type of Christ, and of his church, and of heaven itself, this may represent Christ’s entrance into his church, or into the hearts of his faithful people, who are commanded to set open their hearts for his reception; or of his ascension into heaven, where saints or angels prepare the way for their Lord returning with triumph and glory. The king—the Messiah, called the King of glory (1 Cor 2:8; Jas 2:1).

Shared King-of-glory / ascent christology: open the gates for the Messiah’s entrance—church, heart, and heavenly triumph. Classical Arminians and Reformed alike confess the same Lord of glory.

Reformed

Calvin: David speaks of the future temple to encourage willing service; God condescended to dwell by a visible symbol. The preaching of the word, the sacraments, and the assemblies lift us to God. Everlasting gates stand by God’s promise; by Christ’s coming the visible shadow vanishes as the temple occupies the whole world.

Shared christology: the King of glory is not a lesser guest. Reformed reading joins Wesley’s ascent and entrance—God near his people in pledged presence, fulfilled when the Son dwells among us.

Psalms 24:8

8Who is this King of glory? The LORD, strong and mighty, the LORD, mighty in battle!

Arminian

Wesley: The LORD—he is no ordinary person, no other than Jehovah, who hath given so many proofs of his almightiness, who hath subdued all his enemies, and is now returned in triumph.

Shared confession: the King of glory is Jehovah—strong, mighty, battle-proven. Wesley’s Messiah-king and Calvin’s warrior LORD are the same triumphant God. Ascent christology agrees: the returning victor is no created rival.

Reformed

Calvin: the interrogation and repetition magnify that God does not sit idle in his temple but takes abode to succor his people. He comes armed with invincible power to maintain and save; present by power and grace, not confined as if essence were shut in stone.

Shared accent: the answer is the LORD mighty in battle. Both camps refuse a thin glory—this is Jehovah’s triumph for his people’s defense.

Psalms 24:9

9Lift up your heads, O gates! And lift them up, O ancient doors, that the King of glory may come in.

Arminian

Wesley: Lift up—the same verse is repeated again, to awaken the dulness of mankind, who are so hardly brought to a serious preparation for such solemnities; and to signify the great importance of the matter.

Arminian pastoral: shared—repetition is mercy for dull hearers. Open again; prepare again. The King of glory’s entrance is too weighty for a single shrug. Hearts and assemblies must stay lifted for his reception.

Reformed

Calvin: the repetition teaches that true believers cannot be too constant and diligent in meditation on this subject. God truly dwelt among the people who used temple and service to elevate hearts to heaven; the ark was no vain symbol, for he stretches forth his arm to defend his people.

Reformed accent: shared—say it again until faith wakes. Constancy in welcoming the King is not empty liturgy; it is diligence before promised presence.

Psalms 24:10

10Who is this King of glory? The LORD of hosts, he is the King of glory! Selah

Arminian

Wesley: Of hosts—under whose command are all the hosts of heaven and earth, angels and men, and all other creatures.

Shared climax: the LORD of hosts is the King of glory. Wesley’s Messiah-king and the hosts’ Commander meet in one confession. Selah—pause under that glory. Ascent christology is common ground: Christ the Lord of glory.

Reformed

Calvin: the Son of God, clothed with our flesh, has now shown himself to be King of glory and Lord of hosts, and he is not entered into his temple only by shadows and figures, but really and in very deed, that he may dwell in the midst of us. Nothing hinders boasting that we shall be invincible by his power. Word and sacraments unite us to God—use them with reverence, lest pride drive him away.

Shared christology: both camps name the same King of glory—Jehovah of hosts fulfilled in the incarnate Son who dwells among his people.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the LORD’s claim on the earth, who may ascend his hill with clean hands and pure heart, the blessing of those who seek his face, and the King of glory at the everlasting doors—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 24:1–10.

Arminian Notable voices

“Seas—the whole collection of waters, as well as the sea and the rivers running into it, as that great abyss of waters which is contained in the bowels of the earth. The hill—Zion or Moriah, the place of God’s sanctuary and special presence. Having asserted God’s dominion over all mankind, he now proposes an important question, by whom God will be served, and his blessing enjoyed? Stand—to minister before him; who shall serve God, with God’s acceptation. Clean hands—whose actions and conversation are holy and unblameable. Pure heart—careful to approve itself to God, as well as to men; ordering a man’s very thoughts and affections according to God’s word. Vanity—who doth not value or desire the vain things of this life, such as honours, riches, pleasures; but makes God his portion. The blessing—grace and glory, and all other good things. The generation—the true progeny which God regards. Face—his grace and favour. Lift up—he speaks of the gates and doors of the temple, which by faith and the spirit of prophecy he beheld as already built, whose doors he calls everlasting in opposition to those of the tabernacle. As the temple was a type of Christ, and of his church, and of heaven itself, this place may also contain a representation either of Christ’s entrance into his church, or into the hearts of his faithful people, who are commanded to set open their hearts for his reception; or of his ascension into heaven, where the saints or angels are poetically introduced as preparing the way and opening the heavenly gates to receive their Lord and king returning with triumph and glory. The king—the Messiah, the king of Israel, and of his church, called the King or Lord of glory (1 Cor 2:8; Jas 2:1), both for that glory which is inherent in him, and that which is purchased by him for his members. The LORD—no ordinary person, no other than Jehovah, who hath subdued all his enemies and is now returned in triumph. Of hosts—under whose command are all the hosts of heaven and earth, angels and men, and all other creatures.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 24)

Reformed Notable voices

“As God stands related to all mankind as Creator and Governor, David magnifies the special favor shown to Abraham’s children in choosing them and erecting his sanctuary among them. Although the sanctuary was open to all the Jews, God was not near to all of them, but only to those who feared and served him in sincerity and cleansed themselves from the world’s pollutions for holiness and righteousness. The earth is Jehovah’s: the Jews had nothing of themselves entitling them to approach nearer than the Gentiles; yet, preferred above the nations, they must bear marks peculiar to themselves and cultivate holiness. Who shall ascend? It was of pure grace that God erected his sanctuary; David distinguishes true Israelites from the false and bastards. All whom God receives into his flock he calls to holiness by his adoption; hypocrites who take the holy name with only outside distinctions have no lawful entrance. Clean of hands and pure of heart: under purity of hands and heart he comprehends all religion—true purity seats in the heart and shows fruit in the hands. Lift up your heads, O ye gates: God condescended to dwell by a visible symbol; the preaching of the word, the sacraments, and the assemblies lift us to God. Who is this King of glory? Jehovah strong and mighty, ready to succor his people. The Son of God, clothed with our flesh, has now shown himself to be King of glory and Lord of hosts, and he is not entered into his temple only by shadows and figures, but really and in very deed, that he may dwell in the midst of us.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 24)
