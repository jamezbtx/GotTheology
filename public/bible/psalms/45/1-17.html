Psalms 45

Verses 1–17 · ESV · Voices

To the choirmaster: according to Lilies. A Maskil of the Sons of Korah; a love song. Royal and messianic wedding psalm: My heart overflows with a pleasing theme; I address my verses to the king; my tongue is like the pen of a ready scribe. You are the most handsome of the sons of men; grace is poured upon your lips; therefore God has blessed you forever. Gird your sword on your thigh, O mighty one, in your splendor and majesty! In your majesty ride out victoriously for the cause of truth and meekness and righteousness; let your right hand teach you awesome deeds! Your arrows are sharp in the heart of the king's enemies; the peoples fall under you. Your throne, O God, is forever and ever. The scepter of your kingdom is a scepter of uprightness; you have loved righteousness and hated wickedness. Therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions; your robes are all fragrant with myrrh and aloes and cassia. From ivory palaces stringed instruments make you glad; daughters of kings are among your ladies of honor; at your right hand stands the queen in gold of Ophir. Hear, O daughter, and consider, and incline your ear: forget your people and your father's house, and the king will desire your beauty. Since he is your lord, bow to him. The people of Tyre will seek your favor with gifts, the richest of the people. All glorious is the princess in her chamber, with robes interwoven with gold. In many-colored robes she is led to the king, with her virgin companions following behind her. With joy and gladness they are led along as they enter the palace of the king. In place of your fathers shall be your sons; you will make them princes in all the earth. I will cause your name to be remembered in all generations; therefore nations will praise you forever and ever.

Psalms 45:1

1My heart overflows with a pleasing theme; I address my verses to the king; my tongue is like the pen of a ready scribe.

Arminian

My heart overflows with a pleasing theme; I address my verses to the king; my tongue is like the pen of a ready scribe. Wesley: Enditing—Heb. boileth, or bubbleth up like water over the fire. This denotes that the workings of his heart, were fervent and vehement, kindled by God's grace, and the inspiration of the Holy Ghost. Pen—He was only the pen or instrument in uttering this song; it was the spirit of God, by whose hand this pen was guided. Overflow and ready pen name inspired composition for the king.

Arminian pastoral: shared—let the heart boil over with a pleasing theme for this King. Offer tongue as ready instrument; refuse a cold pen that never needed the Spirit's hand.

Reformed

Calvin: My heart is boiling over with a good matter—preface showing the subject is no common one; Holy Spirit not accustomed to inspire servants to utter great swelling words and empty sounds; therefore not merely a transitory and earthly kingdom but something more excellent. Tongue compared to the pen of a swift and ready writer—harmony between tongue and heart.

Reformed pastoral: shared—treat the boiling preface as warrant that more than bare earthly pomp is sung. Let heart and tongue agree for the King; refuse empty swelling that never serves his praise.

Key contrast Both hear a heart overflowing and a tongue as ready pen under the Spirit. Arminians typically hear Wesley: fervent boiling kindled by grace and inspiration; the writer only the pen guided by God's Spirit. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: boiling preface proving no common earthly kingdom—something more excellent, tongue and heart in harmony.

Psalms 45:2

2You are the most handsome of the sons of men; grace is poured upon your lips; therefore God has blessed you forever.

Arminian

You are the most handsome of the sons of men; grace is poured upon your lips; therefore God has blessed you forever. Wesley: Fairer—Than all other men: which is most true of Christ; but not of Solomon; whom many have excelled, in holiness and righteousness, which is the chief part of the beauty celebrated in this psalm. Grace—God hath plentifully poured into thy mind and tongue the gift of speaking wisely, eloquently, and acceptably. Therefore—And because God hath so eminently qualified thee for rule, therefore he hath blessed thee with an everlasting kingdom.

Arminian pastoral: shared—measure beauty by holiness and grace on the lips. Trace forever blessing to God's qualifying gift; refuse a fairness-cult that never needs his speech.

Reformed

Calvin: Thou art fairer than the sons of men—commendation of beauty, then eloquence; personal excellence because noble mind often shines in the countenance. Grace of oratory belongs to a king who must allure to obedience by argument, not only compel. Al-ken because: Solomon distinguished because God blessed him—not blessed for beauty as wage. Excellence displayed in Solomon shone more fully in Christ, to whom truth serves as scepter.

Reformed pastoral: shared—read handsomeness as gifted excellence for rule, not mere face. Trace forever blessing to God's prior favor; refuse a reading that makes beauty earn the kingdom.

Key contrast Both deny bare Solomonic face-beauty as the psalm's chief point and hear grace on the lips under God's blessing. Arminians typically hear Wesley: fairest true of Christ, not Solomon whom many excelled in holiness; everlasting kingdom because God so qualified him. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: mind-shining beauty and oratory to allure; blessed because God blessed him—type in Solomon, fuller in Christ.

Psalms 45:3

3Gird your sword on your thigh, O mighty one, in your splendor and majesty!

Arminian

Gird your sword on your thigh, O mighty one, in your splendor and majesty! Wesley: Thy sword—To smite thine enemies. And the sword is here put for all his arms, as it is in many other places. Sword girded on the mighty one—splendor and majesty for war against foes.

Arminian pastoral: shared—see the King armed, not only adorned. Honor his majesty that smites enemies; refuse a meekness that never girds a sword.

Reformed

Calvin: Gird thy sword upon thy thigh—praised for warlike valor that strikes terror into enemies and for virtues that secure reverence among subjects. Sword first a token of prowess to repel enemies; secondly of authority that he might not be held in contempt. Glory obtained not merely transient pomp but lasting and increasing.

Reformed pastoral: shared—hold both the King's terror to foes and authority among his people. Seek lasting glory, not pageant; refuse a swordless reign that cannot defend.

Psalms 45:4

4In your majesty ride out victoriously for the cause of truth and meekness and righteousness; let your right hand teach you awesome deeds!

Arminian

In your majesty ride out victoriously for the cause of truth and meekness and righteousness; let your right hand teach you awesome deeds! Wesley: Ride—March on speedily and successfully against thine enemies. The word—That is, the gospel: which is called the word of truth, Eph 1:13, and may no less truly be called the word of meekness, because it is not delivered with terror, as the law was at Sinai, but meekly and sweetly; and the word of righteousness, because it brings in everlasting righteousness. And so the gospel is compared to an horse or chariot, upon which Christ is said to ride, when the gospel is preached. Teach thee—Thou shalt do exploits terrible to thine enemies; his hand should shew him, discover and work before him.

Arminian pastoral: shared—ride with the gospel for truth, meekness, and righteousness. Let his right hand work awesome deeds; refuse a ride that never carries the word.

Reformed

Calvin: Ride upon truth, meekness, and righteousness—virtues compared to chariots in which the king is borne aloft with majesty; opposed not only to vain pomp but to ambition, pride, fierceness, cruelty, and violence by which worldly kings seek renown. Faithfulness and justice, tempered with mercy, are the true foundation of kingdoms. Right hand shall teach terrible things—undertakings prosper when courage combines with justice and mercy.

Reformed pastoral: shared—set the King's ride on truth, meekness, and righteousness, not on force alone. Prefer virtue-chariots to cruel horses; refuse a majesty that never needs equity.

Key contrast Both hear a victorious ride for truth, meekness, and righteousness with awesome deeds by the right hand. Arminians typically hear Wesley: the gospel as horse or chariot Christ rides when preached—word of truth, meekness, and righteousness. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: those virtues themselves as the king's chariots, opposed to worldly ambition and cruelty—foundation of lasting rule.

Psalms 45:5

5Your arrows are sharp in the heart of the king's enemies; the peoples fall under you.

Arminian

Your arrows are sharp in the heart of the king's enemies; the peoples fall under you. Wesley: Arrows—The same with the sword, and this is no other than his word, which is sharp and powerful, and pierceth the hearts of men. Fall—Prostrate at thy feet, after the manner of conquered persons. Sharp arrows and falling peoples—the word piercing hearts.

Arminian pastoral: shared—receive the sharp word that pierces. Fall under the King rather than harden; refuse an arrow-proof heart that never yields.

Reformed

Calvin: Thy arrows are sharp—warlike power again: pierce the hearts of enemies; weapons to strike at a distance all who resist his authority. Peoples fall under him—whoever attempts to shake the kingdom's stability shall perish; sufficiency of power to break stubbornness.

Reformed pastoral: shared—own that resistance to this King meets piercing judgment. Submit before you fall as foe; refuse a stubbornness that tests his arrows.

Psalms 45:6

6Your throne, O God, is forever and ever. The scepter of your kingdom is a scepter of uprightness;

Arminian

Your throne, O God, is forever and ever. The scepter of your kingdom is a scepter of uprightness; Wesley: O God—It is evident, that the speech is still continued to the same person whom he calls king, ver.1,11, and here God, to assure us that he doth not speak of Solomon, but a far greater king, who is not only a man, but the mighty God, Isa 9:6. A right scepter—Thou rulest with exact righteousness and equity. Shared with the Reformed: Hebrews 1:8 cites this of the Son—throne forever, scepter of uprightness.

Arminian pastoral: shared with both camps—confess the King as God with forever throne and upright scepter. Worship the mighty God of Isa 9:6; refuse a reading that strips deity from the addressed King.

Reformed

Calvin: Thy throne, O God, is for ever and ever—Jews who make the address to God alone or read Elohim as genitive wrest Scripture against Messiah's divinity. Simple sense: not tyrannical but just and equal laws; yet Elohim cannot well apply to a mortal without qualification—nowhere is man or angel so titled without restriction. Therefore this psalm relates to a higher than any earthly kingdom; divine majesty of Christ expressly denoted. Hebrews 1:8–9 applies the same words to the Son. Shared with Wesley: the forever throne and upright scepter name Christ's deity and righteous rule.

Reformed pastoral: shared with both camps—hold Elohim here as Christ's divine majesty, forever throne, upright scepter. Rest under the Son's reign cited in Hebrews; refuse a wresting that denies the Messiah's deity.

Psalms 45:7

7you have loved righteousness and hated wickedness. Therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions;

Arminian

you have loved righteousness and hated wickedness. Therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions; Wesley: Therefore—Therefore God hath exalted thee far above all men and angels, to a state of joy and endless glory at his right hand; which is fitly compared by the oil of gladness. Thy God—According to thy human nature, John 20:17, though in respect of thy Divine nature, thou art his fellow, Zech 13:7, and his equal, Phil 2:6, and one with him, John 10:30. Fellows—Above all them who partake with thee in this unction: above all that ever were anointed for priests or prophets, or kings. Shared with the Reformed: love of righteousness and anointing above companions point to the Messiah Hebrews cites.

Arminian pastoral: shared with both camps—rejoice in the anointed who loves righteousness and hates wickedness. Honor him above all fellows; refuse a gladness-oil that never exalts this King.

Reformed

Calvin: Loves righteousness and hates wickedness—strict in punishing iniquity as in maintaining justice; rightful government restrains wickedness and vigorously maintains righteousness. Anointed above fellows: not effect of righteousness but its cause—divinely appointed and furnished with endowments; al-ken because. Oil of gladness: royal dignity whose effect is the Church's felicity under the kingdom promised to David's house. Explained of the Mediator, God manifested in the flesh, subject as our brother, anointed above fellows (Heb 2:17). Shared with Wesley: Hebrews applies this anointing to Christ.

Reformed pastoral: shared with both camps—trace the King's righteousness to divine anointing above companions. Rest the Church's gladness on that oil; refuse a reading that severs Mediator from the forever throne.

Psalms 45:8

8your robes are all fragrant with myrrh and aloes and cassia. From ivory palaces stringed instruments make you glad;

Arminian

your robes are all fragrant with myrrh and aloes and cassia. From ivory palaces stringed instruments make you glad; Wesley: Myrrh—Wherewith they used to perfume their garments: this may denote those glorious and sweet smelling virtues, which, as they were treasured up in Christ's heart, so did they manifest themselves outwardly in the whole course of his life and actions. Whereby—By the sweet smell of thy garments out of those ivory palaces, or the effusion of the gifts and graces of thy spirit from heaven; which rejoices the heart of Christ as demonstration of his power and as instrument of bringing souls to God.

Arminian pastoral: shared—smell the King's fragrant virtues and Spirit-gifts. Let ivory-palace gladness draw you; refuse a scentless Christianity that never manifests grace.

Reformed

Calvin: Garments perfumed with precious odours; Solomon coming forth from ivory palace amidst applause. Superfluity not justified, yet moderate grandeur suitable to dignity need not be condemned with excess austerity. All that is here of Solomon was not simply approved—polygamy displeasing—yet the text records God's liberality in abundance. Spiritually: kingdom replenished with rich abundance under earthly figures; spiritual gifts of more value than worldly riches.

Reformed pastoral: shared—read fragrance as the King's excellent gifts under figure. Prefer spiritual odours to bare pomp; refuse both austere denial of all grandeur and a luxury that forgets God.

Psalms 45:9

9daughters of kings are among your ladies of honor; at your right hand stands the queen in gold of Ophir.

Arminian

daughters of kings are among your ladies of honor; at your right hand stands the queen in gold of Ophir. Wesley: Among—Among them that attend upon thy spouse, as the manner was in nuptial solemnities. As the queen is the church in general, and so these honourable women are particular believers, who are daily added to the church, Acts 2:47. And although the church is made up of particular believers, yet she is distinguished from them, for the decency of the parable. Kings daughters—because many of royal race embraced the faith, and because they are in a spiritual sense Kings unto God, Rev 1:6. Right hand—The most honourable place. Ophir—graces wherewith the church is accomplished.

Arminian pastoral: shared—see the Church as queen at the King's right hand, believers as attendants. Seek Ophir-grace adornment; refuse a solitary faith that never joins the bride.

Reformed

Calvin: Daughters of kings among honorable women; consort at the right hand in gold of Ophir. Right hand the place of dignity. Properly one spouse of Christ consisting of the whole body of the faithful; she sits by the King not exercising peculiar dominion but because Christ rules in her—called mother of us all (Gal 4:26). Particulars need not be pressed curiously so as to invent several churches and rent the body's unity.

Reformed pastoral: shared—honor the one bride at Christ's right hand. Hold unity of the Church under his rule; refuse a multiplication of spouses that splits the body.

Key contrast Both hear the queen at the King's right hand and royal attendants in the wedding scene. Arminians typically hear Wesley: queen as the church in general, honorable women as particular believers daily added, kings' daughters spiritually kings unto God. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: one spouse—the whole body of the faithful—at the right hand because Christ rules in her, without pressing particulars into several churches.

Psalms 45:10

10Hear, O daughter, and consider, and incline your ear: forget your people and your father's house,

Arminian

Hear, O daughter, and consider, and incline your ear: forget your people and your father's house, Wesley: Hearken—The prophet having hitherto spoken to the bridegroom, now addresseth his speech to the bride. O daughter—He speaks like an elder person, and as her spiritual father and counsellor. Incline—He uses several words, signifying the same thing, to shew his vehement desire of her good. Forget—Comparatively. Triple call to hear, consider, incline—then forget people and father's house.

Arminian pastoral: shared—hear the call to leave former allegiances for the King. Forget comparatively what rivaled him; refuse a divided ear that never inclines.

Reformed

Calvin: Hearken, O daughter, and consider, and incline thy ear; forget thy people and thy father's house—exhortation to the new bride to become altogether new; vehemence needed for complete renunciation of what nature and custom delight in. Remarkable prophecy of Gentile calling: Son of God forms alliance with strangers; wall of separation broken. Father's house and people: corruptions from the womb and evil custom; whatever men have of themselves. To deny ourselves is the beginning of sacred union with Christ.

Reformed pastoral: shared—answer Hearken with self-denying forgetfulness of the old house. Enter the holy alliance as Gentile bride; refuse a nostalgia that keeps the dividing wall.

Key contrast Both urge the daughter to hear, consider, incline, and forget people and father's house. Arminians typically hear Wesley: address shifting to the bride; elder counsellor's vehement desire; forget comparatively. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: complete renunciation for Gentile calling—self-denial as beginning of union; father's house as native corruption and custom.

Psalms 45:11

11and the king will desire your beauty. Since he is your lord, bow to him.

Arminian

and the king will desire your beauty. Since he is your lord, bow to him. Wesley: So—So thou shalt be acceptable to thy husband; which will abundantly recompence thee, for the loss of thy father's house. Thy Lord—As he is thy husband, and also as he is thy king, and God. Desire of beauty joined to lordship—bow to him.

Arminian pastoral: shared—let the King's desire and lordship move you to bow. Accept the recompense of his favor; refuse a beauty that never worships.

Reformed

Calvin: King shall greatly desire thy beauty; he is thy Lord; worship him—recompense that allays grief of separation from the old house. Worship: not only outward ceremony but holy desire to yield reverence and obedience. Church must not with unbridled license triumph over her Husband; Christ does not elevate the Church to impair his authority.

Reformed pastoral: shared—bow because he is Lord, not only because beauty is desired. Keep the Church under the Husband's authority; refuse a reverence that crowns the bride above Christ.

Psalms 45:12

12The people of Tyre will seek your favor with gifts, the richest of the people.

Arminian

The people of Tyre will seek your favor with gifts, the richest of the people. Wesley: The daughter—The people of Tyre; he mentions the Tyrians because they among others, and before many others, were to be converted to Christ, but they are here put for all the Gentiles, whom that city fitly represents, as being the mart of the nations. A gift—To testify their homage. The rich—Of other nations.

Arminian pastoral: shared—welcome Gentile homage seeking the bride's favor. Receive gifts as testimony to the King; refuse a closed court that never admits the nations.

Reformed

Calvin: Daughter of Tyre with a gift; rich among the people shall entreat thy face—part of the recompense promised the queen. Tyre formerly renowned and opulent; high honor that men from such a city come to greet and submit. Realized in measure when great men, even if themselves refusing full submission to Christ, act with kindness toward the Church.

Reformed pastoral: shared—expect the nations' gifts at the bride's favor. Hold Tyre as figure of wide homage; refuse despair when the rich still only approach the Church's edge.

Psalms 45:13

13All glorious is the princess in her chamber, with robes interwoven with gold.

Arminian

All glorious is the princess in her chamber, with robes interwoven with gold. Wesley: Daughter—The spouse; so called, because she was the daughter of one king, and the wife of another. Within—In her soul. Her cloathing—She is outwardly adorned with virtuous and honourable actions. Glory within and gold-woven robes—inner and outer beauty.

Arminian pastoral: shared—seek glory in the soul and virtuous outward adorning. Let chamber-glory match gold robes; refuse display that never begins within.

Reformed

Calvin: Daughter of the King all glorious within; clothing of garments embroidered with gold—either always sumptuously apparelled even in private, or splendor of substantial material, not mere dazzle. Church richly apparelled not to attract men's notice but for the King's pleasure. If she lacks spiritual beauty in which Christ's glory shines, the fault is ingratitude that despises or squanders God's goodness.

Reformed pastoral: shared—dress the bride for the King's pleasure, not for human stare. Seek within-glory that is real; refuse a poverty of spiritual beauty blamed on fate.

Key contrast Both hear the princess glorious with gold-woven robes. Arminians typically hear Wesley: within as in her soul; clothing as virtuous and honourable actions. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: within as private chamber or substantial apparel for the King's pleasure—spiritual beauty whose lack is our ingratitude.

Psalms 45:14

14In many-colored robes she is led to the king, with her virgin companions following behind her.

Arminian

In many-colored robes she is led to the king, with her virgin companions following behind her. Wesley: Brought—He alludes to the custom of conducting the bride to the bride-groom's house. Companions—Her bride-maidens attending upon her. Led in many-colored robes with virgin companions—nuptial procession to the King.

Arminian pastoral: shared—enter the procession toward the King. Walk with companions in joyful escort; refuse a lone arrival that never joins the bride's train.

Reformed

Calvin: Brought to the King in raiment of needle-work; virgins after her, her companions, brought—display of the bride's train entering. Figures of earthly ceremony teach the Church's approach to Christ; not every ornament need be pressed into separate allegory.

Reformed pastoral: shared—go in with the companions to the King. Keep the procession Christ-ward; refuse curious straining of every thread of the parable.

Psalms 45:15

15With joy and gladness they are led along as they enter the palace of the king.

Arminian

With joy and gladness they are led along as they enter the palace of the king. Joy and gladness mark the entrance—led into the King's palace, not dragged.

Arminian pastoral: shared—enter the palace with joy and gladness. Let the leading be glad; refuse a grim procession that never rejoices in the King.

Reformed

Calvin: Brought with joy and gladness; they enter into the palace of the King—nuptial gladness consummated in entrance. The Church's approach ends not in outer courts but in the King's own palace under figures of joy.

Reformed pastoral: shared—expect glad entrance into the King's palace. Hold joy as fitting the wedding; refuse a reading that makes arrival only fear.

Psalms 45:16

16In place of your fathers shall be your sons; you will make them princes in all the earth.

Arminian

In place of your fathers shall be your sons; you will make them princes in all the earth. Wesley: Instead—Having directed his speech to the bride, he now returns to the bridegroom, as may be gathered from the Hebrew words of masculine gender; and from the next verse, which belongs unto him; therefore this cannot be understood of Solomon and Pharaoh's daughter, because he had no children by her, and but very few by all his wives; his children were far from princes in all the earth. Fully accomplished in Christ: who instead of his fathers of the Jewish nation, had a numerous posterity of Christians of all nations, called princes and kings because of their great power with God and with men.

Arminian pastoral: shared—look for sons made princes in all the earth under Christ. Count the nations' believers as his posterity; refuse a Solomonic limit that never reaches the earth.

Reformed

Calvin: Instead of thy fathers shall be thy children; make them princes in all the earth—children not inferior in dignity to fathers; empire so extensive it might be divided into many kingdoms. Easy to gather this is spoken of Christ: Solomon's first successor retained only a small part; true successors shut within narrow limits. At Christ's coming children begotten inferior in no respect in number or excellence, set as rulers over the whole world. Sovereignty consists not in persons of men but refers to the head; dominion ascribed to members is properly Christ's—ministers subdue the world by the Gospel scepter.

Reformed pastoral: shared—trace worldwide princes to Christ's headship, not to Solomon's line. Serve as ministers under his scepter; refuse a glory that forgets the cross while numbering the earth.

Key contrast Both deny that Solomon's sons fulfill princes in all the earth and hear Christ's worldwide posterity. Arminians typically hear Wesley: address returns to the bridegroom; Christians of all nations as princes for power with God and men. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: extent beyond any Davidic successor; rulers under the Head—dominion properly Christ's, exercised through Gospel ministers.

Psalms 45:17

17I will cause your name to be remembered in all generations; therefore nations will praise you forever and ever.

Arminian

I will cause your name to be remembered in all generations; therefore nations will praise you forever and ever. Wesley: Remembered—As he began the psalm with the celebration of the king's praises, so now he ends with it, and adds this important circumstance, that this nuptial song should not only serve for the present solemnity, but should be remembered and sung in all successive generations. Name remembered and nations' forever praise close the wedding song.

Arminian pastoral: shared closing with both camps—keep the King's name remembered in every generation. Join the nations' forever praise; refuse a song that ends with one wedding day.

Reformed

Calvin: I will make thy name to be remembered throughout all generations; people shall praise thee for ever and ever—inapplicable to Solomon, who by impious rebellion stained his memory; Rehoboam lost the better part of the kingdom. True accomplishment in Christ: memory of his name continues to prosper; despised and trampled yet surviving in undiminished majesty; enemies unable to extinguish it; proclaimed in every language. Diligent endeavor that the memory of Christ never lose renown, for the eternal salvation of men.

Reformed pastoral: shared closing with both camps—labor that Christ's name be remembered and praised forever. Hold undiminished majesty against blasphemy; refuse a memory that settles for Solomon's stained renown.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the heart boiling over with a pleasing theme and the tongue as ready pen, the king fairer than the sons of men with grace poured on his lips, the sword girded and the ride for truth, meekness, and righteousness, sharp arrows and peoples falling, the throne addressed O God forever with a scepter of uprightness, the anointing with oil of gladness above fellows, fragrant robes from ivory palaces, the queen at the right hand in gold of Ophir, the call Hear O daughter—forget people and father's house—the king's desire and lordship, Tyre's gifts and rich favor, the princess glorious within led in many-colored robes with joy into the palace, sons made princes in all the earth, and the name remembered in all generations with nations' praise forever—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 45:1–17.

Arminian Notable voices

“Enditing—Heb. boileth, or bubbleth up like water over the fire. This denotes that the workings of his heart, were fervent and vehement, kindled by God's grace, and the inspiration of the Holy Ghost. Pen—He was only the pen or instrument in uttering this song; it was the spirit of God, by whose hand this pen was guided. Fairer—Than all other men: which is most true of Christ; but not of Solomon; whom many have excelled, in holiness and righteousness, which is the chief part of the beauty celebrated in this psalm. O God—It is evident, that the speech is still continued to the same person whom he calls king, ver.1,11, and here God, to assure us that he doth not speak of Solomon, but a far greater king, who is not only a man, but the mighty God, Isa 9:6. Thy God—According to thy human nature, John 20:17, though in respect of thy Divine nature, thou art his fellow, Zech 13:7, and his equal, Phil 2:6, and one with him, John 10:30. Fellows—Above all them who partake with thee in this unction: above all that ever were anointed for priests or prophets, or kings. Forget—Comparatively. Thy Lord—As he is thy husband, and also as he is thy king, and God. Within—In her soul.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament, Psalm 45

Reformed Notable voices

“My heart is boiling over with a good matter—preface showing no common subject; Holy Spirit not accustomed to inspire empty swelling; therefore not merely a transitory earthly kingdom but something more excellent. Tongue as pen of a swift writer—harmony of tongue and heart. Thou art fairer than the sons of men—beauty of mind shining in countenance; grace of oratory to allure to obedience; al-ken because God blessed him, not beauty earning the blessing. Gird thy sword—warlike valor and authority; glory lasting, not transient pomp. Ride upon truth, meekness, and righteousness—virtues as chariots; opposed to ambition, cruelty, and vain-glory. Thy throne, O God, is for ever and ever—simple sense: not tyrannical but just; Elohim cannot well apply to a mortal without qualification; divine majesty of Christ expressly denoted; Jews who wrest to God-only address or genitive of God betray presumption against Messiah's divinity. Anointed with oil of gladness above fellows—anointing the cause of righteousness, not its fruit; royal dignity oil of gladness for the Church's felicity under David's promised kingdom. Solomon as type; guided to higher strain comprehending the everlasting Messiah; Mediator subject as God manifested in the flesh. Hearken, O daughter—forget people and father's house; Gentile calling; Church one spouse; worship as holy reverence under Christ's authority, not license above the Husband. I will make thy name remembered—inapplicable to Solomon's stained memory; accomplished in Christ, whose name survives.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms, Psalm 45
