Passage

Psalms 20

Verses 1–9 · ESV · Voices

Psalm 20 is the people’s prayer for the LORD’s anointed in the day of trouble: that the God of Jacob would answer and protect, send help from the sanctuary and support from Zion, remember offerings, grant the heart’s desire, and fulfill petitions—so the congregation may shout for joy and set up banners in God’s name. Confidence rises: the LORD saves his anointed from holy heaven; some trust in chariots and horses, but the faithful trust in the name of the LORD their God, rise while others fall, and cry, O LORD, save the king—answer us when we call.

Psalms 20:1

1May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble! May the name of the God of Jacob protect you!

Arminian

Wesley: God of Jacob—God had made a covenant with Jacob and his posterity. The opening blessing asks answer and protection under that covenant name.

Arminian hearing: trouble is real, and so is the call to seek the LORD’s answer. Shared piety—the name of the God of Jacob is a covenant refuge, not a charm. Grace invites the people to pray for their anointed; the day of trouble does not cancel the duty or the hope of being heard.

Reformed

Calvin: the Spirit puts the people to praying that God would answer the king, and thereby admonishes kings to implore God’s protection in all their affairs. “Day of trouble” shows they will not be exempt from dangers. The name of God is put for God himself—confidence proceeds from his revealed grace and power. Under the temporal kingdom a far more excellent government is described; as Christ never ceases to intercede, the Church unites its prayer with him.

Reformed accent: shared—covenant name as protection. Head and body stay joined in petition; the God of Jacob guards the kingdom he founded.

Psalms 20:2

2May he send you help from the sanctuary and give you support from Zion!

Arminian

Wesley: Sanctuary—from the tabernacle in Zion, where the ark then was; toward which the Israelites directed their prayers.

Arminian pastoral: help is sought where God has pledged his presence. Shared confession—Zion’s sanctuary is the appointed place of appeal. Direct prayer toward God’s dwelling; expect support from the God who meets his people there, not from self-invented shrines.

Reformed

Calvin: may he succor thee out of mount Zion, where he commanded the ark to be placed. Weakness cannot soar to heaven, so God comes down by external means of grace—the ark a pledge of presence, the sanctuary an image of heaven. By joining kingdom and priesthood at Zion, David puts on the people’s lips a prayer for help out of that sacred bond.

Reformed accent: shared—help from Zion. Nearness is God’s condescension through appointed means, drawing minds upward so they do not invent a distant, empty deity.

Psalms 20:3

3May he remember all your offerings and regard with favor your burnt sacrifices! Selah

Arminian

Wesley’s notes pass lightly here; the people ask God to remember and favor the king’s offerings as the ground of answered prayer in battle.

Arminian hearing: shared—God’s regard for sacrifice and prayer is real. Favor is sought, not presumed. Offerings without a heart turned to the LORD are empty; the congregation still pleads that God would accept what is brought in faith.

Reformed

Calvin: “remember” means have regard to—a prayer that God would show the king’s sacrifices acceptable. Under offerings and holocausts David comprehends all sacrifices, and under sacrifices requests and prayers. The fathers’ hope in asking was founded on sacrifices; so now our prayers are acceptable only as Christ sprinkles and sanctifies them with the perfume of his own sacrifice.

Reformed accent: shared—favor on the king’s worship. Acceptability runs through the Mediator’s offering; bare ritual without that covering does not bind God.

Psalms 20:4

4May he grant you your heart’s desire and fulfill all your plans!

Arminian

The people ask God to grant the king’s desire and fulfill his plans—petition tied to the prior plea for favored offerings.

Arminian reading: shared—God answers prayer and orders outcomes. Desire and plans are not blank checks for greed; they are brought before God and must stay under his will. Grace enables the asking; the congregation still must keep praying that the LORD would grant and fulfill.

Reformed

Calvin: they commend to God the desires and counsels of the king. It would be absurd to ask God to grant foolish and wicked desires; the king pictured is not driven by ambition or avarice but intent on his charge and the public good, asking what the Holy Spirit dictates and what God commands him to ask.

Reformed accent: shared—fulfillment from God alone. The heart God grants is one he has already schooled by his Spirit; counsel succeeds because it is tethered to his charge, not because raw wish is sovereign.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD alone grants the heart’s desire and fulfills plans, and both refuse to treat the verse as license for selfish ambition. Arminian readers stress grace-enabled petition the people must keep offering—desire submitted and prayed, not assumed. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress that the desires God fulfills are those the Spirit dictates to a king devoted to God’s charge—so fulfillment rests on God-shaped counsel, not on an unchecked human will.

Psalms 20:5

5May we shout for joy over your salvation, and in the name of our God set up our banners! May the LORD fulfill all your petitions!

Arminian

Wesley: Rejoice—hereby they shew their confidence in God, and their assurance of the victory. Name—to the honour of God. Set up—in way of triumph.

Arminian pastoral: shared joy in the king’s salvation. Banners rise in God’s name, not in self-glory. Confidence and assurance of victory are voiced before the battle ends—faith that still calls, “fulfill all your petitions.”

Reformed

Calvin: the fruit of God’s favor would be common joy—the people are solicitous not for one man’s welfare alone but for the safety of the whole Church. They distinguish their joy from heathen revel by setting up banners in God’s name—grateful acknowledgment and triumph in his praise when he fulfills the king’s petitions.

Reformed accent: shared—shout and banners under God’s name. Salvation of the anointed is corporate mercy; thanksgiving, not carnal boasting, marks the victory song.

Psalms 20:6

6Now I know that the LORD saves his anointed; he will answer him from his holy heaven with the saving might of his right hand.

Arminian

Wesley: Now—we are already sure of victory by the consideration of God’s power and faithfulness and love to David, and to his people. They speak as one person, because they were unanimous in this prayer. Saveth—will certainly save. Strength—this shews how God will hear him, even by saving him with a strong hand.

Arminian hearing: “now I know” is faith’s present assurance from considering who God is. Shared confession—the LORD saves his anointed from heaven. Unanimous prayer and reflection on power, faithfulness, and love ground a sure hope that still keeps calling.

Reformed

Calvin: grateful rejoicing follows—the faithful have experienced God’s goodness in preserving the king. Doctrine of faith: God put forth power to maintain David’s kingdom because it was founded upon his calling. Experience shows he is guardian of the kingdom he himself set up. From past goodness they conclude future preservation; under the heavenly sanctuary his infinite power and majesty are shown, and his right hand’s saving might overcomes every impediment.

Reformed accent: shared—heaven answers the anointed. Certainty rests on God’s founding call and effectual guardianship, which experience confirms rather than creates.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD saves his anointed and answers from holy heaven with the might of his right hand. Arminian readers, with Wesley, hear “now I know” as assurance already sure by considering God’s power, faithfulness, and love—unanimous prayer resting on that character. Reformed readers, with Calvin, hear experiential rejoicing that confirms a deeper ground: God guards the kingdom he founded upon his calling, so saving might is the founder’s fidelity, not confidence that finally hangs on the strength of the people’s consideration alone.

Psalms 20:7

7Some trust in chariots and some in horses, but we trust in the name of the LORD our God.

Arminian

Wesley: Remember—trust in it. The contrast is plain: military hardware versus the LORD’s name.

Arminian reading: shared—refuse chariot-faith. The congregation must actually trust and remember the name. Grace enables that trust; the hearers still turn from horses to the LORD, or they remain among those who trust in flesh.

Reformed

Calvin: not restricted to Israel’s enemies—a comparison between God’s people and the world. Almost all grow confident with riches and forces; the faithful protest that their hope is only in God’s aid. Whenever carnal confidence occupies the mind, forgetfulness of God follows. “Remember” shows that saints must cast off whatever hinders exclusive trust—whether in abundance or in want.

Reformed accent: shared—name against chariots. Remembrance subdues vain hopes; success is ascribed to pure grace, not to apparatus the flesh inflates.

Key contrast Both confess that trust belongs to the name of the LORD our God, not to chariots or horses. Arminian readers stress the people’s required act: grace-enabled remembering and trusting that must be taken up against the lure of visible force. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress that carnal confidence and forgetfulness of God rise together—so exclusive trust is the Spirit’s work of casting off vain props, ascribing every stand to God’s aid alone.

Psalms 20:8

8They collapse and fall, but we rise and stand upright.

Arminian

Wesley: Brought down—from their horses and chariots, to which they trusted. Stand—stand firmly, and keep the field.

Arminian pastoral: shared outcome—those who trusted flesh fall; those who trusted the name stand. The verse teaches by result: keep the field by clinging to the LORD, not by climbing into the enemy’s harness.

Reformed

Calvin: the enemies God overthrew are pointed out—once lifted in cruel pride, now bowed and fallen; the oppressed people rise and stand erect. The event teaches how much more advantageous it is to place confidence in God than to depend on our own strength.

Reformed accent: shared—collapse versus upright stand. Providence reverses proud height; the rise of the faithful is God’s vindication of exclusive trust, not luck on the field.

Psalms 20:9

9O LORD, save the king! May he answer us when we call.

Arminian

Wesley: Let the king—God, the supreme monarch, the king of kings, and in a peculiar manner the king of Israel.

Arminian close: shared cry—save! Answer when we call. Whether the “king” is heard as the LORD himself or as the anointed under him, the people end in petition, not in self-rescue. Keep calling; salvation is from the LORD.

Reformed

Calvin: “Save, O Jehovah” is elliptical and corporate—by blessing the king God shows himself Savior of the whole people. God had not promised salvation apart from the hand of the king he gave; under the type, look to Christ and renounce every other hope. Gathered under one Head, the faithful care for one another’s welfare as they call.

Reformed accent: shared—save and answer. Christ is the King through whom God saves; the Church’s last word is dependence on that saving hand when it calls.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on help from Zion, favor on the king’s offerings, joy in salvation, trust against chariots, and the LORD who saves his anointed—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 20:1–9.

Arminian Notable voices

“A prayer for the king. God of Jacob—God had made a covenant with Jacob and his posterity. Sanctuary—from the tabernacle in Zion, where the ark then was; toward which the Israelites directed their prayers. Rejoice—hereby they shew their confidence in God, and their assurance of the victory. Name—to the honour of God. Set up—in way of triumph. Now—we are already sure of victory by the consideration of God’s power and faithfulness and love to David, and to his people. They speak as one person, because they were unanimous in this prayer. Saveth—will certainly save. Strength—this shews how God will hear him, even by saving him with a strong hand. Remember—trust in it. Brought down—from their horses and chariots, to which they trusted. Stand—stand firmly, and keep the field. Let the king—God, the supreme monarch, the king of kings, and in a peculiar manner the king of Israel.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 20)

Reformed Notable voices

“This psalm contains a common prayer of the Church in behalf of the King of Israel, that God would succor him in danger and maintain his kingdom; for in David’s person the safety of the whole community centred. Under this temporal kingdom is described a government far more excellent, on which the Church’s joy depends—so the faithful must cherish holy solicitude for Christ’s kingdom and continual prayer in its behalf. God comes down by external means: the ark a pledge of presence, the sanctuary an image of heaven. The king’s sacrifices are prayed acceptable; under them prayers are comprehended, and at this day our prayers avail only as Christ sprinkles them with his own sacrifice. The faithful protest that they do not place hope in military forces but only in God’s aid; whenever carnal confidence occupies the mind, forgetfulness of God follows. Experience shows that God is guardian of the kingdom he himself set up. Save, O Jehovah—by blessing the king he shows himself Savior of the whole people; look to Christ, through whose hand God saves, and renounce every other hope.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 20)
