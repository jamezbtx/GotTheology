Passage

Psalms 16

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

Psalm 16 is David’s miktam of refuge and delight: he takes shelter in God, confesses he has no good apart from the LORD, delights in the saints, refuses idol sorrows and bloody libations, and names the LORD as portion, cup, and held lot—pleasant lines and a beautiful inheritance. Counsel by night steadies him; the LORD at his right hand keeps him from being shaken, so heart and flesh rejoice in security. The climax trusts that God will not abandon his soul to Sheol or let his Holy One see corruption, and asks for the path of life—fullness of joy in God’s presence and pleasures forevermore at his right hand. The apostles read the Holy One and incorruption of Christ’s resurrection (Acts 2:25–32; 13:34–37).

Psalms 16:1

1Preserve me, O God, for in you I take refuge.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: David professes trust in God from the outset—refuge is lived adherence, not a slogan, before delight in the saints and satisfaction in God as portion.

Arminian hearing: preserve and refuge name a real cry. Grace enables the soul to take shelter; the hearer must still flee to God and stay under that covering. Safety is sought from God alone, not from mixed trusts.

Reformed

Calvin: David commits himself to God’s protection not only in one emergency but for the whole course of life—and our safety in life and death depends entirely on being under that protection. The Holy Spirit assures that God is ready to succor those who rely on him with sure and steadfast faith, and takes under his care none but those who commit themselves with the whole heart.

Reformed accent: refuge is wholehearted trust under a guarding God. Preservation is God’s office toward those he keeps; steadfast faith rests there amid adversity’s storms.

Psalms 16:2

2I say to the LORD, "You are my Lord; I have no good apart from you."

Arminian

Wesley: To thee—Thou dost not need me or my service, nor art capable of any advantage from it.

Arminian reading: the confession is double—Yahweh is Lord, and every good is from him, not added to him. Grace frees the servant to offer devotion without pretence that God gains by it; humility still speaks and serves.

Reformed

Calvin: David can bestow nothing upon God—God needs nothing, and mortal man cannot merit God’s favor by any service. Two points: God has right to require whatever he pleases as rightful Lord; and our goodness does not extend to him, for we are empty of good things with which to show ourselves liberal toward him. It is impossible by merits to bring God under obligation. When we come before God we must lay aside all presumption; humility is as perfume that procures acceptance.

Reformed accent: “no good apart from you” strips merit and self-credit. Lordship claims the whole person; empty hands, not bargains, approach him.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD is Lord and that we have no goodness that profits or obligates him. Arminian readers, with Wesley, hear humble devotion that still serves without pretending God needs the service. Reformed readers, with Calvin, press further that no human merit can make God a debtor—presumption must be laid aside, and empty acknowledgment of indigence is the posture grace accepts.

Psalms 16:3

3As for the saints in the land, they are the excellent ones, in whom is all my delight.

Arminian

Wesley: But—I bear a singular respect and love to all saints, for thy sake, whose friends and servants they are, and whose image they bear. This more properly agrees to David than to Christ, whose goodness was principally designed for and imparted to sinners.

Arminian pastoral: shared—delight in the excellent ones who bear God’s image. Love of the saints is love for God’s sake. Grace knits affection to the holy community; the hearer must still prefer that fellowship over the world’s glitter.

Reformed

Calvin: after confessing he can bring God no advantage, David sets his affection on the saints—joining devout worshippers in fraternal unity under their common Father. There is no sacrifice more acceptable than sincerely connecting with the society of the righteous. The saints on earth are conspicuous marks of God’s glory; he calls them excellent because righteousness and holiness shine with the brightness of God’s Spirit.

Reformed accent: shared—delight in the saints as God’s visible people. Communion of saints separates from the world’s pollutions; valuing true servants over vain splendor is right worship’s companion.

Psalms 16:4

4The sorrows of those who run after another god shall multiply; their drink offerings of blood I will not pour out or take their names on my lips.

Arminian

Wesley: Sorrows—having shewed affection to the servants of the true God, he declares abhorrence for those that worship idols. Offerings—in which the Gentiles used sometimes to drink part of the blood of their sacrifices. Names—of those other gods.

Arminian pastoral: shared—idol pursuit multiplies sorrow; refuse the cup and the names. Grace trains exclusive loyalty; the hearer must still break from rival gods in practice and speech.

Reformed

Calvin: brotherly concord with the saints requires breaking bonds of impiety and keeping distance from idol pollutions. Idolaters’ sorrows multiply—they busy themselves toward their own destruction, hewing broken cisterns. David will not taste their bloody libations nor take their names on his lips—hatred of idols kept from even the appearance of consent.

Reformed accent: shared—refuse superstition’s rites and names. Full happiness rests in God alone; separating from false worship is how the church stays one body under the true God.

Psalms 16:5

5The LORD is my chosen portion and my cup; you hold my lot.

Arminian

Wesley: The Lord—I rejoice in God as my portion, and desire no better, no other felicity. Cup—the portion put into my cup, as at feasts each had allotted meat and wine. Lot—my inheritance divided by lot.

Arminian hearing: shared—God himself is the chosen share. Grace enables contentment that desires no rival felicity; the hearer must still choose the LORD as portion and cup, not clutch lesser lots.

Reformed

Calvin: David shows why he separates from idolaters—he rests in the only true God as his portion. None are taught aright in true godliness but those who reckon God alone sufficient for happiness. Calling God portion, inheritance, and cup, he protests he is so satisfied as neither to covet anything besides nor be excited by depraved desires. Embrace him with the whole heart. God also engages his power to maintain us in safe enjoyment of so great a good against Satan’s daily assaults.

Reformed accent: shared—God is portion and cup. Contentment is taught godliness: God alone sufficient, and God himself holding the lot so possession is kept.

Psalms 16:6

6The lines have fallen for me in pleasant places; indeed, I have a beautiful inheritance.

Arminian

Wesley: Lines—my portion, measured with lines. Are fallen—in a land flowing with milk and honey, and above all blessed with the presence and knowledge of God.

Arminian pastoral: shared—pleasant lines and a beautiful inheritance center on God’s presence, not bare acreage. Grace opens eyes to call that lot goodly; the hearer must still bless the lines that fall in God rather than envy another’s map.

Reformed

Calvin: David so glories in God as to despise all the world imagines excellent without him. He who has God as portion is destitute of nothing requisite to a happy life. When allurements would lead away from resting in God alone, use this as antidote: he who has absolute fullness of all good has given himself to be enjoyed by us.

Reformed accent: shared—pleasant places are God-given. The beautiful inheritance is God himself possessed; lesser splendors lose their shine beside that lot.

Psalms 16:7

7I bless the LORD who gives me counsel; in the night also my heart instructs me.

Arminian

Wesley: The Lord—hath inspired that wisdom into me by which I have chosen the Lord for my portion, and am so fully satisfied with him. Reins—my inward thoughts and affections, being inspired and moved by the Holy Spirit. Instruct—direct me how to please God and put my whole trust in him. Night—even when others sleep, my mind works upon God, improving silence and solitude in holy meditations.

Arminian reading: counsel is Spirit-given wisdom that enables a real choosing of God as portion. Night instruction is practiced holy meditation—grace moves the heart; the hearer still blesses the LORD and yields the inward life to that direction.

Reformed

Calvin: David confesses it was entirely owing to pure grace that he came to possess so great a good and was made a partaker of it by faith. God offers himself freely, yet the reprobate by ingratitude defraud themselves of the blessing. The counsel is inward illumination of the Holy Spirit preventing us from rejecting the salvation to which he calls us. Those who attribute to free will the choice of accepting or rejecting God’s grace basely mangle that grace. Night and reins mean secret inspirations; God not only begins faith but continually corrects vanity and kindles faith brighter through life.

Reformed accent: night counsel is the Spirit’s secret tuition from first faith onward—not a decisive human accept/reject perched above grace.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD’s counsel and night heart-instruction are Spirit-worked, not bare human cleverness. Arminian readers, with Wesley, hear inspired wisdom by which the believer has chosen the LORD as portion and still practices holy night meditation. Reformed readers, with Calvin, deny that free will accepts or rejects grace as if mangling it; the Spirit’s inward illumination begins and continually advances faith so that possessing God as portion is pure gift received by faith he works.

Psalms 16:8

8I have set the LORD always before me; because he is at my right hand, I shall not be shaken.

Arminian

Wesley: I have set—I have always presented him to my mind as my witness and judge, as my patron and protector. Hitherto David seems to speak of himself; now he is transported by the spirit of prophecy to speak as a type of Christ, in whom this and the following verses were truly accomplished. Christ as man always set his Father’s will and glory before him. Right-hand—to strengthen, protect, assist, and comfort; necessary to Christ as man. Moved—though men and devils seek destruction, he is assured of deliverance.

Arminian hearing: setting the LORD always before oneself is a grace-enabled, continual practice—Christ’s pattern and the member’s path. The right hand steadies; the hearer must still keep God before the mind and not drift to other stays.

Reformed

Calvin: to set God before us is to keep all senses bound that they may not go astray after another object. Faith elevates the mind above the world so we do not turn our back on him. Continually shows constant dependence—amid conflicts, no fear makes him look elsewhere for succor. Because God is at his right hand he reckons himself secure; with eyes of faith he beholds God present. This overthrows the fabrication that the faithful are in doubt of final perseverance—David extends reliance on God’s grace to the time to come.

Reformed accent: unshaken standing rests on God’s present right-hand care and promised continuance—not on trembling uncertainty every moment about whether grace will hold.

Key contrast Both confess that setting the LORD always before oneself and his right-hand presence keep the believer from being shaken, and both hear Christ in the type. Arminian readers, with Wesley, stress continual presenting of God to the mind—grace-enabled practice patterned in Christ as man. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress that faith’s fixed eye on providence yields assurance that extends to the time to come: God at the right hand secures his people, so they need not live in perpetual doubt of perseverance.

Psalms 16:9

9Therefore my heart is glad, and my whole being rejoices; my flesh also dwells secure.

Arminian

Wesley: My glory—my tongue, a man’s glory above other creatures. Rejoiceth—declares inward joy; the word marks outward demonstration more than bare eternal joy. My flesh—my body shall quietly rest in the grave in confident assurance of incorruption there and of resurrection to immortal life.

Arminian pastoral: shared—glad heart, rejoicing being, flesh that dwells secure. Joy runs from trust in the God kept before the eyes; even the body’s rest leans on resurrection hope. Grace produces this security; the tongue still gives it voice.

Reformed

Calvin: faith’s inestimable fruit is not only mental tranquillity but joyful cheer under God’s protection. Calm rejoicing is the lot of those who place confidence in God alone. True believers have spiritual joy in the heart and manifest it by the tongue as they glory in God. God defends soul and body; David does not speak groundlessly when the blessing of dwelling in safety extends to his flesh.

Reformed accent: shared—heart, tongue, and flesh share security rooted in God. Joy is faith’s fruit under protection, not intoxication that never knew true peace.

Psalms 16:10

10For you will not abandon my soul to Sheol, or let your holy one see corruption.

Arminian

Wesley: Hell—in the state of the dead. Holy one—Me thy holy son, whom thou hast sanctified and sent into the world. It is peculiar to Christ to be called the Holy One of God. To see—to be corrupted or putrefied in the grave as others’ bodies are.

Arminian reading: shared with the apostles—Acts 2 and 13 appropriate this to Christ: God did not abandon him to Sheol or let the Holy One see corruption. David as type speaks beyond himself; the resurrection of Jesus is the proper fulfillment. Believers share hope only in union with that Holy One.

Reformed

Calvin: David entertains assurance of eternal salvation that frees from anxiety—there will always be a way of escape from the grave. He raised his mind above the common lot; dust returns to dust for Adam’s children. Peter justly concludes David gloried thus by the spirit of prophecy with special respect to the Author of life. Peter and Paul contend the prophecy was fulfilled in Christ alone—wholly exempted from the grave’s corruption that he might call his members into fellowship. David’s body was reduced to dust; fullness of incorruptible life resides in the Head and falls on members by degrees.

Reformed accent: shared—Acts 2 and 13 fix the Holy One and incorruption on Christ’s resurrection. The church’s hope is participation in the Head who saw no corruption, not a claim that every saint’s flesh escaped the pit as he did.

Psalms 16:11

11You make known to me the path of life; in your presence there is fullness of joy; at your right hand are pleasures forevermore.

Arminian

Wesley: Life—Thou wilt raise me from the grave and conduct me to everlasting felicity. Presence—in that heavenly paradise where thou art gloriously present and fully discover the light of thy countenance. Right-hand—place of greatest honour, where the saints are placed at the last day and where Christ sits (Ps. 110:1). Pleasures—all our joys here are empty and defective; in heaven is fulness of joy. Pleasures here are transient; at God’s right hand are pleasures for evermore—immortal souls in enjoyment of an eternal God.

Arminian pastoral: shared—path of life, presence-joy, forever pleasures. Grace raises and conducts; the hearer’s hope is fixed on God’s presence, not on fading earthly sweets.

Reformed

Calvin: God exempts from death’s bondage by bringing safely to eternal life. Showing the path of life must extend to blessed immortality—not mere prolongation of natural years that reprobate and elect share under the sun. When God is reconciled we have what is necessary to perfect happiness. Fullness of joy contrasts evanescent worldly pleasures that leave votaries famished. True solid joy rests only in God; none but the faithful contented with his grace alone can be truly and perfectly happy.

Reformed accent: shared—path, presence, and forever pleasures are eternal life with God. The special grace that shows this way belongs to his children; joy’s fullness is his countenance, not the world’s short cup.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on refuge in God, delight in the saints, God as portion, night counsel, unshaken confidence, and the Holy One who sees no corruption—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 16:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“David professes his trust in God, his adherence to him and love of his people; his satisfaction in God; and toward the close he speaks in the person of Christ, of God’s presence with him, of his resurrection and the glory that should follow. As David was both a member and an eminent type of Christ, he speaks sometimes in the one and sometimes in the other capacity: having spoken of himself as a member of Christ, he proceeds as a type, and being inspired by the Holy Ghost speaks such things as, though they might be accommodated to himself in a very imperfect sense, yet could not properly belong to any but to Christ, to whom they are justly appropriated in the New Testament. To thee—Thou dost not need me or my service, nor art capable of any advantage from it. But—I bear a singular respect and love to all saints, for thy sake. The Lord—I rejoice in God as my portion, and desire no better. I have set—I have always presented him to my mind as my witness and judge, as my patron and protector; now he is transported by the spirit of prophecy to speak as a type of Christ. Hell—in the state of the dead. Holy one—Me thy holy son; it is peculiar to Christ to be called the Holy One of God. To see—to be corrupted or putrefied in the grave. Life—Thou wilt raise me from the grave and conduct me to everlasting felicity. In heaven there is fulness of joy; at God’s right hand are pleasures for evermore—the pleasures of immortal souls in the enjoyment of an eternal God.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 16)

Reformed Notable voices

“In the beginning David commends himself to the protection of God. He meditates upon the benefits he received and stirs himself up to thanksgiving. By his service he could in no respect be profitable to God, but he surrenders himself entirely to him, protesting that he will have nothing to do with superstitions, for full happiness consists in resting in God alone, who never suffers his own people to want any good thing. God is ready to succor all who rely upon him with sure and steadfast faith, and takes under his protection none but those who commit themselves to him with their whole heart. None are taught aright in true godliness but those who reckon God alone sufficient for their happiness. The counsel of which David speaks is the inward illumination of the Holy Spirit; those who attribute to free will the choice of accepting or rejecting the grace of God basely mangle that grace. To set God before us is to keep all our senses bound that they may not go astray; David extends his reliance on God’s grace to the time to come. When David said God would not leave his soul in the grave nor let his Holy One see corruption, he raised his mind above the common lot; Peter and Paul justly conclude that this prophecy was fulfilled in the person of Christ alone, who was wholly exempted from the corruption of the grave that he might make his members partakers of this blessing. The path of life extends to blessed immortality; fullness of joy is in God’s countenance, and true solid joy will never be found anywhere else but in God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 16)
