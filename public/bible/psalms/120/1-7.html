Passage

Psalms 120

Verses 1–7 · ESV · Voices

A Song of Ascents opens the pilgrimage collection in distress: the singer calls to the LORD from among deceitful neighbors who hate peace. Meshech and Kedar name far-flung, hostile company. The psalm moves from an answered cry to lament over lying tongues and a world that answers peace with war—the pilgrim’s need before Zion’s songs of trust.

Psalms 120:1

1In my distress I called to the LORD, and he answered me.

Arminian

Distress drives a real call; the LORD answers. Pilgrims on the Ascents road do not pretend the journey is easy—they cry out, and Scripture says God hears and replies in kind.

Classical Arminians stress sincere petition that God freely answers. Prayer is not theater before a distant throne; the covenant Lord invites distress to speak and then responds in mercy suited to the need.

Reformed

In distress he called, and the LORD answered. The sequence is grace before relief: God grants both the cry and the hearing that follows it on the pilgrim road to Zion.

Reformed comfort for Ascents travelers: answered prayer rests on the Keeper’s faithfulness, not on perfect composure. The collection begins with a God who has already replied to His own.

Psalms 120:2

2Deliver me, O LORD, from lying lips, from a deceitful tongue.

Arminian

Deliverance is sought from lying lips and a deceitful tongue—words that wound the peacemaker while feet still point toward Zion’s peace.

Arminian pastoral care: ask plainly for rescue from speech that twists truth. God can deliver without making the pilgrim cynical; truthfulness remains a calling even when neighbors traffic in deceit.

Reformed

Lying lips and a deceitful tongue are named as enemies on the way. The plea is for the LORD’s deliverance, not for matching deceit with deceit in bitter return.

Reformed note: God rules even the mouth of the adversary. Deliverance may expose, restrain, or judge false speech—always under His wise hand for the good of His pilgrim people.

Psalms 120:3

3What shall be given to you, and what more shall be done to you, you deceitful tongue?

Arminian

The singer turns to the tongue itself: what recompense fits deceit? Moral seriousness, not a revenge fantasy, frames the question before God on the road.

Classical Arminians hear a warning that deceptive speech invites real consequence. God takes words seriously; pilgrims may name injustice without becoming the liars from whom they flee.

Reformed

Rhetorical judgment falls on the deceitful tongue. The question assumes God’s moral government of every word and of every liar’s eventual harvest.

Reformed accent: recompense is the Lord’s alone to assign. The pilgrim does not seize the gavel; he trusts that falsehood will meet what God in holy justice allots.

Psalms 120:4

4A warrior's sharp arrows, with glowing coals of the broom tree!

Arminian

Sharp arrows and glowing broom-coals answer deceit with vivid justice imagery. Lies that pierce deserve a piercing judgment under God’s righteous hand.

Arminian reading: God’s justice is not soft on destructive speech. Pilgrims may hope that truth will out and that harm done by tongues will not stand forever unaddressed before Him.

Reformed

A warrior’s arrows and broom-tree coals picture fitting judgment. The LORD’s arsenal matches the wound that deceit has made among His pilgrim people.

Reformed pastoral counsel: leave vengeance to God. He alone wields holy fire without becoming unjust; the Ascents traveler walks under that certainty day by day.

Psalms 120:5

5Woe to me, that I sojourn in Meshech, that I dwell among the tents of Kedar!

Arminian

Meshech and Kedar stand for distant, hostile worlds—sojourning far from Zion’s peace. Woe names the ache of a misplaced dwelling on the pilgrimage map.

Arminian pastoral: exile of spirit is real and may be confessed. Longing for the house of the LORD often begins with honest grief that we live among tents that do not love His ways.

Reformed

Woe over Meshech and Kedar: geographic extremes for a people not yet at home. The pilgrim’s map starts in alienation before it climbs toward Zion’s courts.

Reformed note: even foreign tents sit inside providence. God knows where His own sojourn until He gathers them into the peace of His city.

Psalms 120:6

6Too long have I had my dwelling among those who hate peace.

Arminian

“Too long” is pilgrim weariness among peace-haters. Duration itself becomes part of the lament offered honestly to God on the Ascents road.

Classical Arminians honor the cry for change: remaining among hostility is not spiritual indifference. Faith may plead for a shorter exile and a nearer peace without abandoning trust in the LORD.

Reformed

Too long among those who hate peace. The complaint is timed by the pilgrim’s felt delay, yet it is held honestly and humbly before God.

Reformed comfort: “too long” for us is not late for the Lord. He numbers the days of hostile dwelling until His peace prevails for the people He keeps.

Psalms 120:7

7I am for peace, but when I speak, they are for war!

Arminian

The singer is for peace; speech meets war. Peacemaking can be answered with aggression—and still remain the right stance on the Ascents road to Zion.

Arminian pastoral: do not let hostility redefine your calling. Seek peace sincerely; when others choose war, keep bringing the conflict to the God who answered in verse 1.

Reformed

Peace spoken, war returned. The contrast exposes hearts; it is not proof that the pilgrim’s desire for peace was wasted effort before God.

Reformed reading: God sees who is for peace and who is for war. The Ascents road runs through that friction until Zion’s true shalom is given from above.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on distress answered by the LORD, deliverance from lying tongues, and sojourning among those who hate peace—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 120:1–7.

Arminian Notable voices

“In my distress I cried unto the Lord—made strong supplication for help. And he heard—answered my prayer by comforting my soul. From lying lips, and from a deceitful tongue—from a people without faith, without truth, without religion. Sharp arrows of the mighty, with coals of juniper—malignant slanders that pierce and burn. That I sojourn in Mesech—woe that I dwell among the barbarous. I am for peace—we love to be quiet and peaceable; but they are continually engaged in rapine and plunder. The poor captives wished them to cultivate the arts of peace, and live quietly; but they would hear of nothing but their old manner of life.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 120)

Reformed Notable voices

“He sets out with gratitude to God, telling us that he had not called upon Him in vain; and he does this that by his own example he might encourage others, especially when oppressed with adversity, to confidence in prayer. Men have need of God’s help every moment; but there is not a more suitable season for seeking him than when some great danger is immediately menacing us. As God is the maintainer of the innocence of his servants, David rises against his calumniators—an impotent passion for evil speaking which God at length would cause to recoil upon their own heads. It is not enough for the faithful to abstain from hurting others: they must study to allure them by gentleness. Should their moderation be rejected, let them wait in patience, until God at length show himself from heaven as their protector.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 120)
