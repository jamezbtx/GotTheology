Passage

Psalms 38

Verses 1–22 · ESV · Voices

A Psalm of David, for the memorial offering. A penitential psalm: the singer pleads that the LORD not rebuke him in anger nor discipline him in wrath; confesses arrows and hand sunk upon him, no soundness in flesh or health in bones because of indignation and sin, iniquities gone over the head like a burden too heavy; names wounds that stink from foolishness, bowed mourning, sides filled with burning, feeble crushed groaning from the heart's tumult; sets all longing and sighing before the Lord while heart throbs, strength fails, and light of eyes is gone; laments friends and companions aloof from his plague and nearest kin far off, while life-seekers lay snares and meditate treachery; answers as deaf and mute without rebukes; waits for the LORD who will answer, lest enemies rejoice when his foot slips; ready to fall with pain ever before him, he confesses iniquity and is sorry for sin even while vigorous foes hate him wrongfully and render evil for good because he follows after good; closes pleading that the LORD not forsake him or be far, and make haste to help—O Lord, my salvation.

Psalms 38:1

1O LORD, rebuke me not in your anger, nor discipline me in your wrath!

Arminian

O LORD, rebuke me not in your anger, nor discipline me in your wrath! The penitential cry opens under memorial: he does not deny he deserves rebuke, but pleads against anger's unchecked heat.

Arminian pastoral: shared—ask for fatherly discipline without the storm that would crush you. Bring guilt honestly; refuse both denial of sin and a prayer that only wants sin left alone.

Reformed

Calvin: David does not expressly ask that afflictions be removed, but only that God would moderate the severity of his chastisements. He is content with mitigation—chastised, yet not beyond what he is able to bear; anger and wrath mark extreme rigor over against fatherly chastisement.

Reformed pastoral: shared—spread the complaint before God in humble confession, not fretful murmur. Seek tempered severity under his hand; do not secretly bargain for impunity.

Key contrast Both plead that rebuke not fall in unchecked anger. Arminians typically hear the pastoral ask for discipline without crushing wrath. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: not removal of every rod, but moderated severity—content with mitigation, anger and wrath named as extreme rigor contrasted with fatherly chastisement.

Psalms 38:2

2For your arrows have sunk into me, and your hand has come down on me.

Arminian

For your arrows have sunk into me, and your hand has come down on me. Wesley: Arrows—Thy judgments outward and inward. The wound is traced to the Lord's own arrows and hand, not to blind chance.

Arminian pastoral: shared—name the pressure as his judgments, outer and inner. Bring the sunk arrows to him who shot them; do not explain the pain as mere accident.

Reformed

Calvin: he was constrained by dire necessity to ask alleviation; the malady is the arrows and the hand of God. The pronoun thy is emphatic—he has to do with God, who can discharge arrows with overwhelming force. Scarcely one in a hundred discerns the hand of God in judgments.

Reformed pastoral: shared—estimate affliction by more than raw pain. Look to the hand of him who smites; seek deliverance from the same Lord whose arrows have sunk in.

Psalms 38:3

3There is no soundness in my flesh because of your indignation; there is no health in my bones because of my sin.

Arminian

There is no soundness in my flesh because of your indignation; there is no health in my bones because of my sin. Wesley: Sin—Which hath provoked thee to deal thus severely with me. Flesh and bones fail under indignation that his own sin provoked.

Arminian pastoral: shared—join the body's ruin to the sin that stirred severity. Do not separate indignation from guilt; confess the provocation while you feel the lack of soundness.

Reformed

Calvin: as soon as he recognized affliction as from God, he turns to his own sin as the cause of divine displeasure. God is not a tyrant who exercises cruelty at random, but a righteous judge who manifests displeasure when grievously offended. Connect sins with his wrath.

Reformed pastoral: shared—give God the praise of righteousness in the rod. Refuse the charge of divine cruelty; let sin explain the severity so confession can open toward mercy.

Psalms 38:4

4For my iniquities have gone over my head; like a heavy burden, they are too heavy for me.

Arminian

For my iniquities have gone over my head; like a heavy burden, they are too heavy for me. Wesley: Iniquities—Or, the punishment of mine iniquities, as this word is frequently used. Are gone—Like deep waters wherewith I am overwhelmed. Guilt and its weight crest over him like flood and load.

Arminian pastoral: shared—own the overload without minimizing. Iniquities gone over the head are too heavy for self-management; cry for mercy under the deep waters.

Reformed

Calvin: overwhelmed by sins as by a heavy burden, he faints under their weight, yet confesses he deservedly suffered. He does not quarrel like Cain (“My punishment is greater than I can bear”); he defends himself with the shield that he is afflicted by the just judgment of God.

Reformed pastoral: shared—attribute the weight of wrath to your own sins as cause. Refuse the murmur that God is too severe; find rest only in imploring compassion and forgiveness.

Key contrast Both feel iniquities as an overwhelming load under just displeasure. Arminians typically hear Wesley: the word may mean the punishment of iniquities, and “are gone” like deep waters that overwhelm. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: the burden is owned as deserved judgment—shield against Cain-like complaint—so the only rest is divine compassion, not a charge of cruelty.

Psalms 38:5

5My wounds stink and fester because of my foolishness,

Arminian

My wounds stink and fester because of my foolishness, Wesley: Foolishness—Sin. Putrid wounds are not random medical detail; they track to foolishness named as sin.

Arminian pastoral: shared—call foolishness what it is. Do not dress guilt as a mere mistake of manners; let festering wounds drive honest naming of sin.

Reformed

Calvin: long continuance of misery pleads for alleviation; by foolishness he does not extenuate as hypocrites who plead inadvertence, but acknowledges he was out of his right mind when he obeyed fleshly lusts against God. Sin is conjoined with folly or madness—maniacal fury flying in the Maker's face.

Reformed pastoral: shared—own the madness of lust-driven disobedience. Refuse the soft plea of mere imprudence; ask mercy after long putrefying under deserved chastisement.

Key contrast Both tie festering wounds to foolishness that is real guilt. Arminians typically hear Wesley's plain gloss: Foolishness—Sin. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: not a mild excuse of inadvertence, but confession of being out of one's right mind—sin as madness and maniacal fury against God.

Psalms 38:6

6I am utterly bowed down and prostrate; all the day I go about mourning.

Arminian

I am utterly bowed down and prostrate; all the day I go about mourning. Day-long mourning matches a body and spirit already bent low under the rod.

Arminian pastoral: shared—let bowed posture be prayer, not performance. Carry the mourning to the Lord who sees the prostration; do not harden into proud uprightness under his hand.

Reformed

Calvin: bent and bowed beyond measure, this holy man was oppressed with extreme grief; he tacitly contrasts humility with the pride of many who refuse to be humbled by chastisements. Blackness of grief marks the face; there is no consolation but by laying aside stubbornness and submitting under God's mighty hand.

Reformed pastoral: shared—bow rather than brace against the rod. Ask compassion as one already abased; refuse the insensibility that feels pain yet will not humble itself.

Psalms 38:7

7For my sides are filled with burning, and there is no soundness in my flesh.

Arminian

For my sides are filled with burning, and there is no soundness in my flesh. Wesley: Disease—The disease might be some burning fever, breaking forth outwardly in carbuncles, or boils. It is true, this and the other expressions may be taken figuratively, but we should not forsake the literal sense of the words without necessity.

Arminian pastoral: shared—take the burning seriously as real bodily misery under guilt. Pray for relief of flesh that has no soundness; do not rush past literal pain into mere metaphor.

Reformed

Calvin: reins or sides filled with burning or inflammation; covered with putrid sores. He confirms extreme grief—weakened and sore broken—not as cowardice, but as evidence that sufferings were severe in the extreme.

Reformed pastoral: shared—own inflammatory misery without theatrical excess. Bring the burning sides to God; let severity of pain deepen dependence, not rebellion.

Key contrast Both read burning sides as severe bodily distress under the rod. Arminians typically hear Wesley's caution: prefer the literal sense (fever, boils) unless necessity forces figure. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin's physiological grief: inflamed reins and putrid sores proving extreme oppression that still did not break faith.

Psalms 38:8

8I am feeble and crushed; I groan because of the tumult of my heart.

Arminian

I am feeble and crushed; I groan because of the tumult of my heart. Feebleness and crushing meet an inward roar the heart cannot quiet.

Arminian pastoral: shared—groan honestly under tumult. Do not polish the prayer into calm you do not feel; bring the crushed heart's noise to the Lord.

Reformed

Calvin: uncontrollable emotions of the heart forced him to cry out; a courageous man does not ordinarily roar, so the severity must have been extreme. The roaring of the heart names inward disquiet that breaks into voice.

Reformed pastoral: shared—admit the roar without shame before God. Severe grief may force cry and complaint; aim them as humble prayer, not as charge against his justice.

Psalms 38:9

9O Lord, all my longing is before you; my sighing is not hidden from you.

Arminian

O Lord, all my longing is before you; my sighing is not hidden from you. Nothing of desire or sigh is offstage; the memorial prayer assumes he already sees.

Arminian pastoral: shared—spread longing openly rather than hide sighing. Confidence that he sees is fuel for persevering prayer under sickness and guilt.

Reformed

Calvin: either confident assurance that groanings are heard, or a declaration that he has poured all cares into God's bosom—substantially the same encouragement. Unbelief keeps minds captive; persuasion that groanings do not vanish produces alacrity in prayer. He had not howled into the air as the unbelieving do.

Reformed pastoral: shared—disburden sorrows into his bosom. Refuse both secret fretting and public howling that never reaches God; pray as one sure he is witness and hearer.

Psalms 38:10

10My heart throbs; my strength fails me, and the light of my eyes—it also has gone from me.

Arminian

My heart throbs; my strength fails me, and the light of my eyes—it also has gone from me. Heart, strength, and eyesight fail together; the penitential body is nearly spent.

Arminian pastoral: shared—name total weakness without despair of mercy. When light of the eyes is gone, still address the Lord who has all longing before him.

Reformed

Calvin: the heart turns round—agitated, not knowing what to do—yet faith holds thoughts within God's word. Strength fails as of a dead man; the light of the eyes is taken—life's energy gone. Infirmity remains even in the obedient; storms threaten a thousand deaths.

Reformed pastoral: shared—expect disquiet without abandoning the word. Restrain wandering counsel by his promises; pray from failed strength, not from pretended self-sufficiency.

Psalms 38:11

11My friends and companions stand aloof from my plague, and my nearest kin stand far off.

Arminian

My friends and companions stand aloof from my plague, and my nearest kin stand far off. Social abandonment compounds the plague; even nearest kin keep distance.

Arminian pastoral: shared—bring isolation to God when friends stand aloof. Their distance does not cancel his nearness; ask help when human offices of kindness fail.

Reformed

Calvin: friends cease the offices of humanity—from pride that despises, or cowardice that fears odium. It is a sore trial when a man with many friends is abandoned by them all. Lay forsakenness before God, whose province is to succor the betrayed.

Reformed pastoral: shared—pray the empty circle. Do not treat aloof friends as final verdict; ask the Lord to take up what pride and fear have dropped.

Psalms 38:12

12Those who seek my life lay their snares; those who seek my hurt speak of ruin and meditate treachery all day long.

Arminian

Those who seek my life lay their snares; those who seek my hurt speak of ruin and meditate treachery all day long. Wesley: Deceit—They design mischief, but cover it with fair pretences. While friends withdraw, enemies work covered snares all day.

Arminian pastoral: shared—name treachery without matching it. Fair pretences hide ruin-talk; wait on God rather than answer snare with snare.

Reformed

Calvin: enemies vigorously seek his destruction by guileful conspiracy—snares, talk of ruin, secret deceit—while friends sit still. Lay these before God from his Word: he succors the miserable, restrains the wicked, and frustrates deceitful counsels.

Reformed pastoral: shared—report the conspiracy in prayer, not in fleshly panic. Friends' cowardice and enemies' plots both drive you to the defender who alone can anticipate their designs.

Psalms 38:13

13But I am like a deaf man; I do not hear, like a mute man who does not open his mouth.

Arminian

But I am like a deaf man; I do not hear, like a mute man who does not open his mouth. Wesley: Dumb—Was silent, to testify his humiliation for his sins, and his acceptation of the punishment which he had brought upon himself.

Arminian pastoral: shared—practice penitential silence under accepted discipline. Mute endurance can mark humility; refuse the retort that would deny your guilt before God.

Reformed

Calvin: like dumb and deaf—overwhelmed by false judgments and shut from self-defense, yet also patient, trusting God as defender. Those who depend only on the world rage when they cannot avenge; the one persuaded God is defender cherishes hope in silence (cf. Romans 12:19).

Reformed pastoral: shared—give place to wrath by leaving vengeance with God. Hold peace when defense is denied; do not seize God's right to repay.

Key contrast Both praise silence under attack, yet stress different motives. Arminians typically hear Wesley: mute to testify humiliation for sins and acceptation of self-brought punishment. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: patience that trusts God as defender when shut out from reply—hope in silence that leaves vengeance with him (Romans 12:19).

Psalms 38:14

14I have become like a man who does not hear, and in whose mouth are no rebukes.

Arminian

I have become like a man who does not hear, and in whose mouth are no rebukes. The deaf-mute posture repeats: no answering rebuke leaves the mouth.

Arminian pastoral: shared—keep the mouth from retaliating rebuke. Silence can serve repentance; save speech for confession and plea to God.

Reformed

Calvin: he held peace as if already convicted and struck dumb. It is difficult, when conscious of innocence toward men, to bear unjust condemnation as if argument had failed—yet he waits the heavenly Judge.

Reformed pastoral: shared—endure misjudgment without snatching the last word. Wait the Judge; let absent rebukes mark trust, not defeated truth.

Psalms 38:15

15But for you, O LORD, do I wait; it is you, O Lord my God, who will answer.

Arminian

But for you, O LORD, do I wait; it is you, O Lord my God, who will answer. Waiting replaces self-defense; answer is expected from the Lord my God alone.

Arminian pastoral: shared—fix waiting on the one who will answer. Do not abandon hope when mute before enemies; stake the reply on God.

Reformed

Calvin: the source of patience is trusting God's grace so as to overcome worldly temptations. Hope alone frames the mind to meekness; he cherished hope by constant meditation on promises. Thou wilt answer—double defense against fainting (cf. Romans 5:4 joining patience to hope).

Reformed pastoral: shared—feed patience with promise-meditation. When delay tempts despair, set his fidelity before you and wait for the answer he will give.

Psalms 38:16

16For I said, “Only let them not rejoice over me, who boast against me when my foot slips!”

Arminian

For I said, “Only let them not rejoice over me, who boast against me when my foot slips!” Wesley: When—When I fall into any misery, they triumph in the accomplishment of their desires. The fear is their boast over a slipped foot.

Arminian pastoral: shared—pray against enemies' triumph without denying your slip. Ask that misery not become their gloating victory; keep the plea Godward.

Reformed

Calvin: if forsaken, enemies would triumph—mockery that assails faith and treats God's promises as vain. He had already tasted their proud boastings; insolence should increase hope that God will help.

Reformed pastoral: shared—treat their rejoicing as an assault on God's word, not only on your flesh. Plead that he not abandon you to their boast when the foot slips.

Psalms 38:17

17For I am ready to fall, and my pain is ever before me.

Arminian

For I am ready to fall, and my pain is ever before me. Wesley: To halt—just falling into destruction. Before me—I am constantly sensible of thy just hand, and of my sins the cause of it.

Arminian pastoral: shared—keep pain and justice in view together. Ready-to-fall urgency should deepen sense of the just hand and of sin as cause, not erase it.

Reformed

Calvin: ready to halt is metaphorical—without speedy aid, no hope of restoration; he would walk as maimed all his days. Sorrow continually before him means no moment's relaxation; the disease is incurable unless God remedies.

Reformed pastoral: shared—confess near-collapse without inventing a merely medical halt. Direct every request to God alone; aside from him you see only ruin.

Key contrast Both stress imminent falling under constant pain. Arminians typically hear Wesley: halt as just falling into destruction, with continual sense of God's just hand and sin as cause. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: metaphorical readiness to halt—not literal lifelong lameness—incurable misery unless God himself sustains and heals.

Psalms 38:18

18I confess my iniquity; I am sorry for my sin.

Arminian

I confess my iniquity; I am sorry for my sin. Wesley: Declare—To thee. Open confession and real sorrow address the Lord directly in the penitential center of the psalm.

Arminian pastoral: shared—declare iniquity to God with sorrow that is more than regret for consequences. Let confession and grief meet at his feet while enemies still flourish.

Reformed

Calvin: not so much a bare repeat of Psalm 32's confession before God for forgiveness, as bewailing his miserable condition—sin and iniquity naming afflictions that are tokens of wrath, so the world might regard him as condemned. Yet he does not deny the miseries proceed from sins; the godly, unlike the wicked, humble themselves at God's judgment seat.

Reformed pastoral: shared—confess under the rod as one reading cause from effect. Own both the wretched tokens of displeasure and the sins that procured them; appear at his bar, not in self-justifying complaint.

Key contrast Both require real dealing with iniquity under the rod. Arminians typically hear Wesley: Declare—To thee—plain confession and sorrow addressed to God. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin's careful accent: the verse bewails miserable wrath-tokens as much as it narrates Ps 32-style confession, yet still joins adversity to sin so the godly humble themselves at his judgment seat.

Psalms 38:19

19But my foes are vigorous, they are mighty, and many are those who hate me wrongfully.

Arminian

But my foes are vigorous, they are mighty, and many are those who hate me wrongfully. Wesley: Strong—Are thriving and flourishing. Wrongful hatred thrives while the penitent lies low.

Arminian pastoral: shared—name the imbalance without envy turning to sin. Vigorous foes and wrongful hate drive you to God, not to matching their strength.

Reformed

Calvin: while he lies filthy and wretched, enemies live in abundant prosperity and increase in power; they assail him wrongfully and without cause. Take care to injure no one, that God may favor your defense.

Reformed pastoral: shared—contrast their thriving with your low estate before God. Plead innocence toward them without claiming sinlessness before him; ask him to judge wrongful hate.

Psalms 38:20

20Those who render me evil for good accuse me because I follow after good.

Arminian

Those who render me evil for good accuse me because I follow after good. Evil repaid for good, and accusation aimed at the pursuit of good itself.

Arminian pastoral: shared—keep following good when accused for it. Do not answer evil-for-good with revenge; bring the injustice to the Lord.

Reformed

Calvin: more than abstaining from harm—he had done them all the good in his power; yet they hate him because he studies uprightness. Prefer the world's hatred to swerving from duty; God maintains his own cause in theirs.

Reformed pastoral: shared—count opposition for good as expected, not as proof you should quit. Hold the testimony of a good conscience and ask God to defend the upright path.

Psalms 38:21

21Do not forsake me, O LORD! O my God, be not far from me!

Arminian

Do not forsake me, O LORD! O my God, be not far from me! Forsaken by friends and pressed by foes, he asks the Lord not to match their distance.

Arminian pastoral: shared—pray against divine forsaking with urgency. When kin stand far off, plead that God not be far; keep calling him my God.

Reformed

Calvin: the sum of the prayer—forsaken of men and grievously afflicted, that God would receive and raise him. Three forms: not forsake, not be far, make haste. Saints unburden in language of fleshly feeling yet still pray in faith.

Reformed pastoral: shared—ask nearness without pretending you never feel delay. Confess the fear of distance; cling to him as the God who does not finally abandon his own.

Psalms 38:22

22Make haste to help me, O Lord, my salvation!

Arminian

Make haste to help me, O Lord, my salvation! Speed and salvation close the memorial psalm: help from the Lord who is himself salvation.

Arminian pastoral: shared—pray haste without abandoning trust. Call him my salvation while you still wait; urgency and confidence belong together.

Reformed

Calvin: make haste to come to my aid; waiting was wearisome according to the flesh, yet he does not pray in uncertainty—he calls God his salvation, a wall of defense against devices that assail faith. Whatever happens, he is assured of salvation in God.

Reformed pastoral: shared—urge haste while naming him salvation. Let weariness speak, but set assurance of salvation in God as the wall that holds when help seems slow.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on memorial penitence under anger and wrath, arrows and hand of judgment, flesh without soundness because of sin, iniquities as overwhelming burden, wounds from foolishness, bowed mourning and burning sides, longing and sighing open before the Lord, failing strength and light, friends aloof and kin far off, snares and treachery met with deaf-mute patience, waiting for the God who answers, confession and sorrow amid wrongful foes, and the closing plea not to be forsaken but helped in haste by the Lord our salvation—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 38:1–22.

Arminian Notable voices

“To bring to remembrance—Either to God, that by this humble and mournful prayer he might prevail with God to remember and pity him; or to himself, that by reviewing this psalm afterwards he might call to mind his former danger and misery, and God's wonderful mercy in delivering him. Arrows—Thy judgments outward and inward. Sin—Which hath provoked thee to deal thus severely with me. Iniquities—Or, the punishment of mine iniquities; Are gone—Like deep waters wherewith I am overwhelmed. Foolishness—Sin. Disease—The disease might be some burning fever, breaking forth outwardly in carbuncles, or boils; we should not forsake the literal sense without necessity. Deceit—They design mischief, but cover it with fair pretences. Dumb—Was silent, to testify his humiliation for his sins, and his acceptation of the punishment which he had brought upon himself. When—When I fall into any misery, they triumph in the accomplishment of their desires. To halt—just falling into destruction. Before me—I am constantly sensible of thy just hand, and of my sins the cause of it. Declare—To thee. Strong—Are thriving and flourishing.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament, Psalm 38

Reformed Notable voices

“O Jehovah, rebuke me not in thy wrath—he does not ask that afflictions be removed, but that God moderate the severity of chastisements; content with mitigation, anger and wrath denoting extreme rigor contrasted with fatherly chastisement. Thy arrows and hand—necessity drives the plea; the pronoun thy is emphatic; he views the hand of God punishing sin. No soundness because of anger; connect sins with his wrath—God is righteous judge, not a tyrant at random. Iniquities as a heavy burden—deservedly suffered; not Cain's quarrel, but the shield of just judgment. Wounds putrid from foolishness—not extenuation by inadvertence, but confession he was out of his right mind, sin as madness against the Maker. Bowed down—humility against stubborn pride; no consolation but submitting under God's mighty hand. Longing and groaning before God—disburdened into his bosom, not howled into the air. Friends stand away from the sore; enemies lay snares while friends sit still—lay both before God. Deaf and dumb—shut from defense, yet patient, trusting God as defender; give place unto wrath. On thee I wait; thou wilt answer—hope sources patience. Ready to halt—metaphorical near-ruin without remedy from God. I declare my iniquity—bewailing miserable wrath-tokens joined to sin, humbling at his judgment seat. Forsake me not; make haste—he calls God his salvation, wall of defense though waiting wearies the flesh.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms, Psalm 38
