Passage

Psalms 53

Verses 1–6 · ESV · Voices

To the choirmaster: according to Mahalath. A Maskil of David. Near twin to Psalm 14: the fool denies God; all are corrupt; God looks for seekers and finds none who does good; oppressors devour the people; terror falls where none seemed due; Zion’s salvation is longed for.

Psalms 53:1

1The fool says in his heart, “There is no God.” They are corrupt, doing abominable iniquity; there is none who does good.

Arminian

The fool says in his heart, There is no God—corrupt, abominable iniquity—none who does good. Heart-atheism is practical: living as if God were absent. Corruption and abominable deeds follow the denial.

Arminian pastoral: the fool’s verdict is chosen in the heart and issues in deeds. Grace confronts that denial; the hearer is summoned to seek God rather than settle into “none who does good” as an excuse.

Reformed

Calvin (on this text via Ps 14): wickedness is so great men will not repent unless softened by the fear of God. “None who does good” names the race apart from God’s people—corruption is native and universal, not a thin smear on otherwise sound seekers.

Reformed accent: the fool’s heart-speech exposes depravity only grace can break. Shared: no God in the heart yields abominable life.

Key contrast Both confess heart-denial of God tied to real corruption and “none who does good.” Arminians typically stress the fool’s responsible heart-choice and the call still to seek God; Reformed readers typically stress universal native corruption that only fear of God and regenerating grace interrupt.

Psalms 53:2

2God looks down from heaven on the children of man to see if there are any who understand, who seek after God.

Arminian

God looks down to see if any understand, who seek after God. Divine search for seekers—understanding shown in pursuit of God.

Arminian reading: seeking is the mark God looks for. The verse honors real search; refusing to seek is the tragedy the next verse names.

Reformed

Calvin: when the Spirit inspires desire to seek God, then understanding begins. God’s looking down exposes that apart from that work, the children of men do not seek.

Reformed accent: seeking is gift before it is achievement. Shared: God himself inspects whether anyone seeks.

Psalms 53:3

3They have all fallen away; together they have become corrupt; there is none who does good, not even one.

Arminian

All fallen away; together corrupt; none who does good, not even one. Total in scope among those surveyed—no lone hero of goodness outside grace.

Arminian note: the indictment drives everyone to mercy. “Not even one” removes boasting; it does not remove the duty to repent when grace calls.

Reformed

Calvin presses the universality: every one gone back, together filthy. Paul cites this line for the doctrine that all are under sin. Apart from the generation of the righteous, mankind is not partially sound.

Reformed accent: solidarity in corruption magnifies need of sovereign mercy. Shared: not even one does good by nature.

Key contrast Both confess a sweeping “not even one.” Arminians typically hear a leveling that drives all to resistible grace and repentance; Reformed readers typically hear Pauline total inability apart from regenerating grace.

Psalms 53:4

4Have those who work evil no knowledge, who eat up my people as they eat bread, and do not call upon God?

Arminian

Workers of evil—no knowledge?—eat up my people as bread—do not call upon God. Oppression is casual as a meal; prayerlessness marks them.

Arminian pastoral: devouring the people and skipping prayer go together. Knowledge that should restrain is refused; call upon God instead of consuming the flock.

Reformed

Calvin: God speaks in astonishment—those who ought to cherish his people fall on them like beasts. They have no more scruple than eating a morsel of bread. Sound understanding would restrain such cruelty; they do not call on the Lord.

Reformed note: monstrous iniquity against the saints provokes heaven. Shared: eating the people and prayerlessness are one disease.

Psalms 53:5

5There they are, in great terror, where there is no terror! For God scatters the bones of him who encamps against you; you put them to shame, for God has rejected them.

Arminian

Great terror where no terror was; God scatters bones of the besieger; shame—God has rejected them. Wesley: Scattered—broken their strength and dispersed them with no hope of restoration. Thee—against my people. Thou—Jerusalem under siege.

Arminian comfort: God can overturn secure oppressors. Rejection is God’s verdict on those who encamped against his people.

Reformed

Calvin: encourages the faithful—God will not forsake his people. Terror falls suddenly when the wicked think affairs most tranquil; God launches vengeance because he is with the generation of the righteous. Scattering bones of the besieger is deliverance as when a siege is broken.

Reformed accent: sudden judgment is providence for Zion’s defense. Shared: shame follows God’s rejection of the encamped foe.

Psalms 53:6

6Oh, that salvation for Israel would come out of Zion! When God restores the fortunes of his people, let Jacob rejoice, let Israel be glad.

Arminian

Oh that salvation would come out of Zion!—when God restores fortunes, Jacob rejoice, Israel glad. Longing prayer for national deliverance and joy.

Arminian pastoral: keep asking for Zion’s salvation. Restoration is God’s act; rejoicing is the people’s fitting response when fortunes turn.

Reformed

Calvin: after consolation, return to prayer and groanings—do not weary though God leave us to languish. “Who shall give salvation?” is not despair of God’s hand. He will not suffer the faithful under continual sorrow; they that sow in tears shall reap in joy.

Reformed hope: delay does not cancel Zion’s promised gladness. Shared: salvation out of Zion ends in Jacob’s joy.

Key contrast Both long for salvation out of Zion and joy at restored fortunes. Arminians typically stress persistent petition and glad response when God restores; Reformed readers typically stress perseverance in prayer under delay, assured that God will not leave his captive people in endless sorrow.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the fool’s denial, universal corruption, and salvation out of Zion—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 53.

Arminian Notable voices

“Scattered—Hath not only broken their bones, their strength, and force, but also dispersed them hither and thither, so as there is no hope of a restoration. Thee—Against my people. Thou—Thou oh Jerusalem, which they besiege. The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God. Corrupt are they, and have done abominable iniquity: there is none that doeth good.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 53)

Reformed Notable voices

“The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God: they have become corrupt, they have done abominable works: there is none that doeth good… David… teaches that the wickedness of men is so great, that they will never be brought to repentance, unless they are softened by the fear of God… When the Spirit of God inspires men with a desire to seek after God, it is then that they begin to understand… There did they tremble with fear… The prophet now encourages himself and all the faithful… that God will not forsake his people even to the end… when their affairs are in a state of the greatest tranquillity and prosperity, God will suddenly launch against them the bolts of his vengeance… Who shall give salvation to Israel out of Sion? … although God may leave us for a long time to languish, yet we ought not to weary… When the Lord shall have brought back the captivity of his people… God will not suffer the faithful to languish under continual sorrow.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 14 / of Ps 53)
