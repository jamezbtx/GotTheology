Passage

Psalms 130

Verses 1–8 · ESV · Voices

A Song of Ascents from the depths (De profundis): the cry for attentive mercy, the confession that none could stand if the LORD marked iniquities, and the wonder that with Him there is forgiveness—that He may be feared. Waiting and hoping in His word outpace watchmen for morning; Israel is called to hope, for with the LORD is steadfast love and plentiful redemption from all iniquities.

Psalms 130:1

1Out of the depths I cry to you, O LORD!

Arminian

Out of the depths—a cry to the LORD. Depth does not silence prayer; it drives the voice upward to the only Helper.

Classical Arminians welcome this honest cry. From whatever gulf you are in, you may call on Him; the invitation to seek mercy is real even when the heart feels submerged.

Reformed

Out of the depths I cry to You, O LORD. The gulf itself becomes the place from which faith learns to pray.

Reformed comfort: adversity that should quicken us often stupefies—yet God still hears the cry He stirs. Depths do not bar the throne of grace He opens to His own.

Psalms 130:2

2O Lord, hear my voice! Let your ears be attentive to the voice of my pleas for mercy!

Arminian

Hear my voice; let ears be attentive to pleas for mercy. The doubled ask is urgent petition, not empty noise.

Arminian pastoral: keep pleading for mercy. God sincerely invites the cry; attentiveness is what the sinner seeks and may ask with confidence in His kindness.

Reformed

O Lord, hear; let Your ears attend to mercy’s pleas. Cry and repetition mark earnest desire before His majesty.

Reformed accent: prayer from the depths is God’s appointed means. He bears with our urgency; the plea for mercy rests on His character, not on our eloquence.

Psalms 130:3

3If you, O LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand?

Arminian

If You marked iniquities, who could stand at Your tribunal? Accurate justice would leave none upright before Him.

Classical Arminians hear a leveling confession: every sinner needs mercy. The question humbles pride so that forgiveness, not self-defense, becomes the only hope.

Reformed

If the LORD marked iniquities, who could stand? Strict law-accounting would condemn the whole race of Adam.

Reformed note: even the holiest must pass under this sentence and flee to mercy alone. No partial righteousness of works can stand God’s tribunal.

Key contrast Both confess that none could stand if God marked iniquities. Arminians typically stress a humbling that drives every sinner to seek offered mercy; Reformed readers typically stress total inability to stand by works, so righteousness before God is only by pardon.

Psalms 130:4

4But with you there is forgiveness, that you may be feared.

Arminian

With You there is forgiveness—that You may be feared with childlike, not slavish, fear. Mercy founds true religion; without it men rush on in despairing impiety.

Arminian reading: God is able and ready to forgive repenting sinners. Forgiveness welcomed produces holy fear and willing service, not presumption or dread that never comes.

Reformed

With You is forgiveness, that You may be feared. Mercy inseparable from God’s nature raises fallen hearts to pray and serve.

Reformed accent: free remission is the first step of right worship. Sense of judgment without hope of pardon breeds hatred; grace known makes cheerful obedience possible.

Key contrast Both teach that forgiveness with God produces true fear of Him. Arminians typically emphasize readiness to forgive repenting sinners, yielding childlike fear; Reformed readers typically emphasize free remission as God’s gift that alone creates willing, non-slavish service.

Psalms 130:5

5I wait for the LORD, my soul waits, and in his word I hope;

Arminian

I wait; my soul waits; in His word I hope—waiting aimed at pardon. Hope clings to what God has spoken, not to mood alone.

Classical Arminians urge patient, soul-deep waiting. Keep hoping in the promise; delay is not denial of a God who invites trust in His word.

Reformed

I wait for the LORD—soul waits—and hope in His word. Patience is framed by confidence in divine promises.

Reformed pastoral: without the word, grace would vanish from sight. Genuine hope is content with God’s speech and thereby learns to wait.

Key contrast Both commend soul-waiting and hope in God’s word. Arminians typically stress persevering trust while pardon is sought; Reformed readers typically stress that the word alone sustains waiting as God-ordained means.

Psalms 130:6

6my soul waits for the Lord more than watchmen for the morning, more than watchmen for the morning.

Arminian

Soul waits for the Lord more than watchmen for morning—twice said. Eager, repeated longing outruns the night watch’s hunger for dawn.

Arminian pastoral: let desire for God surpass the soldier’s or temple watcher’s craving for day. Persist in the wait; morning is worth the watch.

Reformed

More than watchmen for the morning—repeated for perseverance. Ardor and continuance mark the soul’s gaze on the Lord.

Reformed comfort: do not let delay languish fervor. God may keep us in suspense; still the appointed posture is watchful waiting fixed on Him.

Psalms 130:7

7O Israel, hope in the LORD! For with the LORD there is steadfast love, and with him is plentiful redemption.

Arminian

Hope, O Israel—for with the LORD is steadfast love and plentiful redemption, abundantly sufficient for all who accept it on God’s terms.

Classical Arminians press the corporate call: every true Israelite may hope. Plenteous redemption is sincerely offered; answer with trust, not with despairing scarcity talk.

Reformed

Hope in the LORD—steadfast love with Him, and plentiful redemption. Mercy is the fountain; redemption flows from it in many means.

Reformed accent: hope is regulated by free goodness, not by merits. Even in deep affliction, God’s hand holds multiplied ways to save His people.

Key contrast Both call Israel to hope because steadfast love and plentiful redemption are with the LORD. Arminians typically stress sufficiency for all who accept on God’s terms; Reformed readers typically stress mercy as the sole fountain of a redemption God plentifully applies to His people.

Psalms 130:8

8And he will redeem Israel from all his iniquities.

Arminian

He will redeem Israel from all his iniquities. Full cleansing—not partial—is the hope held out to the waiting people.

Arminian pastoral: bring all sins to the Redeemer. The promise covers the whole account; do not hold back iniquities as if His redemption were thin.

Reformed

He will redeem Israel from all his iniquities. Remission goes first; true deliverance is God blotting out sin in mercy.

Reformed comfort: “all” leaves no iniquity outside His redeeming reach for His people. Temporal relief without pardon is not the full salvation this verse names.

Key contrast Both hear full redemption from all iniquities. Arminians typically emphasize coming with every sin to a Redeemer whose promise is wide enough; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s certain blotting out of His people’s sins as the heart of deliverance.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on crying from the depths, forgiveness that fosters fear, waiting on the LORD’s word, and plentiful redemption—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 130:1–8.

Arminian Notable voices

“Mark—Observe them accurately and punish them as they deserve. Stand—At thy tribunal. Forgiveness—Thou art able and ready to forgive repenting sinners. Feared—Not with a slavish, but with a childlike fear. This mercy of thine is the foundation of all religion, without which men would desperately proceed in their impious courses. I wait—That he would pardon my sins. They—Whether soldiers that keep the night-watches in an army, or the priests or Levites who did so in the temple. Israel—Every true Israelite. Plenteous—Abundantly sufficient for all persons who accept it upon God's terms.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 130)

Reformed Notable voices

“Out of the deep places have I cried to thee, O Jehovah! The Prophet speaks as sending forth his voice from out of a deep gulf, overwhelmed with calamities, yet deriving confidence in coming to the throne of grace from the very troubles into which he was plunged. If thou shouldst mark iniquities, O Lord, who shall stand? Should God deal with us according to the strict demands of his law, not one of the whole human race would be able to stand. All the children of Adam, from first to last, are lost and condemned should God require them to render up an account of their life. Since no man can stand by his own works, all such as are accounted righteous before God are righteous in consequence of the pardon and remission of their sins. But with thee there is forgiveness, that thou mayest be feared. Faith raises hearts cast down with fear and encourages them to pray for forgiveness. God’s mercy cannot be torn away from himself; the very fact that thou art God is a sure guarantee that thou wilt be merciful. Whoever would reap profit from prayer must begin with free remission of sins. Men never serve God aright unless they know that he is gracious and merciful. I have waited for Jehovah—even with the deepest affections of the heart; what supported his patience was confidence in the divine promises. Let Israel hope in Jehovah: the foundation of hope is the mercy of God, the source from which redemption springs—and this redemption is plenteous. He shall redeem Israel from all his iniquities: remission of sins always goes first; true deliverance is when God, by blotting out our sins, shows himself merciful.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 130)
