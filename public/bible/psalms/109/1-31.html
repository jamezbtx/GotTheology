Passage

Psalms 109

Verses 1–31 · ESV · Voices

To the choirmaster. A Psalm of David. An imprecatory lament: the psalmist begs the God of his praise not to be silent while deceitful mouths and causeless hate repay his love with accusation; he gives himself to prayer, then pleads for judicial reciprocity against a cruel accuser—guilt in trial, short days, lost office, cascading household ruin—because the enemy would not show kindness and loved to curse; he turns to ask God to deal for His name’s sake and good steadfast love, confesses poverty and scorn, cries for help that enemies may know it is the LORD’s hand, and ends in thanks, for God stands at the right hand of the needy to save him from those who condemn his soul to death.

Psalms 109:1

1Be not silent, O God of my praise!

Arminian

Be not silent, O God of my praise. Wesley: God—the author and matter of all my praises. Silence from God would leave the calumniated without a vindicator.

Arminian pastoral: both camps take the cry seriously. The psalmist asks the God he praises to speak. Imprecation later sits inside this appeal to the God of praise—not outside mercy’s frame.

Reformed

Calvin: O God of my praise—David entrusts to God the vindication of his innocence when calumny is rampant. Be not silent: when the wicked asperse, it would be improper for the witness of innocence to remain silent. He represents Christ and the church under unjust hatred.

Reformed accent: shared—appeal to God alone as defender. The imprecatory psalm begins with praise’s God, not with private spite.

Psalms 109:2

2For wicked and deceitful mouths are opened against me, speaking against me with lying tongues.

Arminian

Wicked and deceitful mouths opened; lying tongues. The assault is verbal poison—fraud dressed as charge.

Arminian hearing: shared lament—lies wound. The righteous may be falsely accused; the answer is still prayer to God, not matching deceit.

Reformed

Calvin: the mouth of the wicked and of deceit are opened; false tongues surround him so that the ignorant may think the charge plausible.

Reformed pastoral: Satan heaps calumny to tarnish the godly. Shared—take lying tongues as real warfare; flee to God, not to counter-lies.

Psalms 109:3

3They encircle me with words of hate, and attack me without cause.

Arminian

Words of hate encircle; attack without cause. Wesley’s frame: love repaid with enmity.

Arminian pastoral: causeless hatred is named honestly. Both camps refuse to excuse the attackers or to pretend the hurt is unreal.

Reformed

Calvin: encompassed with words of hatred, fought without cause—harder to endure than a great army. What was prefigured in David is fulfilled in Christ; believers fill up what remains of His afflictions.

Reformed accent: shared suffering with Christ under causeless hate. The church is not surprised by verbal siege.

Psalms 109:4

4In return for my love they accuse me, but I give myself to prayer.

Arminian

In return for love they accuse; but I give myself to prayer. Wesley: Adversaries—they requite my love with enmity.

Arminian pastoral: love met by accusation, answered by prayer. Shared—both camps honor prayer instead of revenge as the first move. Classical Arminians hear a real choice to pray when accused; grace enables, the will still turns to God.

Reformed

Calvin: on account of my love they opposed me; but I gave myself to prayer—not rendering evil for evil, but committing himself into God’s hand. The Spirit restrains so that the godly betake themselves by prayer to God alone.

Reformed accent: prayer is the Spirit’s restraint from revenge. Shared—love repaid with hate is answered at God’s tribunal.

Key contrast Both refuse revenge as the first answer to false accusation and both honor prayer. Arminians typically stress the living turn to prayer when love is repaid with hate. Reformed readers typically stress Calvin’s point that the Spirit restrains the godly so they commit the case to God rather than seize fraudulent means.

Psalms 109:5

5So they reward me evil for good, and hatred for my love.

Arminian

Evil for good; hatred for love. The moral inversion is complete—good answered by evil.

Arminian pastoral: shared outrage at injustice. The psalm will ask for judicial answer; it does not deny the goodness that was shown.

Reformed

Calvin: they rendered evil for good and hatred for love. Kindness to enemies makes their hate the more unnatural; Satan’s captivity of hearts is awful.

Reformed pastoral: shared—document the wrong before God. The record of good for evil prepares the later appeal for just reward.

Psalms 109:6

6Appoint a wicked man against him; let an accuser stand at his right hand.

Arminian

Appoint a wicked man against him; let an accuser stand at his right hand. Wesley: A wicked man—who will rule him with rigour; Satan—to accuse him, as accusers stood in Jewish courts.

Arminian pastoral: imprecation begins—read as plea for judicial reciprocity under God, not as a license for personal vendetta. Classical Arminians take the ask seriously: the accuser should meet an accuser, while believers do not turn the psalm into a charm of hate.

Reformed

Calvin: set a wicked person over him; let the adversary stand at his right hand. David is not driven by carnal spite, selfish interest, or zeal without knowledge; he speaks as God’s messenger under the Spirit. Believers must leave room for grace—today’s enemy may become tomorrow’s friend—yet the finally cruel deserve just judgment.

Reformed accent: weigh the three guards Calvin names. Imprecation is serious; it is neither rented cursing nor a shrug that God never judges.

Key contrast Both take the imprecation as serious appeal for just judgment, not as private spite. Arminians typically stress Wesley’s court imagery—rigorous reckoning for the cruel accuser—while refusing to live by curse. Reformed readers typically stress Calvin’s guards: not selfish revenge, not carnal zeal, yet real punishment for the obstinately cruel, with room left for God’s grace to convert.

Psalms 109:7

7When he is tried, let him come forth guilty; let his prayer be counted as sin!

Arminian

When tried, let him come forth guilty; let his prayer be counted as sin. Wesley: Sin—because it is not from his heart.

Arminian hearing: empty prayer from an unrepentant mouth does not avail. Shared—both camps know God is not mocked by religious words covering cruelty.

Reformed

Calvin: when judged, let him depart guilty; let his prayer be turned into sin—either before earthly judges who disregard sham pleas, or before God to whom the wicked’s sacrifices are abomination.

Reformed accent: hypocritical prayer aggravates guilt. Shared refusal to treat pious talk as cover for oppression.

Psalms 109:8

8May his days be few; may another take his office!

Arminian

May his days be few; may another take his office. Cited in Acts 1:20 of Judas.

Arminian pastoral: shared—office can be stripped; life is not a blank check for the cruel. Acts reads this line of the betrayer. Both camps receive the apostle’s use.

Reformed

Calvin: brevity of days as mark of disapprobation; another receives his office. Peter applies this to Judas (Acts 1:20). Temporary evils may touch the godly too, yet God sometimes displays marked judgment on the ungodly.

Reformed pastoral: shared apostolic reading toward Judas. The line comforts the church that lofty office without piety will fall.

Psalms 109:9

9May his children be fatherless and his wife a widow!

Arminian

May his children be fatherless and his wife a widow. Heavy words—consequences cascading through a household.

Arminian pastoral: both camps feel the weight. The psalm targets a persecutor whose cruelty deserves covenant sanctions, not a call for believers to harm families. Classical Arminians stress that such pleas are surrendered to God’s justice, not executed by private hands.

Reformed

Calvin: the malediction extends to wife and children, depending on the brevity of life already named; Scripture sometimes visits iniquity across generations while never licensing personal cruelty.

Reformed accent: leave the severity with God the Judge. Do not soften the text into sentiment, and do not weaponize it for spite.

Psalms 109:10

10May his children wander about and beg, seeking food far from the ruins they inhabit!

Arminian

Children wander and beg from ruins. Wesley: Desolate places—into which they fled for fear and shame.

Arminian hearing: the picture is desolation after judgment. Shared gravity—poverty here is judicial outcome in the psalm’s plea, not a program for the church to inflict.

Reformed

Calvin: children wander without settled habitation, beggars seeking food from waste places. The severity matches the magnitude of guilt against the poor and brokenhearted named later.

Reformed pastoral: shared—read the cascade as prophetic justice language, then practice mercy toward actual orphans you meet.

Psalms 109:11

11May the creditor seize all that he has; may strangers plunder the fruits of his toil!

Arminian

Creditor seize all; strangers plunder his toil. Wesley: Catch—ensnare, by oppression and cunning; stranger—no right to his goods.

Arminian pastoral: wealth got or kept in cruelty is not safe. Shared—economic undoing is part of the pleaded judgment.

Reformed

Calvin: the extortioner seizes all; strangers spoil his labor—poverty overtakes children of one who amassed by wrong.

Reformed accent: God can strip unrighteous gain. Shared warning to oppressors who trust their estate.

Psalms 109:12

12Let there be none to extend kindness to him, nor any to pity his fatherless children!

Arminian

None to extend kindness; none to pity his fatherless children.

Arminian pastoral: the plea asks that the merciless find no mercy—measure for measure. Both camps teach Christians to show kindness widely; this verse is judicial speech about a particular cruel man, not a blank ethic of hardness.

Reformed

Calvin: none prolonging mercy to him or pitying his fatherless—those who were unmerciful justly find hard hearts around them (cf. Deut). David speaks as God’s messenger, not from private anguish alone.

Reformed accent: hardening of others’ pity can be judgment. Shared—do not confuse this plea with a command to withhold mercy from the needy you can help.

Psalms 109:13

13May his posterity be cut off; may his name be blotted out in the second generation!

Arminian

Posterity cut off; name blotted in the second generation.

Arminian hearing: extinction of the name is ultimate earthly judgment. Both camps take the severity seriously—neither soft on evil nor eager for blood.

Reformed

Calvin: posterity cut off; name effaced in the next generation—ruin so complete that the house does not rise again. Heavier than brief affliction.

Reformed pastoral: tremble at severity; improve it as reverence, not as a script for personal feuds.

Psalms 109:14

14May the iniquity of his fathers be remembered before the LORD, and let not the sin of his mother be blotted out!

Arminian

Iniquity of fathers remembered; mother’s sin not blotted out.

Arminian pastoral: generational language is biblical and weighty. Personal responsibility (Ezek 18) stands together with corporate patterns of sin. Both camps confess God just.

Reformed

Calvin: remember the iniquity of fathers and mothers so condemnation is complete—punishing to the third and fourth while mercy runs to a thousand for those who love Him. God prepares vessels; we must not measure His secret judgments by finite minds.

Reformed accent: severity that fills with fear. Shared—God is just; sons are not punished as innocent props of a parent’s crime when they themselves walk the same path.

Psalms 109:15

15Let them be before the LORD continually, that he may cut off the memory of them from the earth!

Arminian

Let them be before the LORD continually; cut off their memory from the earth.

Arminian pastoral: memory cut off is the opposite of the righteous remembrance in Psalm 112. Shared—God’s court keeps the record until judgment lands.

Reformed

Calvin: let sins stay before Jehovah continually; cut off their memorial from the earth. Vengeance made manifest so the world acquiesces in God’s equity.

Reformed pastoral: shared desire that justice be public enough to teach the earth, not merely private satisfaction.

Psalms 109:16

16For he did not remember to show kindness, but pursued the poor and needy and the brokenhearted, to put them to death.

Arminian

For he did not remember kindness, but pursued the poor, needy, and brokenhearted to put them to death. The reason clause—cruelty toward the helpless.

Arminian pastoral: both camps agree this grounds the imprecations. Hate of the brokenhearted is not a small fault. Neither tradition is indifferent to the poor.

Reformed

Calvin: he forgot to show mercy; persecuted the afflicted and poor and sorrowful in heart to slay him. Cruelty against the helpless is cowardly; the Spirit denounces heavy punishment for it.

Reformed accent: pray for all who trouble you, desire salvation of all, yet freely appeal for God to cut off the finally impenitent. Shared—protect the poor; do not romanticize the oppressor.

Psalms 109:17

17He loved to curse; let curses come upon him! He did not delight in blessing; may it be far from him!

Arminian

He loved to curse; let curses come upon him! He did not delight in blessing; may it be far. Wesley: Delighted not—in desiring and promoting others’ welfare.

Arminian hearing: what he loved returns. Shared—cursing as habit invites curse; blessing refused is far from him.

Reformed

Calvin: as he loved cursing, let it come; as he did not delight in blessing, let it be far. Machinations recoil on the head that loved mischief.

Reformed pastoral: shared—love of curse summons its own harvest under God.

Psalms 109:18

18He clothed himself with cursing as his coat; may it soak into his body like water, like oil into his bones!

Arminian

Clothed with cursing as a coat; soak like water and oil into bones. Wesley: Garment—worn constantly; water in the belly—near death; oil in bones—destroys.

Arminian pastoral: curse as clothing becomes inward poison. Shared vividness—do not sanitize the imagery or use it as a spell.

Reformed

Calvin: clothed with cursing as a garment; let it come as water into bowels and oil into bones—cleaving, enveloping, penetrating.

Reformed accent: the wicked man’s chosen coat becomes his judgment. Shared—read as prophetic justice, practice blessing with your own tongue.

Psalms 109:19

19May it be like a garment that he wraps around him, like a belt that he puts on every day!

Arminian

Like a garment he wraps; like a belt every day.

Arminian hearing: daily, habitual curse-identity. Shared—sin worn as uniform is not a moment’s slip.

Reformed

Calvin: mantle and girdle continually—cursing as constant dress.

Reformed pastoral: habit deepens judgment. Shared warning against making malice one’s everyday belt.

Psalms 109:20

20May this be the reward of my accusers from the LORD, of those who speak evil against my life!

Arminian

May this be the reward of my accusers from the LORD, of those who speak evil against my life. Wesley: That speak evil against my soul—with design to take away my life.

Arminian pastoral: the reward is from the LORD—not from the psalmist’s knife. Shared handover of vengeance to God (cf. Rom 12). God does pay wages of sin; discernment still guards against rash personal malice.

Reformed

Calvin: let this be the work/reward from Jehovah of those hostile to me. David did not utter curses unadvisedly but as the Spirit dictated; he depends on God alone when the world frowns.

Reformed accent: reward from the LORD. Shared—accusers who seek a life are left to God’s payday.

Key contrast Both locate the reward with the LORD, not with private retaliation. Arminians typically stress handing the case to God’s justice while refusing to live by curse. Reformed readers typically stress Spirit-guided imprecation against the reprobate persecutor, still warning against rash personal malice.

Psalms 109:21

21But you, O GOD my Lord, deal on my behalf for your name’s sake; because your steadfast love is good, deliver me!

Arminian

But you, O GOD my Lord, deal on my behalf for your name’s sake; because your steadfast love is good, deliver me. Wesley: Is good—above the mercy of all the creatures.

Arminian pastoral: the turn of the psalm—from imprecation to plea on the name and hesed. Shared center: deliverance for God’s name and because mercy is good, not because the speaker is flawless.

Reformed

Calvin: undertake for me for thy name’s sake; deliver because thy mercy is good. David does not plead merit; free grace and mercy are the ground. Even Christ’s human nature depended on the Father’s good pleasure; how much more we.

Reformed accent: name and mercy, not desert. Shared gospel logic inside an imprecatory psalm.

Psalms 109:22

22For I am poor and needy, and my heart is stricken within me.

Arminian

Poor and needy; heart stricken within.

Arminian pastoral: honesty about weakness. Shared—poverty of spirit and stricken heart are the posture that seeks deliverance.

Reformed

Calvin: poor and needy; heart wounded within. Outward affliction without humility is useless; God is physician to those who send up sighs, not to the hardened.

Reformed pastoral: shared—publish your poverty before God; do not hide the stricken heart.

Psalms 109:23

23I am gone like a shadow at evening; I am shaken off like a locust.

Arminian

Gone like a shadow at evening; shaken off like a locust.

Arminian hearing: frailty confessed. Shared—life feels brief and driven; that drives prayer, not despair alone.

Reformed

Calvin: like a shadow when it declines; tossed as the locust—no settled place, incessant persecution.

Reformed accent: the church’s unsettled path under trial. Shared comfort that God knows the shadow’s length.

Psalms 109:24

24My knees are weak through fasting; my body has become gaunt, with no fat.

Arminian

Knees weak through fasting; body gaunt.

Arminian pastoral: embodied grief and discipline. Shared—fasting and gauntness can mark earnest plea, not theatrical holiness.

Reformed

Calvin: knees feeble through fasting; flesh fails of fatness—sorrow and abstinence, or want in flight.

Reformed pastoral: shared realism about the body’s share in lament.

Psalms 109:25

25I am an object of scorn to my accusers; when they see me, they wag their heads.

Arminian

Object of scorn; they wag their heads.

Arminian hearing: public shame. Shared link to other passion psalms—scorn does not prove God has abandoned the cause.

Reformed

Calvin: became a reproach; they shake the head—insolence toward the afflicted and toward faith itself.

Reformed accent: scorn as temptation; hold the warnings of the law without concluding you are cast off forever.

Psalms 109:26

26Help me, O LORD my God! Save me according to your steadfast love!

Arminian

Help me, O LORD my God! Save me according to your steadfast love!

Arminian pastoral: bare cry—help and save by hesed. Shared—steadfast love is the measure of salvation asked.

Reformed

Calvin: help me; save according to thy mercy—repeat the prayer under delay and slander. Desire deliverance that manifests God’s glory, not mere self-safety.

Reformed accent: mercy as the rule of help. Shared insistence that God’s hand be seen.

Psalms 109:27

27Let them know that this is your hand; you, O LORD, have done it!

Arminian

Let them know this is your hand; you, O LORD, have done it!

Arminian pastoral: public theodicy—enemies must recognize God’s act. Shared—salvation that silences boast in human rescue.

Reformed

Calvin: they shall know this is thy hand—special display so the ungodly cannot open their mouths. Point to God’s hand only if your own mind has recognized it.

Reformed pastoral: shared—glory of God in deliverance outranks private relief alone.

Psalms 109:28

28Let them curse, but you will bless! They arise and are put to shame, but your servant will be glad!

Arminian

Let them curse, but you will bless! They arise and are put to shame; your servant will be glad.

Arminian pastoral: curse versus blessing—God’s blessing outweighs human curse. Shared confidence for the servant who stays under mercy.

Reformed

Calvin: they shall curse, but thou shalt bless; when they arise they shall be ashamed; thy servant shall rejoice. Relying on grace, David sets their malice at nought.

Reformed accent: blessing from God nullifies curse from men. Shared joy of the servant under that blessing.

Psalms 109:29

29May my accusers be clothed with dishonor; may they be wrapped in their own shame as in a cloak!

Arminian

Accusers clothed with dishonor; wrapped in their own shame as a cloak.

Arminian hearing: shame as garment mirrors the curse-coat of v. 18. Shared poetic justice—dishonor fits those who sought another’s death.

Reformed

Calvin: adversaries clothed with shame and covered with confusion as a garment.

Reformed pastoral: shared—look beyond present lofty airs of the ungodly to the shame God will wrap them in.

Psalms 109:30

30With my mouth I will give great thanks to the LORD; I will praise him in the midst of the throng.

Arminian

Great thanks with my mouth; praise in the midst of the throng.

Arminian pastoral: vowed public praise after deliverance. Shared—thanksgiving belongs in the assembly, not only in private relief.

Reformed

Calvin: praise Jehovah greatly with the mouth in the assembly of the great/many—public acknowledgment from the heart’s bottom.

Reformed accent: tongue united with heart for mutual edification. Shared return of praise to the throng.

Psalms 109:31

31For he stands at the right hand of the needy one, to save him from those who condemn his soul to death.

Arminian

For he stands at the right hand of the needy one, to save him from those who condemn his soul to death.

Arminian pastoral: climax—God at the needy’s right hand (contrast the accuser at the wicked’s right hand in v. 6). Shared gospel shape: the needy are saved from soul-condemners. Both camps end in comfort for the poor in spirit, not in celebration of cruelty.

Reformed

Calvin: God stands at the right hand of the poor to deliver his soul from condemnations—present even when He seemed far. Judges of his life were formidable; God still saved.

Reformed comfort: the needy one’s advocate is God Himself. Shared—imprecation’s end is salvation of the condemned innocent, not a hobby of hate.

Key contrast Both end with God at the needy one’s right hand to save from death-dealing condemnation—the structural answer to the accuser at the wicked’s right hand (v. 6). Arminians typically stress the open comfort for every needy one who cries to this God of praise and mercy. Reformed readers typically stress God’s sure defense of His afflicted poor against condemning powers, the same God who justly answers the finally cruel.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on prayer instead of revenge, imprecation under the Spirit without carnal spite, mercy for the name’s sake, and God at the needy one’s right hand—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 109:1–31.

Arminian Notable voices

“God—the author and matter of all my praises. Adversaries—they requite my love with enmity. A wicked man—who will rule him with rigour and cruelty. Satan—to accuse him; for this was the place and posture of accusers in the Jewish courts. Sin—because it is not from his heart. Delighted not—in desiring and promoting the welfare of others. Garment—which a man wears constantly. That speak evil against my soul—with design to take away my life. Is good—above the mercy of all the creatures. The psalmist flees to prayer, asks just judgment on the merciless, and casts deliverance on the goodness of God’s mercy for His name’s sake.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 109)

Reformed Notable voices

“O God of my praise—David entrusts to God the vindication of his innocence when calumny is rampant; be not silent. On account of my love they opposed me; but I gave myself to prayer, not rendering evil for evil. When he forms maledictions he is not instigated by immoderate carnal propensity, nor by zeal without knowledge, nor by private personal considerations; he breathes under the Holy Spirit as God’s messenger. Yet leave room for grace—today’s enemy may become a friend—while the finally cruel deserve just judgment. Undertake for me for thy name’s sake; deliver because thy mercy is good—not for merit. He stands at the right hand of the poor to deliver his soul from those who condemn him to death.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 109)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English editions of the sources named. Both camps take imprecatory speech seriously.
