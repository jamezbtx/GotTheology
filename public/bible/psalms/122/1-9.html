Passage

Psalms 122

Verses 1–9 · WEB · Voices

A Song of Ascents Of David: gladness at the summons to the LORD’s house, feet inside Jerusalem’s gates, and praise for a city bound firmly together. Tribes go up to give thanks; Davidic thrones for judgment stand there. The psalm turns to prayer for Jerusalem’s peace—for brothers, companions, and the house of the LORD our God.

Psalms 122:1

1I was glad when they said to me, “Let’s go to the LORD’s house!”

Arminian

Gladness answers the invitation: Let’s go to the LORD’s house. Worship on the Ascents begins in shared joy, not in bare duty trudged alone.

Classical Arminians love this free delight. The summons is sincere; the heart may truly rejoice to seek God’s house with others on the pilgrim way.

Reformed

Glad when they said, “Let’s go.” A corporate call kindles joy toward the LORD’s house among David’s gathered people.

Gladness is gift as well as response. God orders both the invitation and the delight that answers it for Zion’s true worshipers.

Psalms 122:2

2Our feet are standing within your gates, Jerusalem!

Arminian

Feet standing within Jerusalem’s gates—arrival after long longing. Presence in the city of peace is celebrated as gift, not as entitlement earned.

Treasure gathered worship when God grants it. Standing inside the gates is privilege to receive with gratitude and careful love.

Reformed

Our feet are standing within your gates. The plural “our” marks a people arrived together at Zion under God.

God brought feet to Jerusalem’s threshold. The city of peace is entered under His leading, not by private tourism of the holy alone.

Psalms 122:3

3Jerusalem is built as a city that is compact together,

Arminian

Jerusalem built and bound firmly together—unity as living architecture. The city’s compactness images ordered fellowship among God’s pilgrim people.

The passage carries a call to seek firm togetherness in the church. Peace in the walls begins with bonds we cultivate under grace.

Reformed

Built as a city bound firmly together. Compact unity is Zion’s design under the Builder’s wise and holy hand.

The binding is God’s building before it is our project. He gathers what human scatter would loosen among His own people.

Psalms 122:4

4where the tribes go up, even the LORD’s tribes, according to an ordinance for Israel, to give thanks to the LORD’s name.

Arminian

Tribes of the LORD go up as decreed, to give thanks to His name. Pilgrimage is obedience and gratitude walking the same Ascents road.

The decree invites willing ascent. Thanksgiving is the appointed purpose—hearts and feet answering what God has set for Israel.

Reformed

Tribes go up—the LORD’s tribes—by what was decreed for Israel, to thank His name. Ascent and doxology are bound together by God.

God’s ordinance and Israel’s thanksgiving meet in the pilgrimage. The climb is not optional folklore; it is God’s ordered way for His people’s public praise.

Key contrast Both see tribal ascent as God-ordered thanksgiving. Arminians stress a sincere, willing going-up under the decree; Calvinists underline God’s sovereign appointment that gathers the tribes to thank His name.

Psalms 122:5

5For there are set thrones for judgment, the thrones of David’s house.

Arminian

Thrones for judgment—David’s house—sit in the city. Worship and just rule belong together in Zion’s full vision of peace.

Pray that leadership under God would judge fairly. Gladness for the temple does not ignore the need for righteous thrones.

Reformed

Thrones of judgment for David’s house are set there. Royal justice is part of Jerusalem’s gift to the ascending tribes.

God established these seats. The city’s peace includes ordered judgment under the line He chose—pointing beyond David to the greater King.

Psalms 122:6

6Pray for the peace of Jerusalem. Those who love you will prosper.

Arminian

Pray for Jerusalem’s peace; security for those who love her. Intercession is commanded love on the pilgrim’s lips and in the pilgrim’s heart.

Classical Arminians take the imperative seriously: God’s people may and must ask for shalom. Love for Zion expresses itself in earnest petition.

Reformed

Pray for the peace of Jerusalem—security for her lovers. Prayer is the means God has appointed for Zion’s lasting good.

God who decrees peace also decrees prayer for peace. Intercession is not spare emotion; it is how He cares for His city.

Psalms 122:7

7Peace be within your walls, and prosperity within your palaces.

Arminian

Peace within walls, security within towers—inner and outer safety named together. Blessing seeks the city’s full wholeness under the LORD.

Speak peace over the gathered people. Walls and towers matter because persons live inside them under His patient care.

Reformed

Peace in the walls, security in the towers. The blessing covers structure and strength around God’s chosen dwelling place.

Shalom is God’s word over Zion’s defenses. Towers stand because He wills security for the place of His holy name.

Psalms 122:8

8For my brothers’ and companions’ sakes, I will now say, “Peace be within you.”

Arminian

For brothers and companions he will now say, Peace be within you. Love of neighbor fuels the blessing spoken over Jerusalem’s peace.

Wesleyan Arminians hear relational motive: seek the city’s good because people you love live there. Peace is personal, not an abstract slogan.

Reformed

For brothers and companions’ sake—peace within you. Covenant kinship drives the spoken shalom toward Zion’s walls and people.

God binds us to one another so that prayer for Jerusalem is also love of the brethren He has joined in Himself.

Psalms 122:9

9For the sake of the house of the LORD our God, I will seek your good.

Arminian

For the LORD’s house he will seek Jerusalem’s good. Highest motive crowns the psalm: God’s dwelling among His pilgrim people.

Seek the good of God’s people because His house is there. Glad pilgrimage ends in active goodwill, not only warm feeling on the road.

Reformed

The temple motive is decisive for how true pilgrims love the holy city.

Zion’s welfare is bound to God’s name among His people. Seeking her good is obedience to the God who chose to dwell there.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on gladness for the house of the LORD, Jerusalem bound firmly together, and prayer for the city’s peace, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“I was glad when they said—the very first object of their thanksgiving was the kindness of God in permitting them to return to his ordinances. Jerusalem—compact together: it is also a state of great political and spiritual union, the centre of union to all the tribes. Pray for the peace of Jerusalem—let her unanimity never be disturbed; let her prosperity ever be on the increase! They shall prosper that love thee—in the peace and prosperity of the city, they shall find their peace and their prosperity. For my brethren and companions’ sakes I will say, Peace be within thee. Because of the house of the Lord our God—particularly will I wish thee well, because thou art the seat of religion, the place where our merciful God has condescended to dwell.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 122)

Reformed Notable voices

“In this Psalm David congratulates himself and the whole Church upon the fact that a seat had at length been appointed for the ark of the covenant, and that God had chosen a place where his name should be continually called upon. By this example we are taught that our joy should be doubled when God by his Holy Spirit not only frames each of us to the obedience of his word, but also produces the same effect upon others. Pray ye for the peace of Jerusalem: the welfare of the whole Church was inseparably connected with that kingdom and priesthood. If we would order our prayers aright, let us always begin with pleading that the Lord would be pleased to preserve this sacred community. Because of the house of Jehovah our God: if religion is with us a matter of heart-work, we ought to take an interest in the prosperity of the Church.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 122)
