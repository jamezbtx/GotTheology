Passage

Psalms 147

Verses 1–20 · ESV · Voices

Final Hallelujah doxologies (146–150) continue: praise is good and fitting; the LORD builds Jerusalem, gathers outcasts, heals the brokenhearted, numbers the stars, lifts the humble, and feeds beasts and ravens. He delights not in horse or human strength but in those who fear Him and hope in His steadfast love. Zion’s gates, peace, and wheat; snow, frost, and ice obey His swift word. He declares statutes to Jacob as to no other nation—shared praise closing toward the Psalter’s end.

Psalms 147:1

1Praise the LORD! For it is good to sing praises to our God; for it is pleasant, and a song of praise is fitting.

Arminian

Praise the LORD! For it is good to sing praises to our God; for it is pleasant, and a song of praise is fitting. Clarke: praise is comely—decent and proper that every intelligent creature acknowledge the Supreme Being who does good to the children of men.

Shared doxology: both camps gladly sing. Classical Arminians hear a sincere summons to fitting praise under grace—good, pleasant, and comely because God is worthy.

Reformed

Calvin: though benefits extend to all, he addresses God’s people especially; the main celebration is mercy to the chosen. Praise is good, delightful, and pleasant—indirectly censuring weariness at God’s name.

Reformed accent: alacrity in praise. Shared with Clarke: Praise the LORD; song of praise is fitting.

Psalms 147:2

2The LORD builds up Jerusalem; he gathers the outcasts of Israel.

Arminian

The LORD builds up Jerusalem; He gathers the outcasts of Israel. Clarke: walls rising under the eye as outcasts return to the work. Restoration is praise-material.

Arminian pastoral: gathering outcasts is mercy meant to be noticed and sung. Shared: the LORD builds and gathers.

Reformed

Calvin: builder of Jerusalem points less to stones than to spiritual worship; the Church is formed by God’s power, not human counsel. He gathers the dispersed—God forsakes not the work of His hands.

Reformed comfort: restoration as divine architecture. Shared: Jerusalem built; outcasts gathered.

Psalms 147:3

3He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds.

Arminian

He heals the brokenhearted and binds up their wounds. Clarke: the shattered heart—broken by a sense of God’s displeasure—is bound by the divine Surgeon.

Both camps: real healing for real wounds. Classical Arminians preach a Healer who invites the broken to come and be bound up.

Reformed

Calvin: though the Church labor under many diseases, God will speedily recover it; the figure shows the Church ever safe under His care.

Reformed pastoral: healing as covenant care. Shared: brokenhearted healed; wounds bound.

Psalms 147:4

4He determines the number of the stars; he gives to all of them their names.

Arminian

He determines the number of the stars; He gives to all of them their names. Clarke: He whose knowledge tells every star can collect scattered exiles. Wesley: He exactly knows them as we do those we call by name.

Shared: star-naming wisdom—creation praise fuels trust for gathering.

Reformed

Calvin: matchless wisdom regulates the courses of the stars without confusion; each has its fixed office. Gathering outcasts is not harder than numbering stars.

Reformed accent: incomprehensible power and wisdom. Shared: stars numbered and named.

Psalms 147:5

5Great is our Lord, and abundant in power; his understanding is beyond measure.

Arminian

Great is our Lord, and abundant in power; His understanding is beyond measure. Clarke: to His intelligence there is no number—He is infinite; therefore He can know and do all things.

Both camps: greatness without measure. Classical Arminians refuse a small God; praise matches plenitude of power.

Reformed

Calvin: Great is God, boundless in power and understanding. There cannot be greater folly than to make our judgment the measure of God’s works.

Reformed accent: do not scale God by human measure. Shared: abundant power; understanding beyond measure.

Psalms 147:6

6The LORD lifts up the humble; he casts the wicked to the ground.

Arminian

The LORD lifts up the humble; He casts the wicked to the ground. Clarke: the humbled and afflicted are raised; justice and mercy meet in one verse.

Arminian hearing: lifting the meek is pastoral hope; casting the wicked is moral warning. Shared praise of both sides of the LORD’s rule.

Reformed

Calvin: we are brought low that God may lift us up; the wicked are lifted that they may be cast down. Affliction and envy both meet this verse.

Reformed comfort under the cross. Shared: humble lifted; wicked cast down.

Psalms 147:7

7Sing to the LORD with thanksgiving; make melody to our God on the lyre!

Arminian

Sing to the LORD with thanksgiving; make melody to our God on the lyre! Clarke: sing a responsive song—answer one another. Thanksgiving is the mode.

Shared doxology: vocal and instrumental praise. Classical Arminians hear willing melody, not forced noise.

Reformed

Calvin: again he exhorts to sing; abundant matter is not wanting—new proofs of power, goodness, and wisdom meet our eyes.

Reformed accent: thanksgiving as chief exercise. Shared: sing with thanksgiving; melody on the lyre.

Psalms 147:8

8He covers the heavens with clouds; he prepares rain for the earth; he makes grass grow on the hills.

Arminian

He covers the heavens with clouds; He prepares rain for the earth; He makes grass grow on the hills. Clarke: even winds, clouds, and rain are under especial management; providence, not bare chance.

Arminian pastoral: ordinary weather is praise-material. Shared: clouds, rain, grass on hills.

Reformed

Calvin: sudden veiling of the heavens displays power; rain prepares grass—mercy and fatherly care in the same act as power. Second causes should not hide providence.

Reformed accent: particular providence in weather. Shared with Clarke: rain prepared; grass on hills.

Psalms 147:9

9He gives to the beasts their food, and to the young ravens that cry.

Arminian

He gives to the beasts their food, and to the young ravens that cry. Wesley: ravens most contemptible and unclean to Jews, forsaken by dams, wholly left to divine providence.

Both camps: care reaches the overlooked. Classical Arminians hear a Father who feeds what men neglect.

Reformed

Calvin: naming cattle and ravens emphasizes the argument—if God feeds brutes, He will be nurse and father to us. Young ravens cry unto God as confessing want.

Reformed pastoral: providence scaled to the least. Shared: beasts and young ravens fed.

Psalms 147:10

10His delight is not in the strength of the horse, nor his pleasure in the legs of a man,

Arminian

His delight is not in the strength of the horse, nor His pleasure in the legs of a man. Wesley: as if He needed either for His designs. Clarke: horse and well-proportioned legs delight men; they are not God’s peculiar delight.

Shared: human military beauty is not the LORD’s treasure. Classical Arminians warn against trusting strength God did not ask for as His joy.

Reformed

Calvin: men have no strength but what is given from above—this checks pride that trusts its own preparation. Strength of horse stands for any protection that intoxicates.

Reformed accent: false confidence blocks mercy. Shared: not horse-strength; not legs of a man.

Psalms 147:11

11but the LORD takes pleasure in those who fear him, in those who hope in his steadfast love.

Arminian

But the LORD takes pleasure in those who fear Him, in those who hope in His steadfast love. Clarke: the truly religious; and those just beginning to seek salvation—even the penitent’s cry pleases Him.

Shared center of the doxology: fear and hope in hesed. Both camps worship here; classical Arminians stress sincere hope held out to the seeking soul.

Reformed

Calvin: fear of God opposed to horse and human strength; hope in His mercy—worship with reverence and dependence upon grace.

Reformed accent: delight is in God-fearers who hope in mercy, not in self-strength. Shared: pleasure in fear and hope in steadfast love.

Psalms 147:12

12Praise the LORD, O Jerusalem! Praise your God, O Zion!

Arminian

Praise the LORD, O Jerusalem! Praise your God, O Zion! Covenant address: the rebuilt city is called by name to the choir.

Shared: Zion’s duty to praise. Classical Arminians hear a people summoned to grateful song for mercies already tasted.

Reformed

Calvin: having spoken generally, he addresses the Lord’s people who alone can appreciate these mercies. Under Jerusalem he comprises the whole Church.

Reformed accent: Church as choir. Shared: Praise the LORD, O Jerusalem; praise your God, O Zion.

Psalms 147:13

13For he strengthens the bars of your gates; he blesses your children within you.

Arminian

For He strengthens the bars of your gates; He blesses your children within you. Wesley: strength consists not in walls and bars, but in His protection. Clarke: walls completed, gates secured, provision safe.

Arminian pastoral: protection and children blessed—ordinary covenant goods to thank God for.

Reformed

Calvin: bars strengthened means the holy city perfectly guarded from hostile fear; children blessed prosper within.

Reformed comfort: divine guardianship. Shared: gates barred by God; children blessed.

Psalms 147:14

14He makes peace in your borders; he fills you with the finest of the wheat.

Arminian

He makes peace in your borders; He fills you with the finest of the wheat. Clarke: no longer troubled by hostile neighbors; finest wheat fills the city.

Shared: peace and bread as praise-reasons. Classical Arminians thank God for tangible mercies without despising them.

Reformed

Calvin: bounds made peace—enemies restrained; fatness of corn satisfies. Temporal provision under the law led toward higher gifts; faith sees liberality clearer than unbelief.

Reformed accent: peace as divine restraint. Shared: peace in borders; finest wheat.

Psalms 147:15

15He sends out his command to the earth; his word runs swiftly.

Arminian

He sends out His command to the earth; His word runs swiftly. Wesley: commandment sufficient without instruments; the thing is done without delay. Clarke: substantial word personified, running to perform His will.

Both camps: creation obeys speech. His word runs because He speaks with effect.

Reformed

Calvin: sending forth His word is secret influence governing all; without His appointment no movement among the elements. His word runs quickly—all concurs to effect it.

Reformed accent: nature depends on God’s will. Shared: command sent; word runs swiftly.

Psalms 147:16

16He gives snow like wool; he scatters frost like ashes.

Arminian

He gives snow like wool; He scatters frost like ashes. Wesley: like wool in color, shape, softness, and use—keeping earth’s fruits warm. Clarke: large flakes resembling fine white wool; frost spread over nature’s face.

Shared wonder at winter. Both camps praise the Giver, not chance weather.

Reformed

Calvin: sudden snow and hoar-frost show how quickly God’s word runs; avoid senseless natural philosophy that starts without His will.

Reformed accent: meteors as prompt obedience. Shared: snow like wool; frost like ashes.

Psalms 147:17

17He hurls down his crystals of ice like crumbs; who can stand before his cold?

Arminian

He hurls down His crystals of ice like crumbs; who can stand before His cold? Wesley: great hailstones cast like morsels. Clarke: eastern cold can kill man and beast—hence the cry, who can stand?

Shared: cold as the LORD’s. Classical Arminians hear majesty that humbles, not a puzzle of genre.

Reformed

Calvin: ice and cold force recognition of God’s power; piercing cold should compel acknowledgment when wonders alone do not.

Reformed pastoral: creation’s severity as summons. Shared: ice like crumbs; who can stand before His cold?

Psalms 147:18

18He sends out his word, and melts them; he makes his wind blow and the waters flow.

Arminian

He sends out His word, and melts them; He makes His wind blow and the waters flow. Clarke: command; south wind; thaw; waters flow where ice bound them.

Both camps: same word that freezes also melts. Shared praise of sovereign seasons.

Reformed

Calvin: He who binds also looses by His word; changes that would seem incredible cry that there is a Being who reigns above.

Reformed accent: word rules freeze and thaw. Shared: word melts; wind blows; waters flow.

Psalms 147:19

19He declares his word to Jacob, his statutes and rules to Israel.

Arminian

He declares His word to Jacob, His statutes and rules to Israel. Clarke: to no nation beside had God given a revelation of His will. Covenant speech crowns the nature hymn.

Shared: particular word to Israel. Classical Arminians treasure revealed statutes as grace.

Reformed

Calvin: another word than the silent law in nature—God teaches men with articulate language. Crowning proof of love: light of eternal life in the word. Moses and the prophets’ part is ascribed to God Himself.

Reformed accent: written revelation as highest mercy. Shared: word to Jacob; statutes to Israel.

Psalms 147:20

20He has not dealt thus with any other nation; they do not know his rules. Praise the LORD!

Arminian

He has not dealt thus with any other nation; they do not know His rules. Praise the LORD! Clarke: wondrous ordinances unknown to the nations—therefore praise. The doxology ends where it began: Hallelujah.

Shared closing of Psalm 147: both camps worship for the gift of God’s rules. Classical Arminians join the praise for His particular dealing with Israel.

Reformed

Calvin: comparison enhances mercy—He preferred one people; pre-eminence leads to gratuitous election, for Israel differed not by native excellency. Hallelujah.

Reformed confession: free distinction. Shared with Clarke: not so with any other nation; Praise the LORD!

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on fitting praise, Jerusalem rebuilt and outcasts gathered, stars named, meek lifted, fear and hope in steadfast love, and the word given to Jacob—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 147:1–20.

Arminian Notable voices

“Praise is comely—it is decent, befitting, and proper that every intelligent creature should acknowledge the Supreme Being; and as he does nothing but good to the children of men, so they should speak good of his name. The Lord doth build up Jerusalem: the psalmist appears to see the walls rising under his eye, because the outcasts of Israel are now gathered together. He healeth the broken in heart—the shivered in heart. He telleth the number of the stars: he whose knowledge is so exact as to tell every star can find out and collect all the scattered exiles. His understanding is infinite—to his intelligence there is no number. The Lord taketh pleasure in them that fear him, in those that hope in his mercy—even the cry of the penitent is pleasing in the ear of the Lord. He showeth his word unto Jacob: to no nation of the world beside had God given a revelation of his will.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 147)

Reformed Notable voices

“This Psalm also incites the people of God to praise him upon two accounts: first, for the display of his power, goodness, and wisdom in the common government of the world; but more particularly for his special goodness in cherishing and defending the Church which he has chosen of his free grace, in restoring it when fallen down, and gathering it when dispersed. Though the benefits he speaks of are such as God extends to all men indiscriminately, it is plain that he addresses more especially God’s people. The main thing which he celebrates is his mercy, as shown to his chosen people. Praise is good, delightful, and pleasant. Jehovah building up Jerusalem will gather the dispersed of Israel: the Church was not of human erection, but formed by the supernatural power of God; he forsakes not the work of his own hands. Not in the strength of the horse will he take pleasure: let man come in the preparation of his own strength, and this will only issue in smoke and vanity. Jehovah delighteth in those who fear him, who hope in his mercy. He announces his words to Jacob: the crowning proof of his love is that he has set before us in his word the light of eternal life. He hath not done so to every nation—this pre-eminence leads us to gratuitous election as its source.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 147)
