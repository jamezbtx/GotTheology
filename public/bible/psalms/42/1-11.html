Psalms 42

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

To the choirmaster. A Maskil of the Sons of Korah. Book II opens with Korahite lament: As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God. The soul thirsts for God, for the living God—When shall I come and appear before God? Tears have been food day and night while taunters ask all day long, Where is your God? Memory pours out the soul: once with the throng, leading procession to the house of God with glad shouts and songs of praise, a multitude keeping festival. The refrain: Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God. Cast down still, he remembers God from the land of Jordan and of Hermon, from Mount Mizar. Deep calls to deep at the roar of waterfalls; breakers and waves go over him. By day the LORD commands steadfast love; at night his song is with him—a prayer to the God of his life. He says to God his rock: Why have you forgotten me? Why go mourning under the enemy's oppression? Adversaries taunt as with a deadly wound in the bones, again Where is your God? The refrain returns: Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God.

Psalms 42:1

1As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God.

Arminian

As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God. Wesley: Panteth—After the enjoyment of thee in thy sanctuary. Shared thirst: the soul pants for God himself, not for lesser waters.

Arminian pastoral: shared—name the pant as holy desire for God. Seek him where he has pledged to meet his people; refuse a calm that never thirsts.

Reformed

Calvin: As the hart crieth for the fountains—extreme ardor; David preferred sanctuary access above riches and honors, that faith might be strengthened by the Law's exercises. God consults weakness and descends; exclusion from outward service grieved him as separation from God—yet he used the means as a ladder, not as a nest.

Reformed pastoral: shared—pant for the living God. Do not despise the appointed helps; refuse both cold indifference to means and a flight that imagines no need of them.

Key contrast Both hear desperate thirst for God. Arminians typically hear Wesley: panting after enjoyment of God in the sanctuary. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: sanctuary means as a ladder for infirmity—grief at shut access that still will not rest in the elements or claim a moment's unaided flight to heaven.

Psalms 42:2

2My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When shall I come and appear before God?

Arminian

My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When shall I come and appear before God? Wesley: Thirsteth—Not after vain useless idols, but after the only true and living God. Appear—In the place of his special presence and publick worship.

Arminian pastoral: shared—thirst for the living God alone. Ask when you may appear before him; refuse idols that promise and cannot give water.

Reformed

Calvin: thirst for God, the living God, is not bare presence imagined apart from his word and symbols; David sets before himself tabernacle, altar, and ceremonies by which God testified nearness—beginning with those helps to behold glory otherwise hidden.

Reformed pastoral: shared—seek the living God as he has revealed himself. Refuse both idol-thirst and visionary notions of his face apart from his word and worship.

Key contrast Both thirst for the living God and long to appear before him. Arminians typically hear Wesley: not idols but the true God, appearing in the place of special presence and public worship. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: presence tied to God's own symbols and word—a ladder toward his glory, not a gross invention of his face.

Psalms 42:3

3My tears have been my food day and night, while they say to me all the day long, “Where is your God?”

Arminian

My tears have been my food day and night, while they say to me all the day long, “Where is your God?” Tears replace meals under the relentless taunt that God is absent.

Arminian pastoral: shared—bring the taunt to God. Do not swallow Where is your God? as final truth; let grief drive you to him, not away.

Reformed

Calvin: tears as bread day and night—grief so deep that weeping displaced ordinary meals; the wicked's Where is thy God? is a shaft Satan uses to fan despair when sanctuary access seems cut off.

Reformed pastoral: shared—treat the taunt as spiritual war, not idle insult. Refuse indifference when God's name is held up to scorn in your misery.

Psalms 42:4

4These things I remember, as I pour out my soul: how I would go with the throng and lead them in procession to the house of God with glad shouts and songs of praise, a multitude keeping festival.

Arminian

These things I remember, as I pour out my soul: how I would go with the throng and lead them in procession to the house of God with glad shouts and songs of praise, a multitude keeping festival. Wesley: Remember—My banishment from God's presence, and mine enemies triumphs. In me—I breathe out my sorrows and complaints to God within my own breast. Holy-day—the three solemn festival solemnities kept holy unto the Lord.

Arminian pastoral: shared—remember festival joy without denying present exile. Pour out the soul to God within; refuse a nostalgia that never becomes prayer.

Reformed

Calvin: remembrance of former processions aggravates present misery—he who led ranks to the holy assemblies is now shut out; to pour out the soul is to have affections melted within by the greatness of sorrow after a godly sort, grieving sanctuary-loss more than house or wife.

Reformed pastoral: shared—let past public praise deepen hunger for God's house, not bitter envy. Regulate joy and grief by his favor and frown.

Key contrast Both pour out the soul over remembered throngs at God's house. Arminians typically hear Wesley: banished from presence, breathing sorrows within the breast toward God. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: former leadership of festival ranks makes exile sharper—the soul melted by sanctuary-grief after a godly sort.

Psalms 42:5

5Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation

Arminian

Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation Wesley: For—Heb. for the salvations of his face, for those supports, deliverances and comforts which I doubt not I shall enjoy both in his presence and sanctuary, and from his presence, and the light of his countenance.

Arminian pastoral: shared refrain—rebuke the cast-down soul. Hope in God; expect again to praise him for the help of his face; refuse a turmoil that will not hope.

Reformed

Calvin: David forms two opposing parties—faith armed with promises rebukes flesh, cowardice, and distrust within. Discouragement and disquietude meet one remedy: hope in God. I shall yet praise him—yet confesses present praise stopped, while hope extends to deliverance not yet seen; helps of his countenance are salvations from his manifested favor.

Reformed pastoral: shared refrain—fight the internal war against unbelief. Hope when grace seems hidden; refuse both collapse and a hope invented without the promises.

Key contrast Both rebuke the cast-down soul and command hope that will praise again. Arminians typically hear Wesley: salvations of his face—supports and comforts in sanctuary presence and the light of his countenance. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: internal conflict of faith against flesh, with yet as hope toward grace still hidden and praise presently stopped.

Psalms 42:6

6and my God. My soul is cast down within me; therefore I remember you from the land of Jordan and of Hermon, from Mount Mizar.

Arminian

and my God. My soul is cast down within me; therefore I remember you from the land of Jordan and of Hermon, from Mount Mizar. Wesley: Therefore—that I may revive my drooping spirits. Remember—I will consider thy infinite mercy and power, and faithfulness. Mizar—from all the parts of the land to which I shall be driven, whether beyond Jordan or mount Hermon.

Arminian pastoral: shared—when cast down, remember God from wherever you are driven. Revive the spirit by his mercy, power, and faithfulness; refuse a geography that excuses forgetting him.

Reformed

Calvin: cast down from Jordan and Hermons and Mizar—exile's map; remembrance of the sanctuary from afar may deepen sorrow, yet he takes refuge in God as witness and physician, not in forgetting him. Deprived of accustomed helps, he still stirs the mind by the word.

Reformed pastoral: shared—remember God in exile places. Prefer his presence to every recovered comfort; refuse a cure that runs from his hand.

Psalms 42:7

7Deep calls to deep at the roar of your waterfalls; all your breakers and your waves have gone over me.

Arminian

Deep calls to deep at the roar of your waterfalls; all your breakers and your waves have gone over me. Wesley: Deep—One affliction comes immediately after another, as if it were called for by the former. A metaphor from violent successive showers that come down as at the noise or call of God's water spouts.

Arminian pastoral: shared—name successive depths without pretending one wave was enough. Bring stacked afflictions to God; refuse the lie that the next deep will not call.

Reformed

Calvin: depth calleth unto depth—temptations like gulfs cry to one another without end or measure; all thy waves and floods have passed over me. He names foes' cruelty floods of God, that we humble under his mighty hand; when he opens sluices there is no end until he is appeased.

Reformed pastoral: shared—own the billows as under God's hand. Fear his opened waterspouts more than human spite alone; seek his peace when deep calls to deep.

Key contrast Both read stacked afflictions as deep calling to deep. Arminians typically hear Wesley: successive showers at the call of God's waterspouts—one trouble summoning the next. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: gulfs of temptation and waves named God's floods—foes' cruelty under his mighty hand, with no end until he is appeased.

Psalms 42:8

8By day the LORD commands his steadfast love, and at night his song is with me, a prayer to the God of my life.

Arminian

By day the LORD commands his steadfast love, and at night his song is with me, a prayer to the God of my life. Wesley: Command—Will effectually confer upon me. Loving-kindness—His blessings, the effects of his loving-kindness.

Arminian pastoral: shared—expect his commanded love by day and song by night. Pray to the God of your life; refuse a night that has no song because it has no trust.

Reformed

Calvin: Jehovah will command loving-kindness by day; by night his song and prayer to the God of my life—future confidence set against the dreadful tokens of the prior verse. Prayer here is free access's delight, not merely afflicted complaint; cheerfulness rises from knowing him as God of life.

Reformed pastoral: shared—oppose despairing billows with expected kindness and night praise. Keep prayer open when waves have just gone over; refuse a reading that buries tomorrow's mercy under today's flood.

Psalms 42:9

9I say to God, my rock: “Why have you forgotten me? Why do I go mourning because of the oppression of the enemy?”

Arminian

I say to God, my rock: “Why have you forgotten me? Why do I go mourning because of the oppression of the enemy?” The complaint is spoken to the Rock—even forgottenness is brought to him, not away from him.

Arminian pastoral: shared—call him Rock while you ask Why. Mourning under oppression may speak freely; refuse a silence that stops saying my rock.

Reformed

Calvin: I will say to God my rock, Why hast thou forgotten me?—not utter rejection, for then prayer would be vain; forgetfulness relates to outward appearance and flesh's disquiet, while faith still rests assured that God regards and will not be deaf.

Reformed pastoral: shared—hold Rock and Why together. Let flesh's sense of being forgotten drive frank prayer, not abandoned unbelief.

Psalms 42:10

10As with a deadly wound in my bones, my adversaries taunt me, while they say to me all the day long, “Where is your God?”

Arminian

As with a deadly wound in my bones, my adversaries taunt me, while they say to me all the day long, “Where is your God?” Wesley: As—As a sword, which cutteth the very bones, so painful are their reproaches. The repeated taunt lands as bone-deep wound.

Arminian pastoral: shared—admit how sharp the daily Where is your God? feels. Carry the wound to God; refuse matching scorn, and refuse pretending the cut is light.

Reformed

Calvin: reproach as slaughter in the bones—injury felt more acutely than a soft wound; derision that tears God's majesty and aims to overturn faith is cruel persecution, not a trifle. Bear private wrongs more patiently; burn with zeal when faith and the word are trampled.

Reformed pastoral: shared—rank the taunt against God's name as the deeper sword. Ask courage for that wound; refuse a calm that cares only for personal insult.

Key contrast Both feel the taunt as bone-deep. Arminians typically hear Wesley: reproaches cut like a sword that reaches the bones. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: slaughter in the bones because God's majesty and the faith are trampled—persecution of the covenant word, not mere private slight.

Psalms 42:11

11Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God.

Arminian

Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you in turmoil within me? Hope in God; for I shall again praise him, my salvation and my God. The refrain returns unchanged in call: rebuke, hope, and certainty of renewed praise to the God who is salvation.

Arminian pastoral: shared closing hope with both camps—answer turmoil again with Hope in God. Praise will return; he remains my salvation and my God.

Reformed

Calvin: the repetition shows temptation not finished in one encounter—new conflict may renew the same trouble, yet courage must not fail. Helps of my countenance and my God name God himself as visible defender and salvation.

Reformed pastoral: shared closing hope with both camps—return to the refrain when the assault returns. Hold him as salvation and God; refuse despair that treats one victory as the last battle needed.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the soul that pants like a deer for flowing streams and thirsts for the living God and the place of his presence, tears that become food under the daily taunt Where is your God, memory that pours out the soul for throngs once led to the house of God in festival praise, the refrain that rebukes a cast-down soul to hope in God and praise again, remembrance from Jordan and Hermon and Mizar, deep calling to deep under breakers and waves, daytime steadfast love and night song as prayer to the God of life, the rock questioned in forgotten mourning, bone-deep taunts that repeat Where is your God, and the returning hope that shall again praise him as salvation and God—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 42:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“Panteth—After the enjoyment of thee in thy sanctuary. Thirsteth—Not after vain useless idols, but after the only true and living God. Appear—In the place of his special presence and publick worship. Remember—My banishment from God's presence, and mine enemies triumphs. In me—I breathe out my sorrows and complaints to God within my own breast. The multitudes—Israelites, who went thither in great numbers. Holy-day—Or that kept the feast, the three solemn festival solemnities, which they kept holy unto the Lord. For—Heb. for the salvations of his face, for those supports, deliverances and comforts which I doubt not I shall enjoy both in his presence and sanctuary, and from his presence, and the light of his countenance. Therefore—Therefore that I may revive my drooping spirits. Remember—I will consider thy infinite mercy and power, and faithfulness. Mizar—From all the parts of the land, to which I shall be driven; whether from the parts beyond Jordan on the east: or mount Hermon, which was in the northern parts. Deep—One affliction comes immediately after another, as if it were called for by the former. A metaphor taken from violent and successive showers of rain; which frequently come down from heaven, as it were at the noise, or call of God's water spouts. Command—Will effectually confer upon me. Loving-kindness—His blessings, the effects of his loving-kindness. As—As a sword, which cutteth the very bones, so painful are their reproaches.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament, Psalm 42

Reformed Notable voices

“As the hart crieth for the fountains of water, so my soul crieth after thee—David preferred access to the sanctuary above all enjoyments of the world, that faith and piety might be cherished by the exercises of the Law. He cried for the living God not as bare burning desire alone: God consults our weakness and descends; excluded from outward service, David is grieved as if separated from God himself—an example that shames those who bear deprivation of means without concern, or proudly despise them as if they could ascend to heaven in a moment's flight. He used ceremonies as a ladder, finding he had not wings to fly. Tears have been my bread day and night while they say, Where is thy God?—Satan fans grief by that taunt; weeping became his meat. When I remember, I pour out my soul—former leadership of processions to God's house aggravates present exile; the soul melts within. O my soul, why art thou cast down?—he forms two parties: faith armed by promises rebukes flesh, cowardice, and distrust; the remedy is hope in God—I shall yet praise him for the helps of his countenance, elevating thought to grace when hidden. From Jordan and Hermons and Mizar he remembers God in exile; sanctuary-loss grieves more than house or wife. Depth calleth unto depth; all thy waves have passed over me—gulfs of temptation call to one another; Saul's cruelty and other foes are floods of God, that we humble under his mighty hand. Jehovah will command his loving-kindness by day; by night his song and prayer to the God of my life—future hope set against tokens of displeasure. I will say to God my rock, Why hast thou forgotten me?—flesh's sense, while faith still calls him Rock. Reproach is as slaughter in the bones—derision that tramples faith and God's majesty wounds deeper than private wrong. The refrain returns: Hope in God; I shall yet praise him, the helps of my countenance, and my God—temptation renews the conflict; courage must not fail.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms, Psalm 42
