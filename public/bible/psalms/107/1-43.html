Passage

Psalms 107

Verses 1–43 · WEB · Voices

The redeemed are called to give thanks for the Lord's goodness and everlasting steadfast love: wanderers in desert wastes cry and are led to a city; prisoners in darkness who rebelled are freed as bronze doors shatter; fools sick from sin are healed by His sent word; sailors at their wits' end see the storm stilled and reach their haven—each group urged to thank Him for steadfast love and wondrous works. He turns rivers to desert for the evil of inhabitants and deserts to pools for the hungry; He brings princes low and raises the needy; the upright rejoice, wickedness is silenced, and the wise consider His steadfast love.

Psalms 107:1

1Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his loving kindness endures forever.

Arminian

Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his loving kindness endures forever. Wesley opens the psalm as God's providential care for people in distress who cry to Him and are helped.

Both hear: praise frames every deliverance that follows. What comes through is a sincere call to thank the God whose love endures—mercy that invites the redeemed to speak.

Reformed

Calvin: this verse, also opening the previous psalm, was often answered in chorus—Praise Jehovah, because He is good. The penman inserts thanksgiving where Israel so often began.

Steadfast love forever grounds gratitude before any story of rescue. Give thanks; He is good; love endures.

Psalms 107:2

2Let the redeemed by the LORD say so, whom he has redeemed from the hand of the adversary,

Arminian

Let the redeemed by the LORD say so, those he has redeemed from the hand of the adversary. The rescued are not to stay silent; redemption calls for confession of the Deliverer.

"say so" is earnest invitation—those God has redeemed from trouble are urged to testify. Here the reader meets redeeming love that sincerely summons the freed to speak.

Reformed

Calvin: let the redeemed of Jehovah who have returned from captivity come forth and publish His loving-kindness. They are redeemed because God's outstretched hand was their guard; wanderings were not chance.

Redeemed of the Lord name those God has actually brought out. Both see: redeemed; say so; from trouble.

Key contrast Both confess the redeemed must speak of the Lord's rescue. Arminians find a broad summons to all whom God has delivered from trouble to testify. Reformed interpreters usually side with Calvin's particular redeemed—those God has actually brought home under His providence—commanded to publish His loving-kindness.

Psalms 107:3

3and gathered out of the lands, from the east and from the west, from the north and from the south.

Arminian

And gathered out of the lands, from east and west, north and south. Wesley: Gathered—into their own land.

Undisputed: gathering is God's work across the compass. Wesley’s tradition hears a God who sincerely gathers the scattered and calls them home.

Reformed

Calvin: whom He has gathered out of the lands from every quarter. Not casual travel but return under providence from exile and danger.

Gathering proves the Redeemer's hand. Gathered from the lands; every direction.

Psalms 107:4

4They wandered in the wilderness in a desert way. They found no city to live in.

Arminian

They wandered in the wilderness in a desert way. They found no city to live in. Wesley: No city—no town inhabited where they might refresh themselves.

No dispute here: homelessness without a path is misery. Classical Arminians preach a God who sees wanderers and later leads them straight.

Reformed

Calvin: they wandered in the solitary desert and found no city of habitation—lonely places where hunger and thirst threaten death unless the Lord rescue.

Desert as stage for providence, not fortune. Wandered; no city to dwell in.

Psalms 107:5

5Hungry and thirsty, their soul fainted in them.

Arminian

Hungry and thirsty, their soul fainted in them.

Both affirm: bodily need empties the soul. Arminian interpreters hear faintness that drives sincere cry—need that grace meets without mocking the hungry.

Reformed

Calvin: hungry and thirsty until the soul faints—hourly hazard of death in the desert unless God interposes.

Extremity prepares the cry of v. 6. The wanderers were hungry and thirsty, and their soul fainted.

Psalms 107:6

6Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble, and he delivered them out of their distresses.

Arminian

Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble, and he delivered them out of their distresses. Wesley: The Lord—unto Jehovah, the true God; even heathens had some knowledge of Him.

Cry and deliverance are linked—prayer answered. Arminian readers glory that God helps those in distress in answer to their prayers.

Reformed

Calvin: in straits they called upon Jehovah. The verbs show continued action: when hope fails, then they cry. God often delivers even those whose hope does not center on Him, constraining acknowledgment that help is from Him alone.

Cry may be instinct under pressure; deliverance still from God. Where they meet: cried; delivered from distress.

Key contrast Both confess that people cry in trouble and God delivers. Arminians stress sincere prayer God answers and invites. Reformed Christians hear Calvin's note: even those without settled piety are constrained to call, yet deliverance still proves God alone saves.

Psalms 107:7

7He led them also by a straight way, that they might go to a city to live in.

Arminian

He led them also by a straight way, that they might go to a city to live in. Wesley: Forth—out of the wilderness.

God leads; the city is gift. Arminian readers preach guidance as kindness toward the lost who cried.

Reformed

Calvin: by God's hand they were led into the right way to a place of lodging. He connects mercy with wondrous works so silence would be impious.

Straight way is providence, not luck. Both traditions hear: led; straight way; city to dwell in.

Psalms 107:8

8Let them praise the LORD for his loving kindness, for his wonderful deeds to the children of men!

Arminian

Let them praise the LORD for his loving kindness, for his wonderful deeds to the children of men!

Common reading: thanksgiving is owed. Wesley’s heirs hear "children of men" as wide horizon—works that invite all who see them to praise.

Reformed

Calvin: let them celebrate His mercy and marvelous works before the sons of men. To remain silent after such deliverance would obscure God's glory.

Ingratitude after rescue is wickedness. Thank; steadfast love; wondrous works.

Psalms 107:9

9For he satisfies the longing soul. He fills the hungry soul with good.

Arminian

For he satisfies the longing soul. He fills the hungry soul with good things.

Both confess: longing and hunger met with good. Classical Arminians preach a God who fills—not with empty promise but with good for the soul that cries.

Reformed

Calvin: He has satisfied the longing soul and filled the famished with good. The reflective line seals why thanks is due.

Satisfaction is God's verb. Satisfies; fills with good things.

Psalms 107:10

10Some sat in darkness and in the shadow of death, being bound in affliction and iron,

Arminian

Some sat in darkness and in the shadow of death, being bound in affliction and in irons. Wesley: Darkness—in dark prisons or dungeons.

Either reading: bondage named without soft edges. Arminians hear prisoners whose later cry God still hears.

Reformed

Calvin: another species of danger—darkness and shadow of death, bound in affliction and iron. Afflictions are God's rod; remedy is in His grace alone, not chance.

Captivity as judgment and stage for deliverance. Both sides agree: darkness; shadow of death; irons.

Psalms 107:11

11because they rebelled against the words of God, and condemned the counsel of the Most High.

Arminian

For they rebelled against the words of God, and condemned the counsel of the Most High.

Rebellion and contempt of counsel are sins under light. Arminians preach spurning as willful resistance—counsel rejected, not never offered.

Reformed

Calvin: afflictions do not happen by chance. He charges not mere sin but rebellion against God's word—best regulation of life is prompt obedience. Until people see troubles as God's appointment, they will not ask Him for deliverance.

Rebellion explains the irons. Both traditions see: rebelled; spurned counsel.

Key contrast Both confess rebellion against God's words and counsel as the cause of bondage. Arminians typically stress willful spurning of counsel sincerely given. Reformed readers stress Calvin's point: all such trouble is by God's appointment, driving the guilty to cry to the only Deliverer.

Psalms 107:12

12Therefore he brought down their heart with labor. They fell down, and there was no one to help.

Arminian

Therefore he brought down their heart with labor. They fell down, and there was no one to help. Wesley: Heart—the pride and obstinacy of their hearts; Fell—into hopeless miseries.

Pride brought low; no human helper. Arminians hear labor that humbles so the cry of v. 13 may be sincere.

Reformed

Calvin: hard labor bows the heart; they fall with none to help—hopeless until God acts. Brazen gates and iron bars (v. 16) will enhance the later benefit.

Extremity strips false helpers. Both note: bowed down; fell; none to help.

Psalms 107:13

13Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble, and he saved them out of their distresses.

Arminian

Then they cried to the LORD in their trouble, and he saved them out of their distresses.

Common confession: same refrain as the desert cry. This is repeated mercy—God still answers prisoners who call.

Reformed

Calvin: again they cry and He delivers. Sighs and cries testify against later ingratitude—when in straits they confess God as deliverer; peace must not erase that confession.

Remember the cry after the rescue. Both readings hold: cried; delivered.

Psalms 107:14

14He brought them out of darkness and the shadow of death, and broke away their chains.

Arminian

He brought them out of darkness and the shadow of death, and broke away their bonds apart.

Both agree: out of darkness; bonds broken. Wesleyan Arminians preach liberation as pattern of gospel freedom—bonds that God alone bursts.

Reformed

Calvin: brought out of darkness and shadow of death; bands broken. Deliverance from perpetual slavery magnifies grace.

God breaks what humans cannot. God brought them out and burst their bonds.

Psalms 107:15

15Let them praise the LORD for his loving kindness, for his wonderful deeds to the children of men!

Arminian

Let them praise the LORD for his loving kindness, for his wonderful deeds to the children of men!

Agreed: refrain of thanks after prison-rescue. Classical Arminians urge the freed not to mute the praise due.

Reformed

Calvin: again the chorus—celebrate mercy and marvelous works. Ingratitude after wonder is charged as forgetting the Deliverer.

Thanks is duty of the released. Both hear: thank; steadfast love; wondrous works.

Psalms 107:16

16For he has broken the gates of bronze, and cut through bars of iron.

Arminian

For he has broken the gates of bronze, and cut through bars of iron.

Bronze and iron yield to God. Arminians hear strength that frees—no gate too strong for the Lord who answers cry.

Reformed

Calvin: brazen gates and iron bars named to enhance the benefit—chains of perpetual slavery broken asunder.

Only God shatters such doors. Both see: shatters bronze; cuts iron bars.

Psalms 107:17

17Fools are afflicted because of their disobedience, and because of their iniquities.

Arminian

Fools are afflicted because of their disobedience, and because of their iniquities suffered affliction. Wesley: Afflicted—with sickness.

Folly is moral, not merely mental—sinful ways bring affliction. Arminian readers warn that chosen paths of sin still meet a God who can heal when they cry.

Reformed

Calvin: fools afflicted for the way of their transgression. Fools are those who, giving themselves to sensuality, bring destruction; fear of God is wisdom, so shaking off His yoke is folly and fury.

Fatal diseases make grace conspicuous when deliverance comes. Undisputed: fools; sinful ways; affliction.

Psalms 107:18

18Their soul abhors all kinds of food. They draw near to the gates of death.

Arminian

They loathed any kind of food. They draw near to the gates of death.

No dispute here: appetite gone; death's gates near. Arminians hear extremity that still leaves room for the cry of v. 19.

Reformed

Calvin: they loathe all food and approach the gates of death—not slight illness but cases where hope is abandoned, so grace shines when life returns.

Near-death frames the word that heals. Both affirm: loathed food; gates of death.

Psalms 107:19

19Then they cry to the LORD in their trouble, and he saves them out of their distresses.

Arminian

Then they cry to the LORD in their trouble, and he saves them out of their distresses. He sends his word, and heals them, and delivers them from their distress.

Where they meet: third cry-and-deliver refrain. Arminian readers preach that sickness under sin does not bar sincere calling on the Lord.

Reformed

Calvin: they call when reduced to straits, acknowledging they would be undone without His wonderful help.

Cry in illness still reaches God. They cried out, and God delivered them.

Psalms 107:20

20He sends his word, and heals them, and delivers them from their graves.

Arminian

He sends his word, and heals them, and delivers them from their graves. Wesley: Word—His command, or blessing.

Healing word is living command—God speaks and restores. Arminians glory that the same God who wounds by folly can heal by word when they cry.

Reformed

Calvin: He sends His word—God has but to will or speak, and disease and death are expelled. Bodily recovery by the word images how souls live only as His word is apprehended; yet mercy here extends even toward the evil and unthankful.

Word as effective command of the Healer. Both traditions hear: sent His word; healed; delivered from destruction.

Key contrast Both confess healing by God's sent word. Arminians hear Wesley's command or blessing that restores those who cry. Reformed readers hear Calvin's efficacious speech—God wills and disease flees—and the analogy that spiritual life likewise depends on His word received.

Psalms 107:21

21Let them praise the LORD for his loving kindness, for his wonderful deeds to the children of men!

Arminian

Let them praise the LORD for his loving kindness, for his wonderful deeds to the children of man!

Healing should lead to thanksgiving. Arminians urge the recovered to praise, not only privately but as duty.

Reformed

Calvin: again celebrate mercy before the sons of men. Recovery that baffled human skill must not be muted.

Wondrous works demand voice. Common reading: thank; steadfast love; wondrous works.

Psalms 107:22

22Let them offer the sacrifices of thanksgiving, and declare his deeds with singing.

Arminian

And Let them offer the sacrifices of thanksgiving, and declare his deeds in songs of joy!

Both confess: thanks becomes sacrifice and song. The verse presses public joy—deeds told so others may trust the Healer.

Reformed

Calvin: sacrifice of thanksgiving explains how God is robbed if providence is not recognized. To acknowledge His bounty is most acceptable; even nature teaches homage after escape from peril.

Silence after healing is theft of glory. Sacrifices of thanksgiving; songs of joy.

Psalms 107:23

23Those who go down to the sea in ships, who do business in great waters,

Arminian

Those who go down to the sea in ships and do business in great waters see his works. Classical Arminians hear sailors whose later cry God will still answer.

Reformed

Calvin: those who go down to the sea see God's wonders; their lives often hang on storms He raises and calms—as if brought from the tomb to light.

Sea business under providence. Either reading: ships; great waters.

Psalms 107:24

24these see the LORD’s deeds, and his wonders in the deep.

Arminian

These see the LORD’s deeds, and his wonders in the deep.

Both sides agree: the deep displays deeds. Arminian interpreters preach works meant to be seen and later praised.

Reformed

Calvin: mariners are fit witnesses of God's works—more vast wonders than on land—especially as the next lines show storm and calm by His word.

Deep as classroom of providence. Both traditions see: deeds; wondrous works in the deep.

Psalms 107:25

25For he commands, and raises the stormy wind, which lifts up its waves.

Arminian

For he commands, and raises the stormy wind, which lifts up its waves of the sea.

Storm answers His command. Wesleyan Arminians refuse chance—wind rises because God speaks.

Reformed

Calvin: He speaks and the winds blow; sudden changes proceed from God's secret appointment, not from the sea's own will.

Command precedes the swell. Both note: commanded; raised the stormy wind.

Psalms 107:26

26They mount up to the sky; they go down again to the depths. Their soul melts away because of trouble.

Arminian

They mount up to the sky; they go down again to the depths. Their soul melts away in their evil plight;

Common confession: terror that melts courage. Arminian interpreters hear human end so that divine help may be clear.

Reformed

Calvin: they mount to heaven and descend to the deeps—death stares; the picture of peril makes goodness conspicuous when the storm ceases without loss.

Courage that melts in the storm strips away self-trust. Both readings hold: mounted; went down; courage melted.

Psalms 107:27

27They reel back and forth, and stagger like a drunken man, and are at their wits’ end.

Arminian

They reel back and forth, and stagger like a drunken man, and are at their wits’ end.

Both agree: skill fails; wit ends. Arminian readers preach the end of counsel as the door of cry (v. 28).

Reformed

Calvin: they reel like drunkards; all wisdom swallowed up—skill baffled, sails and sounding useless; they give themselves to wind and wave, then cry.

Wits' end is pedagogical. Agreed: reeled; staggered; wits' end.

Psalms 107:28

28Then they cry to the LORD in their trouble, and he brings them out of their distress.

Arminian

Then they cry to the LORD in their trouble, and he brings them out of their distress.

Both hear: fourth cry-and-deliver refrain at sea. Wesleyan Arminians hear prayer when every art fails—and God still delivers.

Reformed

Calvin: all human aid failing, they cry to God—evidence they had been as it were dead. Deliverance from such peril is convincing proof of His care.

Cry at wits' end still heard. Both see: cried; delivered.

Psalms 107:29

29He makes the storm a calm, so that its waves are still.

Arminian

He makes the storm a calm, so that its waves grow still.

Storm still; waves hushed. Classical Arminians glory that the God who raised the wind also stills it for those who cried.

Reformed

Calvin: He makes the storm a calm—not human agency but providence presiding; God restrains raging billows until the deep is smooth as a mirror.

Calm is as much His act as the storm. Undisputed: storm still; waves hushed.

Psalms 107:30

30Then they are glad because it is calm, so he brings them to their desired haven.

Arminian

Then they are glad because it is calm, so he brings them to their desired haven.

No dispute here: gladness and haven are gifts. Arminian interpreters preach arrival as kindness after terror—joy that should become praise.

Reformed

Calvin: after great terror, joy; forgetfulness after remarkable deliverance would be striking ingratitude. They have been instructed by storm and calm that life is in God's hand.

Haven seals the lesson. Glad; quiet waters; desired haven.

Psalms 107:31

31Let them praise the LORD for his loving kindness, for his wonderful deeds for the children of men!

Arminian

Let them praise the LORD for his loving kindness, for his wonderful deeds for the children of men!

Both affirm: fourth thanksgiving refrain. Arminian interpreters urge sailors and all who hear to thank, not whisper only at home.

Reformed

Calvin: gratitude deserves to be magnified in all places, even great assemblies—not only private mention.

Public thanks after public peril. Where they meet: thank; steadfast love; wondrous works.

Psalms 107:32

32Let them exalt him also in the assembly of the people, and praise him in the seat of the elders.

Arminian

Let them exalt him also in the assembly of the people, and praise him in the seat of the elders. Wesley: Exalt Him—in public assemblies; Elders—magistrates; let them not be ashamed to speak of God's works before the greatest.

Praise before people and elders—no fear of rank. Listen here for open confession as duty of the delivered.

Reformed

Calvin: specific mention of elders—the more wisdom and experience, the more capable of listening and witnessing these praises.

Congregation and elders as proper theater of praise. Both traditions hear: extol; congregation; assembly of elders.

Psalms 107:33

33He turns rivers into a desert, water springs into a thirsty ground,

Arminian

He turns rivers into a desert, water springs into a thirsty ground. Wesley: Rivers—well-watered fruitful grounds; Into—dry ground like a barren wilderness.

God reverses fertility. Arminians hear judgment that can still warn and call to repentance.

Reformed

Calvin: fruitful lands become unfruitful—folly to attribute to chance. God pours wrath by taking blessing; moisture that fed plants is withdrawn.

Landscape change as providence. Rivers to desert; springs to thirsty ground.

Psalms 107:34

34and a fruitful land into a salt waste, for the wickedness of those who dwell in it.

Arminian

A fruitful land into a salt waste, for the wickedness of those who dwell in it. Wesley: For—He does not inflict these judgments without cause, but for punishment of sin in some and prevention in others.

Evil of inhabitants is cause—judgment moral, not arbitrary. Classical Arminians preach warning that fruitful places can be lost through persistent evil.

Reformed

Calvin: the earth is cursed on account of the iniquity of its inhabitants, who prove undeserving of His bounty. Saltness images extreme sterility; judgments are tokens of displeasure summoning sinners before His throne.

Evil explains the salty waste. Fruitful to salt; because of evil.

Key contrast Both confess fruitful land ruined because of inhabitants' evil. Arminians hear Wesley's note: judgments punish sin and also prevent it in others—moral warning with room to turn. Reformed readers hear Calvin: curse for iniquity under God's judicial providence, not chance.

Psalms 107:35

35He turns a desert into a pool of water, and a dry land into water springs.

Arminian

He turns a desert into a pool of water, and a dry land into water springs. Wesley: Water—into a well-watered and fruitful land.

Common reading: reverse mercy after reverse judgment. Arminian readers preach a God who can make waste bloom for the hungry He will settle.

Reformed

Calvin: desert turned into a pool displays miraculous power and goodness—parched ground becomes fruitful contrary to former nature.

Same hand that dries also waters. Desert to pools; parched to springs.

Psalms 107:36

36There he makes the hungry live, that they may prepare a city to live in,

Arminian

And a dry land into water springs. There he makes the hungry live, that they may prepare a city to live in. Wesley: Hungry—poor people who could not provide for themselves.

Both confess: hungry housed; city founded. Arminian interpreters hear kindness to the poor who could not secure a home alone.

Reformed

Calvin: the famishing whom the world refuses, expatriated, are comfortably supplied in desert places God blesses with abundance; cities rise where once was no cottage.

God settles the hungry. Either reading: hungry dwell; establish a city.

Psalms 107:37

37sow fields, plant vineyards, and reap the fruits of increase.

Arminian

They sow fields, plant vineyards, and get a fruitful yield.

Both sides agree: sowing and planting succeed under God. Classical Arminians preach fruit as gift that should lead to thanks, not pride.

Reformed

Calvin: they sow and plant where cultivation was unknown—the land assumes a new aspect; fruit of annual increase is perennial by design, not a brief anomaly.

Yield traces to blessing. Sow; plant; fruitful yield.

Psalms 107:38

38He blesses them also, so that they are multiplied greatly. He doesn’t allow their livestock to decrease.

Arminian

By His blessing they are multiplied greatly. He doesn’t allow their livestock diminish.

Both traditions see: blessing multiplies; livestock kept. The passage carries increase as stewardship trust—gift that can be abused if hearts grow secure (v. 39).

Reformed

Calvin: entirely owing to the Divine blessing that those once in want now increase in good things.

Multiply by blessing, not bare effort. Both note: by His blessing; multiply; livestock.

Psalms 107:39

39Again, they are diminished and bowed down through oppression, trouble, and sorrow.

Arminian

When they are diminished and bowed down through oppression, trouble, and sorrow. Wesley: They—men who when exalted grow insolent and secure; Low—by God's just judgment.

Exaltation without humility invites being brought low. Arminians warn that security after blessing is itself sin God may judge.

Reformed

Calvin: mankind do not forever continue the same—decreased by war, pestilence, or turmoil when God withdraws the grace that was the hidden spring of prosperity.

Diminishment when blessing is withdrawn. Common confession: diminished; brought low; oppression, evil, sorrow.

Psalms 107:40

40He pours contempt on princes, and causes them to wander in a trackless waste.

Arminian

He pours contempt on princes, and causes them to wander in a trackless waste. Wesley: Contempt—renders them despicable; Wander—banishes them from courts and kingdoms into desolate wildernesses.

Both readings hold: princes not exempt. The accent falls on leveling justice—rank cannot shield contempt God pours.

Reformed

Calvin: God pours contempt on princes—their inward honor was His gift; when He hurls them from loftiness, even dependants may sport with them. History corroborates this overruling providence.

Majesty of rulers stands by God's gift and falls by His judgment. Both agree: contempt on princes; wander in wastes.

Psalms 107:41

41Yet he lifts the needy out of their affliction, and increases their families like a flock.

Arminian

Yet he lifts the needy out of their affliction, and increases their families like flocks.

Classical Arminians preach reversal that comforts the low and warns the proud.

Reformed

Calvin: as contrast, the poor and ignoble are exalted and houses increased—those held in no estimation suddenly rise. Men would recognize providence if perversity did not blind them.

God lifts the needy. Raises the needy; families like flocks.

Psalms 107:42

42The upright will see it, and be glad. All the wicked will shut their mouths.

Arminian

The upright will see it, and be glad. All wickedness shuts its mouth.

Both hear: upright rejoice; wickedness silenced. Arminian interpreters hear moral clarity—providence that gladdens the just and stops the mouth of evil.

Reformed

Calvin: the righteous see and rejoice—faith illuminates so they delight in God's equity and goodness; the wicked remain perplexed and mute, seeing yet not seeing.

Joy of the upright; mouth of wickedness stopped. Both see: upright glad; wickedness shuts its mouth.

Psalms 107:43

43Whoever is wise will pay attention to these things. They will consider the loving kindnesses of the LORD.

Arminian

Whoever is wise will pay attention to these things. They will consider the loving kindnesses of the LORD.

Wisdom is attending and considering—steadfast love studied in the stories of cry and rescue. Classical Arminians urge every hearer to observe and trust that love.

Reformed

Calvin: men begin to be wise when they turn attention to God's works; all others are fools however sharp they seem. Observe—not bare glance but careful meditation; then they understand Jehovah's loving-kindness, which shines in all He does.

Wisdom equals observing providence and steadfast love. Undisputed: wise; attend; consider steadfast love.

Key contrast Both confess that wisdom is attending to these deliverances and considering the Lord's steadfast love. Arminians emphasize sincere observation that leads any willing heart to trust that love. Reformed interpreters follow Calvin: only those given to observe God's works are wise; neglect of providence is folly however clever.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic Arminian and Reformed comment on Psalm 107:1–43, in the writers’ own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“The psalmist here observes God's providential care of the children of men in general, and shews how he helps those that are in any distress, in answer to their prayers. He instances in banishment and dispersion; captivity and imprisonment; sickness; distress at sea; and the disposal of families and nations. Word—His command, or blessing. For—He doth not inflict these judgments without cause, but for the punishment of sin in some, and the prevention of it in others. Whoso is wise, and will observe these things, even they shall understand the lovingkindness of the Lord.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 107)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Psalmist teaches that human affairs are not regulated by the fickle and uncertain wheel of fortune, but that we must observe the judgments of God in the different vicissitudes which occur in the world. Adversity and all the ills which mankind endure are to be regarded as so many tokens of God's displeasure, by which he summons them, on account of their sins, before his judicial throne. But prosperity ought also to be attributed to his grace, that he may always receive the praise of being a merciful Father and an impartial Judge. Whosoever is wise, so as to observe these things, shall understand the loving-kindness of Jehovah.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Psalm 107)
