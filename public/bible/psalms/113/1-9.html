Passage

Psalms 113

Verses 1–9 · WEB · Voices

Egyptian Hallel opens: servants are summoned to praise the Lord's name from sunrise to sunset and forever. He is high above the nations, yet looks far down; He raises the poor from dust and ash heap to sit with princes, and settles the barren woman in her home as a home as joyous mother. The first Hallelujah frames condescension and mercy together.

Psalms 113:1

1Praise the LORD! Praise, you servants of the LORD, praise the LORD’s name.

Arminian

Praise the Lord: the Egyptian Hallel opens with a triple call—praise, servants, name. Wesley frames the psalm as exhortation for glory and greatness, then grace and goodness. Servants are not spectators; they are the choir.

Hallelujah is owed. Wesley’s heirs hear a sincere summons to willing praise under grace—the name is to be praised because it is worthy, and servants answer freely, not as mute instruments.

Reformed

Calvin: abundant reasons for all men without exception to praise God; yet the faithful, endued with spiritual perception, are addressed in particular. The repetition stirs cold hearts to unwearied zeal. If Levites lead, they show the way so the whole Church may respond in one holy chorus; under Christ all are a royal priesthood.

Praise is commanded because God has already shown Himself. Wesley concurs: Praise the Lord; servants of the Lord.

Psalms 113:2

2Blessed be the LORD’s name, from this time forward and forever more.

Arminian

Blessed be the name from this time forward and forevermore. Duration matches dignity—praise is not a festival mood that expires at sundown.

Forevermore invites lifelong and generational blessing of the name. Arminian readers refuse a praise that treats God's name as seasonal fashion; the time-frame is as wide as His mercy held out.

Reformed

Calvin: the praises of God must be continued throughout the whole course of our life. If His name is to be continually praised, we should at least endeavor, during our brief pilgrimage, that the remembrance of it may flourish after we are dead.

Praise is meant to persevere. Where they meet: from this time forward and forevermore.

Psalms 113:3

3From the rising of the sun to its going down, the LORD’s name is to be praised.

Arminian

From sunrise to sunset the name is to be praised. Geography joins chronology—east to west as well as now to forever.

Both traditions hear: no latitude is exempt. One hears a public name meant for the inhabited earth, not a private password for one zip code. The Hallel already stretches toward the nations.

Reformed

Calvin: extends the glory of God's name to all parts of the earth; apathy is inexcusable if we do not make its praises resound. Under the law, right praise was centered in Judea; His works visible to all nations are worthy of the whole world's admiration.

After the gospel, exaltation above the nations is more evident.

Psalms 113:4

4The LORD is high above all nations, his glory above the heavens.

Arminian

The Lord is high above all nations; His glory above the heavens. Height is not absence—it is supremacy over every people and every sky.

No rival nation-god, no ceiling that outranks Him. Classical Arminians preach a Most High whose glory invites the nations rather than hiding from them.

Reformed

Calvin: in extolling the name so highly, the prophet shows there is no ground for indifference; silence would savor of impiety. When he adds that God is high above all nations, there is implied reproach of apathy in praise among the chosen people who are eye-witnesses.

Loftiness that fills thoughts with admiration. The LORD is high above all nations.

Psalms 113:5

5Who is like the LORD, our God, who has his seat on high,

Arminian

Who is like the Lord our God, seated on high? The question is rhetorical worship—uniqueness, not puzzle. Wesley's outline places glory and greatness here before the descent of grace.

Both traditions confess that no one compares with the LORD. Arminian readers and Reformed together refuse idols and demiurges; the fork is not whether anyone matches Him.

Reformed

Calvin strengthens celebration by contrasting the height of God's glory and power with His unbounded goodness. Not that goodness can be separated from glory; the distinction is made out of regard to men who could not endure majesty unless He kindly drew us.

Seated on high, yet about to look down. Who is like the Lord our God?

Psalms 113:6

6who stoops down to see in heaven and on the earth?

Arminian

Who stoops down to see in heaven and on the earth. Wesley: who is so high that it is wonderful condescension to notice even the heavenly host, and much more sinful and miserable men upon earth. Height and nearness are held together.

Looking far down is paternal notice, not reluctant surveillance. The God who is sincerely good toward creatures does not need to be coaxed into seeing the earth.

Reformed

Calvin: God's dwelling above the heavens does not prevent Him from showing Himself near and providing for our welfare. He might righteously hold even angels in contempt were He not moved by paternal regard; if He humbles Himself toward angels, what of men groveling on the earth? It is not from our proximity but from His free choice that He makes us objects of peculiar care.

Free condescension from infinite distance. Wesley says as much: wonderful looking-down.

Psalms 113:7

7He raises up the poor out of the dust, and lifts up the needy from the ash heap,

Arminian

He raises up the poor out of the dust, and lifts up the needy from the ash heap. Hannah's song echoes; providence reverses what markets and courts ignore.

Either reading: elevation of the low. Arminian interpreters hear mercy that sincerely reaches the ash heap—without requiring the poor to climb into heaven first. The raise is God's act; the needy are truly lifted.

Reformed

Calvin: solely by God's appointment things undergo changes far surpassing anticipation. Vicissitudes teach that all is regulated by His secret counsel. He enjoins admiration of providence in marvellous occurrence—cowherds elevated to power—so attention is arrested by the unexpected.

This is not a matter of chance social mobility. Both sides agree: poor raised from dust; needy from ash heap.

Key contrast Both confess that God raises the poor from dust and ash heap. Arminians hear this as sincere, fatherly mercy toward the lowly in the theater of common and saving kindness. Reformed interpreters typically follow Calvin's accent: unexpected elevations regulated by God's secret counsel, most brightly in the Church as His principal theater.

Psalms 113:8

8that he may set him with princes, even with the princes of his people.

Arminian

To make them sit with princes, even with the princes of his people. Wesley: as He did Joseph, David, and others; His people are far more honourable than heathen princes. The seat is honor among God's own, not mere celebrity.

God can and does exalt; the invitation of the gospel is not contradicted by surprising promotions. Classical Arminians refuse a fatalism that calls every rise and fall meaningless.

Reformed

Calvin: men of mean condition are not merely elevated to petty sovereignty but invested with authority over God's holy people—of far more consequence than rule elsewhere. The Church is the principal theater where God displays tokens of wonderful power, wisdom, and righteousness.

Exaltation unto the people of God. Wesley concurs: sit with princes of His people.

Psalms 113:9

9He settles the barren woman in her home as a joyful mother of children. Praise the LORD!

Arminian

He settles the barren woman in her home as a joyful mother of children. Praise the LORD! Wesley's grace-and-goodness section lands here; Hannah stands behind the line. Home and joy answer barrenness.

Both traditions see: ordinary providence that still astonishes. Arminian interpreters preach a Father who can open the closed womb and the closed house—The Hallel ends as it began: Praise the Lord.

Reformed

Calvin: another work which, if apparently less notable, ought not less to engage us. When a long-barren woman unexpectedly becomes mother of a numerous family, astonishment is constrained. She fills the house with children; joy is attributed to mothers because progeny is preferred to many other advantages. Since God alters the current of events and makes the barren fruitful, insensibility is culpable.

Admire the hand in household mercy. Joyous mother; Praise the Lord.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Voices from both camps

Older voices from both traditions on Psalm 113:1–9.

Arminian Notable voices

“We are here exhorted to praise God, particularly for his glory and greatness, and for his grace and goodness. Who is so high, that it is a wonderful condescension in him to take any notice of his heavenly host, and much more of sinful and miserable men upon earth. He raises the poor to sit with princes—as he did Joseph, David, and others. His people are in God's account far more honourable than the princes of Heathen nations.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 113)

Reformed Notable voices

“This psalm contains abundant reasons for all men without exception to praise God. The faithful alone being endued with spiritual perception to recognize His hand, the prophet addresses them in particular. God's dwelling above the heavens does not prevent Him from showing Himself near and providing for our welfare. He raises the poor from the dust and lifts the afflicted from the dunghill, that He may place him with princes—changes far surpassing our anticipations, regulated by the secret counsel of God. He makes the barren woman dwell in the family, a joyful mother of children.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Psalm 113)
