Psalms 150

Verses 1–6 · ESV · Voices

Closing doxology of the Psalter and last of the final Hallelujahs (146–150): praise God in His sanctuary and in His mighty heavens; praise Him for mighty deeds and according to His excellent greatness. Trumpet, lute, harp, tambourine, dance, strings, pipe, sounding and clashing cymbals—then the widest summons: let everything that has breath praise the LORD. Praise the LORD!

Psalms 150:1

1Praise the LORD! Praise God in his sanctuary; praise him in his mighty heavens!

Arminian

Praise the LORD! Praise God in His sanctuary; praise Him in His mighty heavens! Wesley: sanctuary—in His temple; firmament—in heaven, let angels praise. Clarke: full chorus of the grand Hallelujah’s close; sanctuary as temple or holiness; firmament—utmost expanse of power.

Shared Psalter finale begins: both camps worship in sanctuary and heavens. Classical Arminians join the chorus without shrinking the call.

Reformed

Calvin: spiritual worship of praise; sanctuary as heaven; firmament of power loses us in admiration—lift eyes to the heavenly sanctuary to awaken languid praise.

Reformed accent: heaven’s throne kindles earth’s praise. Shared: Praise the LORD; sanctuary; mighty heavens.

Psalms 150:2

2Praise him for his mighty deeds; praise him according to his excellent greatness!

Arminian

Praise Him for His mighty deeds; praise Him according to His excellent greatness! Clarke: acts in creation, government, mercy, justice; praise matching the multitude of His majesty.

Shared: deeds and greatness as twin reasons. Both camps refuse skimpy praise for a great God.

Reformed

Calvin: celebrate power and greatness mirrored in the heavens; mere taste of immensity deeply affects; God will not reject praises offered according to our capacity.

Reformed pastoral: meditate till praise kindles. Shared: mighty deeds; excellent greatness.

Psalms 150:3

3Praise him with trumpet sound; praise him with lute and harp!

Arminian

Praise Him with trumpet sound; praise Him with lute and harp! Clarke: shofar’s noble sound; nebel and kinnor—stringed praise.

Shared instrumentation. Classical Arminians hear wholehearted skill offered to God, not entertainment as end.

Reformed

Calvin: various instruments under the legal economy teach diligence—bring all powers to this service; multiplicity led a weak people from vain pleasures to holy joy.

Reformed accent: consecrated powers. Shared: trumpet; lute and harp.

Psalms 150:4

4Praise him with tambourine and dance; praise him with strings and pipe!

Arminian

Praise Him with tambourine and dance; praise Him with strings and pipe! Clarke: toph; machol as pipe rather than dance in his reading; minnim—ordered strings; ugab—syrinx.

Shared: more instruments, same Name. Both camps keep the point—zealous praise—not a dispute over organology as theology.

Reformed

Calvin: timbrel, pipe, chords, organ—enumerate to pour forth all joy in God’s praises.

Reformed accent: unrestrained holy gladness. Shared: tambourine; strings and pipe.

Psalms 150:5

5Praise him with sounding cymbals; praise him with loud clashing cymbals!

Arminian

Praise Him with sounding cymbals; praise Him with loud clashing cymbals! Clarke: brass plates clanging; louder high-sounding pair.

Shared crescendo. Classical Arminians allow loud praise that still aims at the LORD, not at the noise itself.

Reformed

Calvin: cymbals of sound and of jubilation—another summons to full-strength praise.

Reformed accent: jubilation consecrated. Shared: sounding and clashing cymbals.

Psalms 150:6

6Let everything that has breath praise the LORD! Praise the LORD!

Arminian

Let everything that has breath praise the LORD! Praise the LORD! Wesley’s brief close matches the width. Clarke: all voices and breath instruments; universal burst of Hallelujah closes the ceremony and the book’s praise.

Shared last line of the Psalter: both camps worship. Classical Arminians hear a sincere, wide call—everything that has breath. Praise the LORD!

Reformed

Calvin: whatever breathes—turning from Israel’s ceremonies to men in general; songs once heard in Judea would resound worldwide; we join the Jews’ symphony until an eternal hallelujah with elect angels.

Reformed hope: global and everlasting praise. Shared with Clarke and Wesley: everything that has breath; Praise the LORD!

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on praise in the sanctuary and mighty heavens, for deeds and greatness, with every instrument, and finally everything that has breath—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 150:1–6, the Psalter’s closing doxology.

Arminian Notable voices

“This Psalm is without title and author in the Hebrew. It is properly the full chorus of all voices and instruments in the temple, at the conclusion of the grand Hallelujah, to which the five concluding Psalms belong. Praise God in his sanctuary—in the temple, or in his holiness; praise him in the firmament of his power—through the whole expanse, to the utmost limits of his power. Praise him for his mighty acts—whether manifested in creation, government, mercy or justice. Praise him according to his excellent greatness—let the praise be such as is becoming so great, so holy, and so glorious a Being. Let every thing that hath breath praise the Lord—let all join together, and put forth all your strength and skill; then let a universal burst with Hallelujah close the grand ceremony. It is evident that this Psalm has no other meaning than merely the summoning up all the voices, and all the instruments, to complete the service in full chorus.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 150)

Reformed Notable voices

“This psalm in general commends the spiritual worship of God, which consists in sacrifices of praise. By the sanctuary there is little doubt that heaven is here meant; the firmament of his power—the expanse where we have proof of matchless power, so that we cannot look to the heavens without being lost in admiration. Praise him in his might; praise him for the plenitude of his greatness—if we would have our minds kindled, meditate upon his power and greatness. Praise him with sound of trumpet, with psaltery and harp, with timbrel and pipe, upon chords and organ, upon cymbals: sundry instruments to teach that we cannot apply ourselves too diligently to the praises of God—bring all powers and devote them wholly. Whatsoever breathes, let it praise God: as yet he addressed those under the ceremonies of the law; now he turns to men in general, tacitly intimating a time when the same songs would resound in every quarter of the globe. And in this prediction we have been joined in the same symphony with the Jews, that we may worship God with constant sacrifices of praise, until being gathered into the kingdom of heaven, we sing with elect angels an eternal hallelujah.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 150)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English editions of the sources named.
