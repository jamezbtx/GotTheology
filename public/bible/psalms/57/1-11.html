Passage

Psalms 57

Verses 1–11 · WEB · Voices

To the choirmaster: according to Do Not Destroy. A Miktam of David, when he fled from Saul, in the cave. Prayer and complaint under storm (vv. 1–6) then steadfast praise (vv. 7–11): refuge under wings; God Most High who fulfills his purpose; shame for the trampler; lions and sharp tongues; exaltation refrain; net and pit rebound; fixed heart, dawn praise, steadfast love to the heavens.

Psalms 57:1

1Be merciful to me, God, be merciful to me, for my soul takes refuge in you. Yes, in the shadow of your wings, I will take refuge, until disaster has passed.

Arminian

Wesley: Al-taschith—destroy not—sum of the plea; the cave frames urgent mercy. Double “be merciful” and refuge under wings until calamities pass.

Mercy is begged because the soul actually takes refuge; wings invite trust that must be exercised till the storm passes. Grace shelters those who flee to God, not those who stand aloof.

Reformed

Calvin: repetition proves uncommon grief; the plea for mercy is hope in God from innermost affections. Shadow of wings—God shelters like a hen; hope lodges under every tempest.

Refuge is God’s gift and the soul’s act of trust. Mercy is not earned calm; it is shelter until destruction passes by God’s timing.

Psalms 57:2

2I cry out to God Most High, to God who accomplishes my requests for me.

Arminian

Wesley: cry to God most high who performs for the sufferer. Prayer follows refuge; Most High is addressed as the one who finishes what he starts for his servant.

Crying out is the appointed means under pressure. Purpose fulfilled does not cancel the need to cry; grace that completes still summons petition.

Reformed

Calvin: to hope he adds prayer; he addresses God as who performs whatsoever he promised and perfects the work begun. God never forsakes the workmanship of his hands.

Most High fulfills purpose; the cry rests on God’s constancy, not the saint’s resolve alone.

Psalms 57:3

3He will send from heaven, and save me, he rebukes the one who is pursuing me. Selah. God will send out his loving kindness and his truth.

Arminian

Wesley: Send forth—will discover mercy and truth by gracious help in pursuance of promises. Shame for the trampler; salvation paired with covenant love.

Confidence that God will save leaves room for the trampler to be shamed and checked. Shared seriousness—imprecation here is not in doubt: trampling invites God’s public reverse, not private revenge.

Reformed

Calvin: he glories in help as if already seen; send from heaven marks signal deliverance. God strikes enemies with shame when he disappoints them; mercy and truth are the hands of assistance.

Shame on the trampler is judicial hope. Steadfast love and faithfulness are God’s sent means, not human leverage.

Key contrast Both take shame on the trampler as serious petition for God’s justice. Arminians underline that the check may yet turn the aggressor; Reformed interpreters stress that God himself sends heaven’s rescue and confounds according to mercy and truth.

Psalms 57:4

4My soul is among lions. I lie among those who are set on fire, even the sons of men, whose teeth are spears and arrows, and their tongue a sharp sword.

Arminian

Wesley: Lions—fierce and bloody men; on fire—fire-brands breathing wrath and incensing Saul. Teeth and tongue name human cruelty.

Lying among lions is honest complaint, not unbelief. Naming spear-teeth and sword-tongues lets prayer be specific; grace does not require pretending the beasts are mild.

Reformed

Calvin: he insists on enemies’ cruelty as plea for speedier help—lions, inflamed hatred, teeth as spears, tongue as sword. Calumny wounds deeper than steel; God can bridle the false tongue.

Fiery beasts are children of man under judgment’s eye. Complaint to God is permitted warfare of the weak.

Psalms 57:5

5Be exalted, God, above the heavens! Let your glory be above all the earth!

Arminian

Wesley: Excited—glorify thy power, goodness, justice, faithfulness by my deliverance; above—to the highest degree. Deliverance and doxology are joined.

Asking God to be exalted includes asking that rescue display his character. Glory over all the earth is the sufferer’s larger frame, not self alone.

Reformed

Calvin: when the wicked’s audacity is unrestrained, the conspiracy demands signal manifestation. God, appearing for his people, advances his own glory; opposition to them is opposition to him.

Exaltation of God is the refrain that outranks the lions. Glory over earth answers blasphemy and fear together.

Psalms 57:6

6They have prepared a net for my steps. My soul is bowed down. They dig a pit before me. They fall into the middle of it themselves. Selah.

Arminian

Wesley’s arc: from complaint to the trap’s rebound. Net and pit aimed at the righteous rebound on diggers—moral order under God.

Bowed soul may still narrate providence’s irony. Shared seriousness—malice digging pits is not a joke; God may let hunters fall into their own works.

Reformed

Calvin: treachery practiced; soul bowed like a bird under the fowler’s net. They fall into the pit themselves—God’s justice reversing the snare.

Providence turns the net. Both camps hear judicial seriousness: the pit is serious danger and grave judgment when diggers fall in.

Key contrast Both treat the rebound of net and pit as God’s serious justice. Arminians typically stress the moral warning that malice may yet be checked and reversed; Calvinists stress that God himself turns the snare on the wicked according to his righteous will.

Psalms 57:7

7My heart is steadfast, God. My heart is steadfast. I will sing, yes, I will sing praises.

Arminian

Wesley: Fixed—in a stedfast belief of thy promises. Double steadfastness turns complaint into song.

The fixed heart is belief that must be held and sung. Steadfastness is living trust answering prior fear; melody is obedience of joy.

Reformed

Calvin: prepared/ready heart—cheerfulness and cordiality opposed to lip-service and sluggishness; or fixed by due meditation. He addresses voluntary sacrifice with sincere fervor.

Steadfast heart precedes singing. God-ward fixity, not mood, funds the melody.

Psalms 57:8

8Wake up, my glory! Wake up, lute and harp! I will wake up the dawn.

Arminian

Wesley: My glory—my tongue, instrument of singing; Awake—employ all powers of soul and body. Dawn is met with praise instruments.

Waking glory, harp, and lyre is active consecration. Grace that steadies the heart still summons the whole self to praise early.

Reformed

Calvin: he calls tongue, psaltery, and harp to celebrate God’s name; heart first, mouth second, instruments third to stimulate ardor. One awakened is unremitting in praise.

Awake the dawn is zeal, not mere poetry. Praise claims the morning before fear reclaims it.

Psalms 57:9

9I will give thanks to you, Lord, among the peoples. I will sing praises to you among the nations.

Arminian

Wesley: The people—among Israelites and Heathens as occasion serves. Thanks goes public beyond the cave.

Nations and peoples as audience imply praise that invites others. Thanksgiving among the nations is mission-shaped gratitude under deliverance.

Reformed

Calvin: nations as auditors imply David representing Christ; Paul cites this toward Gentile praise (Rom 15:9). Proclaiming to the deaf would be absurd—Gentiles are brought to knowledge of God.

Covenant praise spills past Israel’s borders by God’s design.

Psalms 57:10

10For your great loving kindness reaches to the heavens, and your truth to the skies.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: mercy and truth discovered in help. Love to the heavens and faithfulness to the clouds are the reason for worldwide praise.

Greatness of steadfast love grounds confidence to keep seeking. Faithfulness to the clouds means promises reach as high as need.

Reformed

Calvin: sum of the song—world full of goodness and truth. Goodness induces God to promise liberally; faithfulness assures he is constant in fulfilling. Order of perfections matters.

Hesed and faithfulness, not merit, fill the sky. Doxology rests on God’s character spanning heaven and cloud.

Psalms 57:11

11Be exalted, God, above the heavens. Let your glory be over all the earth.

Arminian

Wesley: the refrain returns—glorify by deliverance to the highest degree. Cave prayer ends in earth-wide glory.

Repeated exaltation shows praise that must keep asking God to be seen as great. The needy still beg that glory cover the earth.

Reformed

Calvin: concludes praying God would arise and not suffer glory obscured or wicked audacity intolerable; or hasten Gentile calling and mighty judgments for the nations’ subjection.

Final refrain is God exalted, not self secured. Glory over all the earth closes the miktam.

Key contrast Both end with God exalted above the heavens. Arminians stress the repeated plea as ongoing praise and petition the delivered must keep offering; Reformed Christians emphasize that God arises to vindicate his glory and fulfill the worldwide praise already promised.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on refuge under God’s wings, shame for the trampler, exaltation of God over earth, and a fixed heart that sings among the nations, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“David begins with prayer and complaint, though not as without hope (vv. 1–6), and concludes with joy and praise (vv. 7–11). Al-taschith—destroy not—some take as a preface containing the sum of the psalm. The cave—Adullam or Engedi. Send forth—will discover mercy and truth by gracious help in pursuance of his promises. Lions—fierce and bloody men; on fire—fire-brands breathing wrath. Excited—glorify thy power, goodness, justice, faithfulness by my deliverance. Fixed—in a stedfast belief of thy promises. My glory—my tongue; Awake—employ all powers of soul and body. The people—Israelites and Heathens as occasion serves.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 57)

Reformed Notable voices

“The repetition of the prayer proves that David’s grief and apprehension were of no common description. His plea for mercy is his having hoped in God from his innermost affections. The divine protection is compared to the shadow of wings… He will cry to God Most High, who performs whatsoever he has promised and carries forward to perfection the work begun. He shall send from heaven—a signal, miraculous deliverance—and put to shame him who would swallow him up; mercy and truth are the hands by which assistance is extended. Among lions whose tongues are sharp swords he cries, Exalt thyself, O God. His heart is prepared—ready with cheerfulness, opposed to lip-service and sluggishness. He will praise among the peoples; the whole world is full of God’s goodness and truth.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 57)
