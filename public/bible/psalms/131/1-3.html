Passage

Psalms 131

Verses 1–3 · ESV · Voices

A Song of Ascents Of David: a renunciation of haughty heart and lofty eyes, and of occupying oneself with things too great and marvelous. The soul is calmed and quieted like a weaned child with its mother—then Israel is called to hope in the LORD from this time forth and forevermore. Pilgrim maturity here is humble rest, not restless grasping.

Psalms 131:1

1O LORD, my heart is not lifted up; my eyes are not raised too high; I do not occupy myself with things too great and too marvelous for me.

Arminian

Heart not lifted; eyes not raised too high; no occupying with things too great and marvelous. Humility refuses proud reach beyond one’s call.

Classical Arminians hear a freely embraced lowliness. Under grace, choose the quiet path: do not chase greatness God has not assigned; leave marvelous things in His hands.

Reformed

Heart not elated; eyes not lofty; no walking in great things above me. Contentment keeps to the lot God marks out.

Reformed accent: pride drives rash flights; humility confines us to God’s call. Not one step without His leading—ambition unyoked from His appointment confounds the world.

Key contrast Both commend humble refusal of haughty heart and overreaching ambition. Arminians typically stress a grace-enabled choice to stay low and leave greatness to God; Reformed readers typically stress contentment with the lot and calling God sovereignly assigns.

Psalms 131:2

2But I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother; like a weaned child is my soul within me.

Arminian

Soul calmed and quieted—like a weaned child with its mother. Dependence on God’s providence replaces restless craving.

Arminian pastoral: when the mind is provoked, quiet it toward trust. Weaning means relying on the LORD as the infant relies on its mother—helpless, held, and at rest.

Reformed

Soul set like a weaned child—silenced from ambition’s tumult, satisfied with small things. Quiet rests on the bosom, not in discontented agitation.

Reformed comfort: simplicity that Christ commends stills wayward desire. The soul that abides in the Lord’s calling enjoys peace the restless cannot invent.

Psalms 131:3

3O Israel, hope in the LORD from this time forth and forevermore.

Arminian

O Israel, hope in the LORD—from now forevermore. Personal quiet widens into a people’s lasting trust.

Classical Arminians press the open call: keep hoping. Forevermore invites continual reliance, renewed from this time forth without retiring from faith.

Reformed

Hope in the LORD from this time forth and forevermore. Right hope grows from humble, sober views under God’s leading.

Reformed pastoral: Israel’s safety is tied to hope God appoints. Forevermore is His span; the quieted soul points the church to endless trust in Him alone.

Key contrast Both summon lasting hope in the LORD. Arminians typically emphasize ongoing, freely renewed trust from this time forth; Reformed readers typically emphasize hope ordered by God-given humility within His sovereign care forevermore.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on unlifted heart, a weaned and quieted soul, and Israel’s lasting hope in the LORD—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 131:1–3.

Arminian Notable voices

“Lord, my heart is not haughty—The principle of pride has no place in my heart; and consequently the high, lofty, and supercilious look does not appear in my eyes. I neither look up, with desire to obtain, to the state of others, nor look down with contempt to the meanness or poverty of those below me. I have not exercised myself in high matters, nor in great matters, wonderful or sublime things, too high for me, alien from me, and that do not belong to a person in my sphere and situation in life. I have behaved and quieted myself, as a child—I have been under the rod of others, and when chastised have not complained; my silence under affliction was the fullest proof that I neither murmured nor repined, but received all as coming from the hands of a just God. My soul is even as a weaned child—I felt I must forego many conveniences and comforts which I once enjoyed; and these I gave up without repining. Let Israel hope in the Lord—Act all as I have done; trust in him who is the God of justice and compassion; and, after you have suffered awhile, he will make bare his arm and deliver you. Short as it is, this is a most instructive Psalm.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 131)

Reformed Notable voices

“O Jehovah! My heart has not been elated. David is desirous to show that he had not been influenced by ambition or pride, but had submitted himself with a quiet and humble spirit to the divine disposal. We should be contented with the lot which God has marked out for us, consider what he calls us to, and not aim at fashioning our own lot—be moderate in our desires, avoid rash undertakings, and confine ourselves cheerfully within our own sphere. Pride leads men to dare presumptuous flights and throw the world into confusion. His eyes were not lifted up; all the senses, as well as his heart, were subjected to the restraints of humility. He did not think himself at liberty to move one step unless called to it by God. He likens himself to a weaned child; he dismissed the anxieties which disquiet the man of ambition, and was willing to be satisfied with small things. My soul over me is quieted—sweetly and peacefully on his bosom, undisturbed by inordinate desires. He calls upon Israel to hope in the Lord. Our hope is of the right kind when we cherish humble and sober views of ourselves, and neither wish nor attempt anything without the leading and approbation of God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 131)
