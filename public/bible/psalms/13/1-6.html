Passage

Psalms 13

Verses 1–6 · ESV · Voices

Psalm 13 is David’s fourfold “how long?” under prolonged affliction: he asks whether the LORD will forget him forever and hide his face, while he takes counsel in his soul with sorrow all the day and the enemy is exalted over him. He pleads that God consider and answer, lighten his eyes lest he sleep the sleep of death, and not let the enemy boast of prevailing or foes rejoice at his shaking. Then trust breaks through: he has trusted in steadfast love, his heart shall rejoice in salvation, and he will sing to the LORD because he has dealt bountifully with him.

Psalms 13:1

1How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?

Arminian

Arminian hearing: shared cry—how long of seeming forgetfulness and a hidden face. Wesley frames the psalm as complaint, then prayer, then rejoicing in hope. The “how long” is not blasphemy but perplexed petition; keep directing the question to God rather than quitting under delay.

Pastoral: prolonged silence feels like forever. Grace enables the sufferer to keep crying how long; the face that feels hidden is still the face one asks to see.

Reformed

Calvin: he complains according to the feeling of his own mind of being neglected by God—not that persuasion of the promises was extinguished, or that he did not repose on grace. When calamities weigh long and no sign of aid appears, the thought forces itself that God has forgotten us. Flesh judges forsaken; the eyes of the mind, guided by faith, penetrate to grace hidden in darkness. By this very complaint he evidences that faith rose higher, contrary to the judgment of the flesh—else how could he direct groanings to God?

Reformed accent: shared—“how long” under hidden face. Faith apprehends invisible providence in the conflict; the cry itself is wrestling, not proof that God has truly forgotten.

Key contrast Both confess that the sufferer may cry “how long” when the LORD seems to forget forever and hide his face. Arminian readers stress perplexed petition that grace enables and the hearer must keep lifting—complaint that turns toward God rather than quitting under delay. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress that flesh concludes forsaken while faith apprehends invisible providence: the complaint itself shows faith rising contrary to the judgment of the flesh, securing welfare in God’s hand though grace is hidden in darkness.

Psalms 13:2

2How long must I take counsel in my soul and have sorrow in my heart all the day? How long shall my enemy be exalted over me?

Arminian

Wesley: How long—Shall I be in such perplexities, not knowing what course to take?

Arminian pastoral: shared anguish—inner counsel that cannot settle, daily sorrow, and an exalted enemy. Self-deliberation without light exhausts; bring the tangled counsel to God instead of spinning alone. The enemy’s exaltation is named so it may be opposed in prayer.

Reformed

Calvin: in adversity men look around for remedies and are exhausted to no purpose with a multitude of suggestions; sorrow returns and is renewed every day. God promises the spirit of counsel, yet does not always give it at the beginning—he suffers the faithful for a time to be embarrassed by long deliberation, as if entangled among thorns. Adversaries triumph the more boldly when they see the godly wasted by continual languor—an argument of great weight in prayer, for God will not bear the cruel insolence of enemies who feast on misery.

Reformed accent: shared—fruitless self-counsel and daily sorrow under an exalted foe. Delay of settled counsel is permitted trial; the enemy’s boast becomes plea, not despair’s last word.

Psalms 13:3

3Consider and answer me, O LORD my God; lighten my eyes, lest I sleep the sleep of death,

Arminian

Wesley: Lighten—Revive and comfort, and deliver me from the darkness of death, which is ready to come upon me.

Arminian reading: shared plea—consider, answer, lighten. Eyes near death’s sleep need revival and comfort from the LORD my God. Ask for light before darkness closes; the petition is personal trust joined to urgent need.

Reformed

Calvin: as when God does not promptly assist it seems he does not behold, David asks first to be looked upon, then succored. To enlighten the eyes is to give the breath of life; rigor of life appears chiefly in the eyes. Sleep here is a metaphor for death. Unless God cause the light of life to shine, he will be overwhelmed with death’s darkness—already as a man without life unless God breathe new vigor. Confidence of life depends on this: though the world threaten a thousand deaths, God has numberless means of restoring us to life.

Reformed accent: shared—lighten lest death’s sleep. Life-light is God’s gift when human vigor is gone; the cry assumes he still regards his own.

Psalms 13:4

4lest my enemy say, "I have prevailed over him," lest my foes rejoice because I am shaken.

Arminian

Arminian hearing: shared motive—do not let the enemy boast of prevailing or foes rejoice at a shaken saint. The plea ties God’s honor to the sufferer’s standing: mockery of the godly should not stand unanswered. Keep asking that exaltation of the foe be restrained.

Reformed

Calvin: it would ill become God’s character to abandon his servant to the mockery of the ungodly. Enemies lay in ambush watching the hour of ruin to deride the fall. It is God’s peculiar office to repress the audacity of the wicked when they glory in wickedness. David had in conscience a testimony of integrity and trusted the goodness of his cause—so it would be unbecoming to leave him without succor. We can pray this way only when we fight under God’s standard, so that enemies cannot triumph without wickedly triumphing over God himself.

Reformed accent: shared—restrain the boasting foe. The argument is God’s honor against insolent glorying, not private revenge.

Psalms 13:5

5But I have trusted in your steadfast love; my heart shall rejoice in your salvation.

Arminian

Arminian reading: shared turn—from counsel-in-self to trust in steadfast love, with a heart set to rejoice in salvation. Wesley’s frame: rejoices in hope. Trust is grace-worked confidence the sufferer must actually take up; the heart’s rejoicing looks forward in hope while affliction still presses.

Pastoral: do not wait for every care to vanish before trusting mercy. Turn from exhausting self-counsel to the steadfast love already named as ground.

Reformed

Calvin: he does not as yet feel how much he has profited by praying; but depending on the hope of deliverance which the faithful promise of God enabled him to entertain, he uses that hope as a shield against temptations that urge despair. He declares his resolution to continue firm in reliance upon the grace of God and in the hope of salvation. By faith we apprehend the grace of God hidden from the understanding of the flesh. Goodness is placed first as the cause of deliverance; exult in the future marks continued exercise—no affliction shall shake the joy of faith out of his heart.

Reformed accent: shared—trust in steadfast love, heart rejoicing in salvation. Faith holds the promise when the issue is still unseen; grace apprehended, not flesh’s timeline, sustains perseverance in prayer.

Key contrast Both confess that the turn is trust in the LORD’s steadfast love and a heart that shall rejoice in his salvation. Arminian readers stress grace-enabled trust the sufferer must exercise—rejoicing in hope while turning from self-counsel to mercy. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress that faith apprehends grace still hidden from flesh: goodness is the cause of deliverance, and resolve to continue firm on the promise shields against cares that urge despair—even before prayer’s profit is felt.

Psalms 13:6

6I will sing to the LORD, because he has dealt bountifully with me.

Arminian

Wesley: I will sing—It is a common thing for David and other prophets to speak of future deliverances as if they were already come, that so they may signify both the infallible certainty of the thing, and their firm assurance thereof.

Arminian pastoral: shared song—bounty spoken as ground of praise. Singing before the full sight of relief marks certainty and assurance, not pretend blindness. Let hope put a song in the mouth while still waiting.

Reformed

Calvin: though he had not yet obtained what he desired, being fully convinced God was at hand to grant deliverance, he pledges thanks. Engage in prayer ready also to sing; that is impossible unless persuaded prayers will not be ineffectual. Cheerfulness of faith must rise above sorrow and put a song in the mouth for joy reserved in the future though not yet experienced. The word gamal here means to bestow a benefit from pure grace—not reward as due recompense; that suffices to refute those who wrest the verse to prove the merit of works. He places deliverance apparently at a distance immediately before his eyes.

Reformed accent: shared—sing because of bounty. The bounty is pure grace, and praise runs ahead of the visible issue by faith’s cheerfulness.

Key contrast Both confess that the psalmist will sing to the LORD because he has dealt bountifully—praise that can run ahead of full visible relief. Arminian readers, with Wesley, hear future deliverance spoken as already come to signify infallible certainty and firm assurance. Reformed readers, with Calvin, hear gamal as benefit from pure grace, not due recompense for merit, and cheerfulness of faith that sings before the issue is perceived—persuaded that prayer will not be ineffectual.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on prolonged “how long,” counsel and sorrow in the soul, lightened eyes lest death’s sleep, enemies restrained from boasting, trust in steadfast love, and singing of bounty before the issue is seen—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 13:1–6.

Arminian Notable voices

“David complains to God, ver. 1, 2. Prays for help, ver. 3, 4. Rejoices in hope, ver. 5, 6. How long—Shall I be in such perplexities, not knowing what course to take? Lighten—Revive and comfort, and deliver me from the darkness of death, which is ready to come upon me. I will sing—It is a common thing for David and other prophets to speak of future deliverances as if they were already come, that so they may signify both the infallible certainty of the thing, and their firm assurance thereof.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 13)

Reformed Notable voices

“David, overwhelmed by a long succession of calamities, implores the aid of God, the only remedy which remained; and in the close, taking courage, he entertains the assured hope of life from the promise of God even amidst the terrors of death. How long wilt thou forget me forever?—when we do not perceive any sign of divine aid, the thought forces itself that God has forgotten us; yet by faith we apprehend his invisible providence. The eyes of his mind, guided by faith, penetrated to the grace of God although hidden in darkness; by this very complaint he gives evidence that faith rose higher, contrary to the judgment of the flesh. How long shall I take counsel in my soul?—exhausted to no purpose with a multitude of suggestions, sorrow renewed every day, and adversaries triumphing as he is wasted by languor. Look upon me, answer me; enlighten mine eyes, lest I sleep in death—unless God cause the light of life to shine, he is already as a man without life. But I trust in thy goodness; my heart shall exult in thy salvation—depending on the hope of deliverance which the faithful promise enabled him to entertain, he resolves to continue firm in reliance upon grace. I will sing unto the Lord, because he hath dealt bountifully with me—gamal here means to bestow a benefit from pure grace, not due recompense; he pledges thanks before he perceives the issue of his troubles, cheerfulness of faith rising above sorrow.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 13)
