Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 97–104 · ESV · Voices

The Mem stanza overflows with love for the law and all-day meditation. The commandment makes wise beyond enemies; testimonies yield more understanding than teachers and the aged; feet are held from evil paths; rules are kept because God has taught; words taste sweeter than honey; precepts give understanding that hates every false way. Both camps prize loving meditation and Spirit-taught obedience; they differ on whether “you have taught me” names the Spirit’s particular drawing or the illumination offered to every docile reader of the word.

Psalms 119:97

97Oh how I love your law! It is my meditation all the day.

Arminian

Love for the law shown in all-day meditation. Classical Arminians hear affection that studies—love without musing is empty claim.

Shared fire: unless the law ravish the heart, allurements steal us to vanity. Delight and diligence belong together.

Reformed

Calvin: not content with simple affirmation, he exclaims he was inflamed with incredible love; continual meditation is proof. Whoever boasts love yet neglects study and applies the mind elsewhere betrays grossest hypocrisy. Unless God’s law inflame and ravish hearts, many allurements lead to vanity.

Reformed accent: oh-how love is measured by all-day meditation. Ardor that excludes deceit and corruption is commended.

Psalms 119:98

98Your commandment makes me wiser than my enemies, for it is ever with me.

Arminian

The commandment, ever present, outsmarts enemies. Classical Arminians hear Scripture as sufficient counsel against plots—wisdom for safety without matching cunning.

Shared trust: keep the commandment near; craft of the wicked need not teach you deceit.

Reformed

Calvin: more learned than adversaries because a scholar of God’s law—their cunning availed nothing to destroy him. Malice goads the wicked; we fear simplicity imposed on by deceits unless we use their crafts. He found in the law enough to escape their snares.

Reformed confession: commandment ever with us is wisdom against enemies. God’s school outfits the simple without underhand dealings.

Psalms 119:99

99I have more understanding than all my teachers, for your testimonies are my meditation.

Arminian

Meditation on testimonies may outrun human teachers. Classical Arminians hear God as chief tutor—students may surpass masters when God distributes understanding.

Shared humility: progress beyond instructors is thanksgiving, not contempt of teachers who also learned from the word.

Reformed

Calvin: not denying teachers learned useful things from the word; he thanks God for surpassing in proficiency those from whom he learned first elements. Faithful are instructed by men’s labor, yet God still enlightens; thus the scholar surpasses the master—God uses human service while remaining chief teacher. Commit to his tuition to glory that by his guidance we proceeded farther than man’s instruction could lead.

Reformed accent: testimonies meditated make God the top teacher. Sobriety refuses vain boasting.

Psalms 119:100

100I understand more than the aged, for I keep your precepts.

Arminian

Wesley: Because—The practice of religion is the best way to understand it.

Classical Arminians hear keeping as the path into understanding—obedience schools insight. Shared maxim: aged experience without precept-keeping is not the highest wisdom.

Reformed

Calvin: age polishes by experience men otherwise dull; he acquired by the Divine Law more discretion than aged men. Whoever yields docility to God, keeps thoughts in subjection to the word, and meditates on the Law, derives wisdom to escape stratagems and match eminent masters. He does not boast before the world but warns that nothing is better than learning at God’s mouth. Keeping statutes shows meditation was not cold philosophizing but earnest devotion.

Reformed reading: precept-keeping yields understanding beyond years. Docility, not longevity alone, makes wise.

Psalms 119:101

101I hold back my feet from every evil way, in order to keep your word.

Arminian

Feet restrained from every evil path in order to keep the word. Classical Arminians hear deliberate war on vice—prevention of wandering as love’s strategy.

Shared vigilance: with corrupted nature and many allurements, holding back the feet is necessary miracle of care.

Reformed

Calvin: he proclaimed war against every vice to devote himself wholly to God’s service. To keep the Law we must beware from the start lest feet step into crooked by-paths; with corrupted nature, allurements, and fickle minds, going astray is great danger—rare miracle if any hold a right course without turning. Faithful need greatest circumspection.

Reformed accent: restraining feet serves keeping the word. Early watchfulness is part of holiness.

Psalms 119:102

102I do not turn aside from your rules, for you have taught me.

Arminian

Wesley: Taught me—By thy blessed spirit, illuminating my mind, and working upon my heart.

Classical Arminians hear Spirit-illumination and heart-work as the reason for not turning aside—God teaches; the scholar stays. Teaching is real for those who receive the word with docility; the Spirit makes the lesson effectual.

Reformed

Calvin: constancy since he learned from God the right manner of living. The way is slippery, feet feeble, disposition prone to astray—great exertions needed not to decline. Attend to the manner of teaching: though all who hear the word are taught, scarce one in ten tastes it; scarce one in a hundred profits so as to proceed to the end. A peculiar manner is pointed out—God drawing his chosen to himself. Brought into the way of salvation and preserved by the secret influence of the Holy Spirit.

Reformed confession: “you have taught me” is Spirit’s secret drawing and preserving. External preaching alone does not explain perseverance.

Key contrast Both credit God with teaching that keeps the singer from turning aside, and both refuse bare self-taught constancy. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s Spirit illuminating mind and working on the heart—grace making the word effectual in the docile. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s “peculiar manner”: God drawing his chosen by the Spirit’s secret influence—many hear outwardly, few so taste as to finish the course.

Psalms 119:103

103How sweet are your words to my taste, sweeter than honey to my mouth!

Arminian

Words sweeter than honey. Classical Arminians hear affective delight in Scripture—cheerfulness in following, not only grim duty.

Shared sweetness: without tasted delight, other pleasures win. Love the law’s honey against flesh’s lure.

Reformed

Calvin: so attracted by the Law’s sweetness as to desire no other delight. One may revere yet not cheerfully follow without tasting this sweetness. God requires no slavish service; he will have us come cheerfully—hence sweetness so often commended. He speaks not of the dead letter only but the whole doctrine whose chief part is the free covenant of salvation; grace of adoption in the Law was sweeter than honey.

Reformed accent: honey-sweet words include promise-grace, not bare threat. Struggle against carnal affections until the word out-sweetens them.

Psalms 119:104

104Through your precepts I get understanding; therefore I hate every false way.

Arminian

Understanding from precepts breeds hatred of false ways. Classical Arminians hear moral clarity flowing from Scripture—love of truth includes hate of lies.

Shared ethic: precept-taught minds do not stay neutral toward false paths.

Reformed

Calvin: by statutes he acquired understanding; therefore hated every false way. Withdraw from wanderings to frame life by the word; and already advancing, still need the word’s instruction to hate what remains crooked.

Reformed confession: precept-understanding and hatred of false ways are twins. Light from the statutes trains holy aversion.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Voices from both camps

Historic words on love for the law, wisdom from keeping and meditating, and sweetness that hates false ways—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 119:97–104.

Arminian Notable voices

“Because—The practice of religion is the best way to understand it. Taught me—By thy blessed spirit, illuminating my mind, and working upon my heart.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:100, 102)

Reformed Notable voices

“He was inflamed with incredible love to the law; continual meditation proves it—love of the law without study is hypocrisy. Thou hast made me wiser than adversaries, teachers, and the aged because a scholar of God’s law. I have not declined from thy judgments; for thou hast taught me: a peculiar manner of teaching—God drawing his chosen to himself by the secret influence of the Holy Spirit. Scarce one in ten tastes the word; scarce one in a hundred profits so as to proceed to the end.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:97–98, 102)
