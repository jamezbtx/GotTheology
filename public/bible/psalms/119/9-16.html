Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 9–16 · ESV · Voices

The Beth stanza asks how a young man keeps his way pure—by guarding it according to God’s word. Wholehearted seeking, storing the word against sin, delight above riches, meditation, and a resolve not to forget the word fill the eight verses. Both camps honor Scripture as the rule that cleanses and delights; they differ on whether seeking God begins in anticipating grace or only as grace already holds the heart.

Psalms 119:9

9How can a young man keep his way pure? By guarding it according to your word.

Arminian

Wesley: A young man—or, any man; he names the young because they lack wisdom and face great temptations. Cleanse—purge from all filthiness of flesh and spirit.

Classical Arminians hear a practical, sincere answer: purity comes by taking heed according to God’s word. The word is public lamp; youth (and every hearer) may guard the way by it under grace. Do not invent purity by native resolve apart from Scripture.

Reformed

Calvin: nothing is pure in life until men make a complete surrender to the Lord’s word. Youth is named because the young stand where two ways meet; no advice avails unless the law of God is adopted as rule and guide. Early regulation of manners is urged; delay hardens vice. If the law can restrain youth’s impetuosity, how much more maturity.

Reformed accent: purity is by the word as rule, not by self-pique on works. Surrender to Scripture is the only sure protection.

Psalms 119:10

10With my whole heart I seek you; let me not wander from your commandments!

Arminian

Wesley does not pause here. Wholehearted seeking and a plea not to wander belong together. Classical Arminians hear seeking as grace-enabled sincerity—prevenient grace drawing the heart—while perseverance still requires ongoing help.

“Let me not wander” confesses real danger. Seeking God does not cancel the need to ask for keeping; it deepens it. Do not invent a seeker who needs no further grace.

Reformed

Calvin: conscious of integrity, he still implores help lest he stumble by infirmity. He makes no boast of self-preparation as if he spontaneously began to inquire after God; he praises grace experienced and aspires after steadfastness. It is folly to treat this as free will anticipating the Spirit’s grace. He prays God to continue his work; unless God restrain us, we instantly wander. The passive “be led astray” marks our liability the instant he leaves us.

Reformed confession: whole-heart seeking is already God’s work underway; the plea is for continuance, not for a first self-started move.

Key contrast Both refuse a self-sufficient seeker and both pray against wandering. Arminians typically hear wholehearted seeking as prevenient grace truly enabling the sinner to seek, with further grace needed not to wander. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s insistence that this is not free will anticipating the Spirit—seeking is grace already at work, and the prayer asks God to finish what he began.

Psalms 119:11

11I have stored up your word in my heart, that I might not sin against you.

Arminian

Wesley: Hid—I have laid it up in my mind like a choice treasure, ready upon all occasions to counsel, quicken, or caution me.

Classical Arminians hear storing the word as a means of grace against sin—treasure internalized for living use. The aim is not sinning against God. Hide the word deeply; a shallow acquaintance will not hold in temptation.

Reformed

Calvin: we are well fortified against Satan’s stratagems when God’s law is deeply seated in the heart. Unless it have firm hold there, we readily fall. True safeguard is not slender knowledge or careless perusal, but hiding it deeply. Men convinced of their own wisdom are yet destitute of right judgment except as God teaches them.

Reformed accent: internalized word is God’s appointed defense. The heart-store is pastoral pattern for the church, not a private hobby.

Psalms 119:12

12Blessed are you, O Lord; teach me your statutes!

Arminian

Wesley does not pause here. Blessing the Lord and asking to be taught are one breath. Classical Arminians hear ongoing teachability—even progress does not retire the prayer for instruction.

God’s blessedness grounds the petition. Statutes are learned from him; the student remains a student.

Reformed

Calvin: though proficient enough to be a public teacher, he still asks for the spirit of understanding—all the upright are only on their journey till life’s close. If God do not enlighten us with discernment, we cannot behold the light shining from his law. Many are blind amid clear revelation because they contemn the Spirit’s internal illumination. He asks not on merits but from God’s glory—“Blessed art thou.”

Reformed reading: teachers still plead to be taught. Confidence rests on God’s praiseworthy goodness, not on attainments.

Psalms 119:13

13With my lips I declare all the rules of your mouth.

Arminian

Wesley does not pause here. Lips declare all the judgments of God’s mouth. Classical Arminians hear public confession flowing from a heart that has stored the word (v. 11)—not empty talk.

Declaring “all” resists selective witness. The mouth serves the God whose word is treasured.

Reformed

Calvin: the law was not only engraven on the heart but he earnestly sought to gain fellow-disciples into subjection to God. Heartless abstract talk belongs to hypocrites; what was said of heart-affection he now applies to the lips.

Reformed accent: declaration is strenuous care for others’ obedience, rooted in cordial love of the doctrine—not fluency without faith.

Psalms 119:14

14In the way of your testimonies I delight as much as in all riches.

Arminian

Wesley does not pause here. Delight in the way of the testimonies matches all riches. Classical Arminians hear sincere pleasure in God’s path—not grim duty alone.

Riches are the foil; the testimonies outvalue them. Delight is pastoral fruit of grace, inviting hearers to find the same joy.

Reformed

Calvin: he derived no less pleasure from God’s doctrine than from all the world’s riches. Holy love for the law is contrasted with unholy avarice. As wealth attracts hearts, so he took more exquisite delight in progress in godliness than if he abounded in riches.

Reformed confession: delight displaces avarice. The way of the testimonies is preferred treasure.

Psalms 119:15

15I will meditate on your precepts and fix my eyes on your ways.

Arminian

Wesley does not pause here. Meditation and fixed eyes on God’s ways answer the world’s distraction. Classical Arminians hear a resolve grace sustains—attention as means of not forgetting (v. 16).

Precepts and ways are held before the mind deliberately. This is shared piety both camps commend.

Reformed

Calvin: the prophet sets an example for imitation. Most are so involved in worldly cares that they leave no leisure for meditating on God’s doctrine. Diligence is seasonably commanded; even without worldly snares we lose sight of the law in daily temptations. Constant exercise and attentive consideration of God’s ways are required.

Reformed accent: meditation is warfare against callous indifference and unstable distraction.

Psalms 119:16

16I will delight in your statutes; I will not forget your word.

Arminian

Wesley does not pause here. Delight and memory close the Beth stanza. Classical Arminians hear cheerful clinging to the statutes—love that refuses forgetfulness.

Delight is not antinomian ease; it is affection for God’s statutes that guards the word in the heart. Shared ground: Scripture as treasured lamp.

Reformed

Calvin: he repeats the exquisite pleasure of this pursuit. Proficiency in the law will be small until we cheerfully and heartily set our minds upon it. Commencement of a good life consists in God’s law attracting us by its sweetness; thereby fleshly lusts are subdued. In our natural state what is sinful is more agreeable unless delight in the law carries us the other way.

Reformed reading: sweetness of the law turns the heart; forgetting is refused because delight has taken hold.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on purifying the way by God’s word, hiding the word in the heart, and delighting in the statutes—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 119:9–16.

Arminian Notable voices

“A young man—or, any man. But he names the young man, because such are commonly void of wisdom and experience, and exposed to many and great temptations. Cleanse—purge himself from all filthiness of flesh and spirit. Hid—I have laid it up in my mind like a choice treasure, to be ready upon all occasions to counsel, quicken or caution me.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:9, 11)

Reformed Notable voices

“However much men may pique themselves upon their own works, there is nothing pure in their life until they have made a complete surrender of themselves to the word of the Lord. It is folly to seize upon this and similar passages as if the saints, of their own free will, anticipated the grace of the Holy Spirit, and afterwards were favored with his aid. The prophet does not make a division between God and himself, but rather prays God to continue his work till it is completed. Such is our liability to err, that we immediately relapse into sin the instant he leaves us to ourselves.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:9–10)
