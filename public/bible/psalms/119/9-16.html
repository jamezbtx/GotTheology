Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 9–16 · WEB · Voices

The Beth stanza asks how a young man keeps his way pure? By living according to God’s word. Wholehearted seeking, storing the word against sin, delight above riches, meditation, and a resolve not to forget the word fill the eight verses. Both camps honor Scripture as the rule that cleanses and delights; they differ on whether seeking God begins in anticipating grace or only as grace already holds the heart.

Psalms 119:9

9How can a young man keep his way pure? By living according to your word.

Arminian

Wesley: A young man—or, any man; he names the young because they lack wisdom and face great temptations. Cleanse—purge from all filthiness of flesh and spirit.

Wesleyan Arminians see a practical, sincere answer: purity comes by taking heed according to God’s word. The word is public lamp; youth (and every hearer) may guard the way by it under grace.

Reformed

Calvin: nothing is pure in life until men make a complete surrender to the Lord’s word. Youth is named because the young stand where two ways meet; no advice avails unless the law of God is adopted as rule and guide. Early regulation of manners is urged; delay hardens vice. If the law can restrain youth’s impetuosity, how much more maturity.

Purity is by the word as rule, not by self-pique on works. Surrender to Scripture is the only sure protection.

Psalms 119:10

10With my whole heart I have sought you. Don’t let me wander from your commandments.

Arminian

Wholehearted seeking and a plea not to wander belong together. Arminian interpreters hear seeking as grace-enabled sincerity—prevenient grace drawing the heart—while perseverance still requires ongoing help.

“Let me wander” confesses serious danger. Seeking God does not annul the need to ask for keeping; it deepens it.

Reformed

Calvin: conscious of integrity, he still implores help lest he stumble by infirmity. He makes no boast of self-preparation as if he spontaneously began to inquire after God; he praises grace experienced and aspires after steadfastness. It is folly to treat this as free will anticipating the Spirit’s grace. He prays God to continue his work; unless God restrain us, we instantly wander. The passive “be led astray” marks our liability the instant he leaves us.

Whole-heart seeking is already God’s work underway; the plea is for continuance, not for a first self-started move.

Key contrast Both refuse a self-sufficient seeker and both pray against wandering. Arminians typically hear wholehearted seeking as prevenient grace truly enabling the sinner to seek, with further grace needed not to wander. Reformed interpreters follow Calvin’s insistence that this is not free will anticipating the Spirit—seeking is grace already at work, and the prayer asks God to finish what he began.

Psalms 119:11

11I have hidden your word in my heart, that I might not sin against you.

Arminian

Wesley: Hid—I have laid it up in my mind like a choice treasure, ready upon all occasions to counsel, quicken, or caution me.

Arminians hear storing the word as a means of grace against sin—treasure internalized for living use. The aim is not sinning against God. Hide the word deeply; a shallow acquaintance will not hold in temptation.

Reformed

Calvin: we are well fortified against Satan’s stratagems when God’s law is deeply seated in the heart. Unless it have firm hold there, we readily fall. True safeguard is not slender knowledge or careless perusal, but hiding it deeply. Men convinced of their own wisdom are yet destitute of right judgment except as God teaches them.

Internalized word is God’s appointed defense. The heart-store is pastoral pattern for the church, not a private hobby.

Psalms 119:12

12Blessed are you, LORD. Teach me your statutes.

Arminian

Blessing the Lord and asking to be taught are one breath. Arminians hear ongoing teachability—even progress does not retire the prayer for instruction.

God’s blessedness grounds the petition. Statutes are learned from him; the student remains a student.

Reformed

Calvin: though proficient enough to be a public teacher, he still asks for the spirit of understanding—all the upright are only on their journey till life’s close. If God do not enlighten us with discernment, we cannot behold the light shining from his law. Many are blind amid clear revelation because they contemn the Spirit’s internal illumination. He asks not on merits but from God’s glory—“Blessed art thou.”

Teachers still plead to be taught. Confidence rests on God’s praiseworthy goodness, not on attainments.

Psalms 119:13

13With my lips, I have declared all the ordinances of your mouth.

Arminian

Lips declare all the judgments of God’s mouth. Arminians hear public confession flowing from a heart that has stored the word (v. 11)—not empty talk.

Declaring “all” resists selective witness. The mouth serves the God whose word is treasured.

Reformed

Calvin: the law was not only engraven on the heart but he earnestly sought to gain fellow-disciples into subjection to God. Heartless abstract talk belongs to hypocrites; what was said of heart-affection he now applies to the lips.

Declaration is strenuous care for others’ obedience, rooted in cordial love of the doctrine—not fluency without faith.

Psalms 119:14

14I have rejoiced in the way of your testimonies, as much as in all riches.

Arminian

Delight in the way of the testimonies matches all riches. Arminian interpreters hear sincere pleasure in God’s path—not grim duty alone.

Riches are the foil; the testimonies outvalue them. Delight is pastoral fruit of grace, inviting hearers to find the same joy.

Reformed

Calvin: he derived no less pleasure from God’s doctrine than from all the world’s riches. Holy love for the law is contrasted with unholy avarice. As wealth attracts hearts, so he took more exquisite delight in progress in godliness than if he abounded in riches.

Delight in God’s testimonies displaces the love of riches. The way of the testimonies is preferred treasure.

Psalms 119:15

15I will meditate on your precepts, and consider your ways.

Arminian

Meditation and fixed eyes on God’s ways answer the world’s distraction. Wesley’s tradition hears a resolve grace sustains—attention as means of not forgetting (v. 16).

Precepts and ways are held before the mind deliberately. This is shared piety both camps commend.

Reformed

Calvin: the prophet sets an example for imitation. Most are so involved in worldly cares that they leave no leisure for meditating on God’s doctrine. Diligence is seasonably commanded; even without worldly snares we lose sight of the law in daily temptations. Constant exercise and attentive consideration of God’s ways are required.

Meditation is warfare against callous indifference and unstable distraction.

Psalms 119:16

16I will delight myself in your statutes. I will not forget your word.

Arminian

Delight and memory close the Beth stanza. Arminians hear cheerful clinging to the statutes—love that refuses forgetfulness.

Delight is not antinomian ease; it is affection for God’s statutes that guards the word in the heart. Scripture is a treasured lamp.

Reformed

Calvin: he repeats the exquisite pleasure of this pursuit. Proficiency in the law will be small until we cheerfully and heartily set our minds upon it. Commencement of a good life consists in God’s law attracting us by its sweetness; thereby fleshly lusts are subdued. In our natural state what is sinful is more agreeable unless delight in the law carries us the other way.

Sweetness of the law turns the heart; forgetting is refused because delight has taken hold.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What older interpreters in each camp said about purifying the way by God’s word, hiding the word in the heart, and delighting in the statutes.

Arminian Notable voices

“A young man—or, any man. But he names the young man, because such are commonly void of wisdom and experience, and exposed to many and great temptations. Cleanse—purge himself from all filthiness of flesh and spirit. Hid—I have laid it up in my mind like a choice treasure, to be ready upon all occasions to counsel, quicken or caution me.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:9, 11)

Reformed Notable voices

“However much men may pique themselves upon their own works, there is nothing pure in their life until they have made a complete surrender of themselves to the word of the Lord. It is folly to seize upon this and similar passages as if the saints, of their own free will, anticipated the grace of the Holy Spirit, and afterwards were favored with his aid. The prophet does not make a division between God and himself, but rather prays God to continue his work till it is completed. Such is our liability to err, that we immediately relapse into sin the instant he leaves us to ourselves.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:9–10)
