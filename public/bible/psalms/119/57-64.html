Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 57–64 · ESV · Voices

The Heth stanza names the Lord as portion and vows to keep his words, entreats favor according to the promise, turns the feet after thinking on one’s ways, and hastens without delay to the commandments. Cords of the wicked cannot erase the law from memory; midnight praise, companionship with those who fear God, and a plea to be taught fill the earth-wide mercy of the Lord. Both camps make God their portion and rush toward obedience; they differ on how such haste and wholehearted entreaty are possible.

Psalms 119:57

57The Lord is my portion; I promise to keep your words.

Arminian

Wesley: Said—I have professed and owned it.

Classical Arminians hear God claimed as portion and a real vow to keep his words—profession that aims at practice. The portion is the Lord himself; keeping follows from belonging to him under grace.

Reformed

Calvin prefers: I am persuaded my best portion consists in keeping God’s law—compared with imaginary goods that captivate ambition. Another sound reading: God as portion ought to animate observance; if we rest in him alone, hearts are disposed to keep the law and renounce fleshly lusts.

Reformed accent: portion-language dethrones rival gains. Resolve to keep is bound to God as heritage, not to self-made piety.

Key contrast Both name the Lord as true portion and both join that claim to keeping his words. Arminians typically hear a sincere profession and vow grace enables—God owned, then words kept. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s stress that the best portion is keeping the law, or that God-as-portion itself disposes the heart to obey—satisfaction in God, not rival ambitions, animates observance.

Psalms 119:58

58I entreat your favor with all my heart; be gracious to me according to your promise.

Arminian

Wholehearted entreaty asks grace according to the promise. Classical Arminians hear prayer as faith’s living breath—favor sought, not presumed, on the basis of what God pledged.

Shared ground: the principal request is mercy. The promise keeps prayer from empty wishing.

Reformed

Calvin: without prayer faith becomes languid. He perseveres in prayer; the sum is that he implored mercy, the hope of which he formed from the word. We are aroused to exercise faith by prayer; the principal request is that God of free grace be favorable. God in his promises is set before us as a willing debtor—so we present no meaningless prayers.

Reformed confession: whole-heart entreaty and promise-bound grace belong together. Mercy is the source of every other blessing asked.

Psalms 119:59

59When I think on my ways, I turn my feet to your testimonies;

Arminian

Wesley: Thought—I seriously considered both my former courses, and my duty in all my future actions.

Classical Arminians hear real self-examination that turns the feet—repentance with movement toward the testimonies. Thinking is not morbid stalling; it ends in turning.

Reformed

Calvin: after paying due regard to his manner of life, his only aim was to follow the law’s teaching. Men go astray because they thoughtlessly indulge passions; no one carefully considers his ways. The commencement of a godly life is awaking, examining, and wisely considering how to regulate conduct—then following the Lord’s direction.

Reformed accent: turning follows serious thought under God’s guide. Without that awakening, conflicting impulses distract miserably.

Psalms 119:60

60I hasten and do not delay to keep your commandments.

Arminian

Haste without delay marks eager obedience. Classical Arminians hear prompt keeping—grace that does not license procrastination once the feet have turned (v. 59).

Shared warning: tortuous delays and half-resolves are refused. The commandments are worth running toward.

Reformed

Calvin: diligence and dispatch demonstrate zeal; “delayed not” intensifies haste—run quickly without doubt. Reflecting on listlessness and Satan’s snares shows these words are not vain. Per Paul, we do not the thing we would; impediments within make haste hard. The prophet speaks comparatively against those who loiter and draw near only hesitatingly.

Reformed reading: true haste surmounts obstacles without pretending flesh is willing. Pleas that excuse indolence are frivolous.

Psalms 119:61

61Though the cords of the wicked ensnare me, I do not forget your law.

Arminian

Wesley: Robbed—Done me many injuries, for my respect to thy law.

Classical Arminians hear memory of the law under ensnaring hostility—injuries for piety do not erase Scripture from the heart. Shared fortitude: suffering is no permit to forget.

Reformed

Calvin: whether cords as allurements or companies that rob, the intent is that under grievous assault he continued in love and practice of the law. When exposed as prey and perceiving no help, continuing to love the law is evidence of genuine piety—otherwise we doubt providence and easily lose remembrance of the law.

Reformed accent: ensnarement tests whether the law is forgotten. Steadfast memory under plunder marks real fear of God.

Psalms 119:62

62At midnight I rise to praise you, because of your righteous rules.

Arminian

Midnight rising to praise for righteous rules. Classical Arminians hear secret gratitude for God’s just word—zeal that breaks sleep without needing an audience.

Shared piety: the rules are not only kept but praised. Night praise matches night remembrance (v. 55).

Reformed

Calvin: he gave thanks for being made partaker of so great a blessing. Studies that break sleep imply great earnestness; in secret retirement, with no human eye upon him, he pronounced high encomiums on God’s righteous judgments—far from ostentation.

Reformed confession: approving the law as wholly reasonable is rare; flesh would alter it. Midnight praise marks sincere assent.

Psalms 119:63

63I am a companion of all who fear you, of those who keep your precepts.

Arminian

Companionship with all who fear God and keep the precepts. Classical Arminians hear holy friendship that advances reverence—fellowship defined by fear and obedience, not party alone.

Shared call: stand with those who keep the precepts when the world troops against worship.

Reformed

Calvin: not mere concord, but giving the hand to every God-fearer—helper as well as one of their number. Holy combination fosters worship over against impious associations that overthrow it. Marks of the faithful: fearing God and observing the law; fear is the root, service manifests it.

Reformed accent: true companions are known by fear and precept-keeping. Superstition without commanded faith is refused.

Psalms 119:64

64The earth, O Lord, is full of your steadfast love; teach me your statutes!

Arminian

Earth-wide steadfast love becomes a plea to be taught. Classical Arminians hear mercy filling the world and still ask personal instruction—love that teaches, not only spares.

Do not be content with shallow knowledge while the earth proclaims kindness. Shared hunger: teach me your statutes.

Reformed

Calvin: the earth full of mercy is earnest entreaty—he desires that mercy be shown him by progress in knowing the law. Understanding is an inestimable treasure; he treats not of external teaching only but of inward illumination, the Spirit’s gift. The law was exhibited to all; unless enlightened, it profits little—he prays to be taught effectually by supernatural influence.

Reformed confession: general mercy in creation emboldens the plea for illuminating grace. Sloth that stays on the surface is shameful when such a teacher still begged to know more.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on God as portion, hasty obedience after self-examination, and the earth full of steadfast love that teaches statutes—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 119:57–64.

Arminian Notable voices

“Said—I have professed and owned it. Thought—I seriously considered both my former courses, and my duty in all my future actions. Robbed—Done me many injuries, for my respect to thy law.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:57, 59, 61)

Reformed Notable voices

“I approve this reading: I am fully persuaded that my best portion consists in keeping God’s law. God being our portion ought to animate us to observe his law; if we rest satisfied with him alone, our hearts will be disposed to keep it. Diligence and dispatch demonstrate zeal; to make haste and not delay is to run quickly without doubt. Let a man be ever so desirous of applying himself to righteousness, yet we know that he does not the thing that he would. The earth is full of God’s mercy—he desires that mercy may be manifested by enabling him to progress in the knowledge of the law; he treats of inward illumination of the mind, the gift of the Holy Spirit.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:57, 60, 64)
