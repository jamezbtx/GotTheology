Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 33–40 · ESV · Voices

The He stanza is almost wholly petition: teach, give understanding, lead, incline the heart, turn the eyes, confirm the promise, turn away reproach, and give life in righteousness. Delight in the path and longing for the precepts appear, yet every verb leans on God to form the keeper. Both camps pray this way; they differ most sharply on whether “incline my heart” confesses a will that cannot turn itself until grace bends it, or a grace that assists a seeker already longing.

Psalms 119:33

33Teach me, O Lord, the way of your statutes; and I will keep it to the end.

Arminian

Wesley does not pause here. Teach me the way of the statutes, and I will keep it to the end. Classical Arminians hear perseverance prayed for—teaching that enables lifelong keeping.

“To the end” is vow under petition, not boast of unfinished grace. God teaches; the servant keeps.

Reformed

Calvin: the main thing in life is having God for governor—often inculcated because the world asks almost anything else. Even the advanced must aspire after further advancement; the Spirit of understanding comes from above. “Unto the end” may mark perseverance: those God teaches he strengthens to finish the course without languor. Preferable simple sense: keep the end in view—daily teaching so the eye never leaves the mark.

Reformed accent: lifelong keeping rests on continual teaching. Perseverance is gift asked, not assumed.

Psalms 119:34

34Give me understanding, that I may keep your law and observe it with my whole heart.

Arminian

Wesley does not pause here. Understanding is asked that whole-heart observance may follow. Classical Arminians hear mind and heart enlisted together under grace.

Keeping with the whole heart refuses mere letter-obedience before men. Understanding is gift unto love-obedience.

Reformed

Calvin: true wisdom is being wise according to God’s law so as to preserve us in fear and obedience. Man’s natural blindness aims elsewhere; the world calls wise those who mind their interests and beguile the simple. He asks no other prudence than surrendering to God’s direction—special gift none procure by power or policy. Whole heart: God restrains the heart that genuine uprightness may flourish; letter-obedience that escapes human blame is far from the law’s righteousness.

Reformed confession: understanding for whole-heart keeping is divinely formed, not self-taught.

Psalms 119:35

35Lead me in the path of your commandments, for I delight in it.

Arminian

Wesley does not pause here. Lead me—for I delight in the path. Classical Arminians hear delight as real affection that still needs leading. Grace that gave delight also guides the steps.

Shared ground: delight in the commandments is not legalism; it is love that asks to be led.

Reformed

Calvin: frequent repetition is not redundant—man’s end is profiting in God’s school, yet the world distracts. “In it I take pleasure” marks rare excellence: renouncing flesh’s enticements to delight in God’s service. He had attained this yet was not perfect; he solicits fresh assistance, as Paul: God works to will and to do. He does not boast of nature’s working but sets forth grace received that God may complete it.

Reformed accent: delight is grace begun; leading is grace continued. Pleasure in the path opposes the flesh’s lettered lusts.

Psalms 119:36

36Incline my heart to your testimonies, and not to selfish gain!

Arminian

Wesley: Covetousness—most opposite to God’s testimonies, most commonly hinders receiving and profiting by the word, most pernicious as root of all evil.

Classical Arminians hear prayer against the greed that blocks the word. Incline—God is asked to bend affection toward the testimonies. Longing (v. 40) and this petition coexist: sincere desire still needs the heart inclined away from gain.

Reformed

Calvin: were we naturally inclined to the law’s righteousness, there would be no need to petition “Incline my heart.” Hearts are full of sinful thoughts and wholly rebellious until God by grace change them. Natural corruption seeks anything rather than what is right until turned to new obedience. Covetousness is named as species for the genus—the root of all evils most opposed to God’s righteousness.

Reformed confession: inclination is God’s changing work, not the heart’s native lean. Without it we prefer gain to testimonies.

Key contrast Both refuse covetousness as enemy of the word and both ask God to bend the heart toward the testimonies. Arminians typically hear incline as grace assisting and redirecting a heart that already longs for the precepts. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s confession that without this petition we are not naturally inclined at all—hearts wholly rebellious until God changes them to new obedience.

Psalms 119:37

37Turn my eyes from looking at worthless things; and give me life in your ways.

Arminian

Wesley: Vanity—riches, honours, pleasures; from beholding them with desire. Quicken—make me lively, vigorous, and fervent in thy service.

Classical Arminians hear eyes and life requested together: turn from vanity, quicken in God’s way. Means of grace include guarded senses. Shared pastoral wisdom both camps affirm.

Reformed

Calvin: all senses are so filled with vanity that until refined they are alienated from righteousness. Original sin pervades the whole constitution; if eyes must be turned away by special grace, then as soon as opened they set on Satan’s impostures unless God guards by the Spirit. Seeing, hearing, walking, and willing are precious gifts yet unmingled with no look free of depravity—so he surrenders for mortification of the flesh to begin to live anew.

Reformed accent: turned eyes and quickened life are mortifying grace. Vanity is default until God turns us.

Psalms 119:38

38Confirm to your servant your promise, that you may be feared.

Arminian

Wesley: Stablish—confirm and perform thy promises.

Classical Arminians hear prayer for promise kept, unto fear of the Lord. Confirmation serves reverence, not presumption. The servant waits on God’s performing word.

Reformed

Calvin: the sole end of prayer is to reap the fruits of God’s promises—vague desires without the word are presumption. Argument: because I am devoted to thy fear. He does not abuse promises for fleshly delights but makes fear and reverence his aim. Best assurance of obtaining requests is when they harmonize with God’s service.

Reformed reading: confirmed promise produces fear. Prayer stays inside what God has said.

Psalms 119:39

39Turn away the reproach that I dread, for your rules are good.

Arminian

Wesley: I fear—for my instability in thy ways; which in respect to my own weakness I have great cause to fear.

Classical Arminians hear dreaded reproach tied to feared instability—weakness confessed, rules still affirmed as good. Plea for turned-away shame rests on God’s good judgments, not on self-confidence.

Reformed

Calvin: he may dread disgrace from detected offense under watching calumniators, or unjust slander distorting good actions. Preferable: suppress mockery for keeping the law—or, take away reproach before God’s tribunal so he is not judged reprobate. Holy men dread shame at God’s judgment-seat. The ground: thy judgments are good.

Reformed accent: reproach feared; God’s good rules are the plea. Weakness and calumny alike drive the petition.

Psalms 119:40

40Behold, I long for your precepts; in your righteousness give me life!

Arminian

Wesley: Longed—after a more solid knowledge and constant performance of them. In—according to thy faithfulness.

Classical Arminians hear longing confessed and life asked in God’s righteousness/faithfulness. Desire for the precepts is real; quickening is still petitioned. Closing He: appetite for the word and dependence on God’s right dealing.

Reformed

Calvin: repetition of pious affection and love of righteousness—nothing wanting but God to complete the work begun. Quicken in thy righteousness may mean in the way of the law, or restore me for the sake of thy goodness shown to thy people. Preferable: you have inspired holy desire; one thing remains—that this virtue pervade my whole life.

Reformed confession: longing is grace begun; life in righteousness is grace completing. Behold the desire—and still ask to be quickened.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on being taught to the end, having the heart inclined to the testimonies, eyes turned from vanity, and life given in God’s righteousness—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 119:33–40.

Arminian Notable voices

“Covetousness—he mentions this in particular, because it is most opposite to God’s testimonies, and does most commonly hinder men from receiving his word, and from profiting by it: and because it is most pernicious, as being the root of all evil. Vanity—the vain things of this present world, such as riches, honours, pleasures: from beholding them, with desire or affection. Quicken—make me lively, vigorous and fervent in thy service. Longed—after a more solid knowledge and constant performance of them. In—according to thy faithfulness.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:36–37, 40)

Reformed Notable voices

“Were we naturally and spontaneously inclined to the righteousness of the law, there would be no occasion for the petition, Incline my heart. It remains that our hearts are full of sinful thoughts and wholly rebellious until God by his grace change them. The natural corruption of man is so great that he seeks for anything rather than what is right, until he be turned by the power of God to new obedience. As soon as the eyes are opened they are eagerly set on Satan’s impostures unless God guard us by his Spirit.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:36–37)
