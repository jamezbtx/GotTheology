Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 113–120 · ESV · Voices

The Samekh stanza hates the double-minded and loves the law; God is hiding place and shield; hope rests in the word. Evildoers are told to depart so commandments may be kept; upholding according to the promise is asked for life and unashamed hope; held up, the singer regards statutes continually. God spurns those who stray; the wicked are discarded like dross, therefore testimonies are loved; flesh trembles in fear of God and his judgments. Both camps flee to God for safety and ask to be upheld; they differ on how far that upholding is the necessary cause of living and keeping.

Psalms 119:113

113I hate the double-minded, but I love your law.

Arminian

Hate of the double-minded paired with love of the law. Classical Arminians hear single-hearted affection—law-love that cannot cozy to divided loyalties.

Shared clarity: crooked, wavering thoughts set against God’s right word. Purification of mind begins discipleship.

Reformed

Calvin: seaphim as crooked thoughts—branches of the heart tangled and twisted; inventions of the human heart set against the law alone right. Whoever would truly embrace the law must first divest unhallowed thoughts, or rather go out of his own nature. God acknowledges as disciples those purified from contrary imaginations.

Reformed accent: hate double-mindedness; love the law. Docility begins by opposing crooked thoughts to Scripture.

Psalms 119:114

114You are my hiding place and my shield; I hope in your word.

Arminian

God as hiding place and shield; hope in the word. Classical Arminians hear refuge language that steadies fear—safety sought in God, confidence fed by promise.

Shared sequence: learn from the word that he is refuge; cherish it under felt need of protection.

Reformed

Calvin: persuaded the only way to be safe was lying hid under God’s wings, he confided in promises and feared nothing. First point: preservation entirely owing to God’s protection—flee to him and wait for promised deliverance. Confidence that God is refuge is derived from the word; set the word before us that faith fail not when aid is slow.

Reformed confession: hide and shield plus word-hope. Mutual relation—need drives us into promised protection.

Psalms 119:115

115Depart from me, you evildoers, that I may keep the commandments of my God.

Arminian

Evildoers told to depart for the sake of keeping commandments. Classical Arminians hear holy distance—companionship chosen with a view to obedience.

Shared caution: intimate entanglement with the wicked risks contagion. “My God” stakes loyalty above the crowd.

Reformed

Calvin: not mainly “when they desist I will serve better,” though deliverance should stir zeal. He testifies he will not entangle in their society—distance not of place only but of intercourse. Contract intimacy and scarce avoid corruption; few continue integrity to life’s end because the world is fraught with corruptions. Banish the wicked from intimate fellowship; designate God as his God—more account of him than of all mankind.

Reformed accent: depart, that I may keep. Separation unto commandment-keeping under God alone.

Psalms 119:116

116Uphold me according to your promise, that I may live, and let me not be put to shame in my hope!

Arminian

Uphold according to promise unto life; shame in hope refused. Classical Arminians hear dependence in prayer—live by being upheld, hope kept from disgrace.

Shared plea: promise-based sustaining. Without God’s upholding, life and unashamed hope fail.

Reformed

Calvin: Sustain me by thy word, and I shall live—make me not ashamed of my expectation. He looks entirely to God’s protection; preservation owed to God; leaning on the word, he waits for promised deliverance. Living is tied to being sustained by the word; hope’s shame is fled by clinging to what God said.

Reformed confession: uphold according to promise is the cause of living. Shame-free hope rests on sustained life from God, not self-support.

Key contrast Both ask to be upheld according to the promise so that they may live and not be ashamed of hope. Arminians typically hear earnest dependence—grace sustaining the believer who hopes. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s tighter bond: sustain me by thy word and I shall live—life itself viewed as contingent on God’s upholding, not on native continuance.

Psalms 119:117

117Hold me up, that I may be safe and have regard for your statutes continually!

Arminian

Held up unto safety and continual regard for statutes. Classical Arminians hear perseverance asked—safety and ongoing attention as gifts sought together.

Shared logic: being held up serves continual regard. Do not invent safe wandering from the statutes.

Reformed

Calvin: Establish me, and I shall be safe; I will consider thy statutes continually. Safety and continual consideration of statutes flow from God’s establishing. The prophet binds secure standing to divine upholding.

Reformed accent: hold me up—then safety and continual regard. Continuance in the statutes is asked as fruit of being established.

Psalms 119:118

118You spurn all who go astray from your statutes, for their cunning is in vain.

Arminian

Wesley: Deceit—Their deceitful devices shall bring that destruction upon themselves which they design for others.

Classical Arminians hear God’s rejection of willful straying—cunning that backfires. Shared warning: astray from statutes is not clever; it is vain.

Reformed

Calvin: God has trodden under foot all who wander from statutes; their deceit is falsehood—devices rebound. Soft security in crooked paths is exposed; God’s spurning teaches the lover of testimonies.

Reformed reading: spurned straying and vain cunning sober the church. Statutes mark the path God honors.

Psalms 119:119

119All the wicked of the earth you discard like dross; therefore I love your testimonies.

Arminian

Wicked discarded as dross; therefore testimonies loved. Classical Arminians hear judgment teaching love—God’s refining action deepens affection for his word.

Shared therefore: seeing dross removed, love the testimonies more, not less.

Reformed

Calvin: God made the wicked cease as dross; therefore he loved the testimonies. Judgment on the wicked confirms the worth of what God has spoken and stirs love rather than cynicism.

Reformed accent: dross discarded → testimonies loved. Providence catechizes affection for Scripture.

Psalms 119:120

120My flesh trembles for fear of you, and I am afraid of your judgments.

Arminian

Flesh trembling; fear of God’s judgments. Classical Arminians hear reverent fear joined to love of the law (v. 113)—not slavish despair but holy awe.

Shared balance: affection without fear grows careless; fear without love grows loveless. Tremble and trust.

Reformed

Calvin: flesh trembled for fear of God; afraid of his judgments. Reverent fear accompanies love of the testimonies and hope in the word—not contradiction but fullness of piety before the Judge whose statutes he loves.

Reformed confession: trembling flesh and feared judgments mark real knowledge of God. Soft presumption is refused alongside despair.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Voices from both camps

Historic words on hating double-mindedness, God as shield, being upheld to live and keep, and trembling fear of judgments—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 119:113–120.

Arminian Notable voices

“Deceit—Their deceitful devices shall bring that destruction upon themselves which they design for others.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:118)

Reformed Notable voices

“I have hated crooked thoughts and loved thy law—whoever would truly embrace the law must divest himself of unhallowed thoughts. Depart from me, ye wicked—keep distance from worldly contagion. My flesh trembled for fear of thee—reverent fear joined to love of the testimonies.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:113–117, 120)
