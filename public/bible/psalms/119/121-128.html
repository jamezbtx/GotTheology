Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 121–128 · ESV · Voices

The Ayin stanza pleads from a clean conscience under oppression: judgment and righteousness done, a pledge of good sought, eyes failing for salvation and the righteous promise. Steadfast love is asked to teach; the servant seeks understanding; it is time for the Lord to act because the law is broken; therefore commandments are loved above gold and all precepts esteemed right while every false way is hated. Both camps refuse merit-bargaining and prize Spirit-taught love of the word; they differ on what “I am your servant” implies when understanding is asked.

Psalms 119:121

121I have done what is just and right; do not leave me to my oppressors.

Arminian

Just and right dealing grounds a plea not to be abandoned to oppressors. Classical Arminians hear a good-conscience appeal—not sinless perfection, but sincere equity that may ask God’s defense.

Shared ground: the oppressed who have not repaid evil may cry for help. Do not invent a wage that buys God; do not erase integrity as irrelevant to prayer.

Reformed

Calvin: he implores help against wicked troublers while testifying the harassment was undeserved. If we would have God succor us, we must meet him with a good conscience. He promises aid to the unrighteously afflicted; the prophet protests he had not provoked enemies, restrained himself from injury, and did not requite evil. Doing judgment at all times means persevering in integrity in public and private.

Reformed accent: conscience testimony accompanies the plea. Integrity is witness, not purchase of grace.

Psalms 119:122

122Give your servant a pledge of good; let not the insolent oppress me.

Arminian

Wesley: Surety—Do thou undertake and plead my cause.

Classical Arminians hear God asked to stand between the servant and proud oppressors—comfort in divine advocacy. Shared refuge: when insolence rushes in, God as pledge is enough.

Reformed

Calvin: Become surety for thy servant for good—better than “delight thy servant in good.” Since the proud rush to destroy, interpose as surety. Full of comfort: God performs the office of surety for our deliverance—as if finding us indebted he paid the creditor. Prayer: suffer not the wicked to exercise cruelty at pleasure; interpose as defender. Extreme danger left nothing to hope but God’s help.

Reformed confession: surety-language is gospel-shaped protection. God steps between us and the proud.

Psalms 119:123

123My eyes long for your salvation and for the fulfillment of your righteous promise.

Arminian

Wesley: The word—For the performance of thy faithful or merciful promise.

Classical Arminians hear failing eyes still fixed on salvation and promise—waiting that asks fulfillment. Shared patience: long looking does not quit the righteous word.

Reformed

Calvin: afflicted severely and long, yet heart never sank into despair. Failing for salvation: striving against temptation even to the soul’s fainting—perseverance with arduous effort, opposed to those who faint at once if God delays. Salvation is the continual course of grace unto complete deliverance. He waited by depending on God’s word—only then do we wait for salvation, and only then yield God the praise.

Reformed accent: eyes fail without abandoning the righteous word. Promise, not sight, holds the waiter.

Psalms 119:124

124Deal with your servant according to your steadfast love, and teach me your statutes.

Arminian

Steadfast love and teaching asked together. Classical Arminians hear mercy as the climate in which statutes are learned—kind dealing that instructs.

Shared petition: deal gently by teaching. Happiness is wisdom from the word; hope of it rests on God’s goodness.

Reformed

Calvin: not two separate asks—first deal well, then teach—but deal gently by instructing in the law. Object: teach me thy statutes; he begins with mercy as argument. Whole happiness is true wisdom from God’s word; only hope of obtaining it is God’s mercy and goodness displayed. He magnifies the excellence of being instructed when he requests it as free gift.

Reformed reading: lovingkindness teaches. Statute-learning is mercy’s shape, not a wage after self-prep.

Psalms 119:125

125I am your servant; give me understanding, that I may know your testimonies!

Arminian

Servant-identity asks understanding to know the testimonies. Classical Arminians hear sincere belonging that pleads for light—grace already tasting service, now seeking clearer knowledge.

Do not invent a servant who purchased the title; do not invent understanding that needs no Spirit. Shared hunger: teach me that I may know.

Reformed

Calvin: prayer of v. 124 repeated—ardent importunity. God teaches by illuminating understandings otherwise blind. Law in the ear or on the page, even expounded by man, profits little unless God corrects slowness and renders us docile by his Spirit’s secret influence. He does not boast merit as servant. Men imagine prior preparation then “subsequent grace”; the Prophet declares obligations to God. No man can make himself the Most High’s servant or bring a price for that honor; he adduces grace obtained as argument that God would perfect what he began (cf. Ps 116:16)—member of the Church, not boaster of services.

Reformed confession: servantship is gift; understanding is Spirit-illumination. Grace perfects grace—not wages after readiness.

Key contrast Both ask understanding as servants who need light, and both refuse a self-made claim on God. Arminians typically hear sincere servant-language under grace—belonging confessed, more illumination sought for knowing the testimonies. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s edge: no one makes himself God’s servant; “I am thy servant” adduces free grace already given, and teaching is the Spirit’s secret illumination of otherwise blind minds—not subsequent grace after human preparation.

Psalms 119:126

126It is time for the Lord to act, for your law has been broken.

Arminian

Wesley: To work—To put forth thy power for the restraint of evil-doers. They—The wicked. Made void—Or, abrogated thy law, have openly cast off its authority.

Classical Arminians hear holy urgency for God to restrain law-breakers—zeal for God’s honor more than private revenge. Shared cry: when the law is openly voided, it is time for the Lord to act.

Reformed

Calvin: fit time for executing vengeance had arrived—wanton forwardness against God carried far. “Doing” is emphatic: God would seem to delay too long if he did not now judge. Peculiar work of God to restrain and severely punish when repentance is hopeless. Zeal is not private wrongs but honest grief that the law is destroyed. Prayer that God restore order; when fear of him is almost extinct, call him to maintain his glory. Sustains hope when judgments delay.

Reformed accent: time to act is zeal for the violated law. Wait without denying that God will yet do.

Psalms 119:127

127Therefore I love your commandments above gold, above fine gold.

Arminian

Wesley: Therefore—Because the general apostacy of others makes this duty more necessary.

Classical Arminians hear love intensified when others abandon the word—gold outvalued because fidelity matters more amid collapse. Shared valuation: commandments above fine gold.

Reformed

Calvin: connected with the preceding—therefore (illative). He esteemed the law above gold because persuaded that havoc of equity will not remain unpunished though God connive for a time. The more he saw outrageous wickedness, the more holy indignation incited love of the law. Evil example’s baneful influence is well known; therefore meditate: when the wicked claim unbridled liberty, contemplate God’s judgments by faith to be quickened to observe the law.

Reformed confession: apostasy around us should increase, not cool, love of the commandments. Judgment-faith fuels affection.

Psalms 119:128

128Therefore I consider all your precepts to be right; I hate every false way.

Arminian

All precepts esteemed right; every false way hated. Classical Arminians hear whole-Bible approval—no picking commandments that suit us.

Shared integrity: love of the law includes hate of lying ways. Do not erase precepts that cross the flesh.

Reformed

Calvin: waiting for judgments and calling for their infliction, he subscribed to the law in every particular without exception, and hated every false way. “All commandments of all”—all God ordained, whatever they enjoined. Nothing more natural than to despise whatever in the law disagrees with us; we cannot add or take away; to separate one command is unwarrantable. Holy jealousy contended against rebellion. Approve the law wholly; hate every way of lying opposed to its purity.

Reformed accent: all precepts right; every false way hated. Partial subscription is refused.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on surety against the proud, servantship that asks understanding, and love of the commandments above gold—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 119:121–128.

Arminian Notable voices

“Surety—Do thou undertake and plead my cause. The word—For the performance of thy faithful or merciful promise. To work—To put forth thy power for the restraint of evil-doers. They—The wicked. Made void—Or, abrogated thy law, have openly cast off its authority. Therefore—Because the general apostacy of others makes this duty more necessary.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:122–123, 126–127)

Reformed Notable voices

“Become surety for thy servant for good: interpose as a defender; God metaphorically becomes surety to effect our deliverance. Deal with thy servant according to thy goodness, and teach me: our happiness is true wisdom from the word, hoped for only as God displays mercy. I am thy servant, give me understanding: God teaches by illuminating understandings otherwise blind; the law in the ear profits little without the Spirit. The Prophet does not boast worth but adduces grace already obtained, that God would perfect what he had begun—not subsequent grace after human preparation.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:122, 124–125)
