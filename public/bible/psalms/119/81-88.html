Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 81–88 · ESV · Voices

The Kaph stanza is a cry of delayed hope: the soul faints for salvation, the eyes fail for the promise, and the singer is dried like a wineskin in smoke—yet the statutes are not forgotten. Pits, falsehood, and near-consumption press hard; the close asks life according to steadfast love so that the testimonies may be kept. Both camps honor perseverance under delay and refuse despair; they differ on whether the life that enables keeping is God’s free quickening of the nearly dead or grace restoring a sincere waiter.

Psalms 119:81

81My soul longs for your salvation; I hope in your word.

Arminian

Wesley: Fainteth—With long desire and earnest expectation.

Classical Arminians hear earnest waiting that still hopes in the word—longing without unbelief. Delay may wear the soul; hope remains fixed on what God has spoken. Shared piety: salvation sought, word trusted.

Reformed

Calvin: though worn with grief and seeing no issue, trouble had not prevented confidence in God. He affirms he trusts; intending invincible constancy, he patiently endured distresses under which others succumb. Fainting here marks patience that continues to cherish hope when strength seems gone—opposed to those who cannot suffer long delay.

Reformed accent: hope in the word outlasts visible help. True trust faints without quitting God.

Psalms 119:82

82My eyes long for your promise; I ask, “When will you comfort me?”

Arminian

Eyes fail watching for the promise; the question “When?” is honest prayer, not rebellion. Classical Arminians hear sincere hunger for comfort God pledged—waiting that may ask timing while still clinging to the word.

Shared ground: comfort is sought from God alone. Do not invent consolations that bypass the promise.

Reformed

Calvin: what was said of the soul is transferred to the eyes—longing after God’s promise, for salvation is often delayed while the word holds us in expectation. He kept eyes fixed on the word until sight failed; asking “When wilt thou comfort me?” shows he was long as it were cast off—yet still looking to the word.

Reformed confession: failed eyes still face the promise. Wonderful power of patience under flesh’s infirmity.

Psalms 119:83

83For I have become like a wineskin in the smoke, yet I have not forgotten your statutes.

Arminian

Wesley: Bottle—A leathern bottle. My natural moisture is dried and burnt up.

Classical Arminians hear severe, lingering affliction that still leaves the statutes remembered. Shared fortitude: shriveled strength is no excuse to forget God’s word.

Reformed

Calvin: like a bottle in the smoke—parched by continual heat of adversities, tormented as at a slow fire. Yet he had not been withdrawn from the fear of God. Genuine godliness: though plunged into deepest afflictions, we cease not to submit ourselves to God.

Reformed accent: statutes remembered under drying grief mark true piety. Long sorrow did not erase the word.

Psalms 119:84

84How long must your servant endure? When will you judge those who persecute me?

Arminian

Wesley: The days—The days of my life. I have but a little while to live; give me some respite before I die.

Classical Arminians hear a humble “how long” from a short-lived servant—plea for timely help, not a demand to dethrone God. Shared lament: persecution asks for God’s judgment.

Reformed

Calvin: not a general complaint of life’s brevity, but how long abandoned to the ungodly—when wilt thou oppose their cruelty. He expostulates without murmuring spirit; asking how long, he humbly prays God not delay. Vengeance desired is such as suits God; he had divested corrupt affections for pure zeal for God’s judgment.

Reformed reading: warfare days feel too long; prayer seeks God’s defense more than private revenge.

Psalms 119:85

85The insolent have dug pitfalls for me; they do not live according to your law.

Arminian

Wesley: Who—Who have no respect to thy law.

Classical Arminians hear fraud and pits from the lawless—secret malice as well as open force. Shared plea: God sees pits dug against his servants.

Reformed

Calvin: not only open force but malicious secret arts. “Not according to thy law” stirs God to mercy—he is inclined to succor when assaults involve violation of his law. The clause also marks the singer’s innocence: he attempted nothing contrary to the Divine Law.

Reformed accent: lawless pits call for the Lawgiver’s help. Patient restraint under fraud is confessed.

Psalms 119:86

86All your commandments are sure; they persecute me with falsehood; help me!

Arminian

Wesley: Faithful—Just and true.

Classical Arminians hear sure commandments against false persecution—truth of God versus deceit of men. Help is cried for on that contrast. Shared confidence: God’s word does not waver when enemies lie.

Reformed

Calvin: armed by this—that the faithful under God’s conduct wage a prosperous warfare, salvation hoped from his word being certain. Commandments are truth; those who rely on God’s word are out of danger. He complains of enemies’ treachery; equity disregarded leads him to hope of deliverance—God succoring the wrongfully oppressed.

Reformed confession: sure commandments steady the cry for help. Falsehood’s persecution drives to God, not to matching deceit.

Psalms 119:87

87They have almost made an end of me on earth, but I have not forsaken your precepts.

Arminian

Nearly consumed on earth, yet precepts not forsaken. Classical Arminians hear residual fidelity under extreme pressure—almost ended, still holding.

Shared marvel: many faint before conflict; here near-death still clings to the precepts.

Reformed

Calvin: though sorely tempted he kept footing because he had not given up true religion. Weakness forgets the law when shaken; many lose courage before they engage. Wonderful strength: almost reduced to death, yet courage revived by continual meditation on the law. “Upon the earth” hints that when death’s fears presented on all sides, he elevated his mind above the world.

Reformed accent: almost consumed yet not forsaking marks faith that reaches heaven and emerges from despair.

Psalms 119:88

88In your steadfast love give me life, that I may keep the testimonies of your mouth.

Arminian

Life asked according to steadfast love so that testimonies may be kept. Classical Arminians hear mercy restoring the servant unto obedience—quickening for keeping, not for self-indulgence.

The purpose clause matters: revived life aims at the testimonies. Shared pattern: love gives life; keeping follows.

Reformed

Calvin: he represents life as depending on God’s mercy—human frailty, daily exposure to death, and conviction that if God’s power withdrew he would lie as dead. Promising, when restored, he will not be ungrateful but acknowledge the blessing in life, not tongue only. Additional time allotted is to be dedicated to God’s service. The law is the testimony of God’s mouth—authority plainly asserted.

Reformed confession: steadfast love quickens the nearly dead unto keeping. Life and obedience are mercy’s fruit, not self-restarted strength.

Key contrast Both ask life from steadfast love so that God’s testimonies may be kept, and both refuse a revival unto self-will. Arminians typically hear mercy restoring a sincere waiter to renewed obedience—grace enabling the keeping longed for under delay. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s dependence: without God’s mercy he lies as dead; quickening is free goodness that then claims the restored life for the testimony of God’s mouth.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on fainting hope fixed on the word, statutes remembered in affliction, and life asked according to steadfast love—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 119:81–88.

Arminian Notable voices

“Fainteth—With long desire and earnest expectation. Bottle—A leathern bottle. My natural moisture is dried and burnt up. The days—The days of my life. I have but a little while to live; give me some respite before I die. Who—Who have no respect to thy law. Faithful—Just and true.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:81, 83–86)

Reformed Notable voices

“Though worn out with continual grief, trouble had not prevented him from always reposing with confidence in God. This fainting is opposed to the delicacy of those who cannot suffer a long delay. He kept his eyes fixed on the Divine word until his sight failed him. Though plunged into the deepest afflictions, we yet cease not to submit ourselves to God. Quicken me according to thy goodness: he represents his life as depending on God’s mercy; when restored to life he will keep the testimony of God’s mouth.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:81–83, 88)
