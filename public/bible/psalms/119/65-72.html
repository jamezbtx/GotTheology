Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 65–72 · ESV · Voices

The Teth stanza thanks God for dealing well according to his word, asks good judgment and knowledge, and confesses that affliction checked wandering and taught the statutes. God is good and does good; lies and unfeeling hearts oppose delight in the law; yet affliction is called good, and the law outweighs gold and silver. Both camps bless fatherly correction and prize the word above wealth; they differ on how affliction recovers the wanderer and what “I believe” implies when more light is asked.

Psalms 119:65

65You have dealt well with your servant, O Lord, according to your word.

Arminian

Good dealing according to the word is confessed. Classical Arminians hear gratitude for promise-kept kindness—experience confirming that God does not tease his servants with empty speech.

Shared praise: benefits flow along the channel of the word. Thanksgiving fits the servant who has tasted faithfulness.

Reformed

Calvin: he celebrates God’s faithfulness in performing the grace promised—not a vague “all is for the best,” but experienced truthfulness. Free goodness induces God to deal bountifully, yet we hope for nothing until he binds himself by his word.

Reformed accent: well-dealt and according to the word stand together. Promises are the conduit of benefits from the fountain of free goodness.

Psalms 119:66

66Teach me good judgment and knowledge, for I believe in your commandments.

Arminian

Wesley: Judgment—Whereby I may rightly discern between truth and falsehood. Knowledge—A spiritual experimental knowledge.

Classical Arminians hear faith that already embraces the commandments asking for discernment and experimental knowledge—docility that wants to grow. Belief is reason to request more light, not a claim to need none.

Reformed

Calvin: after experiencing God’s faithfulness, he asks to grow in right understanding—taste and knowledge together, sound discretion. He believed the commandments—cheerfully embraced the law—yet that holy zeal made him desire conformity and fear going astray; he pleads for another gift: sound taste and good understanding. Right affection and good understanding are both indispensable; after God frames the heart to obedience, we must ask wisdom to regulate our zeal.

Reformed confession: believing is not the end of need. Spirit-wrought docility asks further illuminating grace.

Psalms 119:67

67Before I was afflicted I went astray, but now I keep your word.

Arminian

Affliction checked wandering and restored keeping. Classical Arminians hear fatherly discipline that recovers those who stray—correction unto obedience, not cruelty for its own sake.

“Now I keep” is confession of mercy in the rod. Shared pastoral wisdom: pain can be a tutor back to the word.

Reformed

Calvin: in his own person he describes the wantonness common to all—we never yield obedience until compelled by chastisements; so long as God deals gently, we break into insolence. Even a prophet required forcible means. The flesh is obstreperous; God applies appropriate remedies—poverty, shame, disease, distress, labor—according to our vices. The fruit of chastisement should assuage its bitterness.

Reformed accent: affliction is God’s compulsory schooling of hard hearts. Wandering yields when the rod teaches; gentleness alone often leaves us insolent.

Key contrast Both bless affliction that ends wandering and restores keeping of the word, and both refuse a cruel God. Arminians typically hear fatherly correction that recovers stray servants under grace—discipline unto restored obedience. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s anthropology: we do not yield until compelled; while God deals gently we break into insolence, so the rod is needful schooling of rebellious flesh.

Psalms 119:68

68You are good and do good; teach me your statutes.

Arminian

God’s goodness and good-doing ground the plea to be taught. Classical Arminians hear character and action together—the Good One asked to teach statutes, not merely to increase comforts.

Shared aim: progress in the law as the kindness most desired.

Reformed

Calvin: nearly the same as the close of the prior octave—he beseeches God to exercise goodness not by riches, honors, or pleasures, but by enabling progress in knowing the law. The world asks bounty for fleshly desires; David would be satisfied if God were liberal in this one particular most men disdain.

Reformed reading: teach me is the shape goodness takes for the servant. Statute-learning outranks worldly “good dealing.”

Psalms 119:69

69The insolent smear me with lies, but with my whole heart I keep your precepts;

Arminian

Wholeheart keeping continues under smears. Classical Arminians hear integrity that will not trade the precepts for a repaired reputation among liars.

Shared fortitude: calumny is no excuse to loosen the heart’s grip on the word.

Reformed

Calvin: though the wicked put a malignant twist on all he did to turn him from uprightness, his mind remained unaltered. It is severe to be loaded with infamy while innocent; many good people shake when so rewarded. He continues observing the law; the proud—great men inflated with honors—trample as if he were a dead dog. Leave children of the world their reward; our crown is in heaven.

Reformed accent: whole-heart precept-keeping under woven lies is invincible constancy. Cast eyes to the heavenly stage.

Psalms 119:70

70their heart is unfeeling like fat, but I delight in your law.

Arminian

Wesley: Fat—They are stupid and insensible.

Classical Arminians hear contrast: callous hearts versus delight in the law. Insensibility is refused; pleasure in Scripture is cultivated under grace.

Reformed

Calvin: their heart is fat as grease—a vice common among despisers; grossness overgrows the heart so they are stupefied and even frenzied in obstinacy. Wonderful is the prophet’s magnanimity who found all delight in the law—as food that refreshed him—which could not be were the heart not cleansed from unhallowed pleasures.

Reformed confession: fat insensibility and law-delight are opposed. Delight marks a heart freed for the word’s sweetness.

Psalms 119:71

71It is good for me that I was afflicted, that I might learn your statutes.

Arminian

Affliction called good because it taught the statutes. Classical Arminians hear willing after-the-fact praise of the tutor—learning valued above ease.

Shared confession: the end of the rod is schooling in God’s word. Taste that fruit to sweeten correction.

Reformed

Calvin: confirms that it was profitable to be subdued by chastisements and brought to obedience. He confesses he was not exempt from the perverse obstinacy infecting all; otherwise the profit would be pretended. None willingly submits until God softens hardness with the hammer. Taste continually the fruit of corrections that they become sweet.

Reformed accent: “good for me” is learned theology of the rod. Affliction’s aim is statute-learning through mortified flesh.

Psalms 119:72

72The law of your mouth is better to me than thousands of gold and silver pieces.

Arminian

The law outvalues vast wealth. Classical Arminians hear reordered treasure—Scripture better than thousands of coins.

Shared verdict against the world’s immoderate desire of riches. Prefer the mouth of God to the market’s pile.

Reformed

Calvin: he preferred God’s law to all the world’s riches, whose immoderate desire infatuates most. He does not compare with riches he owned; he affirms the law more precious than a vast inheritance.

Reformed confession: law-as-better-than-gold is settled valuation. Desire is catechized away from infatuation toward the word.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on good dealing according to the word, affliction that ends wandering, and the law valued above gold—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 119:65–72.

Arminian Notable voices

“Judgment—Whereby I may rightly discern between truth and falsehood. Knowledge—A spiritual experimental knowledge. Fat—They are stupid and insensible.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:66, 70)

Reformed Notable voices

“I have really experienced that Thou art true and dost not delude thy servants with empty words. Teach me goodness of taste and knowledge: he asserts he believed God’s commandments—docile and obedient—and pleads for another gift, sound taste and good understanding. Right affection and good understanding are both necessary. Before I was brought low I went astray: we never yield obedience until compelled by chastisements; so long as God deals gently, we break into insolence. Even a prophet required forcible correction. It has been good for me that I was afflicted, that I might learn thy statutes.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:65–67, 71)
