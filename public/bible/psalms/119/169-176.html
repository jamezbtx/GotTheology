Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 169–176 · ESV · Voices

The Taw stanza closes the psalm with cry and plea for understanding and deliverance according to the word; lips and tongue will pour praise and sing because God teaches and all commandments are right. The hand is asked for help because precepts have been chosen; salvation is longed for and the law delighted in; the soul asks to live and praise with rules for help. The last line is a lost-sheep plea: gone astray, seek your servant, for the commandments are not forgotten. Both camps end on the Shepherd seeking the servant and refuse despair; they differ on whether the stray primarily confesses remaining-grace wander under grace or, with Calvin, hunted exile that still remembers the law while God must bring the servant home to safety.

Psalms 119:169

169Let my cry come before you, O Lord; give me understanding according to your word!

Arminian

Cry for understanding according to the word. Classical Arminians hear earnest plea for light promised in Scripture—progress in the law above secondary wants.

Shared priority: understanding that approaches God outranks earthly advantage. Ask according to the word’s promise.

Reformed

Calvin: repeats that his chief desire, regarding all else secondary, was progress in the divine law. Cry denotes earnestness—chiefly inflamed that the light of understanding by which we excel beasts and approach God may be preferred to earthly advantages. According to thy word: prefers fulfillment of the promise to endue with understanding (parallel to deliver according to thy word in v. 170). God liberally promises blessings; enlightening by his Spirit unto true wisdom ranks among the chief promises. Taught: nothing more desired than God’s guiding light; this is the peculiar gift of the Holy Spirit—vain to ask what he had naturally or might attain by painstaking; the promise invites the faithful to offer themselves to be enlightened—guide of the blind, teacher of little ones and the humble.

Reformed confession: cry for Spirit-given understanding according to the promise. Not native wit perfected by effort alone.

Psalms 119:170

170Let my plea come before you; deliver me according to your word.

Arminian

Plea for deliverance according to the word. Classical Arminians hear promise-shaped rescue—safety asked on what God said.

Shared comfort: God assures help in all dangers. Commend life to his protection when shut up by many deaths.

Reformed

Calvin: after asking right understanding, implores deliverance—continually involved in multiplied dangers impossible to escape unless God stretch forth his hand. Does not specify particular distress: in general terms he thought how shut up on every side by innumerable deaths. Inestimable comfort: God assures that in all dangers he will be ready to help.

Reformed accent: plea; deliver according to the word. Promise-backed rescue from surrounding deaths.

Psalms 119:171

171My lips will pour forth praise, for you teach me your statutes.

Arminian

Praise poured forth because God teaches statutes. Classical Arminians hear fluent thanks as the fruit of being taught—privilege of discipleship.

Shared order: taught first, then bubbling praise. Outward sound is not enough without inward teaching.

Reformed

Calvin: high privilege to be admitted among God’s disciples and profit in his school—if so privileged, he will hasten to render thanks with fluent tongue. Naba: metaphor from bubbling fountains—not simply speak but pour forth speech copiously. Rejoicing will testify he desires nothing more than to be thoroughly imbued with heavenly truth. Truly wise: first submit to the Word, not follow imaginations; second, God opens understanding and subdues it to obedience. Joins both: when God sets the law before us he also teaches inwardly. Ears stricken with outward sound are not enough unless God illuminate by the Spirit of understanding and correct obduracy by the Spirit of docility. Teachers labor in vain until virtue is given; the truly taught are not led from law and Scriptures by secret revelations like fanatics who trample the Word for foolish fancies.

Reformed confession: lips pour praise when God teaches. Outward word plus inward Spirit; no Word-despising revelations.

Psalms 119:172

172My tongue will sing of your word, for all your commandments are right.

Arminian

Tongue sings of the word because all commandments are right. Classical Arminians hear teaching that overflows to others once truth roots in the heart.

Shared duty: according to measure of faith, communicate what was received for the church’s edification. Do not bury doctrine.

Reformed

Calvin: when he has profited in the law he will also teach others. Order: divine truth take root in hearts before we teach; yet every man according to measure of faith ought to communicate what he received, that doctrine for common edification may not be buried. Reason that stirs the godly: by this means righteousness is spread. Honoring commandments as righteousness does not merely approve them but shows that until this rule bears sway the world is sad horrible confusion.

Reformed accent: tongue sings of the word—all commandments righteousness. Rooted truth shared; world unordered without this rule.

Psalms 119:173

173Let your hand be ready to help me, for I have chosen your precepts.

Arminian

Hand ready to help because precepts chosen. Classical Arminians hear devoted choice as the plea’s companion—not merit purchase, but sincere preference that still needs God’s hand.

Shared realism: those yielded to the word continually need help. Choosing the law costs a struggle with fleshly impulses.

Reformed

Calvin: devoted to the law’s doctrine, he requests God’s hand stretched forth for aid. Those who yield to be governed by the word continually need help. The more sincerely one studies to be good, the more Satan troubles and enemies multiply. When God sees those who embraced the truth remaining steadfast, he is so much the more inclined to aid. Choose: nothing hindered him from devoting himself to the law. No one applies this mind to love of the law without great struggle—thoughts drawn away by depraved flesh. Choosing shows it is not ignorance or inconsiderate zeal; partaking flexibility common to men and feeling flesh’s impulses, they purposely subdue minds to God’s obedience.

Reformed confession: hand to help—I have chosen precepts. Steadfast choosers still plead for continual aid.

Psalms 119:174

174I long for your salvation, O Lord, and your law is my delight.

Arminian

Longing for salvation; law as delight. Classical Arminians hear patient desire comforted by Scripture—delight in the law while waiting for deliverance.

Shared warning: many want safety while fleeing God; true longing aims at God’s salvation and finds consolation in the word.

Reformed

Calvin: all desire happy circumstances and none avowedly repudiates God’s favor; yet ideas of happiness are so confused that few direct aspirations to God—ambition, avarice, lust imagining prosperity the farther they recede from him. In proportion as each desires to be safe he provokes God by seeking safety elsewhere. Hebrew construction denotes steadfast constancy of desire—had longed, not begun only now. Manner of patiently longing: seeking consolation in all calamities from the word; whoever does not comfort himself by reliance on grace promised in the word will quail at the slightest assault. Kept thoughts close upon the divine word lest turned from hoping for God’s salvation.

Reformed accent: long for salvation; law my delight. Word-anchored longing versus self-made safety.

Psalms 119:175

175Let my soul live and praise you, and let your rules help me.

Arminian

Wesley: Judgments—Thy word or testimonies, which are the only ground of my hope in thy help.

Classical Arminians hear life asked for praise’s sake—live to thank, helped by the word. Shared end: prolonged life aimed at God’s praise; rules/word as succor.

Reformed

Calvin: if futures, a rejoicing—when thou hast bestowed life I will show I am not ungrateful; life safe under God’s faithful guardianship though hidden under death’s shadow; assured confidence from quickening grace in the word. Majority take optative: asks life prolonged that he may exercise himself in singing praises. Judgments as commandments giving help is harsh; better—perceiving himself among wolves, he calls God to protect by restraining the wicked by secret providence. Or, as judgments often means commandments in this psalm: attribute to the word the office of giving succor—God does not feed with delusive promises but confirms the word by palpable operation of his hand. Calling the law to help is encomium on the word’s efficacy. Some: uprightness in keeping commandments as defense—he offers no objection.

Reformed accent: live and praise; judgments/word help. Life for praise; worded providence for succor.

Psalms 119:176

176I have gone astray like a lost sheep; seek your servant, for I do not forget your commandments.

Arminian

Lost-sheep confession with a servant’s plea and memory of the commandments. Classical Arminians hear remaining grace in the very asking: still God’s servant, still not forgetting what he commanded—strayed yet not abandoned to blank forgetfulness, and therefore bold to cry Seek me.

Shared pastoral close: both camps end on the Shepherd seeking the servant. Do not invent a sheep that needs no seeking; do not invent seeking that erases real stray. The psalm closes in dependence, not self-rescue.

Reformed

Calvin: not to be understood as confessing sins—as if drawn into Satan’s trails—an opinion many hold erroneously; that contradicts the second clause denying he had forgotten God’s law. Poor solutions: wandering only before calling, or partly withheld by godly affection—the same time is in both clauses. Connect by although: Although I have wandered like a lost sheep, yet I have not forgotten the law. Meaning: chased by enemies’ force, he moved place to place in fear seeking retreats. David so hunted that exile found no secure place. Similitude fits: driven and hunted, yet never turned aside from the law. Wolves everywhere—he prays God to bring him back and give safety and tranquillity, to cease wandering as a vagabond. Good ground for being heard: though provoked by manifold wrongs he never swerved from the fear of God—referred to the general course of life more than particular acts (even noting adultery’s temporary insensibility does not erase holy patience in adversities).

Reformed confession: lost sheep under the hunt; seek thy servant—Shepherd restores to safety. Memory of the statutes marks fidelity under persecution more than a merit ticket; God must seek and settle the vagabond.

Key contrast Both close with Seek your servant and refuse a self-finding sheep, sharing the pastoral plea that the Shepherd must seek. Arminians typically hear remaining grace in the servant who still does not forget the commandments—strayed yet still able to ask, memory proving grace not extinguished. Reformed readers typically follow Calvin: the verse is not primarily a confession of falling into Satan’s trails (which would clash with “I have not forgotten”); it is the hunted exile’s wander—although driven like a lost sheep, yet unswerved from the law—and the plea that God bring his servant back to safety.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on cry for understanding, deliverance by the word, praise taught by statutes, and the lost sheep seeking the Shepherd’s search—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 119:169–176.

Arminian Notable voices

“Judgments—Thy word or testimonies, which are the only ground of my hope in thy help.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:175)

Reformed Notable voices

“Let my cry come near; give me understanding according to thy word: chief desire is progress in the law; understanding is the Spirit’s peculiar gift, in fulfillment of the promise—not what we have naturally or by painstaking. Let thy hand succor me; for I have chosen thy commandments: those yielded to the word continually need help; choosing marks purposeful subduing of the flesh. I have wandered like a lost sheep; seek thy servant, for I have not forgotten thy statutes: not to be read as confessing being drawn into Satan’s trails—that contradicts the second clause. Although hunted from place to place by enemies, yet he never turned aside from the law; he prays God to bring him back to safety. Ground of being heard: though provoked, he never swerved from the fear of God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:169, 173, 176)
