Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 49–56 · ESV · Voices

The Zayin stanza pleads that God remember the word in which he made the servant hope, finds comfort and life in the promise under affliction, and holds the law amid insolent derision. Old judgments comfort; hot indignation rises at those who forsake the law; statutes become songs in sojourning, and the name is remembered at night. Both camps cling to the promise under scorn; they differ on whether the hope itself is God’s caused work in the servant or the sincere response the promise invites.

Psalms 119:49

49Remember your word to your servant, in which you have made me hope.

Arminian

Wesley: Thy word—Thy promises.

Classical Arminians hear prayer that God perform what he pledged, and frank confession that hope rests on that word. The promise is public ground on which servants may hope; “made me hope” names the word’s power to awaken trust under grace.

Reformed

Calvin: he prays God would really perform what he promised—the event proves God does not forget. Cause was given him to hope; there would be no place for hope unless grace had been presented. He speaks of the promises.

Reformed accent: God made the hope by presenting grace in the word. Remembering the word is asking performance for the servant in whom hope was caused—not a self-started optimism.

Key contrast Both rest hope on God’s promise and both ask God to “remember” by performing it. Arminians typically hear the promise as sincere public ground that awakens hope in servants who trust. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s note that hope has no place unless grace has been presented—God caused the hope in the servant; the plea asks him to finish what his word began.

Psalms 119:50

50This is my comfort in my affliction, that your promise gives me life.

Arminian

The promise itself comforts and quickens under affliction. Classical Arminians hear Scripture as living consolation—not a distraction from pain, but life given by what God has said.

Shared pastoral ground: when other refuges fail, the word revives. Do not invent comforts that bypass the promise.

Reformed

Calvin: though God kept him in suspense, he reposed in the word. He did not hunt vain consolations as the world does; satisfied with the word, he found life full and perfect when other refuges failed—and covertly confesses that without courage from the word he would be as a dead man.

Reformed confession: the promise animates in affliction. Life in the midst of death comes by careful meditation on what God has spoken.

Psalms 119:51

51The insolent utterly deride me, but I do not turn away from your law.

Arminian

Derision does not pry the singer from the law. Classical Arminians hear steadfastness under scorn—honesty may draw insult, yet turning away is refused.

Hold the law when mockery is “utter.” Shared call: do not let the majority’s liberty in unbelief make you dislike God’s word.

Reformed

Calvin: though honesty render us obnoxious to insults, we must by unflinching constancy repel pride lest we take a dislike to the law. Unbelievers are called proud—their wisdom despises God. “Very much” means the attack continued day by day; the wicked, being the majority, arrogate greater liberty.

Reformed accent: stability of faith requires disregarding reviling. Pride’s contempt is met by not turning aside.

Psalms 119:52

52When I think of your rules from of old, I take comfort, O Lord.

Arminian

Wesley: Judgments—Thy ancient dispensations to the children of men in punishing the ungodly.

Classical Arminians hear comfort from remembering how God has long judged and ruled—history catechizes hope. Old rules steady present scorn.

Reformed

Calvin: “of old” points to examples by which God made himself known as righteous Judge. Remembering ancient judgments confirms the law when God does not always bare his arm; vengeance on the ungodly confirms what was spoken. The faithful also find consolation learning that after the wicked revel, they face God’s seat, while those who combat under such a Guardian need not doubt their preservation.

Reformed reading: old judgments comfort by proving God has not spoken in vain. Ingratitude, not lack of demonstrations, hides them from us.

Psalms 119:53

53Hot indignation seizes me because of the wicked, who forsake your law.

Arminian

Wesley: Horror—A mixed passion, made up of abhorrence of their sins, and dread and sorrow at the consideration of the judgments of God coming upon them.

Classical Arminians hear holy grief and dread over law-forsaking—not smug rage. Love of the law includes sorrow at its abandonment.

Reformed

Calvin: either grief at seeing the law violated, or horror at the thought of the wicked’s perdition; he abides by fear marking ardent zeal—held in detestation the impious boldness of those who lightly esteem the law. It is no new offense if numbers throw off God’s yoke; fear of God kindled holy indignation rather than discouragement.

Reformed accent: horror at forsaking the law counters the flattery that “what is common is lawful.” God’s long-suffering does not cancel coming judgment.

Psalms 119:54

54Your statutes have been my songs in the house of my sojourning.

Arminian

Wesley: The house—In this world, wherein I am a stranger and pilgrim, as all my fathers were.

Classical Arminians hear the statutes as pilgrimage songs—joy in God’s word while still not home. Shared piety: sojourners sing the law, not only lament exile.

Reformed

Calvin: singing indicates joy; saints are pilgrims with inheritance in heaven. David in exile ceased not to draw consolation—or rather joy above sadness—from the law, though denied temple and sacrifices; he never departed from his God.

Reformed confession: statutes as songs mark rare virtue under deprivation. Pilgrimage is not excuse for silence about the word.

Psalms 119:55

55I remember your name in the night, O Lord, and keep your law.

Arminian

Wesley: Thy name—Thy holy nature and attributes, thy blessed word, and thy wonderful works. In the night—When darkness causes fear in others, I took pleasure in remembering thee; and when others gave themselves up to sleep, my thoughts and affections were working towards thee.

Classical Arminians hear night remembrance joined to daytime keeping. Affection in the dark feeds obedience in the light.

Reformed

Calvin’s stanza ties night remembrance to keeping the law—secret devotion, not ostentation. The name remembered when others sleep marks earnest desire that breaks ordinary rest for praise and fidelity.

Reformed accent: nocturnal piety and law-keeping belong together. God-fearing memory is not idle sentiment.

Psalms 119:56

56This blessing has fallen to me, that I have kept your precepts.

Arminian

Wesley: This—This comfortable remembrance.

Classical Arminians hear the comfort of vv. 54–55 as gift tied to keeping—not wages that purchase God, yet a real blessing attending those who hold the precepts. Gratitude, not boasting, fits the line.

Reformed

Calvin connects this “this is done to me” with having kept the statutes—comfort experienced in the path of fidelity. Keeping is not the root of mercy but the place where promised consolation is tasted.

Reformed reading: blessing fallen and precepts kept are confessed together without Pelagian purchase. The servant traces comfort to the life of obedience under the word.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the remembered promise that causes hope, comfort in affliction, and songs in the house of pilgrimage—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 119:49–56.

Arminian Notable voices

“Thy word—Thy promises. Judgments—Thy ancient dispensations to the children of men in punishing the ungodly. Horror—A mixed passion, made up of abhorrence of their sins, and dread and sorrow at the consideration of the judgments of God coming upon them. The house—In this world, wherein I am a stranger and pilgrim, as all my fathers were. Thy name—Thy holy nature and attributes, thy blessed word, and thy wonderful works. In the night—When darkness causes fear in others, I took pleasure in remembering thee. This—This comfortable remembrance.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:49, 52–56)

Reformed Notable voices

“He prays that God would really perform what he promised; for the event proves that he does not forget his word. Cause was given him to hope, for which there would be no place unless grace had been presented to him. During troubles he did not search after vain consolation as the world does; he was satisfied with the word of God itself—there he found life. If we meditate carefully on his word, we shall live even in the midst of death. Though the proud have treated me with scorn, I have not turned aside from thy law.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:49–51)
