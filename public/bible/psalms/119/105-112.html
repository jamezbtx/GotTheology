Passage

Psalms 119

Verses 105–112 · ESV · Voices

The Nun stanza names the word as lamp and light, confirms an oath to keep righteous rules, and asks life under severe affliction according to the word. Freewill offerings of praise seek acceptance and teaching; life held in the hand still remembers the law; snares do not produce wandering; testimonies are everlasting heritage and heart-joy; the heart is inclined to perform the statutes forever. Both camps walk by Scripture’s light and prize vowed obedience; they differ on whether inclining the heart is a work we begin or a grace we also ask God to work in us.

Psalms 119:105

105Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path.

Arminian

Word as lamp and light for feet and path. Classical Arminians hear Scripture as practical guide—not optional ornament but necessary brightness for walking.

Shared rule: oppose God’s word to private “what seems good.” Open eyes to the unerring light given.

Reformed

Calvin: Divine Law as schoolmaster and guide; by example he prescribes the same rule—highly necessary, for while each follows what seems good, we tangle in mazes. Word set against all human counsels; what the world judges right is often crooked before God. Metaphor implies more: unless the word enlighten, whole life is darkness and miserable wandering; submitting docilely, we are in no danger of going astray. Unerring light is found there if we open our eyes (cf. 2 Peter 1:19).

Reformed accent: lamp and light require renouncing flesh’s wisdom first. Docility lets the word shine.

Psalms 119:106

106I have sworn an oath and confirmed it, to keep your righteous rules.

Arminian

Oath sworn and confirmed to keep righteous rules. Classical Arminians hear solemn resolve—hearty, uniform dedication, not a passing mood.

Shared seriousness: vowing to God offers life to his service. Courage to swear leans on promised help, not bravado.

Reformed

Calvin: constancy of purpose; swear intimates solemn pledge not to alter determination. True keeping receives heartily and uniformly lest ardor abate. Proper vowing offers ourselves and dedicates life. Is the oath rash beyond ability? The faithful, vowing, look not to what they can do of themselves but depend on God’s grace and Spirit—who supplies strength. Not sufficient of ourselves to think anything (2 Cor 3:5). No rashness when, confiding in promises that anticipate us, we offer ourselves. They also look to free forgiveness along the way.

Reformed confession: sworn keeping rests on Spirit-strength and mercy. Oath is grace-dependent consecration.

Psalms 119:107

107I am severely afflicted; give me life, O Lord, according to your word!

Arminian

Severe affliction; life asked according to the word. Classical Arminians hear prayer at the edge—quickening sought by promise, not by claim of desert.

Shared key: according to your word unlocks boldness in sorrow. Promise inspires courage to ask.

Reformed

Calvin: under the law the fathers were exercised with grievous temptations—afflicted above measure. Quicken implies point of death; yet he leaned upon God. Extreme fear often extinguishes confidence; he implores grace to recover life lost—low condition and continued confidence. Observe: according to thy word—without the promise we pray coldly or not at all.

Reformed accent: severe affliction meets word-based plea for life. Promise is the key to the throne of grace.

Psalms 119:108

108Accept my freewill offerings of praise, O Lord, and teach me your rules.

Arminian

Wesley: Offerings—The sacrifices of prayer and praise.

Classical Arminians hear praise offered and teaching asked together—worship that wants to learn. Shared desire: accept our lips; teach us your rules.

Reformed

Calvin: prefer reading as one sentence—accept sacrifices to this end, that thou teach me. Nothing more precious than understanding the law. Free-will offerings allude to law custom; praises as calves of the lips (Hos 14:2). He acknowledges unworthiness—if God hears, it is free grace. Ratsah: favor of mere good will; nothing meritorious in prayers; whenever God hears, it is free goodness.

Reformed confession: accepted praise and taught rules are both mercy. Chief desire is to be instructed aright.

Psalms 119:109

109I hold my life in my hand continually, but I do not forget your law.

Arminian

Wesley: Is—Exposed to perpetual danger.

Classical Arminians hear endangered life that still remembers the law—danger without amnesia. Shared courage: snares need not erase Scripture from the heart.

Reformed

Calvin: no calamities withdrew him from service and observance. Soul in hand = life in danger, abandoned as to the wind. Not “I govern my life as I please”—rather, though tossed among shipwrecks with death in a hundred forms, he had not cast off love and study of the law.

Reformed accent: life in hand, law not forgotten. Extreme exposure tests memory of the word.

Psalms 119:110

110The wicked have laid a snare for me, but I do not stray from your precepts.

Arminian

Snares laid; precepts not strayed from. Classical Arminians hear refusal to wander into retaliatory craft—path of the word under trap-setters.

Shared counsel: when surrounded by wiles, follow where God calls; attempt nothing against his will.

Reformed

Calvin: specifies carrying life in hand—hemmed by snares, scarcely any hope. Hard not to wander when enemies’ subtle arts seek destruction; depraved desire incites retaliation and howling among wolves. Best: follow where God calls; attempt nothing but what agrees with his will.

Reformed confession: snare without straying honors God’s ways. Refuse the wicked’s methods as path to safety.

Psalms 119:111

111Your testimonies are my heritage forever, for they are the joy of my heart.

Arminian

Testimonies as forever heritage and heart-joy. Classical Arminians hear reordered treasure—word valued above riches and pleasures.

Shared delight: love sets the value; joy in the testimonies begins true observance.

Reformed

Calvin: law more precious than pleasures, riches, possessions—repeated because few aim at the law while the world boils for lusts. Love alone leads us to value; begin with delight. Testimonies bring joy—heaven opened, adoption, pardon, God’s care. Inheritance: hold in highest estimation, content to lose all else for safe possession of that one thing. Other goods adventitious; word alone as inheritance.

Reformed accent: forever heritage and heart-joy dethrone rival treasures. Delight is the gateway to reverent observance.

Psalms 119:112

112I incline my heart to perform your statutes forever, to the end.

Arminian

Heart inclined to perform statutes forever, to the end. Classical Arminians hear willing, lifelong obedience—cheerfulness against constrained slavery.

The incline is real resolve under grace; perseverance to the end is intended. Shared aim: statutes performed from the heart, not hands only.

Reformed

Calvin: right observance begins with cheerfully and heartily preparing to do what the law commands; slavish obedience differs little from rebellion. He applied not only hands and feet but began with the heart’s affection. Inclination opposed to wandering lusts. Papists triflingly infer free will from “I inclined”; but he had prayed “Incline my heart,” acknowledging the Spirit’s peculiar work. It is no new thing for that to be ascribed to us which God works in us (Phil 2:13). He does not separate endeavor from the Spirit’s grace by whose inspiration he declared the whole was done.

Reformed confession: forever incline is Spirit-worked constancy confessed as ours. Free-will boasts from this verse miss the prayer and the Spirit.

Key contrast Both want a heart inclined to perform the statutes for life, and both refuse bare external constraint. Arminians typically hear sincere, grace-assisted resolve—cheerfully setting the heart to lifelong obedience. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s rebuttal of free-will boasts: the same psalm prays “Incline my heart,” and what is ascribed to us is what God works in us by the Spirit.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the word as lamp, oaths to keep, heritage-joy in the testimonies, and a heart inclined to the statutes—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 119:105–112.

Arminian Notable voices

“Offerings—The sacrifices of prayer and praise. Is—Exposed to perpetual danger.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 119:108–109)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Divine Law was his schoolmaster; the word is set against all human counsels—without it life is darkness. I have sworn: he solemnly pledged himself; the true manner of keeping is to embrace heartily and uniformly. When the faithful vow, they depend upon the grace of God and the Spirit’s strength—Not that we are sufficient of ourselves. I have inclined my heart: Papists triflingly infer free will; but he had prayed “Incline my heart,” acknowledging it as the Spirit’s peculiar work. That is ascribed to us which God works in us.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 119:105–106, 112)
