Passage

Psalms 17

Verses 1–15 · ESV · Voices

Psalm 17 is David’s prayer of a just cause: he asks the LORD to hear a cry from lips free of deceit, to let vindication come from God’s presence, and to behold the right. Night-tried and tested, he claims a heart where God will find nothing of the charge, a mouth purposed not to transgress, and steps held to God’s paths by the word of God’s lips—away from the ways of the violent. He calls on God as Savior of those who seek refuge, asking to be kept as the apple of the eye and hidden in the shadow of wings from surrounding deadly enemies—fat-hearted, arrogant, lion-like. He pleads that God arise, confront, and deliver him from men of the world whose portion is this life, and ends contrasting their treasure and children with his own hope: to behold God’s face in righteousness and, when he awakes, to be satisfied with God’s likeness.

Psalms 17:1

1Hear a just cause, O LORD; attend to my cry! Give ear to my prayer from lips free of deceit!

Arminian

Wesley: The right—Regard my righteous cause. David opens by appealing to God for integrity: a cry and prayer that do not go out of feigned lips.

Arminian hearing: shared—ask God to hear a just cause from honest lips. Grace enables sincere prayer; the hearer must still refuse deceit and bring a real cause, not a covered sin, before the LORD.

Reformed

Calvin: David sets forth the goodness of his cause because God has promised not to suffer the innocent to be forever oppressed. Confiding in integrity, he interposes God as Judge; yet adds earnest cry and prayer—hypocrites cover sins with loud piety, but he protests he does not speak deceitfully. The finest grace before God is pure simplicity.

Reformed accent: shared—sincere prayer under a just cause. A good conscience alone is not enough; the cause must be committed to God in faith and prayer, not clenched in stubborn silence.

Psalms 17:2

2From your presence let my vindication come! Let your eyes behold the right!

Arminian

Wesley: Sentence—Judgment on my behalf. Come—From thy tribunal. Vindication is asked from God’s presence, not from human courts stacked against the accused.

Arminian pastoral: shared—let the Judge’s eyes behold what is right. Grace steadies the appeal; the hearer still seeks sentence from God alone when calumny would rewrite the case.

Reformed

Calvin: if God does not rise as vindicator, the innocent will be overwhelmed with calumnies and counted guilty. David asks no other judge and does not shrink from God’s seat, bringing a pure heart and a good cause. He beseeches God actually to show he does not connive at wickedness when the defenseless are mistreated.

Reformed accent: shared—vindication from God’s face. Providence, not chance reputation, must settle the right when falsehood spreads darkness.

Psalms 17:3

3You have tried my heart, you have visited me by night, you have tested me, and you will find nothing; I have purposed that my mouth will not transgress.

Arminian

Wesley: Proved—searched or tried by many temptations and afflictions. Night—when minds act more freely according to their inclinations. Tried—as goldsmiths do metals. Nothing—nothing of unrighteousness. Purposed—resolved upon deliberation. Mouth—so far from practicing against Saul’s life as charged, that he will not wrong him even in a word.

Arminian reading: night trial and deliberate purpose name a real integrity in the matter charged—grace-enabled resolve the pray-er has actually taken and must keep. God finds nothing of the violence alleged; the mouth’s purpose is a lived restraint, not a slogan.

Reformed

Calvin: God, who understands all secret affections, knows David is not double and cherishes no deceit. Unjustly charged, he appeals to God as judge—but not rashly: he subjects himself to impartial examination, for God searches the heart’s recesses and cannot be deceived by appearance. By night, coverings removed, neither disguise nor deceit will be found; thought shall not pass beyond mouth—no heart thinking one thing while the tongue says another.

Reformed accent: integrity is God-searched sincerity under false charge, offered to the Judge who alone tries the heart—not a self-certified innocence that never needed divine scrutiny.

Key contrast Both confess that God has tried the heart by night and will find nothing of the alleged deceit or violence, and that the mouth is purposed not to transgress. Arminian readers, with Wesley, stress deliberated resolve and lived restraint in the charge—integrity the pray-er has actually purposed and must keep under temptation. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress submitting that claim to God’s searching judgment: sincerity without disguise, thought matching speech, because only God tries the secret heart and cannot be fooled by fair appearance.

Psalms 17:4

4With regard to the works of man, by the word of your lips I have avoided the ways of the violent.

Arminian

Wesley: Concerning—observing the wicked works of the age, he took more care in ordering his own actions. By—by the help of thy blessed word. Paths—customs of the violent man: Saul, courtiers, and soldiers.

Arminian pastoral: shared—God’s word keeps the steps off the violent path. Grace supplies the word’s help; the hearer must still watch men’s works and refuse to copy the destroyer’s ways.

Reformed

Calvin: though malicious men provoked him to evil, David was restrained by the word of God from exercising violence or rendering evil for evil. He hung, as it were, upon God’s mouth rather than matching injury for injury. When enemies provoke revenge, meditate the word: God withholds us from harming neighbors not only by forbidding but by promising to take vengeance into his own hand.

Reformed accent: shared—the word restrains from violent paths. Patience under injury is Word-governed; vengeance is God’s office, not the provoked heart’s right.

Psalms 17:5

5My steps have held fast to your paths; my feet have not slipped.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: held goings and unslipped feet continue the integrity claim—paths kept by the word against the destroyer. Steps that hold fast are practiced obedience under pressure.

Arminian hearing: grace enables clinging to God’s paths; the hearer must still hold fast so feet do not slip. Perseverance here is real walking that can be asked to continue, not a bare label.

Reformed

Calvin: though David boasted of practicing righteousness with a pure heart amid grievous temptations, conscious of weakness he commits himself to God to be governed and prays for grace to persevere—keep my steps from sliding for the time to come. The more one excels in grace, the more one ought to fear falling; Satan uses God-given strength to breed carnal confidence. Alternatively, paths may mean God’s upholding power that makes affairs prosper under his protection.

Reformed accent: unslipped feet are prayed and kept under God’s governance—perseverance asked from the One who upholds, not presumed from past integrity alone.

Key contrast Both confess that steps held to God’s paths and feet that have not slipped belong to a life kept from violent ways. Arminian readers stress grace-enabled holding fast the hearer must continue—lived clinging so the foot does not slip. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress conscious weakness that commits future steps to God’s governing hand and asks grace to persevere, lest past strength breed careless confidence.

Psalms 17:6

6I call upon you, for you will answer me, O God; incline your ear to me; hear my words.

Arminian

Wesley’s movement: the integrity appeal turns to confident petition—calling because God will hear. Prayer is earnest speech God is asked to incline toward.

Arminian pastoral: shared—call with assurance that God answers. Grace grounds the confidence; the hearer must still actually call and speak words God is asked to hear.

Reformed

Calvin: the past tense denotes continued act. David animates himself to call from confident hope of being heard—since I call, surely thou wilt not despise my prayers—then asks the blessings of which he entertains assured hope. Faith depending on God’s promise hopes prayer will not be vain.

Reformed accent: shared—calling rests on God who hears. Assurance to pray is hope in God’s faithfulness, not self-confidence that browbeats heaven.

Psalms 17:7

7Wondrously show your steadfast love, O Savior of those who seek refuge from their adversaries at your right hand.

Arminian

Wesley: By—By thy great power. Marvelous lovingkindness is asked from the Savior who saves those who put their trust in him from those that rise against them.

Arminian hearing: shared—wondrous steadfast love for refuge-seekers. Grace enables fleeing to God’s right hand; the hearer must still seek that refuge and trust the Savior against adversaries.

Reformed

Calvin: perceiving deliverance required singular means, David betakes himself to God’s wonderful power. God is honored as preserver of those who trust in him—ready to succor if faith relies on his grace. When every ordinary way is shut, remember his inconceivable means of help. The ungodly who molest the faithful exalt themselves against God’s hand; outrage against the righteous is war against their guardian.

Reformed accent: shared—marvelous hesed from the Savior of trusters. Refuge is wholehearted trust under a God who preserves his own, even by extraordinary deliverance.

Psalms 17:8

8Keep me as the apple of your eye; hide me in the shadow of your wings,

Arminian

Wesley’s plea continues into the eye and wing images: tender keeping and shelter under God when enemies press. The apple of the eye and shadow of wings are pastoral pictures of near protection.

Arminian pastoral: shared—ask to be kept and hidden. Grace invites that trust; the hearer must still take shelter under the wings rather than invent another cover.

Reformed

Calvin: God compares his care to a hen covering her young and declares his people as dear as the apple of the eye. Whenever men rise to injure the righteous, war is waged against God. This form of prayer, put in David’s mouth by the Spirit, contains a promise—astonishing goodness that lifts faith above fleshly conceptions.

Reformed accent: shared—eye and wing are God’s pledged care. Protection is covenant nearness; assault on the hidden ones strikes at their Keeper.

Psalms 17:9

9from the wicked who do me violence, my deadly enemies who surround me.

Arminian

Wesley: the defense begged in vv. 1–9 meets violent, deadly enemies who compass about. Surrounding threat makes the eye-and-wing plea concrete.

Arminian pastoral: shared—name the violence and ask cover. Grace does not deny danger; the hearer still flees under wings when deadly enemies close in.

Reformed

Calvin: by accusing enemies David sets forth his innocence and complains of cruelty so God may be more inclined to aid. They burn with desire to destroy him and besiege him in his soul until they accomplish his death. The greater the terror, the more the faithful should quicken ardor in prayer, disburdening calamities into God’s bosom.

Reformed accent: shared—deadly surrounding drives prayer. Cruelty does not erase innocence before God; it sends the oppressed to the Protector who hears.

Psalms 17:10

10They close their hearts to pity; with their mouths they speak arrogantly.

Arminian

Wesley: They—They live in splendour and prosperity. Fat-closed hearts and proud mouths mark enemies swollen with ease.

Arminian warning: shared—prosperity without pity breeds arrogant speech. Grace softens the opposite heart; refuse their hard compassion and proud talk as a path to walk.

Reformed

Calvin: fat denotes pride filling and choking the heart—enclosed on all sides with arrogance, strangers to humanity. This shows in speech: with mouths widely opened they pour scorn that testifies to inward pride. The more luxuriously the ungodly are pampered, the more outrageously they often conduct themselves.

Reformed accent: shared—fat hearts and arrogant mouths. Inward presumption, not mere manners, drives the proud speech God will answer.

Psalms 17:11

11They have now surrounded our steps; they set their eyes to cast us to the ground.

Arminian

Wesley: Steps—In all our ways; to rocks, caves, and woods, yet they surround us. Eyes—fixed to watch the fittest opportunity. Bowing—couching to surprise.

Arminian pastoral: shared—no human route shakes the hunt. Grace sends the cry upward when steps are ringed; do not trust a new cave more than God.

Reformed

Calvin: enemies are so bent on destruction that wherever he turns they pursue. Now marks present and pressing danger. They fix eyes to cast down to the ground—applying all ingenuity to overthrow. Without God’s hand from heaven, no escape remains.

Reformed accent: shared—surrounded steps need divine rescue. Human flight alone cannot outpace eyes set to cast down; God must intervene.

Psalms 17:12

12He is like a lion eager to tear, as a young lion lurking in ambush.

Arminian

Wesley’s portrait of the enemy peaks in the lion image—greedy to tear, lurking in secret. The violence of vv. 9–11 is given a face.

Arminian pastoral: shared—name the predator without flattery. Grace keeps the prey praying; refuse both despair and matching the lion’s ways.

Reformed

Calvin: they are like lions and whelps skilled in stratagem, with power to execute mischief. The more proudly the wicked exercise cruelty, the nearer God’s hand to restrain wild beasts who delight in shedding blood.

Reformed accent: shared—lion eagerness meets a stronger hand. Ambush does not rewrite providence; God subdues tearers for his people’s sake.

Psalms 17:13

13Arise, O LORD! Confront him, subdue him! Deliver my soul from the wicked by your sword,

Arminian

Wesley: Sword—Thy instrument to execute vengeance upon thine enemies. Do not punish me with this rod: let me fall into thy hands, and not into the hands of men.

Arminian hearing: shared—ask God to arise, confront, and deliver. Grace teaches preferring God’s hand to men’s cruelty; the hearer still cries for the LORD’s sword rather than private revenge.

Reformed

Calvin: the more furiously David was persecuted, the more earnestly he asks immediate aid. When death shows itself at hand, God has remedies to effect deliverance in a moment. He arms God to crush the wicked so far as needed to stop outrage—then again asks deliverance of his soul. If God himself does not take vengeance and draw his sword, no hope of deliverance remains.

Reformed accent: shared—Arise and deliver by God’s sword. Rescue is God’s office with ready power; human aids are not the final stay when the wicked press.

Psalms 17:14

14from men by your hand, O LORD, from men of the world whose portion is in this life. You fill their womb with treasure; they are satisfied with children, and they leave their abundance to their infants.

Arminian

Wesley: Thy hand—Wherewith thou dost correct me. World—Who set their hearts upon this world and neither have nor desire any other portion. Belly—mind or appetite. Treasure—extraordinary wealth and glory. Children—when many faithful servants are barren, these are blessed with numerous posterity.

Arminian pastoral: shared—worldly men may have this-life portion, treasure, and heirs while the godly wait. Grace trains another desire; do not envy their filled appetite or measure God’s love by their abundance.

Reformed

Calvin: deliver by thy hand from men who have prevailed too long and wallowed in prosperity. Their portion is in life—exempt from common troubles, abounding in pleasures as if by hereditary right. God often treats them delicately, filling them with rare dainties as secret goods; they transmit riches to children’s children. This is severe temptation if one measures God’s favor by earthly prosperity—yet David complains to console himself, directing groanings to heaven, not murmuring against God. Fattened outside the children of God, they are for the day of slaughter; he asks God to hasten vengeance on abused liberality.

Reformed accent: shared—this-life portion is not covenant favor. Prosperity of the worldly is often soft rope, not proof that God prefers them to the oppressed righteous.

Psalms 17:15

15As for me, I shall behold your face in righteousness; when I awake, I shall be satisfied with your likeness.

Arminian

Wesley: I will—I do not place my portion in earthly treasures, but in beholding God’s face, in the enjoyment of God’s presence and favour; enjoyed in part in this life, but not fully. Satisfied—the time is coming wherein I shall be abundantly satisfied with beholding thy face. Awake—when I arise from the dead. Likeness—with the image of God stamped upon my glorified soul.

Arminian reading: the fork from worldly portion is deliberate—beholding God’s face in righteousness, waking to satisfaction with his likeness. Grace opens that hope now in part and fully at resurrection; the hearer must still refuse earth as final portion and aim at God’s face and image.

Reformed

Calvin: rising by faith above confusion, David assures himself he will again enjoy familiar beholding of God. To behold God’s face is to sense fatherly favor that transports even to heaven. By righteousness he means he will not be disappointed of the reward of a good conscience—not that works are the meritorious ground of salvation, but that those who serve God do not lose their labor; God causes them again to behold his bright countenance. Satisfaction is God’s grace alone giving contentment; true happiness is sought in God alone. Awaking refers to David obtaining respite from sorrow’s sleep when God’s favor shines—already tasting spiritual satisfaction until God brings us to enjoy him face to face.

Reformed accent: face and likeness are reconciled favor and lasting joy in God—good conscience answered in due season, without making works the meriting cause of acceptance.

Key contrast Both confess that David’s portion is not earthly treasure but beholding God’s face in righteousness, and that waking satisfaction comes with God’s likeness—hope beyond this-life abundance. Arminian readers, with Wesley, stress choosing that portion now in part and fully at rising from the dead, with God’s image stamped on the glorified soul. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress fatherly favor restored to the upright conscience without treating works as the meritorious ground of salvation—satisfaction as God’s grace alone, already tasted when favor shines, until face-to-face enjoyment.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on a just cause, night-tried integrity, the word that keeps from violence, refuge under God’s wings, worldly portion versus God’s face, and waking satisfaction with his likeness—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 17:1–15.

Arminian Notable voices

“David appealing to God for his integrity, begs for defense against his enemies, describes their wickedness, prays for deliverance, and rests in hope. The right—Regard my righteous cause. Sentence—Judgment on my behalf; Come—From thy tribunal. Proved—searched or tried by many temptations and afflictions. Night—when minds, freed from business and company, act more vigorously according to their inclinations. Tried—as goldsmiths do metals. Nothing—nothing of unrighteousness. Purposed—resolved upon deliberation. Mouth—so far from practicing against Saul’s life as they charge, that he will not wrong him so much as in a word. Concerning the works of men—observing how wicked they are, he took more care in ordering his own actions; By—by the help of thy blessed word; Paths—of the violent man such as Saul and his courtiers. Steps—wheresoever we go they surround us. Sword—thy instrument of vengeance; let me fall into thy hands, not into the hands of men. World—who set their hearts upon this world and neither have nor desire any other portion. I will—I do not place my portion in earthly treasures, but in beholding God’s face, enjoyed in part in this life but not fully. Awake—when I arise from the dead. Likeness—with the image of God stamped upon my glorified soul.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 17)

Reformed Notable voices

“This psalm contains a mournful complaint against the cruel pride of David’s enemies. He protests that he did not deserve such inhumanity and beseeches God, as his protector, to put forth his power for deliverance. Confiding in his integrity, he interposes God as Judge between himself and his enemies; yet it would not be enough to have a good conscience—he adds earnest prayer. The faithful not only depend upon the goodness of their cause; they commit it to God that he may defend it. He subjects himself to impartial examination, for God searches the secret recesses of the heart and cannot be deceived by appearance; by night, when coverings are taken away, if God examines him there will be found neither disguise nor deceit. By the word of God’s lips he was restrained from rendering evil for evil though provoked. He asks God to make marvelous his mercies as the preserver of those who trust in him, to keep him as the apple of the eye and hide him under the shadow of his wings. Arise, O Jehovah—God is provided with remedies to effect deliverance in a moment. As for me, I shall behold thy face in righteousness: he does not represent works as the meritorious ground of salvation, but lays it down that they who serve God do not lose their labor. Satisfaction is the grace of God alone which can give contentment; those only are partakers of true happiness who seek felicity in the enjoyment of God alone.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 17)
