Passage

Psalms 60

Verses 1–12 · ESV · Voices

To the choirmaster: according to Shushan Eduth. A Miktam of David; for instruction; when he strove with Aram-naharaim and with Aram-zobah, and when Joab on his return struck down twelve thousand of Edom in the Valley of Salt. After national shaking and a sense of rejection, the people plead restore us; God sets a banner for those who fear him, claims Israel’s land and humiliates the nations, and the close confesses that vain is man’s salvation—with God we shall do valiantly.

Psalms 60:1

1O God, you have rejected us, broken our defenses; you have been angry; oh, restore us.

Arminian

Wesley: Rejected us—withdrawn thy favour and help; Broken—defeated us; Restore—bring us into thy favour again. The psalm opens by naming anger and asking restoration, not denial of guilt.

Arminian pastoral: restore us is a living plea after real defeat. Grace that once fought for Israel still invites a people to seek favour again; rejection felt is not treated as final when restore is prayed.

Reformed

Calvin: David complains that God had cast off his people and broken their power, yet turns at once to prayer for restoration. Anger is confessed as God’s; the remedy is God himself restoring.

Reformed accent: shared—broken defenses are read theologically, not merely militarily. Restore us rests on covenant mercy, not human regrouping.

Psalms 60:2

2You have made the land to quake; you have torn it open; repair its breaches, for it totters.

Arminian

Wesley: Quake—by thine anger and by our enemies; Breaches—the ruins made by the enemy. The land totters; prayer asks God to repair what his judgment has torn.

Arminian hearing: breaches can be mended when God is asked. Shared seriousness—national tottering is not ignored; the asker seeks divine repair, not self-made stability alone.

Reformed

Calvin: the shaking of the land pictures the disordered state of the kingdom under divine chastisement. Repair is begged from the same hand that tore.

Reformed pastoral: shared—tottering breaches call for God’s mend. Providence that shakes also alone can heal the rift.

Key contrast Both read the quake as divine chastening and repair as God’s work. Arminians typically stress that the people must keep seeking restoration under the breach; Reformed readers typically stress that the same sovereign hand that tore must mend.

Psalms 60:3

3You have made your people see hard things; you have given us wine to drink that made us stagger.

Arminian

Wesley: Hard things—sore calamities; Wine—the cup of thy wrath. Staggering wine names judgment that confuses and weakens.

Arminian note: hard things are faced honestly in prayer. Grace does not require pretending the cup was sweet; it invites confession of staggering need.

Reformed

Calvin ties hard things and staggering wine to God’s severe dealing—discipline that makes a people reel. The cup is from his hand.

Reformed accent: shared—stagger is theological, not mere bad luck. Wrath’s cup drives the cry that follows for banner and salvation.

Psalms 60:4

4You have set up a banner for those who fear you, that they may flee to it from the bow. Selah

Arminian

Wesley: Banner—a token of thy presence and protection for those who fear thee, that they may flee to it from the bow. Fear of God gathers under a visible hope.

Arminian pastoral: fleeing to the banner is a living act. Those who fear are invited to gather under God’s signal; the bow does not erase the call to take refuge there.

Reformed

Calvin: God has given a banner to them that fear him—a signal of hope under the crossbow of enemies. Fear of God, not military pride, marks those for whom the banner is raised.

Reformed note: shared—the banner is God’s gift. Flight to it is faith under fire, not cowardice.

Psalms 60:5

5That your beloved ones may be delivered, give salvation by your right hand and answer us!

Arminian

Wesley: Beloved—thy people whom thou lovest; deliverance begged by the right hand; answer us joins salvation to prayer heard.

Arminian hope: beloved ones may still ask to be delivered. Right-hand salvation is sought in petition; answer us keeps the dialogue open.

Reformed

Calvin: beloved ones are delivered by God’s right hand alone; answer us stakes the plea on his hearing. Salvation is gift, not wage.

Reformed accent: shared—beloved is God’s naming. Deliverance by the right hand excludes boasting in man’s arm.

Psalms 60:6

6God has spoken in his holiness: "With exultation I will divide up Shechem and portion out the Vale of Succoth.

Arminian

Wesley: Spoken—promised; Holiness—or, sanctuary; Divide—as a conqueror. God’s holy speech claims the land with exultation.

Arminian hearing: the oracle invites trust in what God has spoken. Shared—holiness underwrites the promise; Shechem and Succoth are portioned by his word, not chance.

Reformed

Calvin cites God’s own oracle spoken in his holiness—divine claim on the land with conquering joy. The word, not circumstance, settles the map.

Reformed pastoral: shared—God has spoken stands over defeat. Faith leans on the holy utterance when armies fail.

Psalms 60:7

7Gilead is mine; Manasseh is mine; Ephraim is my helmet; Judah is my scepter.

Arminian

Wesley: Helmet—defence; Sceptre—the seat of my kingdom. God claims tribes as his own armor and rule.

Arminian note: mine repeats ownership that summons loyalty. Grace that claims Ephraim and Judah still calls those tribes to live under the helmet and scepter.

Reformed

Calvin: Gilead, Manasseh, Ephraim, Judah are God’s—helmet and scepter picture defense and royal rule from him. The people are instruments of his dominion.

Reformed accent: shared—identity is “mine.” Kingdom security rests on God’s claim, not tribal strength alone.

Psalms 60:8

8Moab is my washbasin; upon Edom I cast my shoe; over Philistia I shout in triumph."

Arminian

Wesley: Wash-pot—a vessel of no esteem; Cast my shoe—take possession of, as of a mean thing. Enemies are humbled in God’s speech.

Arminian pastoral: triumph shouted is God’s, not fleshly gloating as final ethic. The nations’ low place under the oracle warns pride and comforts the afraid.

Reformed

Calvin: Moab, Edom, and Philistia are despised in the divine oracle—washbasin and cast shoe mark subjection under God’s triumph.

Reformed note: shared—enemy humiliation is God’s verdict. The shout belongs to the Lord who owns the map.

Key contrast Both hear contempt of Moab/Edom/Philistia as God’s holy claim, not private spite. Arminians typically stress that hearers must still respond in fear and trust under that word; Reformed readers typically stress the sovereign decree that already assigns the nations their place.

Psalms 60:9

9Who will bring me to the fortified city? Who will lead me to Edom?

Arminian

Wesley: Fortified city—the chief city of Edom. The question seeks a leader into the stronghold still untaken.

Arminian hearing: who will bring me keeps asking for guidance into hard places. Faith does not skip the fortified city; it seeks the one who leads.

Reformed

Calvin: even after the oracle, David asks who will lead to Edom’s strong city—promise drives petition for actual leading.

Reformed accent: shared—oracle and need meet. God who claimed Edom is asked to bring the army there.

Psalms 60:10

10Have you not rejected us, O God? You do not go forth, O God, with our armies.

Arminian

Wesley: Rejected—seems to have cast us off; God not going forth with armies explains the felt absence in battle.

Arminian pastoral: honest lament of rejection still speaks to God. The complaint is prayer, not atheism—seeking that he would go forth again.

Reformed

Calvin returns to the complaint of rejection and God’s absence from the armies—victory fails when he does not march.

Reformed note: shared—military loss is read as God’s withholding. The cure is his presence, not better tactics alone.

Psalms 60:11

11Oh, grant us help against the foe, for vain is the salvation of man!

Arminian

Wesley: Vain—insufficient and deceitful. Help against the foe is begged because man’s salvation cannot save.

Arminian hope: grant us help is still asked. Shared—human rescue is named vain so that prayer turns wholly to God.

Reformed

Calvin: human help is vanity; therefore grant us help against the foe is addressed to God alone. Man’s salvation cannot underwrite the war.

Reformed accent: shared—vain is confession of creaturely emptiness. Help is grace from above the foe.

Psalms 60:12

12With God we shall do valiantly; it is he who will tread down our foes.

Arminian

Wesley: Valiantly—triumphantly; Tread down—subdue. With God courage becomes possible; he treads the foes.

Arminian pastoral: we shall do valiantly still includes the people’s action under God. Grace enables valor; the treading remains his—cooperation under divine might, not self-salvation.

Reformed

Calvin: only with God shall we do valiantly, for it is he who treads down our foes. Valor is real, but the decisive trampling is God’s.

Reformed accent: shared—with God closes the psalm. Human arm is vain (v. 11); divine treading secures the field.

Key contrast Both end with God-wrought victory over foes. Arminians typically stress that with God the people still do valiantly as living agents; Reformed readers typically stress that he alone treads down, so valor is gift resting on his act.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on rejected defenses, a quaking land, the banner for those who fear God, the holy claim on Shechem and Judah, and valiant help that is God’s alone—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 60.

Arminian Notable voices

“Rejected us—withdrawn thy favour and help. Broken—defeated us. Restore—bring us into thy favour again. Quake—by thine anger and by our enemies. Breaches—the ruins made by the enemy. Hard things—sore calamities. Wine—the cup of thy wrath. Banner—a token of thy presence and protection. Beloved—thy people whom thou lovest. Spoken—promised. Holiness—or, sanctuary. Divide—as a conqueror. Helmet—defence. Sceptre—the seat of my kingdom. Wash-pot—a vessel of no esteem. Cast my shoe—take possession of, as of a mean thing. Fortified city—the chief city of Edom. Rejected—seems to have cast us off. Vain—insufficient and deceitful. Valiantly—triumphantly. Tread down—subdue.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 60:1–12)

Reformed Notable voices

“David complains that God had cast off his people and broken their power, yet he turns at once to prayer for restoration… The shaking of the land pictures the disordered state of the kingdom under divine chastisement… Yet God has given a banner to them that fear him, a signal of hope under the crossbow of enemies… He then cites God’s own oracle claiming Shechem, Succoth, Gilead, Manasseh, Ephraim, and Judah, and despising Moab, Edom, and Philistia… Human help is vanity; only with God shall we do valiantly, for it is he who treads down our foes.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 60)
