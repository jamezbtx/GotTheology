Passage

Psalms 60

Verses 1–12 · WEB · Voices

To the choirmaster: according to Shushan Eduth. A Miktam of David; for instruction; when he strove with Aram-naharaim and with Aram-zobah, and when Joab on his return struck down twelve thousand of Edom in the Valley of Salt. After national shaking and a sense of rejection, the people plead restore us; God sets a banner to those who fear him, claims Israel’s land and humiliates the nations, and the close confesses that vain is man’s salvation—with God we will do valiantly.

Psalms 60:1

1God, you have rejected us. You have broken us down. You have been angry. Restore us, again.

Arminian

Wesley: Rejected us—withdrawn thy favour and help; Broken—defeated us; Restore—bring us into thy favour again. The psalm opens by naming anger and asking restoration, not denial of guilt.

Restore us is a living plea after defeat. Grace that once fought for Israel still invites a people to seek favour again; rejection felt is not treated as final when restore is prayed.

Reformed

Calvin: David complains that God had cast off his people and broken their power, yet turns at once to prayer for restoration. Anger is confessed as God’s; the remedy is God himself restoring.

Broken defenses are read theologically, not merely militarily. Restore us rests on covenant mercy, not human regrouping.

Psalms 60:2

2You have made the land tremble. You have torn it. Mend its fractures, for it quakes.

Arminian

Wesley: Quake—by thine anger and by our enemies; Breaches—the ruins made by the enemy. The land totters; prayer asks God to repair what his judgment has torn.

Breaches can be mended when God is asked. Shared seriousness—national tottering is not ignored; the asker seeks divine repair, not self-made stability alone.

Reformed

Calvin: the shaking of the land pictures the disordered state of the kingdom under divine chastisement. Repair is begged from the same hand that tore.

Tottering breaches call for God’s mend. Providence that shakes also alone can heal the rift.

Key contrast Both read the quake as divine chastening and repair as God’s work. Arminians typically emphasize that the people must keep seeking restoration under the breach; Reformed readers emphasize that the same sovereign hand that tore must mend.

Psalms 60:3

3You have shown your people hard things. You have made us drink the wine that makes us stagger.

Arminian

Wesley: Hard things—sore calamities; Wine—the cup of thy wrath. Staggering wine names judgment that confuses and weakens.

Hard things are faced honestly in prayer. Grace does not require pretending the cup was sweet; it invites confession of staggering need.

Reformed

Calvin ties hard things and staggering wine to God’s severe dealing—discipline that makes a people reel. The cup is from his hand.

Stagger is theological, not mere bad luck. Wrath’s cup drives the cry that follows for banner and salvation.

Psalms 60:4

4You have given a banner to those who fear you, that it may be displayed because of the truth. Selah.

Arminian

Wesley: Banner—a token of thy presence and protection for those who fear thee, that it may be displayed because of the truth. Fear of God gathers under a visible hope.

Fleeing to the banner is a living act. Those who fear are invited to gather under God’s signal; the bow does not erase the call to take refuge there.

Reformed

Calvin: God has given a banner to them that fear him—a signal of hope under the crossbow of enemies. Fear of God, not military pride, marks those for whom the banner is raised.

The banner is God’s gift. Flight to it is faith under fire, not cowardice.

Psalms 60:5

5So that your beloved may be delivered, save with your right hand, and answer us.

Arminian

Wesley: Beloved—thy people whom thou lovest; deliverance begged by the right hand; answer us joins salvation to prayer heard.

Beloved ones may still ask to be delivered. Right-hand salvation is sought in petition; answer us keeps the dialogue open.

Reformed

Calvin: beloved ones are delivered by God’s right hand alone; answer us stakes the plea on his hearing. Salvation is gift, not wage.

Beloved is God’s naming. Deliverance by the right hand excludes boasting in man’s arm.

Psalms 60:6

6God has spoken from his sanctuary: “I will triumph. I will divide Shechem, and measure out the valley of Succoth.

Arminian

Wesley: Spoken—promised; Holiness—or, sanctuary; Divide—as a conqueror. God’s holy speech claims the land with exultation.

The oracle invites trust in what God has spoken. Shared—holiness underwrites the promise; Shechem and Succoth are portioned by his word, not chance.

Reformed

Calvin cites God’s own oracle spoken in his holiness—divine claim on the land with conquering joy. The word, not circumstance, settles the map.

God has spoken stands over defeat. Faith leans on the holy utterance when armies fail.

Psalms 60:7

7Gilead is mine, and Manasseh is mine. Ephraim also is the defense of my head. Judah is my scepter.

Arminian

Wesley: Helmet—defence; Sceptre—the seat of my kingdom. God claims tribes as his own armor and rule.

Mine repeats ownership that summons loyalty. Grace that claims Ephraim and Judah still calls those tribes to live under the helmet and scepter.

Reformed

Calvin: Gilead, Manasseh, Ephraim, Judah are God’s—helmet and scepter picture defense and royal rule from him. The people are instruments of his dominion.

Identity is “mine.” Kingdom security rests on God’s claim, not tribal strength alone.

Psalms 60:8

8Moab is my wash basin. I will throw my sandal on Edom. I shout in triumph over Philistia.”

Arminian

Wesley: Wash-pot—a vessel of no esteem; Cast my shoe—take possession of, as of a mean thing. Enemies are humbled in God’s speech.

Triumph shouted is God’s, not fleshly gloating as final ethic. The nations’ low place under the oracle warns pride and comforts the afraid.

Reformed

Calvin: Moab, Edom, and Philistia are despised in the divine oracle—washbasin and cast shoe mark subjection under God’s triumph.

Enemy humiliation is God’s verdict. The shout belongs to the Lord who owns the map.

Key contrast Both hear contempt of Moab/Edom/Philistia as God’s holy claim, not private spite. Arminians typically stress that hearers must still respond in fear and trust under that word; Calvinists typically stress the sovereign decree that already assigns the nations their place.

Psalms 60:9

9Who will bring me into the strong city? Who has led me to Edom?

Arminian

Wesley: Fortified city—the chief city of Edom. The question seeks a leader into the stronghold still untaken.

Who will bring me keeps asking for guidance into hard places. Faith does not skip the fortified city; it seeks the one who leads.

Reformed

Calvin: even after the oracle, David asks who will lead to Edom’s strong city—promise drives petition for actual leading.

God’s oracle and Israel’s need meet here. God who claimed Edom is asked to bring the army there.

Psalms 60:10

10Haven’t you, God, rejected us? You don’t go out with our armies, God.

Arminian

Wesley: Rejected—seems to have cast us off; God not going forth with armies explains the felt absence in battle.

Honest lament of rejection still speaks to God. The complaint is prayer, not atheism—seeking that he would go forth again.

Reformed

Calvin returns to the complaint of rejection and God’s absence from the armies—victory fails when he does not march.

Military loss is read as God’s withholding. The cure is his presence, not better tactics alone.

Psalms 60:11

11Give us help against the adversary, for the help of man is vain.

Arminian

Wesley: Vain—insufficient and deceitful. Help against the foe is begged because man’s salvation cannot save.

Grant us help is still asked. Shared—human rescue is named vain so that prayer turns wholly to God.

Reformed

Calvin: human help is vanity; therefore Give us help against the adversary is addressed to God alone. Man’s salvation cannot underwrite the war.

Vain is confession of creaturely emptiness. Help is grace from above the foe.

Psalms 60:12

12Through God we will do valiantly, for it is he who will tread down our adversaries.

Arminian

Wesley: Valiantly—triumphantly; Tread down—subdue. With God courage becomes possible; he treads the foes.

We shall do valiantly still includes the people’s action under God. Grace enables valor; the treading remains his—cooperation under divine might, not self-salvation.

Reformed

Calvin: only with God shall we do valiantly, for it is he who treads down our foes. Valor is true, but the decisive trampling is God’s.

With God closes the psalm. Human arm is vain (v. 11); divine treading secures the field.

Key contrast Both end with God-wrought victory over foes. Arminians emphasize that with God the people still do valiantly as living agents; Reformed readers emphasize that he alone treads down, so valor is gift resting on his act.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

What earlier interpreters on each side wrote about Psalm 60.

Arminian Notable voices

“Rejected us—withdrawn thy favour and help. Broken—defeated us. Restore—bring us into thy favour again. Quake—by thine anger and by our enemies. Breaches—the ruins made by the enemy. Hard things—sore calamities. Wine—the cup of thy wrath. Banner—a token of thy presence and protection. Beloved—thy people whom thou lovest. Spoken—promised. Holiness—or, sanctuary. Divide—as a conqueror. Helmet—defence. Sceptre—the seat of my kingdom. Wash-pot—a vessel of no esteem. Cast my shoe—take possession of, as of a mean thing. Fortified city—the chief city of Edom. Rejected—seems to have cast us off. Vain—insufficient and deceitful. Valiantly—triumphantly. Tread down—subdue.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Ps 60:1–12)

Reformed Notable voices

“David complains that God had cast off his people and broken their power, yet he turns at once to prayer for restoration… The shaking of the land pictures the disordered state of the kingdom under divine chastisement… Yet God has given a banner to them that fear him, a signal of hope under the crossbow of enemies… He then cites God’s own oracle claiming Shechem, Succoth, Gilead, Manasseh, Ephraim, and Judah, and despising Moab, Edom, and Philistia… Human help is vanity; only with God shall we do valiantly, for it is he who treads down our foes.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms (on Ps 60)
