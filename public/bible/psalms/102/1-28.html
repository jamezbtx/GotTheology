Psalms 102

Verses 1–28 · WEB · Voices

A prayer of one afflicted, when he is faint and pours out his complaint before the LORD: days like smoke, bones like a furnace, loneliness and taunts under God's indignation—then the turn to the LORD enthroned forever, the appointed time to pity Zion, nations fearing his glory, a record for A people which will be created, and the unchanging Creator whose years have no end, so that the children of his servants dwell secure. Verses 25–27 are applied to the Son in Hebrews 1.

Psalms 102:1

1Hear my prayer, LORD! Let my cry come to you.

Arminian

The title names one afflicted who pours out complaint before the LORD. Wesley: a prayer for true Israelites on behalf of the church, near the end of captivity.

The psalmist moves from distress to petition. The cry is to be uttered; God invites the overwhelmed to speak. Grace stirs prayer; the afflicted must still cry.

Reformed

Calvin: this earnestness shows the words were not for the careless. The captive Jews testify severe distress and an ardent desire for alleviation. The Holy Spirit stirs prayer for the common welfare of the Church; scarcely one in a hundred is affected as he ought by the Church's calamities.

The cry is Spirit-taught liturgy for a people, not a light mouth-habit. Heart and tongue help each other when grief shuts us up.

Psalms 102:2

2Don’t hide your face from me in the day of my distress. Turn your ear to me. Answer me quickly in the day when I call.

Arminian

Do not hide your face; incline your ear; answer speedily. The day of distress is named twice—face and ear sought with urgency.

Speed is the plea of the faint, not a denial of God's wisdom. Wesleyan Arminians pray as those who may truly be heard; the face may feel hidden while the promise still stands.

Reformed

Calvin: after nearly seventy years it might seem God had forever turned away his favor; yet they are commanded in extreme affliction to have recourse to prayer. God permits us to lay open our infirmities without reserve and patiently bears with our foolishness—inviting the weak to approach familiarly.

The seeming hidden face does not nullify the covenant command to call. Tenderness is providence, not permission for irreverence.

Psalms 102:3

3For my days consume away like smoke. My bones are burned as a torch.

Arminian

Wesley: An hearth—an hearth is heated or burnt by the coals which are laid upon it. Days like smoke; bones like a furnace—body and time both wasting.

Complaint is honest physiology under grief. The verse names felt wasting before God and asks for mercy.

Reformed

Calvin treats these verses as the Church's and the sufferer's shared language of languishing. Smoke and furnace picture a life nearly spent under captivity's weight.

The complaint is recorded so the faint may pray it. Frailty is not hidden from the eternal King who will answer.

Psalms 102:4

4My heart is blighted like grass, and withered, for I forget to eat my bread.

Arminian

Heart struck down like grass; bread forgotten. Grief reaches appetite and cheer. Wesley's later note on ashes as bread (v. 9) already begins in this withering.

Depression's symptoms are not unbelief by default. Pour them out; then hold the turn of v. 12 when it comes.

Reformed

The heart withers like grass; even necessary bread is forgotten. Calvin's frame: such language belongs to those distressed for the desolate Church, not to the light-hearted.

God receives this honesty. The grass image will return when steadfast love is set against human frailty.

Psalms 102:5

5By reason of the voice of my groaning, my bones stick to my skin.

Arminian

Wesley: Skin—my flesh being quite consumed. Loud groaning has wasted him to bone and clinging flesh.

The body keeps the score of unanswered days. The psalm does not scold the groan; it carries it to God.

Reformed

Groaning reduces him to skin and bone. Calvin: when shut up by grief, the gate is not shut against prayer—then is a proper season to pour out the heart before God.

Extremity is invitation, not exile from the throne of grace.

Psalms 102:6

6I am like a pelican of the wilderness. I have become as an owl of the waste places.

Arminian

Wesley: A pelican—is a solitary and mournful bird. Wilderness and waste places picture isolation.

Loneliness is named without romance. The afflicted may feel cast to the wastes while still belonging to Zion's hope.

Reformed

Solitary birds of desert and ruin: the sufferer is cut off from ordinary company. Calvin's title stress: this form is for those shut up and pouring out meditation before Jehovah.

Isolation does not erase election's people; it drives them to the God who builds Zion.

Psalms 102:7

7I watch, and have become like a sparrow that is alone on the housetop.

Arminian

Sleepless; a sparrow that is alone on the housetop. Wakefulness and exposure join the owl's solitude.

Night watches become prayer watches. The housetop sparrow is seen and unsheltered—still heard by the LORD of v. 1.

Reformed

Lying awake in lonely exposure. Wesley reads it the same way: the image is mournful solitude under the LORD's ear.

The Church in exile may feel housetop-naked; the memorial of God's name (v. 12) will answer.

Psalms 102:8

8My enemies reproach me all day. Those who are mad at me use my name as a curse.

Arminian

All-day taunts; the name used for a curse. Social shame compounds bodily wasting.

Enemies are agents. Their mockery does not prove God absent; it proves the need of the cry.

Reformed

Enemies taunt and make the name a curse. Calvin's setting: Babylon's weight on a people who must still pray for Zion.

Derision is inside providence that will yet favor Zion at the appointed time—not chance cruelty alone.

Psalms 102:9

9For I have eaten ashes like bread, and mixed my drink with tears,

Arminian

Wesley: Bread—dust and ashes are as familiar to me as the eating of my bread; I cover my head with them; I sit, yea, lie down in them, as mourners often did. Tears mix with drink.

Mourning rites become daily fare. The verse legitimizes grief's diet before God.

Reformed

The Church's sorrow is liturgical as well as personal.

Such mourning looks toward pity on Zion's stones and dust (v. 14), not toward despair as a final word.

Psalms 102:10

10because of your indignation and your wrath; for you have taken me up and thrown me away.

Arminian

Wesley: Lifted me—as a man lifts up a thing as high as he can, that he may cast it to the ground with greater force. Indignation and anger are confessed as God's.

The Most High's anger is true; so is the prayer that seeks mercy. Human agency in sin and the nations' agency in oppression stand under that rule without erasing second causes.

Reformed

The cause is God's indignation: taken up and thrown down. Calvin elsewhere: captivity is judgment, yet the covenant people are taught to plead mercy from God's free goodness, not from their deserts.

Effectual discipline, not bare permission. The same God who threw down will arise and pity Zion.

Key contrast Both confess that the throw-down is the LORD's indignation, not mere bad luck. Arminians typically hold human and imperial agency under that rule—governed without making every choice a puppet-string. Reformed commentators hear effectual judgment inside the covenant story: God actually took up and threw down, and the same decree holds the appointed mercy of the verses that follow.

Psalms 102:11

11My days are like a long shadow. I have withered like grass.

Arminian

Evening shadow; grass withering—time nearly gone. The complaint section (vv. 1–11) closes in frailty.

Feel the shortness honestly; then turn. The next verse's "But you" is the hinge of hope, not a denial of the shadow.

Reformed

Days as evening shadow; grass again. Calvin will set this frailty against God's eternity so the Church may not despond.

Human withering is the dark backdrop for "thou, O LORD, art enthroned forever."

Psalms 102:12

12But you, LORD, will remain forever; your renown endures to all generations.

Arminian

Wesley: Remembrance—thy name, Jehovah, which is called by this very word, God's remembrance, or memorial, and that unto all generations, Exodus 3:15. The turn: forever enthroned over withering days.

God's everlasting memorial grounds prayer. The afflicted cling to who God is when their own days smoke away.

Reformed

Calvin: calling to remembrance the promises—especially "I will dwell in the midst of you"—he represents the godly, though languishing, as partakers of the celestial glory in which God dwells. The memorial is the gracious covenant that begets confidence of mutual relationship.

Eternity helps because God has bound himself by word to his people—not as abstract metaphysics alone.

Psalms 102:13

13You will arise and have mercy on Zion, for it is time to have pity on her. Yes, the set time has come.

Arminian

Wesley: The set time—the end of those seventy years which thou hast fixed. Pity and favor arrive on an appointed clock God named.

Prophecy invites prayer, not passivity. The set time is fixed; the people still plead. Mercy is sincerely sought as the captivity nears its end.

Reformed

Calvin: God is eternal, therefore he will have compassion upon Zion. He sets forth divine mercy as cause—good deserts of men entirely out of the question; free goodness alone. The appointed time refers to Jeremiah's seventy years. Although the time was fixed, the prophet does not cease from prayer; promises do not obscure grace.

The moed is God's good pleasure and promise; prayer uses the promise as argument, tracing safety to mercy alone.

Key contrast Both confess an appointed time to pity Zion fixed by God and pleaded in prayer. Arminians emphasize that the set promise sincerely invites the people's cry and hope as captivity ends. Calvinists emphasize free goodness as sole cause—deserts out of the question—while the fixed word stirs confident petition without making mercy hang on human worth.

Psalms 102:14

14For your servants take pleasure in her stones, and have pity on her dust.

Arminian

Servants love Zion's stones and pity her dust. Attachment to ruins is covenant affection, not archaeology.

Love the Church in her brokenness. Prefer her dust to pagan splendor; keep praying for favor.

Reformed

Calvin: not to be restricted to Cyrus and Darius. God's people alone are his servants who aim to obey his will. The deformed ruins are still the stones of Zion; the faithful's attachment continued when only hideous desolation was seen. The sadder the desolation, the less ought affections to be alienated.

Love of the rubble is love of God's consecrated place and promised restoration.

Psalms 102:15

15So the nations will fear the LORD’s name, all the kings of the earth your glory.

Arminian

Nations and kings will fear the name and glory. Restoration spills outward into the earth's reverence.

The true God's glory is for the nations' fear and worship, matching Wesley's later note on Gentiles gathering (v. 22).

Reformed

Calvin's arc: deliverance leads to kings and nations in subjection to God. The rebuilding of Zion is not a private ethnic comfort only.

God's glory publicly vindicated among the nations when he builds Zion.

Psalms 102:16

16For the LORD has built up Zion. He has appeared in his glory.

Arminian

The LORD builds; he appears in glory. Human builders serve; the builder is the LORD.

Glory's appearing answers the hidden face of v. 2. Pray for building; credit God when stones rise.

Reformed

The LORD himself builds Zion and appears in his glory. Calvin: arising is God making his majesty apparent in the external act while remaining immutable.

Zion's rise is God's work displayed, the mercy of which vv. 13–14 pleaded.

Psalms 102:17

17He has responded to the prayer of the destitute, and has not despised their prayer.

Arminian

He regards and does not despise the destitute's prayer. The cry of vv. 1–2 is answered in principle here.

Destitution is not a disqualification. Bring the faint prayer; it is not despised.

Reformed

God regards the prayer of the destitute. Calvin's opening: the Spirit dictates this form so the shut-up may still pour out meditation before Jehovah.

The destitute are heard because of covenant mercy, not because poverty purchases favor.

Psalms 102:18

18This will be written for the generation to come. A people which will be created will praise the LORD,

Arminian

Wesley: This—this wonderful deliverance shall be carefully recorded by thy people. A people which will be created will praise.

Praise spans generations. Record mercy so the unborn church may sing; evangelism and memory belong together.

Reformed

Let it be written for a future generation and a people yet created. Calvin stresses encouraging greater care for the Church's welfare and confidence from God's word.

Providence writes history for praise; the future people are God's to create and keep.

Psalms 102:19

19for he has looked down from the height of his sanctuary. From heaven, the LORD saw the earth,

Arminian

Wesley: Looked—from heaven. Holy height to earth: transcendence that still sees.

Heaven's look is attentive mercy. The God enthroned forever (v. 12) is not distant in care.

Reformed

From his holy height the LORD saw the earth. The look precedes loosing prisoners (v. 20).

Divine survey is the prelude to redemption acts, not bare observation.

Psalms 102:20

20to hear the groans of the prisoner, to free those who are condemned to death,

Arminian

Wesley: To loose—to release his poor captives out of Babylon, and from the chains of sin and eternal destruction. Double freedom: exile and deeper bondage.

Groans heard; the doomed set free. The release is sincerely for captives who cry; both temporal and eternal loosing are in view.

Reformed

To hear prisoners' groans and loose those appointed to death. Calvin's captivity frame: God restores temple and city and frees his people according to promise.

The loosing is God's effectual pity on Zion's prisoners, the fruit of the appointed time.

Psalms 102:21

21that men may declare the LORD’s name in Zion, and his praise in Jerusalem,

Arminian

Wesley: To declare—that they might publish the name and praises of God in his church. Freedom's end is praise in Zion.

Salvation aims at declaration. Freed mouths fill Jerusalem with the name.

Reformed

Declaration of the name and praise in Zion and Jerusalem. Restoration serves worship.

The Church's public praise is why prisoners are loosed—God's glory in the midst of his people.

Psalms 102:22

22when the peoples are gathered together, the kingdoms, to serve the LORD.

Arminian

Wesley: When—when the Gentiles shall gather themselves to the Jews, and join with them in the worship of the true God. Peoples and kingdoms assemble.

One worship for Jew and Gentile. The gathering is hope for the nations' true praise.

Reformed

Peoples and kingdoms gather to worship. Calvin: the poem beseeches God to bring kings and nations in subjection to himself.

Zion's rebuilding has a catholic horizon—the nations' worship under the one LORD.

Psalms 102:23

23He weakened my strength along the course. He shortened my days.

Arminian

Wesley: He—God. The way—in the midst of the course of our lives. Some think the psalmist speaks of the whole commonwealth as of one man. Strength broken midcourse; days shortened.

Personal and corporate frailty intertwine. The plea that follows seeks life until promises ripen—especially the Messiah.

Reformed

Calvin: the manifestation of Christ was the goal of the race; they complain of being afflicted in midcourse. Days shortened as they looked to the fullness of time at Christ's revealing. They do not find fault as if God deceived; they strengthen hope of a favorable issue.

Midcourse is measured toward the Messiah's day; captivity felt like a violent shortening of that life.

Psalms 102:24

24I said, “My God, don’t take me away in the middle of my days. Your years are throughout all generations.

Arminian

Wesley: Do not wholly destroy thy people Israel. In the midst—before they come to a full possession of thy promises and especially of that fundamental promise of the Messiah. Thy years—though we die, yet thou art the everlasting God.

On a christology both sides share, the fundamental promise of the Messiah anchors the plea. Keep a people until the Christ is possessed in fullness of promise.

Reformed

Calvin: cut me not off in the middle of my days—the Church's perfect age arrives at Christ's coming. They plead the covenant that God would maintain them until he renewed the world under the Anointed. His years from generation to generation ground confidence because he is eternal in his word as well as essence.

The midst of days is before the Messianic fullness; God's everlasting years secure the plea.

Psalms 102:25

25Of old, you laid the foundation of the earth. The heavens are the work of your hands.

Arminian

Of old: earth founded; heavens the work of your hands. Creator address in the afflicted's mouth.

Shared christology: Hebrews 1:10–12 applies this to the Son. Wesley's Messiah-promise (v. 24) meets the apostolic reading—both camps confess the Creator spoken of here as the eternal Christ.

Reformed

Calvin: compared with God the whole world is a form that vanishes; yet the Church is upheld by his word. Hebrews 1 applies these verses to Christ—Jehovah, Creator, eternal and immutable—proof of his essential divinity.

Shared with Arminians: apostolic christology stands. The hands that founded earth are the Son's; camps agree here against Arian subtraction.

Psalms 102:26

26They will perish, but you will endure. Yes, all of them will wear out like a garment. You will change them like a cloak, and they will be changed.

Arminian

Wesley: Perish—as to their present nature and use. Heavens and earth wear out; God remains. Change like a robe.

Shared christology: Hebrews cites the wearing out and remaining of the Son. Creation's mortality underlines the Savior's permanence both camps preach.

Reformed

Calvin: heavens shall perish—subject to corruption through the fall, and so renewed as to warrant saying they perish. Seek stability nowhere but in God. The apostolic use in Hebrews identifies this remaining One as Christ.

Creation changes; the Son does not. The Church's hope is not the garment of the cosmos but the One who changes it.

Psalms 102:27

27But you are the same. Your years will have no end.

Arminian

You are the same; years have no end. Immutability comforts the midcourse cry.

Shared christology: "thou art the same" is read of Christ in Hebrews 1 and echoes in Hebrews 13:8. Both camps refuse a mutable redeemer.

Reformed

Calvin: But thou art He—the Eternal, necessarily eternal, unchangeable and imperishable. Years shall not fail. Applied to Christ: eternity and immutability ascribed to him who is called Jehovah.

Shared confession: the sameness of God is the sameness of the Son. Affliction's answer is an unchanging Christ.

Psalms 102:28

28The children of your servants will continue. Their offspring will be established before you.”

Arminian

Wesley: Continue—though the heavens and earth perish, yet we rest assured that our children, and their children after them, shall enjoy an happy restitution to, and settlement in their own land. Security of offspring after cosmic wearing.

Promise to servants' children invites faith and obedience across generations. Restitution is trusted; children must still walk as servants' seed, not presume on blood alone.

Reformed

Calvin: he asks the Church's preservation not as a mere part of the human race but because God raised it above the world's revolutions. Dwell means an abiding inheritance. Seed means not all descendants without exception—many degenerate—but those who do not turn aside from their parents' faith. The covenant extends to future ages; cast the anchor of faith in heaven.

Establishment before God's face is holy union and electing faithfulness, not bare biology.

Key contrast Both confess that servants' children will dwell when heavens wear out, and both read vv. 25–27 of Christ with Hebrews. Arminians catch a sincere multigenerational promise of restitution that children must receive and keep as living servants. Reformed Christians hear God establishing the Church's seed by covenant—true offspring being those who do not turn from the faith—secured by the immutable Christ rather than by an unbroken human hold alone.

Voices from both camps

An afflicted cry, the appointed time to pity Zion, and the unchanging LORD whose years do not fail: older Arminian and Reformed writers, each in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“This psalm contains a prayer for the use of all true Israelites, in the name and behalf of the church of Israel. It seems to have been composed in the time of their captivity, and near the end of it. An hearth—heated or burnt by the coals. Skin—my flesh being quite consumed. A pelican—a solitary and mournful bird. Bread—dust and ashes are as familiar as eating bread. Lifted me—as a man lifts a thing high that he may cast it down with greater force. Remembrance—thy name, Jehovah, memorial unto all generations. The set time—the end of those seventy years which thou hast fixed. This wonderful deliverance shall be carefully recorded. Looked—from heaven. To loose—to release his poor captives out of Babylon, and from the chains of sin and eternal destruction. When the Gentiles shall gather themselves to the Jews and join in the worship of the true God. Do not wholly destroy thy people Israel before they come to full possession of thy promises, especially of that fundamental promise of the Messiah. Though the heavens and earth perish, yet we rest assured that our children shall enjoy a happy restitution.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 102)

Reformed Notable voices

“This prayer seems to have been dictated to the faithful when they were languishing in captivity in Babylon. They bewail their afflictions, plead for the restoration of the holy city, and draw consolation from the eternity of God. God is eternal, and therefore he will have compassion upon Zion—the time appointed according to his good pleasure is come. The good deserts of men are entirely out of the question; he traces the cause solely to the free goodness of God. Of old thou laidst the foundation of the earth; they shall perish, but thou shalt endure; but thou art the same, and thy years shall not fail. The Apostle applies these words to Christ in Hebrews 1, in proof of his deity. The children of thy servants shall dwell, and their seed shall be established before thee—not because the Church is a mere part of the human race, but because God has raised it above the revolutions of the world by his word.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 102)
