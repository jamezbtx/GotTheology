Passage

Psalms 124

Verses 1–8 · ESV · Voices

A Song of Ascents of David: corporate thanksgiving after narrow escape. If the LORD had not been on Israel's side when enemies rose, flood and snare would have finished them. Blessed be the LORD—help is in His name, Maker of heaven and earth. Pilgrim praise names deliverance as God's alone.

Psalms 124:1

1If it had not been the LORD who was on our side— let Israel now say—

Arminian

If the LORD had not been on our side—let Israel say it aloud. Corporate memory opens with a conditional that drives thanksgiving, not idle speculation about what might have been.

Classical Arminians hear a people invited to confess real deliverance together. God truly sided with them in trouble; they freely join the refrain that keeps gratitude awake on every ascent.

Reformed

If the LORD had not been on our side—Israel must say so. The counterfactual exposes how completely safety hung on God's presence with His covenant people, not on their skill.

Reformed accent: being "on our side" is covenant initiative. The church's survival past rising foes is not a near-miss of luck but the LORD's prior commitment to those He claims as His own.

Key contrast Both urge Israel to confess that only the LORD's being "on our side" explains survival. Arminians typically stress a real, welcomed siding with those who trust Him; Reformed readers typically stress God's covenant commitment that precedes and explains the people's escape.

Psalms 124:2

2if it had not been the LORD who was on our side when people rose up against us,

Arminian

Again: if the LORD had not been on our side when people rose against us. Hostility is named plainly; God's siding is confessed as the decisive difference between ruin and pilgrimage.

Arminian pastoral: when opposition rises, do not credit your own grit first. Join Israel's confession—His help met real enemies, and warm thanksgiving should meet that help on the road.

Reformed

The LORD on our side when people rose up. Human uprising meets divine allegiance; without that allegiance, the story of the people ends differently and darker.

Reformed comfort: people's rising does not surprise the covenant Lord. His being for His own is the reason the ascent continues past the threat instead of ending under it.

Psalms 124:3

3then they would have swallowed us up alive, when their anger was kindled against us;

Arminian

Swallowed alive—anger kindled against us. The "then" paints how total the loss would have been without the LORD standing between Israel and kindled rage.

Classical Arminians use this honesty pastorally: name how close ruin felt, then praise. Gratitude grows when the alternative is faced without minimizing either the danger or the mercy that intervened.

Reformed

Swallowed alive when anger kindled—the counterfactual is mercilessly clear. Apart from God, Israel's story is consumption by foes, not continued pilgrimage toward Zion.

Reformed note: the church learns its frailty by imagining life without God's restraint of wrathful enemies. Deliverance magnifies Him, not our near-escape skills or nerve.

Psalms 124:4

4then the flood would have swept us away, the torrent would have gone over us;

Arminian

Flood and torrent would have swept and gone over us. Chaos imagery says the threat was overwhelming, not merely inconvenient or briefly alarming on the way.

Arminian reading: bring flood-memories to God in honest praise. He rescued; you did not swim the torrent alone. Let that truth reshape how you trust Him when the next surge rises.

Reformed

Flood swept away; torrent gone over—waters that drown the unprotected. Without the LORD, the people are debris in a current they cannot manage.

Reformed pastoral: providence includes holding back floods that would erase His people. The Ascents song refuses to treat survival as a self-navigated near miss.

Psalms 124:5

5then over us would have gone the raging waters.

Arminian

Raging waters over us—the third "then" finishes the dread picture. Total submersion was the alternative to God's help, and the psalm will not soften that fact.

Classical Arminians urge lingering here long enough to feel the "almost," then move to blessing. Fear faced honestly fuels freer, warmer thanksgiving on the ascent.

Reformed

Raging waters would have gone over us. The stack of "thens" leaves no residual credit for Israel's strength, strategy, or luck under the flood.

Reformed accent: the church's existence past such waters is grace upon grace. God alone stands between His people and the flood that would have closed over them forever.

Psalms 124:6

6Blessed be the LORD, who has not given us as prey to their teeth!

Arminian

Blessed be the LORD—He has not given us as prey to their teeth. Blessing answers the rescue with speech that honors Him rather than our escape artists.

Arminian pastoral: do not stop at relief; speak blessing aloud. Prey spared should become people who praise, freely ascribing the escape to the LORD's kindness and power.

Reformed

Blessed be the LORD who refused to give us as prey. Teeth were ready; God withheld the gift of His people to the predators who hungered for them.

Reformed comfort: not being given as prey is God's decision before it is our relief. Blessing is the fitting response to a will that kept His own from the jaws.

Psalms 124:7

7We have escaped like a bird from the snare of the fowlers; the snare is broken, and we have escaped!

Arminian

Escaped like a bird; the snare is broken—and we have escaped! Freedom is celebrated twice so joy will not stay muted after such a trap.

Classical Arminians hear invitation to rejoice in real liberation. The broken snare calls for glad testimony on the road: God freed us; we walk on unbound and grateful.

Reformed

Bird from the fowlers' snare—snare broken, escape secured. Capture was certain until God snapped the trap that human skill could not open.

Reformed note: escape is His breaking, not our clever twisting free. The doubled "escaped" underlines a deliverance wrought for the trapped, not achieved by them.

Psalms 124:8

8Our help is in the name of the LORD, who made heaven and earth.

Arminian

Help is in the name of the LORD—Maker of heaven and earth. Past escape becomes present confession for every remaining mile of the ascent.

Arminian pastoral: keep seeking help where creation's Lord invites trust. The name that rescued still receives pilgrim dependence with open welcome and real aid.

Reformed

Our help is in the LORD's name, Maker of heaven and earth. Creator power and covenant name together ground the people's ongoing safety beyond one escape.

Reformed accent: help located in His name is not a technique but a confession of who He is. The Maker remains the only adequate help for His own forever.

Key contrast Both locate help in the LORD who made heaven and earth. Arminians typically stress continued, willing reliance on that name; Reformed readers typically stress that help resides in God's name as the sure source for His people, not in creaturely alternatives.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the LORD on Israel's side, escape from flood and snare, and help in the Maker's name—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 124:1–8.

Arminian Notable voices

“If it had not been the Lord—If God had not, in a very especial manner, supported and defended us, we had all been swallowed up alive, and destroyed by a sudden destruction, so that not one would have been left. Our soul is escaped as a bird out of the snare—This is a fine image; and at once shows the weakness of the Jews, and the cunning of their adversaries. Humanly speaking there was no prospect of their escape: but the Lord was on their side. God thus broke the snare, and the bird escaped; while the poacher was caught in his own trap. Our help is in the name of the Lord—In open assaults, and in insidious attacks, we have no helper but God; and from him our deliverance must come.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 124)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Church having been providentially delivered from extreme peril, David exhorts true believers to thanksgiving, and teaches them by this memorable example, that their safety depends solely upon the grace and power of God. He affirms that the deliverance and the salvation of the people proceeded from nothing else than God's succor. He only truly attributes his deliverance to God, who acknowledges himself to have been lost before he was delivered. Our help is in the name of Jehovah: he not only gives thanks for one benefit, but affirms that the Church cannot continue safe except in so far as she is protected by the hand of God. His object is to animate the children of God with the assured hope that their life is in perfect safety under the divine guardianship. The name of God is nothing else than God himself; yet it implies that as he has disclosed to us his grace by his word, we have ready access to him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 124)
