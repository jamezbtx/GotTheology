Passage

Psalms 18

Verses 1–50 · ESV · Voices

Psalm 18 is David’s long song of deliverance—also in 2 Samuel 22—sung when the LORD had saved him from all his enemies and from Saul. He opens with whole-souled love to the LORD his strength, heaps names of refuge, and recalls cords of death and torrents of destruction that drove him to cry until God heard from his temple. Then a theophany: earth quaking, heavens bowed, cherub and wind, darkness and hail, thunder, arrows, and foundations laid bare, until God draws him from many waters, rescues him from foes too mighty, and brings him into a broad place because he delighted in him. David pleads righteousness and clean hands in the controversy, kept ways and statutes, blamelessness kept from guilt; God shows himself merciful to the merciful and opposes the crooked, saves the humble, lights the lamp, and proves a perfect way and true word as shield for all who take refuge. Equipped and trained, shielded by salvation and gentleness, he pursues and consumes enemies who cry in vain to the LORD, is made head of nations, and ends blessing the living rock, praising among the nations, and confessing great salvation and forever steadfast love to the anointed—to David and his offspring, and above all to the Messiah.

Psalms 18:1

1I love you, O LORD, my strength.

Arminian

Wesley: Love—Most affectionately, and with my whole soul; as the Hebrew word signifies. David opens the song of deliverance with whole-souled love to the LORD his strength.

Arminian hearing: shared—love God with the whole heart as the first response to saving strength. Grace kindles affection; the hearer must still love the LORD who has been his strength, not treat deliverance as a trophy without devotion.

Reformed

Calvin: love to God is the principal part of true godliness; there is no better way of serving God than to love him. He requires to possess all the affections of the heart. There is no sacrifice he values more than when we are bound to him by free and spontaneous love. David makes as it were a solemn vow: Lord, as thou art my strength, I will continue united and devoted to thee by unfeigned love.

Reformed accent: shared—affectionate love answered to free goodness. Deliverance must not breed ingratitude; the heart stays bound to the Strength who saved.

Psalms 18:2

2The LORD is my rock and my fortress and my deliverer, my God, my rock, in whom I take refuge, my shield, and the horn of my salvation, my stronghold.

Arminian

Wesley: Rock—To which I flee for refuge, as the Israelites did to their rocks. Horn—It is a metaphor from those beasts whose strength lies in their horns. Fortress, deliverer, shield, and stronghold heap names for the God who shelters.

Arminian pastoral: shared—take refuge in this many-named God. Grace supplies the rock; the hearer must still flee to him rather than invent another fortress.

Reformed

Calvin: David heaps titles to honor God because it is hard to keep mind and heart stayed in him. Those God intends to succor are surrounded by impregnable ramparts; though a thousand deaths appear, they ought not to fear. The design is to fortify faith so that whatever afflictions befall, we have recourse to God. I will trust in him.

Reformed accent: shared—God alone is complete armor. Multiple titles train the heart not to seek support elsewhere at the first temptation.

Psalms 18:3

3I call upon the LORD, who is worthy to be praised, and I am saved from my enemies.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: calling upon the LORD who is worthy to be praised issues in being saved from enemies. Praise and petition belong together in the day of war.

Arminian hearing: shared—call on the praiseworthy LORD and expect rescue. Grace enables the cry; the hearer must still actually call rather than praise in theory while enemies go unanswered in prayer.

Reformed

Calvin: calling upon God here signifies recourse to him for protection and asking deliverance by prayer. Having said he trusted, David subjoins evidence of trust: every one who confides will earnestly beseech aid in need. He will be saved because he will have recourse to God. Prayers mingled with praises; remembrance of past grace animates free petition.

Reformed accent: shared—trust proves itself in calling. Praise-woven prayer is faith’s evidence, not bare self-confidence.

Psalms 18:4

4The cords of death encompassed me; the torrents of destruction assailed me;

Arminian

Wesley: Death—Dangerous and deadly troubles. Floods—Their multitude, and strength, and violent assaults, breaking in upon me like a flood. Cords and torrents name real extremity, not mild inconvenience.

Arminian pastoral: shared—name the danger honestly before God. Grace meets flood-level trouble; the hearer must still cry from within the cords rather than deny the waters.

Reformed

Calvin: David recounts illustrious proofs that God’s hand is strong enough to repel dangers and calamities. Death is personified as a conqueror binding the vanquished. The torrents of destruction made him afraid—yet the narrative moves toward the God who hears.

Reformed accent: shared—extremity is real, and so is the Helper. Faith does not minimize the flood; it recalls that God has already answered such distress.

Psalms 18:5

5the cords of Sheol entangled me; the snares of death confronted me.

Arminian

Wesley: Sorrows—Which brought me to the brink of the grave. Death—Had almost taken hold of me, before I was aware. Sheol’s cords and death’s snares press the same nearness to the end.

Arminian hearing: shared—own how near the snare came. Grace delivers from brink-of-grave sorrow; the rescued still remember how close entanglement felt.

Reformed

Calvin: cords of the grave and snares of death prevented him—nearly seized him. The psalm piles images so that later deliverance will look as great as the danger was.

Reformed accent: shared—death’s snares are not rhetorical padding. Providence lets the faithful feel the brink that God’s later rescue will answer.

Psalms 18:6

6In my distress I called upon the LORD; to my God I cried for help. From his temple he heard my voice, and my cry to him reached his ears.

Arminian

Wesley: Temple—Out of his heavenly habitation. In distress he called; from the temple God heard, and the cry reached his ears.

Arminian pastoral: shared—distress prayer reaches heaven’s temple. Grace opens the ear of God; the hearer must still cry for help rather than assume silence is piety.

Reformed

Calvin: in distress he called upon Jehovah and cried to his God; God heard from his temple, and the cry came into his ears. Recourse in trouble is the fruit of prior trust. Heaven’s temple is not deaf to the servant’s voice.

Reformed accent: shared—God hears from his dwelling. Answered cry is covenant faithfulness, not lucky coincidence in the storm.

Psalms 18:7

7Then the earth reeled and rocked; the foundations also of the mountains trembled and quaked, because he was angry.

Arminian

Wesley: Then—Then God appeared on my behalf in a glorious manner, to the terror and confusion of all mine enemies, which is here compared to an earthquake. Anger shakes earth and mountain foundations.

Arminian hearing: shared—God’s anger for his servant’s sake can rock the world-picture of the psalm. Grace delivers by holy wrath against oppressors; the hearer still trusts the God who appears, not a tame spectator.

Reformed

Calvin: David, unable to extol the aid enough, sets forth an image in sky and earth. Sudden changes awaken those asleep to a presiding God. The earthquake similitude removes doubt and confirms faith in power and providence; he does not narrate bare history but uses similitudes so slow minds may apprehend God by external signs.

Reformed accent: shared—deliverance is God-sized. The quaking image teaches providence, not that every rescue must look like geology.

Psalms 18:8

8Smoke went up from his nostrils, and devouring fire from his mouth; glowing coals flamed forth from him.

Arminian

Wesley: There went, &c.—All these seem to be figurative expressions, denoting the greatness of his anger. Smoke, fire, and coals picture holy wrath, not human temper.

Arminian pastoral: shared—take the figures as greatness of anger on the servant’s behalf. Grace confronts enemies with holy fire; the hearer must not shrink the God who saves into mere mildness.

Reformed

Calvin: smoke from nostrils and devouring fire mark God clothed in anger for his people’s deliverance. The imagery wakes dull hearts to the majesty that fights for the oppressed.

Reformed accent: shared—figurative wrath is still real opposition to the enemy. God is not a neutral referee when he delivers his own.

Psalms 18:9

9He bowed the heavens and came down; thick darkness was under his feet.

Arminian

Wesley: Bowed—By producing thick and dark clouds, whereby the heavens seem to come down to the earth. Came—Not by change of place, but by the manifestation of his presence and power on my behalf.

Arminian hearing: shared—God “comes down” by manifested power, not relocation. Grace is presence-for-help; the hearer trusts the God who bows the heavens for the distressed.

Reformed

Calvin: he bowed the heavens and came down; thick darkness under his feet. God draws near in storm theophany so that deliverance is seen as his act.

Reformed accent: shared—condescension without change of essence. The Most High manifests power on earth for his servant’s rescue.

Psalms 18:10

10He rode on a cherub and flew; he came swiftly on the wings of the wind.

Arminian

Wesley: Cherub—Or, upon the cherubim, upon the angels, who are also called God’s chariots, upon which he is said to sit and ride. Not to be understood grossly, but to denote God’s using the ministry of angels in raising storms. Fly—As swiftly as the wind.

Arminian pastoral: shared—God’s swift help may use angelic ministry. Grace moves faster than the enemy’s plot; the hearer still looks to the Rider, not to secondary causes alone.

Reformed

Calvin: he rode upon a cherub and flew; carried on the wings of the wind. The psalm clothes providence in chariot imagery so faith sees speed and sovereignty in the rescue.

Reformed accent: shared—swift heavenly agency. Secondary winds do not dethrone the God who rides them for his people’s sake.

Psalms 18:11

11He made darkness his covering, his canopy around him, thick clouds dark with water.

Arminian

Wesley: Darkness—He covered himself with dark clouds. Waters—Watery vapors. Covering and canopy hide and reveal at once the God who comes to save.

Arminian hearing: shared—God may veil himself in dark waters even while acting. Grace is not always sunny clarity; the hearer still trusts the covered Majesty who fights for him.

Reformed

Calvin: he made darkness his secret place; pavilion of dark waters and clouds. God is present in the storm-canopy that terrifies enemies and shelters purpose.

Reformed accent: shared—hiddenness is not absence. Providence can work under thick cloud without ceasing to be for the servant.

Psalms 18:12

12Out of the brightness before him hailstones and coals of fire broke through his clouds.

Arminian

Wesley: At—His glorious and powerful appearance. Passed—Or, passed away, vanished, being dissolved into showers. Brightness breaks into hailstones and coals of fire.

Arminian pastoral: shared—glory can erupt through the cloud in judgment-imagery. Grace for the servant may mean storm for the foe; hold both without softening either.

Reformed

Calvin: at the brightness before him the clouds resolve into hail and coals of fire. Sudden changes give a livelier sense of God’s power than uniform course.

Reformed accent: shared—brightness that breaks the cloud is still the same LORD. Deliverance-theophany is not random weather.

Psalms 18:13

13The LORD also thundered in the heavens, and the Most High uttered his voice, hailstones and coals of fire.

Arminian

Wesley’s movement: thunder and the Most High’s voice continue the theophany—hailstones and coals again. Heaven speaks against the oppressors.

Arminian hearing: shared—hear the LORD’s voice in the deliverance. Grace thunders for the distressed; the hearer ascribes the peal to God, not chance sky.

Reformed

Calvin: Jehovah thundered; the thunder is the voice of God, not mere chance or nature independent of his will. David rises above phenomena to the supreme governor who launches thunderbolts as from his mouth.

Reformed accent: shared—secondary causes do not erase the Speaker. The Most High’s voice is the meaning of the storm for faith.

Psalms 18:14

14And he sent out his arrows and scattered them; he flashed forth lightnings and routed them.

Arminian

Wesley: Arrows—Lightnings. Them—Mine enemies. Arrows and lightnings scatter and rout the foes.

Arminian pastoral: shared—God’s weapons put enemies to flight. Grace fights; the rescued still give God the credit for the rout rather than their own arm alone.

Reformed

Calvin: enemies scattered by the arrows of God—overthrown not by the hands of men alone but by God launching thunderbolts. Metaphorical language brings the slow to acknowledge God as author of deliverance.

Reformed accent: shared—victory’s first cause is God. Human swords, if used, do not steal the glory of the lightning’s Lord.

Psalms 18:15

15Then the channels of the sea were seen, and the foundations of the world were laid bare at your rebuke, O LORD, at the blast of the breath of your nostrils.

Arminian

Wesley: Discovered—By mighty earthquakes, which overturned the earth, and made its lower parts visible. Channels of the sea and foundations laid bare at rebuke and blast of nostrils recall Red Sea-scale power.

Arminian hearing: shared—rebuke can bare the world’s foundations in the psalm’s imagery. Grace remembers covenant wonders; the hearer trusts the same rebuking breath that once made a path through waters.

Reformed

Calvin: sources of the waters seen alludes to the Red Sea miracle. Special benefits recall the covenant with the whole people, assuring that the God who split the sea still delivers his servant.

Reformed accent: shared—personal rescue tied to covenant memory. The blast of nostrils is the LORD of the Exodus still acting.

Psalms 18:16

16He sent from on high, he took me; he drew me out of many waters.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: sent from on high, he took me; drew me out of many waters—personal snatch from the flood after the cosmic storm.

Arminian pastoral: shared—God reaches down and draws out. Grace is vertical rescue; the hearer still knows he was taken, not that he swam free by cleverness alone.

Reformed

Calvin: he sent from on high, took me, drew me from many waters. After the theophany, the point is personal: God himself extracts his servant from overwhelming flood.

Reformed accent: shared—deliverance is from above. Many waters do not cancel election to be taken by God’s hand.

Psalms 18:17

17He rescued me from my strong enemy and from those who hated me, for they were too mighty for me.

Arminian

Wesley’s movement: rescued from the strong enemy and those who hated—because they were too mighty. Weakness confessed, strength located in God.

Arminian hearing: shared—admit foes too strong for you. Grace rescues the overmatched; the hearer must still cry rather than pretend parity with hatred.

Reformed

Calvin: rescued from the strong enemy and haters too mighty for him. David’s victories are traced to God’s favor; the inequality of strength magnifies the Helper.

Reformed accent: shared—overmatch is the stage for providence. God maintains his champion when human odds fail.

Psalms 18:18

18They confronted me in the day of my calamity, but the LORD was my support.

Arminian

Wesley: Prevented—They had almost surprized me. Confronted in the day of calamity, yet the LORD was support.

Arminian pastoral: shared—calamity’s day can nearly ambush; God remains the support. Grace meets surprise attack; lean on the LORD when enemies arrive early.

Reformed

Calvin: they confronted in the day of calamity, but Jehovah was his support. Near-surprise does not mean God arrived late; support held when the day was darkest.

Reformed accent: shared—support in calamity is covenant presence. Almost-surprised is still under the God who upholds.

Psalms 18:19

19He brought me out into a broad place; he rescued me, because he delighted in me.

Arminian

Wesley: Brought—Out of my straits and difficulties, into a state of freedom and comfort. So he ascribes all his mercies to God’s good pleasure, as the first spring of them.

Arminian reading: broad place and rescue because God delighted in him. Delight is real favor; the hearer still receives freedom as gift rooted in God’s good pleasure—and answers with loyal love, not presumption that delight cannot be grieved by later unfaithfulness.

Reformed

Calvin: brought into a broad place because God delighted in him. A little before, blessings are traced to God’s good pleasure; election to the kingdom and free favor stand first. Deliverance flows from God’s delight, not from David inventing his own crown.

Reformed accent: broad place is the fruit of divine good pleasure. Rescue because he delighted grounds the servant in free grace before any talk of reward.

Key contrast Both confess that God brought David into a broad place and rescued him because he delighted in him, and that mercies spring from God’s good pleasure. Arminian readers, with Wesley, stress ascribing all mercies to that good pleasure as first spring while still hearing delight as favor the servant must not presume upon by later departure. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress free good pleasure and delight as the electing fountain of the kingdom and of rescue—grace first, before integrity is even named.

Psalms 18:20

20The LORD dealt with me according to my righteousness; according to the cleanness of my hands he rewarded me.

Arminian

Wesley: Righteousness—Just cause. The innocency of my actions towards Saul, from whose blood I kept my hands pure. Reward according to cleanness of hands is plea of integrity in the controversy, not claim to sinless perfection before God.

Arminian reading: God deals according to a real righteousness in the case—clean hands toward the enemy. Grace enabled that integrity; the hearer must still keep hands clean in conflict and may plead a just cause without claiming merit that buys salvation wholesale.

Reformed

Calvin: David seems to contradict tracing blessings to good pleasure, yet both are needed: God freely chose him king, and he yielded faithful obedience in the charge. God is like a combat president who chose his champion and then maintained him who performed the office faithfully. Aid fails not those who follow their calling within its limits. He does not varnish iniquity; he boasts of help given to an innocent man often near death.

Reformed accent: reward according to righteousness is God vindicating his called servant’s integrity in office and controversy—never a ladder of merit that replaces free election to the kingdom.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD dealt with David according to his righteousness and rewarded according to clean hands. Arminian readers, with Wesley, hear a just-cause plea: innocence toward Saul and clean hands in the conflict, grace-enabled integrity one may plead without claiming absolute sinlessness. Reformed readers, with Calvin, hear the same vindication only after free good pleasure chose the king: God maintains his champion’s faithful calling—reward as covenant defense of integrity, not wages that purchased the throne.

Psalms 18:21

21For I have kept the ways of the LORD, and have not wickedly departed from my God.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: kept the ways of the LORD; not wickedly departed from God. Integrity is continued adherence, not a single gesture.

Arminian pastoral: shared—keep the ways; do not depart wickedly. Grace enables loyalty; the hearer must still refuse departure when pressure invites another path.

Reformed

Calvin: he kept Jehovah’s ways and did not wickedly depart from his God. Faithful performance of the charge committed to him—staying inside the call despite attempts to shake faith.

Reformed accent: shared—perseverance in the assigned ways. Departure refused is obedience under a prior call, not self-made religion.

Psalms 18:22

22For all his rules were before me, and his statutes I did not put away from me.

Arminian

Wesley: Judgments—I diligently studied and considered them. Rules before him; statutes not put away—Word kept in view.

Arminian hearing: shared—keep God’s rules before you; do not shelve statutes. Grace supplies the Word; the hearer must still study and not put it away under temptation.

Reformed

Calvin: all his judgments were before me; statutes not put away. The servant keeps ordinances in sight as the path of the calling.

Reformed accent: shared—Word-before-eyes obedience. Statutes retained are how integrity is walked, not optional ornament.

Psalms 18:23

23I was blameless before him, and I kept myself from my guilt.

Arminian

Wesley: Iniquity—From that sin which I was most inclined or tempted to. Blameless before him and kept from guilt names watched temptation, especially the lure to seize the kingdom violently against Saul.

Arminian reading: blamelessness here is real self-keeping from a pressing guilt—grace-enabled restraint the servant practiced. The hearer must still keep himself from the sin that most beckons in his conflict.

Reformed

Calvin: upright with him and kept from his iniquity—integrity in the matter charged, under God’s eye. Not a denial that sinners need mercy, but a claim that in this controversy he had not turned aside into the guilt his enemies alleged or the shortcut temptation offered.

Reformed accent: blameless before him is sincerity in the calling under trial—God-ward integrity in the office, not a boast of impeccability that needs no Savior.

Psalms 18:24

24So the LORD has rewarded me according to my righteousness, according to the cleanness of my hands in his sight.

Arminian

Wesley’s refrain: so the LORD rewarded according to righteousness and clean hands in his sight—the vindication repeated after the integrity claim.

Arminian pastoral: shared—God sees clean hands and answers with reward in the controversy. Grace noticed the integrity; continue the clean-handed way rather than treat vindication as license.

Reformed

Calvin: therefore Jehovah recompensed according to righteousness and cleanness before his eyes. The “therefore” ties reward to integrity in the combat God assigned—eyes of God, not flattering human courts.

Reformed accent: shared—recompense under God’s sight. Vindication is judicial and covenantal, flowing from the God who already delighted to set him in the fight.

Key contrast Both confess the refrain: the LORD rewarded according to righteousness and cleanness of hands in his sight. Arminian readers stress that God really answers lived integrity in the just cause—clean hands that must be kept. Reformed readers stress that this “therefore” still sits under prior good pleasure and calling: recompense is the Judge maintaining his servant, not the servant collecting wages that founded the relationship.

Psalms 18:25

25With the merciful you show yourself merciful; with the blameless man you show yourself blameless;

Arminian

Wesley: Upright—Thou metest to every one the same measure, which he meteth out to others; and therefore thou wilt perform mercy and truth to those who are merciful and true to others.

Arminian reading: with the merciful God shows himself merciful—moral symmetry that encourages mercy and blamelessness. Grace forms merciful people; they must still practice mercy expecting God’s like dealing, without turning the verse into a cold bargain that empties gift.

Reformed

Calvin: God, in delivering an innocent man, showed he is merciful toward the merciful and pure toward the pure. General doctrine: God does not disappoint his servants who wait with meekness; every one shall reap the fruit of his righteousness. Differ from hypocrites who abuse prosperity to claim divine favor while sacrificing to Fortune.

Reformed accent: God’s dealing matches the servant’s way as moral government under grace—encouragement to integrity, not a marketplace that replaces mercy with earnings.

Key contrast Both confess that with the merciful God shows himself merciful and with the blameless he shows himself blameless. Arminian readers, with Wesley, hear measure for measure that urges merciful and true living toward others. Reformed readers, with Calvin, hear the same moral correspondence as proof God favors integrity—and as general comfort that servants who wait meekly are not disappointed—without letting hypocrites turn success into a claim against heaven.

Psalms 18:26

26with the purified you show yourself pure; and with the crooked you make yourself seem tortuous.

Arminian

Wesley: Pure—Free from the least mixture or appearance of unrighteousness, or unfaithfulness. Froward—Thou wilt cross him and walk contrary to him.

Arminian pastoral: shared—purity met with purity; crookedness met with God’s opposing path. Grace warns the froward; straighten rather than force God to walk contrary to you.

Reformed

Calvin: with the pure, pure; with the perverse, God shows himself perverse—strange wording to awaken hypocrites who sleep under judgment. Like Leviticus: if you walk contrary, I will walk contrary—obstinacy makes God harden himself to break them. Stubbornness gains a God who feels like iron against stone.

Reformed accent: shared—opposition to the crooked is holy, not capricious. God may take a form of severity the perverse themselves provoke.

Psalms 18:27

27For you save a humble people, but the haughty eyes you bring down.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: save the humble; bring down haughty eyes. Deliverance has a moral tilt toward the lowly.

Arminian hearing: shared—humility saved, pride lowered. Grace lifts the humble; the hearer must still refuse haughty eyes that God promises to bring down.

Reformed

Calvin: thou wilt save the afflicted/humble people and bring down haughty eyes. God’s pattern in David’s story: rescue for the low, humbling for confident pride.

Reformed accent: shared—providence opposes haughty looks. Salvation of the humble is God’s settled way, not a mood.

Psalms 18:28

28For it is you who light my lamp; the LORD my God lightens my darkness.

Arminian

Wesley: Lightest—Given me safety, and comfort, and glory, and posterity also. God lights the lamp and lightens darkness.

Arminian pastoral: shared—ask God to light your lamp in the dark. Grace is illumination and safety; do not curse the dark without seeking the LORD who lightens it.

Reformed

Calvin: God lights the lamp—safety and continuance where darkness threatened extinction. The servant’s hope of light is located in “you” and “the LORD my God.”

Reformed accent: shared—light from God alone. Darkness does not master the one whose lamp God lights.

Psalms 18:29

29For by you I can run against a troop, and by my God I can leap over a wall.

Arminian

Wesley: Troop—Broken through the armed troops of mine enemies. Wall—I have scaled the walls of their strongest cities. By God: run against a troop; leap over a wall.

Arminian hearing: shared—feats of war credited to God. Grace enables the impossible push; the hearer still says “by you” and “by my God,” not “by my legend.”

Reformed

Calvin: by God he ran through a troop and leaped a wall—extraordinary enablement for God-assigned war. Strength for what flesh alone could not do.

Reformed accent: shared—military success as divine enablement. The leap is gift, not proof of autonomous prowess.

Psalms 18:30

30This God—his way is perfect; the word of the LORD proves true; he is a shield for all those who take refuge in him.

Arminian

Wesley: Perfect—His providence, though it may sometimes be dark, yet is always wise and just, and unblameable. Tried—The truth of God’s promises is approved by innumerable experiences. Shield for all who take refuge.

Arminian pastoral: shared—trust the perfect way and tried word; take refuge under the shield. Grace invites refuge; actually hide in him when providence looks dark.

Reformed

Calvin: God’s way perfect; the word proves true; he is a shield to all who take refuge. After personal feats, the confession widens: this God’s path and promise hold for every truster.

Reformed accent: shared—perfect way, proven word, universal offer of the shield to refuge-takers. Experience confirms what the mouth of God swore.

Psalms 18:31

31For who is God, but the LORD? And who is a rock, except our God?—

Arminian

Wesley: Who—Our Lord is the only God, and therefore there is none that can hinder him from accomplishing his own work. Exclusive deity and rock.

Arminian hearing: shared—confess one God, one rock. Grace clears rivals; refuse other rocks that cannot save.

Reformed

Calvin: who is God but Jehovah, and who a rock except our God? Monotheism as pastoral comfort: no rival can block his work for his people.

Reformed accent: shared—exclusive God means unhindered purpose. The rhetorical questions shut the door on other saviors.

Psalms 18:32

32the God who equipped me with strength and made my way blameless.

Arminian

Wesley: Perfect—Perfectly plain and smooth, and clear from impediments. God equipped with strength and made the way blameless/smooth.

Arminian pastoral: shared—strength and a cleared path are from God. Grace equips; walk the way he has made passable rather than invent another.

Reformed

Calvin: the God who girded with strength and made the way perfect/blameless. Enablement and path-clearing both from him who alone is rock.

Reformed accent: shared—equipment and way are divine gifts. Blameless path here is God-made passage, not self-generated perfection.

Psalms 18:33

33He made my feet like the feet of a deer and set me secure on the heights.

Arminian

Wesley: High-places—Confirms me in that high estate into which he hath advanced me. Deer-feet and secure heights.

Arminian hearing: shared—God gives sure footing in high places he assigned. Grace stabilizes advancement; do not climb heights God did not set.

Reformed

Calvin: feet like hinds—unusual swiftness to climb inaccessible rocks; high places are strongholds taken by right of the war God authorized. Nothing seized as another man’s by private ambition.

Reformed accent: shared—swift secure heights under calling. The deer-feet are gift for God-given fortresses.

Psalms 18:34

34He trains my hands for war, so that my arms can bend a bow of bronze.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: trains hands for war; arms bend a bow of bronze—martial skill as tutelage from God.

Arminian pastoral: shared—credit training and strength to God’s schooling. Grace teaches the hands; refuse to treat skill as self-made.

Reformed

Calvin: hands taught to war; arms strong enough for a bow of bronze/brass—dexterity not from mere practice but singular goodness. Something surpassing natural strength.

Reformed accent: shared—war-craft as secret virtue from God. Training is providence, not bare technique.

Psalms 18:35

35You have given me the shield of your salvation, and your right hand supported me, and your gentleness made me great.

Arminian

Wesley: Salvation—Thy protection, which hath been to me like a shield. Held—Kept me from falling into mischiefs enemies designed. Gentleness—Thy clemency, whereby thou hast pardoned my sins; thy grace and benignity.

Arminian reading: shield, right hand, and gentleness that made him great—clemency and pardon inside the warrior’s song. Grace is not only power; the hearer needs supporting gentleness and forgiven sin even while God makes him great.

Reformed

Calvin: shield of thy salvation; right hand held him up; clemency/gentleness increased him—continuation of divine favor. Without God’s shield he would have taken deadly wounds; free goodness carried him forward in honor.

Reformed accent: salvation-shield and upholding right hand are free preservation. Greatness grows by God’s gentleness, not by raw force alone.

Psalms 18:36

36You gave a wide place for my steps under me, and my feet did not slip.

Arminian

Wesley: Slipt—As they are apt to do in narrow and uneven ways. Wide place under steps; feet did not slip.

Arminian pastoral: shared—ask for room underfoot so you do not slip. Grace widens the path; walk carefully still on what was once narrow.

Reformed

Calvin: enlarged steps—opened even pathway where there was no access; when reduced to distress with no escape, God brought him out. Feet did not stagger. Corrects distrust that trembles unless it sees a pleasant plain.

Reformed accent: shared—God’s office to enlarge and level ways. Unslipped feet are gift in former straits, not proof we never needed help.

Psalms 18:37

37I pursued my enemies and overtook them, and did not turn back till they were consumed.

Arminian

Wesley’s movement: pursued, overtook, did not turn back till consumed—thoroughness in God-authorized pursuit.

Arminian hearing: shared—finish the conflict God assigned without quitting early. Grace strengthens pursuit; motives must stay under God’s command, not private cruelty.

Reformed

Calvin: victories traced to God’s favor prove the cause good. David speaks as one executing judgment God committed—not bloodthirst, but vocation and pure zeal free of fleshly perturbation. As type of Christ, severity fell on the inflexibly obstinate.

Reformed accent: shared—pursuit under divine warrant. Consumed enemies are God’s judgment through his servant, not a template for ungentle private revenge.

Psalms 18:38

38I thrust them through, so that they were not able to rise; they fell under my feet.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: thrust through; unable to rise; fell under feet—complete overthrow.

Arminian pastoral: shared—God can grant total defeat of oppressors. Grace decides the fall; do not glory in the thrust apart from the God who armed you.

Reformed

Calvin: enemies made to sink; destruction of haters under God’s enabling. Effect shows God approved the servant’s cause.

Reformed accent: shared—overthrow as token of divine favor on a just war. The fallen underfoot do not authorize sadism outside vocation.

Psalms 18:39

39For you equipped me with strength for the battle; you made those who rise against me sink under me.

Arminian

Wesley’s refrain of enablement: equipped with strength for battle; risers made to sink. God again named as the source.

Arminian hearing: shared—“you equipped… you made.” Grace for the fight; keep attributing sinkage of foes to God.

Reformed

Calvin: girded with strength for the war; enemies bowed under him. Insists the issue traces to God’s favor and thus to a just cause.

Reformed accent: shared—battle strength is gift. Those who rise sink because God bowed them, not because fortune flipped.

Psalms 18:40

40You made my enemies turn their backs to me, and those who hated me I destroyed.

Arminian

Wesley’s movement: enemies turned backs; haters destroyed—rout and removal.

Arminian pastoral: shared—God can make enemies flee. Grace turns backs; destruction of hatred still asks clean hands in how victory is used.

Reformed

Calvin: given the necks/backs of enemies; haters destroyed. God-authorized king, invincible by God’s power—type of Christ’s iron rod against the finally obstinate, while Christ also allures to repentance.

Reformed accent: shared—rout under Messiah’s shadow. David’s wars preview the kingdom that pardons the penitent and breaks the inflexible.

Psalms 18:41

41They cried for help, but there was none to save; they cried to the LORD, but he did not answer them.

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: they cried for help—none to save; cried to the LORD—he did not answer. Hypocrites’ prayers in extremity go unanswered.

Arminian reading: unanswered cry exposes false refuge in God while practicing injustice. Grace invites sincere calling; the hearer must not treat emergency piety as a substitute for repentance and truth.

Reformed

Calvin: they cried even to Jehovah, but he did not answer—hypocrites who abused God’s name by claiming his favor for unjust proceedings. Not that sincere callers are refused (Ps 145:18); they call with presumption, not truth, without changing purpose. Long prosperity made them seem God-backed while David cried in vain—until God cast them down.

Reformed accent: God is nigh to all who call in truth; he is not bound to advance wicked enterprises dressed in prayer.

Key contrast Both confess that the enemies cried for help and even to the LORD, yet he did not answer them. Arminian readers stress that emergency cries without turned lives are empty—sincere calling and amendment matter. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress the same refusal as judgment on hypocrites who claimed God’s favor for injustice: not a denial that God hears truth-callers, but proof he will not underwrite wicked enterprises.

Psalms 18:42

42I beat them fine as dust before the wind; I cast them out like the mire of the streets.

Arminian

Wesley’s image: beaten fine as dust; cast out like street mire—utter humiliation of the foe.

Arminian pastoral: shared—God can reduce oppressors to dust and mire. Grace decides the grinding; refuse to become mire-hearted in how you remember the win.

Reformed

Calvin: beat them small as dust before the wind; tread as mire. Continuation of God-given supremacy over false claimants to divine support.

Reformed accent: shared—total defeat under providence. Dust and mire language is judgment imagery, not a cue for perpetual spite.

Psalms 18:43

43You delivered me from strife with the people; you made me the head of the nations; people whom I had not known served me.

Arminian

Wesley: People—From contentions and seditions under Saul, Ishbosheth, and Absalom. Heathens—Ammonites, Moabites, Edomites, Syrians, and others. Not known—Even barbarous and remote nations. Delivered from strife; made head of nations.

Arminian hearing: shared—God can quiet internal strife and set his servant over peoples unknown. Grace expands rule; stay servant of the LORD while heading nations.

Reformed

Calvin: delivered from contentions of the people; made head of nations; unknown peoples serve. Domestic strife and foreign submission both from God’s hand.

Reformed accent: shared—kingdom scope by gift. Headship over nations is providence on the anointed, previewing wider rule.

Psalms 18:44

44As soon as they heard of me they obeyed me; foreigners came cringing to me.

Arminian

Wesley: Hear—As soon as they understand my will and pleasure, they shall instantly comply. Foreigners cringe at the hearing.

Arminian pastoral: shared—reputation of God’s king can win obedience at a word. Grace spreads the name; do not confuse cringing fear with converted love—yet still thank God for subdued opposition.

Reformed

Calvin: at the hearing of the ear they obey; foreigners feign submission from fear. Even half-hearted compliance shows God enlarging David’s sway.

Reformed accent: shared—fame that subdues is God’s tool. Cringing may be fear not faith—and still serves the anointed’s security.

Psalms 18:45

45Foreigners lost heart and came trembling out of their fortresses.

Arminian

Wesley: Close places—Out of their strong holds, where they shall lurk for fear of me. Lost heart; trembling from fortresses.

Arminian hearing: shared—God can empty fortresses by fear. Grace breaks morale; the king still must not trust fear alone as if it were discipleship.

Reformed

Calvin: foreigners lose courage and tremble out of concealment. Strongholds fail when God melts hearts before his servant.

Reformed accent: shared—fortress psychology under providence. Lost heart is God’s doing against those who opposed his anointed.

Psalms 18:46

46The LORD lives, and blessed be my rock, and exalted be the God of my salvation—

Arminian

Wesley: The Lord—He and he only is the true living God. Blessed rock; exalted God of salvation.

Arminian pastoral: shared—bless and exalt the living LORD. Grace that saved must end in doxology; refuse dead idols after living help.

Reformed

Calvin: Jehovah lives—not mere bare essence, but life shown in works that preserve the world. Blessed be my strength; exalted the God of salvation. When God hides his working, the sense “God lives” fades from minds; when he shows power, praise rises.

Reformed accent: shared—living God means active Savior. Blessing the rock is faith’s response to evident vital energy in deliverance.

Psalms 18:47

47the God who gave me vengeance and subdued peoples under me,

Arminian

Wesley’s frame: God who gave vengeance and subdued peoples—vengeance as God’s gift, not private heat.

Arminian hearing: shared—ascribe vengeance and subdued peoples to God. Grace may grant just recompense; the heart must still refuse taking vengeance as a personal toy.

Reformed

Calvin: God who gives vengeance and subdues peoples—victories attributed solely to him. Nothing but what God gave; power for what he could not have expected without confidence in aid.

Reformed accent: shared—vengeance as divine gift through the office. Subdued peoples magnify the Giver, not the ego of the king.

Psalms 18:48

48who rescued me from my enemies; yes, you exalted me above those who rose against me; you delivered me from the man of violence.

Arminian

Wesley: Violent man—From Saul: whom for honour’s sake he forbears to mention. Rescued, exalted above risers, delivered from the man of violence.

Arminian pastoral: shared—name deliverance from violent men without needless dishonor. Grace lifts above aggressors; remember the Rescuer more than nursing the grudge.

Reformed

Calvin: deliverer from enemies; lifted above those who rose; delivered from the violent man. Epithets confirm God displays vital energy in government and rescue.

Reformed accent: shared—exaltation over violent opposition is God’s act. The type points beyond Saul to every violence that cannot overthrow the anointed line.

Psalms 18:49

49For this I will praise you, O LORD, among the nations, and sing to your name.

Arminian

Wesley: Heathen—David is transported beyond himself, and speaks in special relation to Christ who was to be his seed, of whom he was an eminent type, and by whom alone this was done. Justly applied to Christ and the calling of the Gentiles (Rom 15:9).

Arminian hearing: shared—praise among the nations looks past David to Messiah’s Gentile mission. Grace widens the song; join the praise among the nations rather than keep salvation tribal.

Reformed

Calvin: therefore praise among the heathen and sing to the name. The thanksgiving expands to the nations—fitting the type of Christ’s kingdom victorious despite world resistance.

Reformed accent: shared—doxology among the nations. Paul’s citation seals that David’s praise foresaw Gentile inclusion under the greater Son.

Psalms 18:50

50Great salvation he brings to his king, and shows steadfast love to his anointed, to David and his offspring forever.

Arminian

Wesley: His king—To the king whom God himself chose and anointed, and to all his posterity; and especially to the Messiah, who is called David’s seed (Acts 13:23; Rom 1:3). Great salvation and steadfast love forever.

Arminian reading: covenant love to the anointed and offspring forever—centered in the Messiah. Grace keeps the promise; the hearer trusts forever-love in David’s greater Son and lives as one claimed by that steadfast love, not as if forever were a blank check for unfaithfulness without a Redeemer.

Reformed

Calvin: great deliverances for his king; mercy to David his anointed and to his seed forever. The psalm ends in covenant hesed to the royal line—image and type of Christ’s kingdom, always victorious by the Father’s power despite the world’s resistance.

Reformed accent: forever steadfast love is God’s oath to the anointed line fulfilled in Christ. Great salvation is covenant faithfulness, not uncertain mood.

Key contrast Both confess great salvation for the king and steadfast love to David’s anointed and offspring forever, reaching especially to the Messiah. Arminian readers, with Wesley, stress the forever promise focused in Christ the seed—covenant love that claims the hearer for loyal trust and praise among the nations. Reformed readers, with Calvin, stress the same forever hesed as God’s invincible maintenance of the anointed kingdom typed in David and fulfilled in Christ—covenant loyalty secured by the Father’s power, not left hanging on the frailty of the earthly throne alone.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on affectionate love for the LORD our strength, theophany and rescue from many waters, delight and good pleasure, righteousness and clean hands in a just cause, merciful dealing, refuge under a perfect way, victory over enemies, and forever steadfast love to the anointed—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 18:1–50.

Arminian Notable voices

“David triumphs in God, praises him for past deliverances, takes comfort of his integrity which God had cleared, gives God the glory of all his victories, and expresses hope of what God would do farther. This psalm, with small variations, is written in 2 Samuel 22; composed toward the end of his life after deliverance from all enemies and from Saul. Servant—who esteems it greater honor to be thy servant than king of Israel. Love—most affectionately, and with my whole soul. Rock—to which I flee for refuge; horn—strength as in beasts whose force is in their horns. Death—dangerous troubles; floods—violent assaults like a flood. Then God appeared on my behalf to the terror of enemies, compared to an earthquake. Came—not by change of place, but by manifestation of presence and power. Brought into freedom and comfort: he ascribes all mercies to God’s good pleasure as the first spring. Righteousness—just cause; innocency toward Saul, from whose blood he kept his hands pure. Upright—thou metest the same measure he meteth to others; therefore mercy and truth to the merciful and true. Perfect—providence, though sometimes dark, always wise, just, unblameable; the truth of promises approved by innumerable experiences. Gentleness—clemency pardoning sins; grace and benignity. Heathen—transported beyond himself in relation to Christ his seed, by whom alone this is done, applied to the calling of the Gentiles. His king—the king God chose and anointed, and all his posterity, especially the Messiah, David’s seed.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 18)

Reformed Notable voices

“David came to the kingdom through almost insurmountable obstacles—fugitive under Saul, then harassed by tumults and foreign enemies to old age. These calamities he would never have surmounted without God’s power; he does not sing triumph to himself but magnifies God by lofty epithets. His reign was an image and type of Christ’s kingdom, victorious by the Father’s power despite the world’s resistance. Love to God is the principal part of true godliness; nothing he values more than free spontaneous love. Heaping titles fortifies faith: those God defends are surrounded by impregnable ramparts. He cried in distress and was heard from the temple; theophany similitudes confirm power and providence. Brought into a broad place because God delighted in him—good pleasure first. When he speaks of reward according to righteousness, both are needed: free election to the kingdom, and faithful obedience in the charge; God maintained his champion. With the merciful he shows himself merciful—yet hypocrites must not abuse prosperity. Victories prove the cause just under vocation, not cruelty. Enemies cried to Jehovah but he did not answer hypocrites who claimed his favor for injustice; he hears those who call in truth. Jehovah lives in the evidence of his works; great deliverances and forever mercy to David his anointed and his seed.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 18)
