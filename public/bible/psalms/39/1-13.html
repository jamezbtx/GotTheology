Passage

Psalms 39

Verses 1–13 · ESV · Voices

To the choirmaster: to Jeduthun. A Psalm of David. The singer resolves to guard his ways and muzzle his mouth while the wicked are present, yet mute silence proves no avail as distress worsens; heart heats in musing until fire burns and the tongue speaks. He asks the LORD to make him know his end and the measure of his days—how fleeting he is; days are a few handbreadths, lifetime nothing before God, and all mankind stands as a mere breath. Man goes about as a shadow, in turmoil for nothing, heaping wealth without knowing who will gather. The turn: And now, O Lord, for what do I wait? My hope is in you—deliverance from all transgressions, not the scorn of the fool; mute again because the Lord has done it; plea to remove the stroke as he is spent by the hostility of God's hand; discipline with rebukes for sin consumes what is dear like a moth, and mankind is again a mere breath. He asks hearing for prayer, cry, and tears as sojourner and guest like the fathers, and closes: Look away from me, that I may smile again, before I depart and am no more.

Psalms 39:1

1I said, “I will guard my ways, that I may not sin with my tongue; I will guard my mouth with a muzzle, so long as the wicked are in my presence.”

Arminian

I said, “I will guard my ways, that I may not sin with my tongue; I will guard my mouth with a muzzle, so long as the wicked are in my presence.” Wesley: I said—I fully resolved. Take heed—To order all my actions right, and particularly to govern my tongue. Resolve aims first at the tongue under the wicked's eye.

Arminian pastoral: shared—set a deliberate watch on speech before the wicked. Guard ways and muzzle the mouth; do not let presence of the ungodly bait you into sinful talk.

Reformed

Calvin: I said marks purpose of the heart before God, not boasting before men; conscious of weakness and Satan's devices, he muzzles the mouth that not one impatient word escape. While the wicked standeth—even while the worst prosper, exalted and despising, he determined not to be troubled at it.

Reformed pastoral: shared—bridle affections so no impious dissatisfaction against God slips out. Watch while the wicked prosper; patience is proved when exaltation of the ungodly is before your eyes.

Key contrast Both prize resolved silence under provocation. Arminians typically hear Wesley's plain resolve: fully determined to order actions right and govern the tongue. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin's sharper reading of standeth: not only scorn-avoidance in their presence, but steadfast quiet even while the wicked prosper, rule, and despise.

Psalms 39:2

2I was mute and silent; I held my peace to no avail, and my distress grew worse.

Arminian

I was mute and silent; I held my peace to no avail, and my distress grew worse. Wesley: Dumb—Two words put together, expressing the same thing, to aggravate or increase it. I held—I forbear to speak, what I justly might, lest I should break forth into some indecent expressions. Stirred—My silence did not assuage my grief, but increase it.

Arminian pastoral: shared—own that forced silence can deepen grief. Forbear indecent speech without pretending the heart is quiet; bring the stirred distress to God.

Reformed

Calvin: long and steadfast silence, as if deaf—not a momentary whim. From good: though able adequately to defend himself with just grounds of complaint, he refrained of his own mere will rather than indulge intemperate sorrow; yet at length the violence of grief broke through the barriers.

Reformed pastoral: shared—prefer forgoing even a righteous self-defense to murmuring. When silence finally breaks, fear the same fall; learn from David's failure mid-course.

Key contrast Both see silence that failed to quiet grief. Arminians typically hear Wesley: he forbore speaking what he justly might, lest indecent words break out—and silence increased sorrow. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin's from good: though able to prosecute a just defense of innocence, he chose restraint of will; even so, grief at last broke the muzzle.

Psalms 39:3

3My heart became hot within me. As I mused, the fire burned; then I spoke with my tongue:

Arminian

My heart became hot within me. As I mused, the fire burned; then I spoke with my tongue: Inward heat under musing breaks the resolve; speech follows fire.

Arminian pastoral: shared—watch the musing that kindles. Heat within is not harmless private thought; bring burning meditation to God before the tongue runs.

Reformed

Calvin: suppressed grief became the more inflamed; the more one strives to obey and exercise patience, the more vigorously Satan assails. Like hot exhalations meeting clouds, resistance of the flesh raises thunder; at last he uttered foolish and unadvised words—a mirror of infirmity.

Reformed pastoral: shared—expect hotter conflict when you resist. Do not despair when fire burns in musing; take warning and seek shelter under God's wings rather than loud complaint.

Psalms 39:4

4“O Lord, make me know my end and what is the measure of my days; let me know how fleeting I am!

Arminian

“O Lord, make me know my end and what is the measure of my days; let me know how fleeting I am! Wesley: My end—Make me sensible of the shortness and uncertainly of life, and the near approach of death. The broken silence turns to a plea for felt frailty.

Arminian pastoral: shared—ask to feel how fleeting you are. Sensibility of short days should humble pride and hurry you toward God, not toward despair.

Reformed

Calvin: transported by improper and sinful excess of passion, he finds fault with God—why deal so severely with a mortal whose life is but a moment? There is irony: he does not ask to learn brevity as if unknown, but presses whether remaining years can recompense such miseries.

Reformed pastoral: shared—learn frailty without murmuring at severity. Feel your nothingness so as to seek mercy; refuse the complaint that treats God's rigor as unjust toward a short life.

Key contrast Both hear a plea about life's measure. Arminians typically hear Wesley: make me sensible of shortness, uncertainty, and death's near approach. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: the request rises from improper passion that faults God's severity toward a frail mortal—irony more than innocent inquiry—and must be corrected toward humble mercy-seeking.

Psalms 39:5

5Behold, you have made my days a few handbreadths, and my lifetime is as nothing before you. Surely all mankind stands as a mere breath!

Arminian

Behold, you have made my days a few handbreadths, and my lifetime is as nothing before you. Surely all mankind stands as a mere breath! Wesley: Before thee—If compared with thee, and with thy everlasting duration. Handbreadths and breath measure every standing human life before the Eternal.

Arminian pastoral: shared—compare your days with his everlasting duration. Let mere breath cut down pretension; seek the God before whom lifetime is nothing.

Reformed

Calvin: a hand-breadth—life flies so that end touches beginning; wholly vanity even while standing—in prime and seeming power, still a bubble. Prosperity intoxicates men into dreaming an immortal state on earth; adversity makes them feel nothingness before God.

Reformed pastoral: shared—confess vanity at full strength, not only in weakness. Refuse earth-immortality dreams; let felt frailty drive you as a humble suppliant to his compassion.

Psalms 39:6

6Surely a man goes about as a shadow! Surely for nothing they are in turmoil; man heaps up wealth and does not know who will gather!

Arminian

Surely a man goes about as a shadow! Surely for nothing they are in turmoil; man heaps up wealth and does not know who will gather! Wesley: Vain shew—Heb. in a shadow or image; in an imaginary rather than a real life… appearances and dreams, than truths and realities. Disquieted—bustling tumult seeking riches, vexing themselves and others.

Arminian pastoral: shared—name the empty bustle around wealth. Do not mistake shadow-life for solid happiness; release anxious heaping to the God who outlasts gatherers.

Reformed

Calvin: nothing substantial in man—only display and ostentation; the fashion passes away. Height of vanity: they disquiet themselves in vain, heaping riches without knowing who shall gather—insatiable desire as if to live a hundred lives. Recollection of hand-breadth life is the remedy for unnecessary cares.

Reformed pastoral: shared—cut unbridled acquisition by remembering brevity. Refuse the madness that confounds heaven and earth as if mortal man would never leave his heap.

Key contrast Both expose shadow-walking and vain turmoil over wealth. Arminians typically hear Wesley: life as imaginary show—appearances and dreams rather than realities—and noisy bustle that vexes self and neighbor. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: substance-less display plus the height of vanity in heaping without a known gatherer—cured by hand-breadth recollection, not by better technique of gain.

Psalms 39:7

7“And now, O Lord, for what do I wait? My hope is in you.

Arminian

“And now, O Lord, for what do I wait? My hope is in you. Wesley: Now Lord—Seeing this life and all its enjoyments are so vain and short. My hope—I will seek for happiness no where but in God. Brevity drives hope to the only solid refuge.

Arminian pastoral: shared—answer vanity with hope in God alone. When enjoyments prove short, wait for him; seek happiness nowhere else.

Reformed

Calvin: having felt ardent emotion and disquiet, he returns to a calm settled mind—the turn from inconsiderate complaint to appropriate prayer. Now has force: flattery no longer deceives; yet conviction of vanity must press forward until expectation depends on God who creates something out of nothing.

Reformed pastoral: shared—pass through felt frailty into hope toward him. Do not stop at emptied vanity like worldly men who grow insensible; wait on the Lord as your only support.

Psalms 39:8

8Deliver me from all my transgressions. Do not make me the scorn of the fool!

Arminian

Deliver me from all my transgressions. Do not make me the scorn of the fool! Hope asks first for deliverance from sins, then shielding from fools' reproach.

Arminian pastoral: shared—seek pardon as the root of relief. Ask to be spared the scorn of the foolish; keep transgression, not mere circumstance, at the center of the plea.

Reformed

Calvin: no longer murmuring, he humbly acknowledges guilt and has recourse to mercy—deliverance from all transgressions, not one only. Trace miseries to their source; as soon as reconciled, chastisement ceases. Reproach of the foolish: either not abandoned to the wicked's mockery, or not involved in the disgrace of the reprobate.

Reformed pastoral: shared—pray forgiveness before you pray only for eased pain. Dry the source; ask that fools not make your chastening their sport.

Psalms 39:9

9I am mute; I do not open my mouth, for it is you who have done it.

Arminian

I am mute; I do not open my mouth, for it is you who have done it. Silence returns on a new ground: the Lord's own doing shuts the mouth.

Arminian pastoral: shared—close the mouth because he has done it. Accept the stroke as from his hand; refuse the open complaint that denies his agency.

Reformed

Calvin: not unbroken restraint as praise, but self-correction—what doest thou, murmuring against God? The profitable lesson: we have to do not with mortal man but with God, who maintains his righteousness. Affliction proceeded from him; therefore keep silence and submit under his mighty hand as to a righteous Judge.

Reformed pastoral: shared—recall whose hand it is when grief swells. Humble yourself under the Judge; let persuasion of his righteousness kill outrageous accusation.

Psalms 39:10

10Remove your stroke from me; I am spent by the hostility of your hand.

Arminian

Remove your stroke from me; I am spent by the hostility of your hand. Wesley: Remove—Take off the judgment which thou hast inflicted upon me. I am—Help me before I am utterly lost. Spent under hostility, he asks the stroke lifted before ruin is complete.

Arminian pastoral: shared—plead for removal while owning the hand that struck. Ask help before you are utterly lost; urgency and confession belong together.

Reformed

Calvin: confirms the prayer for pardon joined to gentle dealing; such prayer, framed by the word, is not noisy provocation but proceeds from calm faith and patience. He has fainted by the blow of God's hand—bearing infirmity, not excusing fault.

Reformed pastoral: shared—ask mitigated stroke after seeking forgiveness. Own that you faint under his hand; seek relief as a penitent, not as a plaintiff against his justice.

Psalms 39:11

11When you discipline a man with rebukes for sin, you consume like a moth what is dear to him; surely all mankind is a mere breath!

Arminian

When you discipline a man with rebukes for sin, you consume like a moth what is dear to him; surely all mankind is a mere breath! Wesley: Beauty—His comeliness and all his excellencies or felicities. Moth—As a moth consumeth a garment, to which God compares himself and his judgments, secretly and insensibly consuming a people.

Arminian pastoral: shared—tremble at secret consuming under rebuke for iniquity. What is dear wastes like cloth under the moth; let breath-vanity send you to mercy.

Reformed

Calvin: rebukes here mark severe punishments—tokens of strict justice, not the tempered fatherly rod that mingles comfort for believers. No man of valor or excellence withstands when this rigor is felt; God as moth secretly gnaws what pride esteems precious, though the world may not hear thunder.

Reformed pastoral: shared—distinguish crushing rebuke from tempered chastisement. Let moth-like loss of excellency cure pride; search the heart until vanity unseats presumption.

Key contrast Both see moth-like loss under rebuke for sin. Arminians typically hear Wesley: beauty as comeliness and felicities, moth as God's judgments secretly and insensibly consuming. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: rebukes as severe justice-tokens distinct from fatherly tempered rods—secret curse that consumes even the excellent man's precious things until vanity is confessed.

Psalms 39:12

12“Hear my prayer, O Lord, and give ear to my cry; hold not your peace at my tears! For I am a sojourner with you, a guest, like all my fathers.

Arminian

“Hear my prayer, O Lord, and give ear to my cry; hold not your peace at my tears! For I am a sojourner with you, a guest, like all my fathers. Wesley: A stranger—I am only in my journey or passage to my real home, which is in the other world. Prayer, cry, and tears rise from a guest's brief stay.

Arminian pastoral: shared—pray as a sojourner headed home. Hold tears before him; refuse to live as if this lodging were the final house.

Reformed

Calvin: gradation—prayer, then crying, then tears—shows sincere bottom-heart pleading, a rule for prayer. Stranger and sojourner before God: he knows without informant how short the paid lodging of human life; from heaven he sees how miserable we would be without his sustaining mercy.

Reformed pastoral: shared—increase vehemence without losing reverence. Plead as a guest whose fathers also passed through; ask hearing because he already knows the brevity.

Psalms 39:13

13Look away from me, that I may smile again, before I depart and am no more!”

Arminian

Look away from me, that I may smile again, before I depart and am no more!” Wesley: No more—Among the living, or in this world. The close asks relief of the gaze of judgment so joy may return before death ends earthly life.

Arminian pastoral: shared—ask for restored smile under mercy before departure. Own that life in this world ends; seek the look that heals rather than consumes, while hope remains in him.

Reformed

Calvin: cease from me—grant relaxation that he may recover strength before departure. He speaks from disquiet of the flesh, asking respite at least to die; he errs in exceeding proper limits, yet still acknowledges no restoration without God's ceased displeasure. Be no more names despairing form, not denial of salvation.

Reformed pastoral: shared—pray for relief without demanding a merely quiet death as the goal. Correct fleshly excess; seek calm to commit the soul into his hands, hope still anchored in the Lord.

Key contrast Both hear a closing plea for relief before departure. Arminians typically hear Wesley: no more means among the living, or in this world—earthly exit in view. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin's caution: the ask for respite can exceed holy limits toward a mere space to die; correct the flesh, yet still seek mercy under the stroke with hope in God.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on guarded ways and a muzzled mouth before the wicked, mute silence that only stirred distress, heart-fire that broke into speech, the plea to know life's end and measure, days as handbreadths and mankind as breath, shadow-walking and vain heaping of wealth, hope fixed in the Lord alone, deliverance from transgressions, silence because God has done it, the stroke of his hand and moth-like consuming under rebuke, sojourner tears before the fathers' God, and the closing ask to look away that he may smile before departure—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 39:1–13.

Arminian Notable voices

“I said—I fully resolved. Take heed—To order all my actions right, and particularly to govern my tongue. Dumb—Two words put together, expressing the same thing, to aggravate or increase it. I held—I forbear to speak, what I justly might, lest I should break forth into some indecent expressions. Stirred—My silence did not assuage my grief, but increase it. My end—Make me sensible of the shortness and uncertainly of life, and the near approach of death. Before thee—If compared with thee, and with thy everlasting duration. Vain shew—Heb. in a shadow or image; in an imaginary rather than a real life: in the pursuit of vain imaginations, in which there is nothing solid or satisfactory: man in and his life, and all his happiness in this world, are rather appearances and dreams, than truths and realities. Disquieted—Heb. They make a noise, bustling, or tumult, with unwearied industry seeking for riches, and troubling and vexing both themselves and others in the pursuit of them. Now Lord—Seeing this life and all its enjoyments are so vain and short. My hope—I will seek for happiness no where but in God. Remove—Take off the judgment which thou hast inflicted upon me. I am—Help me before I am utterly lost. Beauty—His comeliness and all his excellencies or felicities. Moth—As a moth consumeth a garment, to which God compares himself and his judgments, secretly and insensibly consuming a people. A stranger—I am only in my journey or passage to my real home, which is in the other world. No more—Among the living, or in this world.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament, Psalm 39

Reformed Notable voices

“I said, I will take heed—resolution of the heart before God, not display before men; conscious of weakness and Satan's devices, he watched every side, muzzle on the mouth that not one impatient word escape. While the wicked standeth—not merely scorn-avoidance, but while the worst prosper, exalted and despising, he determined not to be troubled. Dumb in silence; from good—though able to defend innocence, he preferred to forgo prosecution rather than indulge intemperate sorrow; yet grief at length broke the barriers. Heart hot; musing fire burned; then he spoke—suppressed grief inflamed; Satan assails the careful more than the careless. Make me know my end—transported by improper passion, he finds fault with severity toward a frail mortal; count the years—will they recompense such miseries? Days a hand-breadth; every man while standing is wholly vanity—even in prime and seeming power, a bubble; prosperity intoxicates into dreams of earth-immortality. Walks in a shadow; disquieted in vain; heaps riches and knows not who gathers—vain show without substance. And now, Lord, what wait I for? my hope is toward thee—return from rash complaint to settled hope; none depends on God until made to feel frailty. Deliver from all my sins—humble acknowledgment; dry the source that miseries may cease. Dumb because thou hast done it—self-correction: we have to do with God, righteous Judge; submit under his mighty hand. Remove thy stroke; chastisest with rebukes—not fatherly tempered rods but tokens of strict justice; as a moth he secretly consumes excellency. Hear prayer, cry, tears; stranger and sojourner—brief lodging before him. Let me alone that I recover strength before I depart and be no more—flesh asks respite even to die; he exceeds proper limits in grief, yet still seeks mercy under the stroke.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms, Psalm 39
