Passage

Psalms 79

Verses 1–13 · ESV · Voices

Of Asaph. The nations have entered God's inheritance, defiled the holy temple, and laid Jerusalem in ruins; the bodies of his servants are given to birds and beasts, blood poured out with none to bury them, and Israel becomes a taunt to neighbors. How long will anger and jealousy burn? Pour out wrath on the nations that do not know him—they have devour Jacob. Do not remember former iniquities; let compassion meet the low; help and atone for the glory of the name. Why should the nations ask where their God is? Let the avenging of servants' blood be known; hear the prisoners' groans; preserve those doomed to die; return sevenfold the taunts aimed at the Lord. Yet his people, sheep of his pasture, will give thanks forever and recount his praise from generation to generation.

Psalms 79:1

1O God, the nations have come into your inheritance; they have defiled your holy temple; they have laid Jerusalem in ruins.

Arminian

O God, the nations have come into your inheritance; they have defiled your holy temple; they have laid Jerusalem in ruins. Shared: both camps hear invasion of holy space as outrage against God's claim, not mere politics.

Arminian pastoral: Wesley has no separate lemma here; the plain grief stands—inheritance, temple, and city ruined. Lament the desecration before God who owns the land and the house.

Reformed

Calvin: when the heathen came into God's heritage, the order of nature seemed inverted; destruction of the temple extinguished earthly service; Jerusalem reduced to heaps is hideous overthrow. He begins with heaven-daring impiety that should provoke wrath, then turns to the slaughter of the saints.

Reformed pastoral: shared—inheritance invaded, temple defiled, city ruined. The Church's ruin is first measured by what it does to God's name and worship.

Psalms 79:2

2They have given the bodies of your servants to the birds of the heavens for food, the flesh of your faithful to the beasts of the earth.

Arminian

They have given the bodies of your servants to the birds of the heavens for food, the flesh of your faithful to the beasts of the earth. Shared: servants and faithful are God's own; exposure of the dead is barbarity.

Arminian pastoral: name the cruelty without hardening into hatred of persons. The faithful remain "yours" even when birds and beasts are fed.

Reformed

Calvin: almost every word expresses cruelty—not only death, but bodies given to fowls and beasts instead of burial. Men usually shrink from denying even enemies sepulture; those who delight in torn corpses more resemble beasts than humans. Temporal punishments may fall on elect and reprobate alike; the difference is in the issue—God converts what signals wrath into means of his children's salvation.

Reformed pastoral: shared—bodies of servants exposed. Death of the saints is still precious in his sight; he will avenge.

Psalms 79:3

3They have poured out their blood like water all around Jerusalem, and there was no one to bury them.

Arminian

They have poured out their blood like water all around Jerusalem, and there was no one to bury them. Shared: indiscriminate slaughter; burial denied.

Arminian pastoral: blood like water marks cheapened life around the holy city. Cry the fact to God; do not aestheticize the horror.

Reformed

Calvin: blood shed like water shows insatiable thirst for slaughter; none to bury applies to brethren stricken with terror. God intended burial as a testimony to resurrection; deprivation is double indignity—yet the faithful, rising by faith, suffer no final loss toward blessed immortality.

Reformed pastoral: shared—blood poured out, none to bury. Historic calamity under God's providence, not empty spectacle.

Psalms 79:4

4We have become a taunt to our neighbors, mocked and derided by those around us.

Arminian

We have become a taunt to our neighbors, mocked and derided by those around us. Shared: surrounding scorn heaps shame on the people and, indirectly, on their God.

Arminian pastoral: neighbor mockery is real suffering. Bring the taunt into prayer rather than answering scorn with scorn.

Reformed

Calvin: another complaint to excite mercy. The more the ungodly mock, the more confidently deliverance may be expected—God will not bear insolence that redounds to reproach of his name. The faithful complain not only of personal derision but of what is leveled against God and his law.

Reformed pastoral: shared—taunt and derision. Zeal for the name outranks wounded pride.

Psalms 79:5

5How long, O LORD? Will you be angry forever? Will your jealousy burn like fire?

Arminian

How long, O LORD? Will you be angry forever? Will your jealousy burn like fire? Shared: prolonged anger and jealousy named before God.

Arminian pastoral: "how long" is the cry of worn faith, not a denial of justice. Ask the duration; confess the burn; keep praying.

Reformed

Calvin: how long and forever joined mark lengthened, uninterrupted calamity—complaint not ended within a month or two. They confess the accumulation is from God's wrath; the wicked injure only as he permits. They do not charge him with unjust displeasure but acknowledge the justice of the punishment.

Reformed pastoral: shared—anger and jealousy burned. Look to his hand when conscience also accuses.

Psalms 79:6

6Pour out your anger on the nations that do not know you, and on the kingdoms that do not call upon your name!

Arminian

Pour out your anger on the nations that do not know you, and on the kingdoms that do not call upon your name! Shared: the plea redirects wrath toward those who neither know nor call on God—nations that have assaulted his people.

Arminian pastoral: imprecation here is prayer for public justice, not a private license to hate. Desire that God, not the flesh, pour out anger; keep mercy (vv. 8–9) in the same breath as judgment.

Reformed

Calvin: the prayer seems to clash with charity, yet it is right only under zeal for the public welfare—forgetting individual injury, regarding the Church's salvation, imploring discretion, and setting eyes chiefly on Christ against enemies whose repentance is hopeless. They do not rashly break forth nor anticipate judgment; they desire the reprobate involved in deserved condemnation while patiently waiting for God to separate elect from reprobate. They do not cast aside charity; they know some reformation is hopeless.

Reformed pastoral: shared—pour out anger on unknowing nations. Imprecation disciplined by the Spirit is zeal for God's cause, not carnal revenge.

Key contrast Both treat the imprecation as prayer under God's eye for justice against nations that devour Jacob—not as a blank check for private rage. Arminians typically stress the plain plea joined to coming mercy (vv. 8–9): ask God to pour anger where knowledge and calling on his name are refused, while compassion meets the low. Reformed readers typically stress Calvin's hedges—public welfare over personal spite, Spirit-given discretion, patience until God separates the reprobate—so the same lines stay holy zeal, not fleshly vengeance.

Psalms 79:7

7For they have devoured Jacob and laid waste his habitation.

Arminian

For they have devour Jacob and laid waste his habitation. Shared: reason for the plea—Jacob eaten, dwelling wasted.

Arminian pastoral: name the devouring as ground of appeal. The habitation laid waste is covenant people under assault.

Reformed

Calvin: they arraign the cruelty of enemies who have eaten up Jacob and made his dwelling desolate. The argument presses how inconsistent it would be if God afflicted those who call on his name and spared heathen who despise him—yet judgment may begin at the house of God.

Reformed pastoral: shared—Jacob devour, habitation waste. Plead without prescribing God's timetable.

Psalms 79:8

8Do not remember against us our former iniquities; let your compassion come speedily to meet us, for we are brought very low.

Arminian

Do not remember against us our former iniquities; let your compassion come speedily to meet us, for we are brought very low. Wesley: Prevent—prevent our utter extirpation. Shared: confession of old sins; mercy must meet the low before ruin is total.

Arminian pastoral: ask that tender mercies arrive first—prevent means get ahead of destruction. Former iniquities are owned; compassion is begged as the only path out.

Reformed

Calvin: they confirm they justly deserved chastisement; relief comes only by reconciliation. Former iniquities may mean long continued stubbornness with the fathers—not one or two recent faults. Make haste contrasts with ages of sin; compassions must speedily prevent them.

Reformed pastoral: shared—do not remember former iniquities; compassion meet us. Low estate is confession, not excuse.

Psalms 79:9

9Help us, O God of our salvation, for the glory of your name; deliver us, and atone for our sins, for your name's sake!

Arminian

Help us, O God of our salvation, for the glory of your name; deliver us, and atone for our sins, for your name's sake! Shared: help, deliverance, and atonement sought for the name's glory—not self-credit.

Arminian pastoral: salvation's God is addressed; purge of sins is joined to deliverance. Plead the name; bring no bargain of merit.

Reformed

Calvin: afflictions traced to anger; comfort only if God is reconciled. God of our salvation; they bring nothing of their own—only his glory. Sinners are reconciled by free, unmerited forgiveness. Chief concern is God pacified, not mere relief of symptoms. Be merciful/purge (kpr) points toward cleansing and sacrifice—without shedding of blood, no remission.

Reformed pastoral: shared—help and atone for the name's sake. Grace is the source; the name is the plea.

Psalms 79:10

10Why should the nations say, “Where is their God?” Let the avenging of the outpoured blood of your servants be known among the nations before our eyes!

Arminian

Why should the nations say, "Where is their God?" Let the avenging of the outpoured blood of your servants be known among the nations before our eyes! Shared: God's honor and servants' blood stand together in the plea.

Arminian pastoral: the taunt "Where is their God?" must be answered by God himself. Desire visible vindication of shed blood without craving cruelty for its own sake.

Reformed

Calvin: the name is urged so sacred honor not be exposed to blasphemy. Prayer joins concern for salvation with zeal for God's glory. This form is not for all men driven by passion, but under the Spirit for the Church's cause. They do not desire to be glutted with bloodshed; they want confirmation that God avenges wrongs to his people. Servants may be justly chastened yet not cast off—their blood still precious.

Reformed pastoral: shared—silence the taunt; avenge servants' blood. Pure zeal, not personal bloodlust.

Psalms 79:11

11Let the groans of the prisoners come before you; according to your great power, preserve those doomed to die!

Arminian

Let the groans of the prisoners come before you; according to your great power, preserve those doomed to die! Wesley: The prisoner—of thy poor people now in captivity. Shared: captive groan and death-sentence need great power to reverse.

Arminian pastoral: bring the sighing of the captive before God. Preserve the doomed by arm-strength that matches the low of v. 8.

Reformed

Calvin: the people were so shut up that to stir was death; children of death means appointed or condemned. Greatness of arm—signal interposition—is implored; without extraordinary help, restoration is hopeless. Proud spirits once vaunting are now broken.

Reformed pastoral: shared—prisoners' groans; preserve the doomed. Great power answers deep captivity.

Psalms 79:12

12Return sevenfold into the lap of our neighbors the taunts with which they have taunted you, O Lord!

Arminian

Return sevenfold into the lap of our neighbors the taunts with which they have taunted you, O Lord! Shared: the taunt was against the Lord; sevenfold return is asked into their bosom.

Arminian pastoral: measure the offense as blasphemy against God, not only insult to us. Sevenfold is fullness of just recompense prayed, not a private feud scored in flesh.

Reformed

Calvin: the faithful show they are moved less by personal injuries than inflamed with holy zeal when God's name is blasphemed. If this affection reigns, it moderates fleshly ungovernableness; with the Spirit's wisdom, prayers accord with just judgment.

Reformed pastoral: shared—sevenfold return of taunts aimed at Jehovah. Fair hearing of imprecation: both camps refuse carnal revenge and own the prayer as zeal for the blasphemed name.

Psalms 79:13

13But we your people, the sheep of your pasture, will give thanks to you forever; from generation to generation we will recount your praise.

Arminian

But we your people, the sheep of your pasture, will give thanks to you forever; from generation to generation we will recount your praise. Shared close: identity as people and sheep issues in endless thanks and recounted praise.

Arminian pastoral: vow of praise is the fruit of hoped deliverance. Teach the next generation the Lord's praise—do not end the lament without the sheep's song.

Reformed

Calvin: fruit of deliverance is that God's name be celebrated; preservation is not desired for any other end. Grateful acknowledgement will not be brief but transmitted from age to age. Thy people and sheep of thy pasture—if that people were destroyed, memory of the name would perish.

Reformed pastoral: shared—forever thanks; praise to all generations. The sheep live to show forth his praise.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on defiled temple and ruined Jerusalem, the cry how long, wrath poured on nations that devour Jacob, mercy meeting the low, blood avenged, prisoners preserved, sevenfold return of taunts, and everlasting thanks from the sheep of his pasture—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 79:1–13.

Arminian Notable voices

“Prevent—Prevent our utter extirpation. The prisoner—Of thy poor people now in captivity. The psalm sets the heathen's invasion of God's inheritance, the temple defiled, Jerusalem heaped, servants' bodies given to birds and beasts, blood shed like water with none to bury, and Israel a scorn to neighbors. How long will anger burn? Pour out wrath on nations that know thee not—they have devour Jacob. Remember not former iniquities; let tender mercies speedily prevent us, for we are brought very low. Help for the glory of thy name; purge away our sins. Let the heathen not say, Where is their God? Let the sighing of the captive come before thee; preserve those appointed to die; render sevenfold the reproach aimed at thee. Then we thy people and sheep of thy pasture will give thanks forever.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament, Psalm 79

Reformed Notable voices

“The heathen have come into God's inheritance; the temple defiled and Jerusalem laid in heaps seemed to invert the order of nature. Cruelty is marked word by word: saints murdered, bodies exposed to birds and beasts, blood shed like water, none daring to bury. Reproach from neighbors redounds against God's name. How long and forever mark prolonged wrath; the faithful confess calamity from his anger and seek his hand. Pour out fury on nations that know him not—not fleshly revenge, but zeal for the Church's welfare under the Spirit, waiting for God to separate the reprobate. Remember not former iniquities; compassions prevent us. Help, O God of our salvation, for the glory of thy name; deliver and purge sins—free forgiveness, not merit. Why should the heathen say, Where is their God? Let vengeance of servants' blood be known—precious life avenged, not thirst for bloodshed. Sighing of the prisoner; preserve children of death by the greatness of thy arm. Render sevenfold reproach aimed at Jehovah. We thy people and sheep of thy pasture will confess forever.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms, Psalm 79
