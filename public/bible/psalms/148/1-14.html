Passage

Psalms 148

Verses 1–14 · WEB · Voices

Cosmic Hallelujah in the final doxologies: praise the LORD from the heavens—angels, hosts, sun, moon, shining stars, highest heavens, and waters above—for He commanded and they were created, established by a decree that shall not pass. From the earth: great sea creatures, deeps, fire, hail, snow, clouds, stormy wind, fulfilling his word, mountains, trees, beasts, birds; then kings, peoples, princes, young and old. His name alone is exalted; He has raised the horn of his people, the praise of all his saints, Israel near to Him.

Psalms 148:1

1Praise the LORD! Praise the LORD from the heavens! Praise him in the heights!

Arminian

Praise the LORD! Clarke: the most sublime hymn in the book; min hashshamayim—all that belongs to the heavens.

Shared doxology opener: both camps join the heights. Wesleyans hear a universe meant to show forth the Creator’s goodness.

Reformed

Calvin: he seems to include stars as well as angels, heaven, air, and all gendered in it. The call starts above so earth may learn.

The psalm summons a choir from the whole cosmos. Praise the LORD from the heavens; in the heights.

Psalms 148:2

2Praise him, all his angels! Praise him, all his army!

Arminian

Praise him, all his angels! Praise him, all his army! Angels lead the litany; hosts stand ready.

No dispute here: angelic praise is not in doubt. Arminian readers do not reduce angels to metaphor in order to keep the doxology soft.

Reformed

Calvin: angels created to be instant in this service are placed first; David joins their concert rather than scolding their zeal. Hosts: they stand ready to receive orders.

The angels and heavenly armies are called to praise. Both affirm: angels and hosts praise Him.

Psalms 148:3

3Praise him, sun and moon! Praise him, all you shining stars!

Arminian

Clarke: every work partakes of His perfections—study it and wisdom, power, and goodness appear. Stars of light: the brightest; perhaps planets.

Inanimate nature as witness—creation shows the Maker.

Reformed

Calvin: not Plato’s intelligent stars; glory is seen as if they sang. Tacit reproof of human ingratitude—deafness to the symphony.

Sun and moon as audible praise to attentive ears. Where they meet: sun, moon, shining stars.

Psalms 148:4

4Praise him, you heavens of heavens, you waters that are above the heavens.

Arminian

Praise him, you heavens of heavens, you waters that are above the heavens! Clarke: heavens exceeding heavens—systems of systems; waters as in Genesis 1:7—clouds, vapors, rain, snow, meteors.

Both traditions hear: expanse and upper waters. Both camps praise the Architect of layered skies.

Reformed

Calvin: heavens of heavens—spheres in order, not confusion; waters above point to rain’s descent, popular speech not idle philosophy of a sea upstairs.

Ordered spheres. Common reading: highest heavens; waters above.

Psalms 148:5

5Let them praise the LORD’s name, for he commanded, and they were created.

Arminian

Let them praise the LORD’s name, for he commanded, and they were created. Clarke: He spoke the idea in His infinite mind; they sprang into being according to that idea.

Both confess: creation by command. Arminian interpreters refuse a world that made itself; praise answers speech that made.

Reformed

Calvin: the world is not eternal nor a concourse of atoms, but sprang forth upon God’s commandment. He Himself created—emphatic against chance.

Creation came into being by God’s command. He commanded; they were created.

Psalms 148:6

6He has also established them forever and ever. He has made a decree which will not pass away.

Arminian

And He has also established them forever and ever. He has made a decree which will not pass away. Clarke: revolutions so exact they never passed the line of His decree nor intercepted each other.

Either reading: stable decree—the ordinance holds because He holds it.

Reformed

Calvin: many grant creation yet fancy nature now stands of itself with God idle; the Psalmist insists the heavens still move at His disposal.

God continues to govern what he made; this is no distant deism. Established forever; decree that shall not pass.

Psalms 148:7

7Praise the LORD from the earth, you great sea creatures, and all depths,

Arminian

Praise the LORD from the earth, you great sea creatures, and all depths. Clarke: second half—earth and all belonging to it; tanninim—whales and sea-monsters; deeps—seas, tides, eddies.

Both sides agree: ocean praise. Both camps let the deeps join without allegory-as-theology-fork.

Reformed

Calvin: now the lower world; whales and abysses—matter for praising God from the sea fraught with wonders.

Earth’s choir begins in the deep. Both traditions see: great sea creatures; all deeps.

Psalms 148:8

8lightning and hail, snow and clouds, stormy wind, fulfilling his word,

Arminian

Lightning and hail, snow and clouds, stormy wind, fulfilling his word! Clarke: meteors of water and fire; air from breeze to tornado—each accomplishing an especial purpose and particular will.

Both note: stormy wind obeys. Wesleyan Arminians hear providence in weather without denying second causes.

Reformed

Calvin: lightning, snow, ice, storms fulfill His word—not chance; whether mercy by rain or punishment by hail, all depend on secret will. Nothing without present disposal.

Elements as messengers. Fire, hail, snow, mist; wind fulfilling His word.

Psalms 148:9

9mountains and all hills, fruit trees and all cedars,

Arminian

Clarke: mountains with mines and minerals; fruit trees and forest cedars—hue, savor, structure proclaim wisdom and goodness.

Both traditions hear the hills and trees summoned to praise. Both camps let hills and trees glorify without forcing a genre dispute.

Reformed

Calvin: mountains, hills, fruitful tree, cedars—wherever we turn, evidences of power.

Terrestrial witnesses. Mountains, hills, fruit trees, cedars.

Psalms 148:10

10wild animals and all livestock, small creatures and flying birds,

Arminian

Clarke: wild and domestic; reptiles great and microscopic; fowls whose flight is mathematical wonder.

The whole living earth is called to praise. Wesleyan Arminians see lavish wisdom in creatures as call to praise, not to pride.

Reformed

Calvin: wild beasts, cattle, reptile, winged fowl—the catalog presses every eye toward the Maker.

No creature left silent. Common confession: beasts, livestock, creeping things, flying birds.

Psalms 148:11

11kings of the earth and all peoples, princes and all judges of the earth,

Arminian

Clarke: kings as representatives; princes and judges—praise God from whom power derives; people thank Him for civil order.

Rulers must praise. What comes through is authority under God, not autonomy.

Reformed

Calvin: kings blinded by station think the world made for them; he calls them first and thrice names princes because they are slow and need urging.

Greater obligation for the high. Both readings hold: kings, peoples, princes, rulers.

Psalms 148:12

12both young men and maidens, old men and children.

Arminian

Clarke: bloom of youth and vigor; old and children—beginning and end of life—both show His sustaining goodness.

Every age and both sexes are called to praise. Both camps refuse an age-exempt choir.

Reformed

Calvin: division by age and sex—all without exception created for this end. Old men: longer life, more exercise in praise; young: acknowledge mercy in vigor; maidens also—emphatic inclusion.

No group is left silent. Both agree: young men, maidens, old men, children.

Psalms 148:13

13Let them praise the LORD’s name, for his name alone is exalted. His glory is above the earth and the heavens.

Arminian

Let them praise the LORD’s name, for his name alone is exalted. His glory is above the earth and heaven. Clarke: He excels all beings; glory over earth and heaven—all space shows manifold wisdom.

Shared climax before the people-clause: name alone exalted. Both camps worship; no rival name shares the throne.

Reformed

Calvin: let them praise—exalted is His name only; His praise above earth and heavens.

The LORD’s name alone is to be exalted. Name alone exalted; majesty above earth and heaven.

Psalms 148:14

14He has lifted up the horn of his people, the praise of all his saints, even of the children of Israel, a people near to him. Praise the LORD!

Arminian

He has lifted up the horn of his people, the praise of all his saints, even of the children of Israel, a people near to him. Praise the LORD! Clarke: horn—power and authority; near—those who know Him and approach as He prescribed. Universal chorus ends with Israel’s nearness.

Shared close: both camps praise the horn and the near people. Classical Arminians treasure nearness as covenant grace that summons praise.

Reformed

Calvin: horn means strength or dignity—Church protected by divine hand. Nearness from preferring Abraham’s line by mere love, not native worth. Hallelujah.

God draws near to his people in grace. Shared with Clarke: horn raised; saints’ praise; Israel near; Praise the LORD!

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What earlier interpreters on each side wrote about Psalm 148:1–14.

Arminian Notable voices

“As a hymn of praise, this is the most sublime in the whole book. Praise ye the Lord from the heavens—whatever belongs to the heavens, all their inhabitants. Every work of God’s hand partakes so much of his perfections that it requires only to be studied and known to show forth the manifold wisdom, power, and goodness of the Creator. He commanded, and they were created: he spake the word expressive of the idea in his infinite mind, and they sprang into being. He hath also stablished them—determined their revolutions so exactly that they have never passed the line marked out by his decree. He also exalteth the horn of his people: raises to power and authority his people. A people near unto him—the only people who know him and make their approaches unto him as he has prescribed. O what a hymn of praise is here! It is a universal chorus!”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 148)

Reformed Notable voices

“Praise Jehovah from the heavens. With regard to the angels, created that they might be instant in this religious service, we need not wonder they are placed first. David would testify that it was the height of his happiness to join in sacred concert with elect angels. Praise him, sun and moon: the glory of God is everywhere to be seen, as if they sang his praises with audible voice; here he tacitly reproves the ingratitude of man. Let them praise the name of Jehovah; for he commanded, and they were created—the world is not eternal, nor made by a concourse of atoms, but suddenly sprang forth upon the commandment of God. He established them for ever; he hath set for them a decree—not that God sits idle while nature stands of itself, but the works above still move at his disposal. Kings of the earth: by thrice specifying princes he suggests they are slow to praise and need urging. He hath exalted the horn of his people: God’s blessing is apparent in his Church. Children of Israel, a people near unto him—nearness from gracious covenant, not from excellency; the cause is the mere love of God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 148)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English editions of the sources named.
