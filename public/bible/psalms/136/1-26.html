Psalms 136

Verses 1–26 · WEB · Voices

Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his loving kindness endures forever. The refrain answers every line: God of gods and Lord of lords; Maker of heavens, earth, and lights; Redeemer who struck Egypt’s firstborn, brought Israel out with strong hand, divided the Red Sea, led through the wilderness, struck Sihon and Og, and gave the land as heritage to Israel His servant; He remembered us in our low estate, rescued us, and gives food to every creature. Give thanks to the God of heaven, for his loving kindness endures forever.

Psalms 136:1

1Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his loving kindness endures forever.

Arminian

Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his loving kindness endures forever. Wesley opens: praise God as great and good in Himself. Goodness and enduring steadfast love are twin reasons, not rivals.

Thanks is sincerely owed because He is good. The refrain is not empty chorus—it trains the heart to confess mercy that truly endures.

Reformed

Calvin: unless assiduous in praises, we defraud Him of what is due; each benefit notes mercy so we own all as bestowed gratuitously. Reiteration presses what we barely recognize: graciousness of bounty.

Mercy principally to be glorified. Give thanks; He is good; steadfast love forever.

Key contrast Both confess thanks to the LORD who is good, for his loving kindness endures forever. Arminians, with Wesley, typically stress praise of God as truly good in Himself and mercy that sincerely endures for the thankful. Readers in Calvin’s line stress that every benefit must be owned as bestowed gratuitously—the refrain drills the graciousness of bounty we barely recognize.

Psalms 136:2

2Give thanks to the God of gods, for his loving kindness endures forever.

Arminian

Give thanks to the God of gods, for his loving kindness endures forever. Wesley: infinitely superior to all called gods—angels, princes, or idols.

Both see: no peer deity. Arminians refuse a crowded pantheon; thanksgiving goes to the one God of gods.

Reformed

Calvin: God of gods declares supreme Deity—not that comparative language implies other true deities, but men split glory and frame gods of wood and stone. Fullest perfection of deity in the one God.

Against multiplicity of gods. Common ground with Wesley: God of gods.

Psalms 136:3

3Give thanks to the Lord of lords, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

Give thanks to the Lord of lords, for his loving kindness endures forever. Dominion language matches goodness: the One over all lords still loves forever.

Lordship and steadfast love are held together. Absolute rule is not excuse to withhold thanks.

Reformed

Calvin: Lord of lords—fullest perfection of dominion in the one God (plural form stressing completeness, not many lords).

Undivided dominion. Lord of lords; refrain.

Psalms 136:4

4to him who alone does great wonders, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

To him who alone does great wonders, for his loving kindness endures forever. Wonders are not shared credits; “alone” fences off rival wonder-workers.

Undisputed: alone does great wonders. Arminian interpreters praise exclusive agency in marvels without needing a genre debate.

Reformed

Calvin: alone does great wonders—whatever is admirable is exclusively His; transferring the smallest praise is sacrilege; no vestige of divinity elsewhere to equal Him.

Alone. Great wonders under forever love.

Psalms 136:5

5to him who by understanding made the heavens, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

To him who by understanding made the heavens, for his loving kindness endures forever. Creation by understanding—skill, not accident—sits under the mercy refrain.

Wise making invites thanks. Steadfast love frames even the sky as gift, not brute backdrop.

Reformed

Calvin: wisdom displayed in the skill with which the heavens are framed—surprising discrimination in their adornment.

Intelligent making. Heavens made by understanding.

Psalms 136:6

6to him who spread out the earth above the waters, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

To him who spread out the earth above the waters, for his loving kindness endures forever. Habitable earth is mercy before redemption history is named.

No dispute here: dry land as kindness. The accent falls on common mercy that still deserves the forever refrain.

Reformed

Calvin: earth stretched above waters—habitation secured by mercy; only reason for displacing waters was infinite goodness toward human interests.

Dry land as mercy. Both affirm: earth above the waters.

Psalms 136:7

7to him who made the great lights, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

To him who made the great lights, for his loving kindness endures forever. Sun and moon enter the thanksgiving list as great lights under mercy’s banner.

Creation praise is not optional preface. Lights exist; therefore give thanks with the refrain.

Reformed

Calvin: borrows Moses’ “great lights”; Spirit uses popular language so none shelter under obscurity—teaching meant for the unlearned as well as the skilled.

Practically: clear creation praise. Great lights made.

Psalms 136:8

8the sun to rule by day, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

The sun to rule by day, for his loving kindness endures forever. Rule here is assigned service, not independent divinity.

Sun appointed for day. Both camps refuse sun-worship while thanking the Appointing LORD.

Reformed

Calvin: sun for rule by day—not independent government, but administrative power of God manifest in the distribution.

The sun rules the day by God’s appointment. Where they meet: sun to rule the day.

Psalms 136:9

9the moon and stars to rule by night, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

The moon and stars to rule by night, for his loving kindness endures forever. Night is not godless; lesser lights still serve under steadfast love.

Even night falls inside forever mercy. Fear of the dark is answered by the refrain.

Reformed

Calvin: moon and stars for night—yielding reverential homage in their shining. Distribution displays providence.

Night lights as servants. Moon and stars; refrain.

Psalms 136:10

10to him who struck down the Egyptian firstborn, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

To him who struck down the Egyptian firstborn, for his loving kindness endures forever. Redemption history begins with a severe mercy that broke Egypt’s grip.

Judgment and steadfast love are not opposites here—love to Israel endures through Egypt’s stroke.

Reformed

Calvin: Egyptians smitten in their firstborn—obstinacy broken by this last plague. Summed mercy: led forth with mighty outstretched arm.

Extraordinary power in redeeming His people. Both traditions hear: firstborn of Egypt.

Psalms 136:11

11and brought out Israel from among them, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

And brought out Israel from among them, for his loving kindness endures forever. Exodus is named as mercy’s act, not Israel’s self-extraction.

God brought them out. Classical Arminians stress deliverance in history that still calls for thanks.

Reformed

Calvin: brought out from the midst—pressed on every side, escape only by wonderful divine power.

Exodus as mercy’s work. Israel brought out.

Psalms 136:12

12with a strong hand, and with an outstretched arm, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

With a strong hand, and with an outstretched arm, for his loving kindness endures forever. Strength language blocks any small-god reading of the exit.

Outstretched arm is public power for a people. Mercy endures in the manner of the rescue, not only in its start.

Reformed

Calvin: stretched-out arm for great effort—extraordinary, not common or slight display of power in redeeming His people.

Strong hand as mercy. Common reading: strong hand and outstretched arm.

Psalms 136:13

13to him who divided the Red Sea apart, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

To him who divided the Red Sea apart, for his loving kindness endures forever. Sea divided—path where there was none—under the same refrain as creation.

Red Sea divided. The forever love is the reason the split is sung, not a footnote.

Reformed

Calvin: divisions (plural) of the sea refer to troops passing abreast in a broad pathway—not rabbinic fancies of many passages; heaps stood on both sides.

Mercy enhanced by the spacious crossing. Red Sea divided.

Psalms 136:14

14and made Israel to pass through the middle of it, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

And made Israel to pass through the middle of it, for his loving kindness endures forever. Passage is God’s making; Israel walks a road He opened.

Cooperative walking through a God-made path—agency under grace, not self-salvation.

Reformed

Calvin: made Israel pass through—mercy shown in safe transit while judgment waits for Pharaoh.

Passage as gift. Israel through the midst.

Psalms 136:15

15but overthrew Pharaoh and his army in the Red Sea, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

But overthrew Pharaoh and his army in the Red Sea, for his loving kindness endures forever. Same waters, opposite ends—mercy distinguished Israel from Pharaoh.

Both confess: overthrow as steadfast love toward the redeemed. Arminian readers refuse a soft mercy that never judges oppressors.

Reformed

Calvin: Pharaoh drowned shortly after—different issue proves no mere natural cause; conspicuous display of mercy in saving His people.

Distinguished ends in one sea. Both readings see Pharaoh overthrown in the sea.

Psalms 136:16

16to him who led his people through the wilderness, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

To him who led his people through the wilderness, for his loving kindness endures forever. Wesley’s outline: Israel’s God in the long middle, not only at the sea.

Leading through wilderness is enduring love in scarcity—forty years of refrain, not one night of wonder.

Reformed

Calvin: led through the wilderness—under this, manna, water from rock, cloud, fire, preserved raiment, and more; succession of mercies for forty years.

Wilderness leading as continued mercy. Led His people through the wilderness.

Psalms 136:17

17to him who struck great kings, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

To him who struck great kings, for his loving kindness endures forever. Conquest praise continues; great kings fall under forever love to Israel.

Great kings struck. Mercy to the people includes judgment on tyrants who blocked the heritage.

Reformed

Calvin (with the parallel history): striking great kings continues the same goodness that settled them toward the inheritance.

Kings fall for the people’s sake. Both sides agree: great kings struck down.

Psalms 136:18

18and killed mighty kings, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

And killed mighty kings, for his loving kindness endures forever. Parallel intensifies: might is no shield against the LORD’s steadfast love for His own.

Historical verbs stay historical. Praise remembers kings, deaths, mercy.

Reformed

Mighty kings killed—same refrain teaches that conquest mercies are gratuitous gifts, not wages Israel earned.

Forever love in the killing of mighty kings. Both traditions see: mighty kings killed.

Psalms 136:19

19Sihon king of the Amorites, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

Sihon king of the Amorites, for his loving kindness endures forever. Named enemy at the border—memory with a face.

Even Sihon’s defeat is sung under the refrain of God’s loving kindness. Both camps keep the story particular.

Reformed

Sihon named—particular providence in the first gatekeeper’s fall, under the chorus of enduring mercy.

Sihon, king of the Amorites; refrain.

Psalms 136:20

20Og king of Bashan, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

And Og king of Bashan, for his loving kindness endures forever. Og paired with Sihon as in Psalm 135—twin gatekeepers of the land.

Both note: Og included. Steadfast love endures in the defeat of Bashan’s king.

Reformed

Og of Bashan—paired mercy; Calvin’s larger frame: every age displays the same goodness shown to the fathers.

Og’s defeat, too, is sung with the refrain of God’s steadfast love.

Psalms 136:21

21and gave their land as an inheritance, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

And gave their land as an inheritance, for his loving kindness endures forever. Gift again—heritage language matches 135:12 under the forever chorus.

Land as gift stirs thanks and faithful tenure, not swagger.

Reformed

Land given as heritage—crowning gift under forever love; possession is mercy’s grant.

Heritage gratuitous. Common confession: land as heritage.

Psalms 136:22

22even a heritage to Israel his servant, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

A heritage to Israel his servant, for his loving kindness endures forever. Wesley: Israel spoken of as one man, united in one body in the worship of God.

Servant-peoplehood is corporate vocation. Heritage lands on a worshiping body, not isolated heroes only.

Reformed

Heritage to Israel His servant—Calvin’s psalm treats Israel as the people of peculiar mercy; servant names owned relationship.

Servant-Israel receives the land by mercy. Wesley says as much: Israel as one worshiping body.

Key contrast Both confess a heritage given to Israel His servant under forever steadfast love. Arminians, with Wesley, typically hear corporate Israel spoken of as one worshiping body united in God’s service. Reformed readers hear servant-Israel as the people of peculiar, gratuitous mercy—the land as gift of enduring love, not wages.

Psalms 136:23

23who remembered us in our low estate, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

He remembered us when we were brought low, for his loving kindness endures forever. Low estate remembered—mercy that notices humiliation.

Remembrance is active help, not bare recall. When we are low, steadfast love still endures and acts.

Reformed

Calvin: remembered us in humiliation—more notable to interpose when nearly overwhelmed than to preserve in even affairs; emergency arrests the view. Deliverances accompany remission of sins.

God remembered his people when they were brought low. He remembered us when low.

Key contrast Both confess that He remembered us in our low estate, for his loving kindness endures forever. Arminians hear active, fatherly remembrance that meets humiliation with enduring mercy. Reformed commentators hear interposition when the nation is nearly overwhelmed—deliverance as notable mercy often joined with remission of sins.

Psalms 136:24

24and has delivered us from our adversaries, for his loving kindness endures forever;

Arminian

And has delivered us from our adversaries, for his loving kindness endures forever. Rescue completes remembrance—enemies do not get the last word.

Both readings hold: rescued from foes. What comes through is deliverance that still obliges thanks.

Reformed

Calvin: rescued from oppressors—continued succession of deliverances; same goodness in every age.

Rescue as mercy, not entitlement. Rescued from foes.

Psalms 136:25

25who gives food to every creature, for his loving kindness endures forever.

Arminian

He who gives food to every creature, for his loving kindness endures forever. Wesley: food to all living creatures; we must praise for them because those most concerned often cannot or do not.

Common kindness to all flesh under the same forever refrain as exodus—widen thanks beyond Israel’s story alone.

Reformed

Calvin: food to all flesh—paternal providence to every living creature; reason from less to greater: if He cares for cattle and sparrows, how much more for His adopted children.

Common care that argues special mercy. Common ground with Wesley: food to all flesh deserves praise.

Key contrast Both confess that He gives food to every creature under the forever refrain. Arminians, with Wesley, stress praise owed for creatures who cannot or will not thank Him. Calvin’s heirs reason from less to greater: care for all flesh argues the incomparably superior mercy God shows His children.

Psalms 136:26

26Oh give thanks to the God of heaven, for his loving kindness endures forever.

Arminian

Give thanks to the God of heaven, for his loving kindness endures forever. The psalm closes as it opened: thanks, with the burden unchanged.

God of heaven receives the same forever confession. The refrain has trained the mouth; now seal it.

Reformed

Calvin’s close matches the opening: make acknowledgments to the God of heaven—supreme thanks under the same forever mercy.

God of heaven; refrain unchanged. Give thanks; steadfast love endures forever.

Voices from both camps

The Great Thanksgiving’s refrain—His mercy/steadfast love endures forever—through creation, exodus, conquest, low estate remembered, and food to all flesh: older Arminian and Reformed writers, each in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“We must praise God as great and good in himself; as the Creator of the world; as Israel's God; as our Redeemer; as God over all. The God of gods—who is infinitely superior to all that are called gods, whether angels, or princes, or idols. Israel—he speaks of all that people as of one man, because they were united together in one body in the worship of God. Food—to all living creatures. For which God deserves great praises, which the psalmist teaches us to render to God for them, because those who are most concerned, either cannot, or do not perform this duty. The burden returns at every verse: for his mercy endureth for ever.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 136)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Psalmist reminds the Lord’s people that unless they were assiduous in his praises, they were chargeable with defrauding him of what was justly due for his benefits. In mentioning each benefit he takes particular notice of the mercy of God, to teach us that we must own everything we receive from him to be bestowed gratuitously. The reiteration may seem vain, but chorus-verses are allowed even among poets—and here the best reasons appear: men may not deny divine goodness as the fountain of blessings, yet the graciousness of his bounty is far from being fully and sincerely recognised. While God should be praised for all his works, it is his mercy principally that we should glorify. It was customary for the Levites to sing by response, “for his mercy endureth for ever.” Who alone hath done great wonders: whatever is worthy of admiration is exclusively made and done by God—we cannot transfer the smallest portion of praise without sacrilege. He remembered us in our humiliation and rescued us; he gives food to all flesh—reasoning from the less to the greater for the incomparably superior mercy shown to his children.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 136)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English editions of the sources named.
