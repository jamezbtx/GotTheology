Psalms 136

Verses 1–26 · ESV · Voices

Give thanks to the LORD, for He is good—for His steadfast love endures forever. The refrain answers every line: God of gods and Lord of lords; Maker of heavens, earth, and lights; Redeemer who struck Egypt’s firstborn, brought Israel out with strong hand, divided the Red Sea, led through the wilderness, struck Sihon and Og, and gave the land as heritage to Israel His servant; He remembered us in our low estate, rescued us, and gives food to all flesh. Give thanks to the God of heaven—for His steadfast love endures forever.

Psalms 136:1

1Give thanks to the LORD, for he is good, for his steadfast love endures forever.

Arminian

Give thanks to the LORD, for He is good—for his steadfast love endures forever. Wesley opens: praise God as great and good in Himself. Goodness and enduring steadfast love are twin reasons, not rivals.

Arminian hearing: thanks is sincerely owed because He is good. The refrain is not empty chorus—it trains the heart to confess mercy that truly endures.

Reformed

Calvin: unless assiduous in praises, we defraud Him of what is due; each benefit notes mercy so we own all as bestowed gratuitously. Reiteration presses what we barely recognize: graciousness of bounty.

Reformed accent: mercy principally to be glorified. Shared: give thanks; He is good; steadfast love forever.

Key contrast Both confess thanks to the LORD who is good, for His steadfast love endures forever. Arminians, with Wesley, typically stress praise of God as truly good in Himself and mercy that sincerely endures for the thankful. Reformed readers, with Calvin, typically stress that every benefit must be owned as bestowed gratuitously—the refrain drills the graciousness of bounty we barely recognize.

Psalms 136:2

2Give thanks to the God of gods, for his steadfast love endures forever.

Arminian

Give thanks to the God of gods—for his steadfast love endures forever. Wesley: infinitely superior to all called gods—angels, princes, or idols.

Both camps: no peer deity. Classical Arminians refuse a crowded pantheon; thanksgiving goes to the one God of gods.

Reformed

Calvin: God of gods declares supreme Deity—not that comparative language implies other true deities, but men split glory and frame gods of wood and stone. Fullest perfection of deity in the one God.

Reformed confession: against multiplicity of gods. Shared with Wesley: God of gods.

Psalms 136:3

3Give thanks to the Lord of lords, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

Give thanks to the Lord of lords—for his steadfast love endures forever. Dominion language matches goodness: the One over all lords still loves forever.

Arminian pastoral: Lordship and steadfast love are held together. Absolute rule is not excuse to withhold thanks.

Reformed

Calvin: Lord of lords—fullest perfection of dominion in the one God (plural form stressing completeness, not many lords).

Reformed accent: undivided dominion. Shared: Lord of lords; refrain.

Psalms 136:4

4to him who alone does great wonders, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

To Him who alone does great wonders—for his steadfast love endures forever. Wonders are not shared credits; “alone” fences off rival wonder-workers.

Shared: alone does great wonders. Classical Arminians praise exclusive agency in marvels without needing a genre debate.

Reformed

Calvin: alone does great wonders—whatever is admirable is exclusively His; transferring the smallest praise is sacrilege; no vestige of divinity elsewhere to equal Him.

Reformed warning: alone. Shared: great wonders under forever love.

Psalms 136:5

5to him who by understanding made the heavens, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

To Him who by understanding made the heavens—for his steadfast love endures forever. Creation by understanding—skill, not accident—sits under the mercy refrain.

Arminian accent: wise making invites thanks. Steadfast love frames even the sky as gift, not brute backdrop.

Reformed

Calvin: wisdom displayed in the skill with which the heavens are framed—surprising discrimination in their adornment.

Reformed accent: intelligent making. Shared: heavens made by understanding.

Psalms 136:6

6to him who spread out the earth above the waters, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

To Him who spread out the earth above the waters—for his steadfast love endures forever. Habitable earth is mercy before redemption history is named.

Both camps: dry land as kindness. Classical Arminians hear common mercy that still deserves the forever refrain.

Reformed

Calvin: earth stretched above waters—habitation secured by mercy; only reason for displacing waters was infinite goodness toward human interests.

Reformed comfort: dry land as mercy. Shared: earth above the waters.

Psalms 136:7

7to him who made the great lights, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

To Him who made the great lights—for his steadfast love endures forever. Sun and moon enter the thanksgiving list as great lights under mercy’s banner.

Arminian pastoral: creation praise is not optional preface. Lights exist; therefore give thanks with the refrain.

Reformed

Calvin: borrows Moses’ “great lights”; Spirit uses popular language so none shelter under obscurity—teaching meant for the unlearned as well as the skilled.

Reformed pastoral: clear creation praise. Shared: great lights made.

Psalms 136:8

8the sun to rule over the day, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

The sun to rule over the day—for his steadfast love endures forever. Rule here is assigned service, not independent divinity.

Shared: sun appointed for day. Both camps refuse sun-worship while thanking the Appointing LORD.

Reformed

Calvin: sun for rule by day—not independent government, but administrative power of God manifest in the distribution.

Reformed accent: assigned rule under God. Shared: sun to rule the day.

Psalms 136:9

9the moon and stars to rule over the night, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

The moon and stars to rule over the night—for his steadfast love endures forever. Night is not godless; lesser lights still serve under steadfast love.

Arminian comfort: even night falls inside forever mercy. Fear of the dark is answered by the refrain.

Reformed

Calvin: moon and stars for night—yielding reverential homage in their shining. Distribution displays providence.

Reformed accent: night lights as servants. Shared: moon and stars; refrain.

Psalms 136:10

10to him who struck down the firstborn of Egypt, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

To Him who struck down the firstborn of Egypt—for his steadfast love endures forever. Redemption history begins with a severe mercy that broke Egypt’s grip.

Arminian reading: judgment and steadfast love are not opposites here—love to Israel endures through Egypt’s stroke.

Reformed

Calvin: Egyptians smitten in their firstborn—obstinacy broken by this last plague. Summed mercy: led forth with mighty outstretched arm.

Reformed accent: extraordinary power in redeeming His people. Shared: firstborn of Egypt.

Psalms 136:11

11and brought Israel out from among them, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

And brought Israel out from among them—for his steadfast love endures forever. Exodus is named as mercy’s act, not Israel’s self-extraction.

Both camps: God brought them out. Classical Arminians stress real deliverance in history that still calls for thanks.

Reformed

Calvin: brought out from the midst—pressed on every side, escape only by wonderful divine power.

Reformed confession: exodus as mercy’s work. Shared: Israel brought out.

Psalms 136:12

12with a strong hand and an outstretched arm, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

With a strong hand and an outstretched arm—for his steadfast love endures forever. Strength language blocks any small-god reading of the exit.

Arminian pastoral: outstretched arm is public power for a people. Mercy endures in the manner of the rescue, not only in its start.

Reformed

Calvin: stretched-out arm for great effort—extraordinary, not common or slight display of power in redeeming His people.

Reformed accent: strong hand as mercy. Shared: strong hand and outstretched arm.

Psalms 136:13

13to him who divided the Red Sea in two, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

To Him who divided the Red Sea in two—for his steadfast love endures forever. Sea divided—path where there was none—under the same refrain as creation.

Shared: Red Sea divided. The forever love is the reason the split is sung, not a footnote.

Reformed

Calvin: divisions (plural) of the sea refer to troops passing abreast in a broad pathway—not rabbinic fancies of many passages; heaps stood on both sides.

Reformed accent: mercy enhanced by the spacious crossing. Shared: Red Sea divided.

Psalms 136:14

14and made Israel pass through the midst of it, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

And made Israel pass through the midst of it—for his steadfast love endures forever. Passage is God’s making; Israel walks a road He opened.

Arminian accent: cooperative walking through a God-made path—agency under grace, not self-salvation.

Reformed

Calvin: made Israel pass through—mercy shown in safe transit while judgment waits for Pharaoh.

Reformed pastoral: passage as gift. Shared: Israel through the midst.

Psalms 136:15

15but overthrew Pharaoh and his host in the Red Sea, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

But overthrew Pharaoh and his host in the Red Sea—for his steadfast love endures forever. Same waters, opposite ends—mercy distinguished Israel from Pharaoh.

Both camps: overthrow as steadfast love toward the redeemed. Classical Arminians refuse a soft mercy that never judges oppressors.

Reformed

Calvin: Pharaoh drowned shortly after—different issue proves no mere natural cause; conspicuous display of mercy in saving His people.

Reformed accent: distinguished ends in one sea. Shared: Pharaoh overthrown.

Psalms 136:16

16to him who led his people through the wilderness, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

To Him who led His people through the wilderness—for his steadfast love endures forever. Wesley’s outline: Israel’s God in the long middle, not only at the sea.

Arminian pastoral: leading through wilderness is enduring love in scarcity—forty years of refrain, not one night of wonder.

Reformed

Calvin: led through the wilderness—under this, manna, water from rock, cloud, fire, preserved raiment, and more; succession of mercies for forty years.

Reformed comfort: wilderness leading as continued mercy. Shared: led His people through the wilderness.

Psalms 136:17

17to him who struck down great kings, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

To Him who struck down great kings—for his steadfast love endures forever. Conquest praise continues; great kings fall under forever love to Israel.

Shared: great kings struck. Mercy to the people includes judgment on tyrants who blocked the heritage.

Reformed

Calvin (with the parallel history): striking great kings continues the same goodness that settled them toward the inheritance.

Reformed accent: kings fall for the people’s sake. Shared: great kings struck down.

Psalms 136:18

18and killed mighty kings, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

And killed mighty kings—for his steadfast love endures forever. Parallel intensifies: might is no shield against the LORD’s steadfast love for His own.

Arminian hearing: historical verbs stay historical. Praise remembers real kings, real deaths, real mercy.

Reformed

Mighty kings killed—same refrain teaches that conquest mercies are gratuitous gifts, not wages Israel earned.

Reformed confession: forever love in the killing of mighty kings. Shared: mighty kings killed.

Psalms 136:19

19Sihon, king of the Amorites, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

Sihon, king of the Amorites—for his steadfast love endures forever. Named enemy at the border—memory with a face.

Shared: Sihon under the refrain. Both camps keep the story particular.

Reformed

Sihon named—particular providence in the first gatekeeper’s fall, under the chorus of enduring mercy.

Shared: Sihon, king of the Amorites; refrain.

Psalms 136:20

20and Og, king of Bashan, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

And Og, king of Bashan—for his steadfast love endures forever. Og paired with Sihon as in Psalm 135—twin gatekeepers of the land.

Both camps: Og included. Steadfast love endures in the defeat of Bashan’s king.

Reformed

Og of Bashan—paired mercy; Calvin’s larger frame: every age displays the same goodness shown to the fathers.

Shared: Og; steadfast love forever.

Psalms 136:21

21and gave their land as a heritage, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

And gave their land as a heritage—for his steadfast love endures forever. Gift again—heritage language matches 135:12 under the forever chorus.

Arminian comfort: land as gift stirs thanks and faithful tenure, not swagger.

Reformed

Land given as heritage—crowning gift under forever love; possession is mercy’s grant.

Reformed accent: heritage gratuitous. Shared: land as heritage.

Psalms 136:22

22a heritage to Israel his servant, for his steadfast love endures forever.

Arminian

A heritage to Israel His servant—for his steadfast love endures forever. Wesley: Israel spoken of as one man, united in one body in the worship of God.

Arminian reading: servant-peoplehood is corporate vocation. Heritage lands on a worshiping body, not isolated heroes only.

Reformed

Heritage to Israel His servant—Calvin’s psalm treats Israel as the people of peculiar mercy; servant names owned relationship.

Reformed confession: servant-Israel receives the land by mercy. Shared with Wesley: Israel as one worshiping body.

Key contrast Both confess a heritage given to Israel His servant under forever steadfast love. Arminians, with Wesley, typically hear corporate Israel spoken of as one worshiping body united in God’s service. Reformed readers typically hear servant-Israel as the people of peculiar, gratuitous mercy—the land as gift of enduring love, not wages.

Psalms 136:23

23It is he who remembered us in our low estate, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

It is He who remembered us in our low estate—for his steadfast love endures forever. Low estate remembered—mercy that notices humiliation.

Arminian pastoral: remembrance is active help, not bare recall. When we are low, steadfast love still endures and acts.

Reformed

Calvin: remembered us in humiliation—more notable to interpose when nearly overwhelmed than to preserve in even affairs; emergency arrests the view. Deliverances accompany remission of sins.

Reformed comfort: memory in low estate. Shared: He remembered us when low.

Key contrast Both confess that He remembered us in our low estate, for His steadfast love endures forever. Arminians typically hear active, fatherly remembrance that meets real humiliation with enduring mercy. Reformed readers, with Calvin, typically hear interposition when the nation is nearly overwhelmed—deliverance as notable mercy often joined with remission of sins.

Psalms 136:24

24and rescued us from our foes, for his steadfast love endures forever;

Arminian

And rescued us from our foes—for his steadfast love endures forever. Rescue completes remembrance—enemies do not get the last word.

Both camps: rescued from foes. Classical Arminians hear real deliverance that still obliges thanks.

Reformed

Calvin: rescued from oppressors—continued succession of deliverances; same goodness in every age.

Reformed accent: rescue as mercy, not entitlement. Shared: rescued from foes.

Psalms 136:25

25he who gives food to all flesh, for his steadfast love endures forever.

Arminian

He who gives food to all flesh—for his steadfast love endures forever. Wesley: food to all living creatures; we must praise for them because those most concerned often cannot or do not.

Arminian accent: common kindness to all flesh under the same forever refrain as exodus—widen thanks beyond Israel’s story alone.

Reformed

Calvin: food to all flesh—paternal providence to every living creature; reason from less to greater: if He cares for cattle and sparrows, how much more for His adopted children.

Reformed accent: common care that argues special mercy. Shared with Wesley: food to all flesh deserves praise.

Key contrast Both confess that He gives food to all flesh under the forever refrain. Arminians, with Wesley, stress praise owed for creatures who cannot or will not thank Him. Reformed readers, with Calvin, reason from less to greater: care for all flesh argues the incomparably superior mercy God shows His children.

Psalms 136:26

26Give thanks to the God of heaven, for his steadfast love endures forever.

Arminian

Give thanks to the God of heaven—for his steadfast love endures forever. The psalm closes as it opened: thanks, with the burden unchanged.

Arminian close: God of heaven receives the same forever confession. The refrain has trained the mouth; now seal it.

Reformed

Calvin’s close matches the opening: make acknowledgments to the God of heaven—supreme thanks under the same forever mercy.

Reformed close: God of heaven; refrain unchanged. Shared: Give thanks; steadfast love endures forever.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the Great Thanksgiving’s refrain—His mercy/steadfast love endures forever—through creation, exodus, conquest, low estate remembered, and food to all flesh—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 136:1–26.

Arminian Notable voices

“We must praise God as great and good in himself; as the Creator of the world; as Israel's God; as our Redeemer; as God over all. The God of gods—who is infinitely superior to all that are called gods, whether angels, or princes, or idols. Israel—he speaks of all that people as of one man, because they were united together in one body in the worship of God. Food—to all living creatures. For which God deserves great praises, which the psalmist teaches us to render to God for them, because those who are most concerned, either cannot, or do not perform this duty. The burden returns at every verse: for his mercy endureth for ever.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 136)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Psalmist reminds the Lord’s people that unless they were assiduous in his praises, they were chargeable with defrauding him of what was justly due for his benefits. In mentioning each benefit he takes particular notice of the mercy of God, to teach us that we must own everything we receive from him to be bestowed gratuitously. The reiteration may seem vain, but chorus-verses are allowed even among poets—and here the best reasons appear: men may not deny divine goodness as the fountain of blessings, yet the graciousness of his bounty is far from being fully and sincerely recognised. While God should be praised for all his works, it is his mercy principally that we should glorify. It was customary for the Levites to sing by response, “for his mercy endureth for ever.” Who alone hath done great wonders: whatever is worthy of admiration is exclusively made and done by God—we cannot transfer the smallest portion of praise without sacrilege. He remembered us in our humiliation and rescued us; he gives food to all flesh—reasoning from the less to the greater for the incomparably superior mercy shown to his children.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 136)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English editions of the sources named.
