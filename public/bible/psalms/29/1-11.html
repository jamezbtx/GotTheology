Passage

Psalms 29

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

Of David. The psalmist summons heavenly beings to ascribe to the LORD glory and strength; to ascribe the glory due his name and worship him in the splendor of holiness; then hears the voice of the LORD over the waters as the God of glory thunders; names that voice powerful and full of majesty; watches it break Lebanon's cedars, make Lebanon and Sirion skip, flash forth flames of fire, and shake the wilderness of Kadesh; sees it make the deer give birth and strip the forests bare while in his temple all cry, "Glory!"; confesses the LORD enthroned over the flood and as king forever; and prays that the LORD give strength to his people and bless them with peace.

Psalms 29:1

1Ascribe to the LORD, O heavenly beings, ascribe to the LORD glory and strength.

Arminian

Ascribe to the LORD, O heavenly beings, ascribe to the LORD glory and strength. Wesley: Ye—Ye potentates and rulers of the earth. Glory—By an humble and thankful acknowledgment of it. The doubled call presses great ones to give God what is already his.

Arminian pastoral: shared piety—glory and strength belong to the LORD alone; the mighty must confess them, not keep them as private stock. Humble and thankful acknowledgment is the fitting answer when the storm displays who reigns.

Reformed

Calvin: it is more difficult to reduce great men to order, so he addresses the sons of the mighty—princes who abuse lofty station and arrogate divine prerogatives. Give strength unto Jehovah: deluded imaginations think power comes from elsewhere than heaven. Lay aside haughtiness and false opinion of your own strength, and glorify God as he deserves. The repetition shows they must be vehemently urged ere proper acknowledgement is extorted.

Reformed pastoral: shared—ascribe glory and strength to the LORD, not to rank. Pride in high place is the special target; force of the summons humbles those who will not stand in awe otherwise.

Key contrast Both press the great to ascribe glory and strength to the LORD alone, not to themselves. Arminians typically hear Wesley's accent: potentates and rulers of the earth giving humble, thankful acknowledgment. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin's sharper rebuke of princes intoxicated with pride who arrogate divine prerogatives and must be compelled to glorify God as he deserves.

Psalms 29:2

2Ascribe to the LORD the glory due his name; worship the LORD in the splendor of holiness.

Arminian

Ascribe to the LORD the glory due his name; worship the LORD in the splendor of holiness. Wesley: Give—The honour which he deserves: own him as the Almighty, and the only true God. Holiness—Or, in his holy and beautiful house.

Arminian pastoral: shared—due glory means owning him as Almighty and only true God. Worship aims at his holy house, not at empty display. Give the name the honour it deserves.

Reformed

Calvin: the glory of God's name is that which is worthy of his majesty, of which the great are wont to deprive him. By the brightness of his sanctuary is meant not heaven but the tabernacle of the covenant, adorned with symbols of divine glory. The prophet names this place that all, bidding adieu to superstition, betake themselves to the pure worship of the one unchangeable God.

Reformed pastoral: shared—ascribe what is due his name; worship where he has manifested himself. Flee forged services; cling to the pure worship pledged in his sanctuary.

Psalms 29:3

3The voice of the LORD is over the waters; the God of glory thunders, the LORD, over many waters.

Arminian

The voice of the LORD is over the waters; the God of glory thunders, the LORD, over many waters. Wesley: The waters—Above in the clouds, which are called waters. The Divine power displays itself in those high places, far above the reach of all earthly potentates. Many—Upon the clouds, in which there are vast treasures of water, and upon which God is said to sit or ride.

Arminian pastoral: shared glory—the thunder-voice over the waters is the God of glory, not a rival force. Let high places above every earthly reach teach who thunders. Adore; do not explain the voice away.

Reformed

Calvin: he selects works that prove the world is governed by God's power and that awaken the torpid, dragging them humbly to adore. Not the gentle sun or soft rain, but thunders and violent tempests that smite hearts with dread. God speaks in all creatures, yet here the loud sounds rouse from lethargy those who cast off thought of God.

Reformed pastoral: shared—the God of glory thunders over many waters. Hear the voice that humbles stubborn recklessness; let storm awaken worship, not mere meteorology.

Psalms 29:4

4The voice of the LORD is powerful; the voice of the LORD is full of majesty.

Arminian

The voice of the LORD is powerful; the voice of the LORD is full of majesty. Twin lines refuse a thin god: power and majesty meet in one voice.

Arminian pastoral: shared—do not split force from glory. The same LORD who thunders is majestic; tremble and ascribe. Power without majesty would terrify without worship; majesty without power would be empty praise.

Reformed

Calvin: thrice he repeats that God's voice is heard in great and violent tempests, and adds that it is full of power and majesty. The language subdues by fear the obstinacy that will not yield otherwise.

Reformed pastoral: shared—powerful and majestic name the one voice. Let dread serve reverence; refuse a philosophy that separates the noise from the Lord who speaks.

Psalms 29:5

5The voice of the LORD breaks the cedars; the LORD breaks the cedars of Lebanon.

Arminian

The voice of the LORD breaks the cedars; the LORD breaks the cedars of Lebanon. Wesley: Lebanon—A place famous for strong and lofty cedars. What men prize as strength splits at his voice.

Arminian pastoral: shared—Lebanon's pride falls when the LORD speaks. Do not trust the tallest supports of earth. Glory still belongs to the breaker of cedars, not to the trees.

Reformed

Calvin: to subdue stubbornness he shows God terrible in every word. He rebukes the madness of the proud who swell with presumption yet hearken not to God's voice in thunders that rend the air and overthrow the loftiest trees. What a monstrous thing that irrational creation trembles while men alone are unmoved!

Reformed pastoral: shared—cedars of Lebanon name the highest earthly strength broken by the LORD. Let created height teach humility; do not weave veils of secondary causes that hide God's hand.

Psalms 29:6

6He makes Lebanon to skip like a calf, and Sirion like a young wild ox.

Arminian

He makes Lebanon to skip like a calf, and Sirion like a young wild ox. Wesley: Them—The cedars; broken by the thunder, their parts are suddenly and violently hurled hither and thither. Sirion—An high mountain beyond Jordan joining to Lebanon. Lebanon and Sirion skip by a poetical hyperbole.

Arminian pastoral: shared—mountains named as leaping show the storm's reach under God's hand. Hyperbole serves worship, not disbelief. Sirion and Lebanon move at his bidding.

Reformed

Calvin: a highly poetical figure with hyperbole—Lebanon skips like a calf, Sirion like a unicorn, one of the swiftest animals. He alludes to thunder that seems almost to shake mountains to their foundations. Philosophers who fix contemplation on nature and turn it from God weave veils lest they acknowledge his hand.

Reformed pastoral: shared—skipping mountains magnify the voice, not cancel it. Refuse a learning that stops at mediate causes and never approaches God.

Psalms 29:7

7The voice of the LORD flashes forth flames of fire.

Arminian

The voice of the LORD flashes forth flames of fire. Wesley: The flames—The lightnings. Voice and fire belong together in the theophany.

Arminian pastoral: shared glory—lightning is the LORD's flashing voice, not a random spark. Fear and wonder meet; ascribe the flames to him who speaks.

Reformed

Calvin: the Lord striketh out flames of fire when vapours, struck as with his hammer, burst forth into lightnings and thunderbolts. Proximate causes may be studied profitably if they lead to the Author of Nature; nothing is more preposterous than to be stopped by them from approaching God.

Reformed pastoral: shared—flames of fire are the LORD's voice made visible. Let shrewdness raise the heart to heaven so the voice penetrates, not merely a confused noise.

Psalms 29:8

8The voice of the LORD shakes the wilderness; the LORD shakes the wilderness of Kadesh.

Arminian

The voice of the LORD shakes the wilderness; the LORD shakes the wilderness of Kadesh. Wesley: Kadesh—An eminent wilderness, vast and terrible, and well known to the Israelites, wherein possibly they had seen some such effects of thunder.

Arminian pastoral: shared—even empty, feared places shake at his voice. Memory of Kadesh can catechize: the LORD reaches the waste as well as Lebanon. Let known deserts remind you who shakes them.

Reformed

Calvin: he names Lebanon, Hermon, and the wilderness of Kadesh because these places were best known to the Jews. Wildernesses dreadful of themselves are more so when filled with thunders, hail, and storms. The voice that makes the wilderness tremble is meant to move hearts that remain unmoved.

Reformed pastoral: shared—Kadesh shaken is localized glory, not local folklore alone. Hear the same LORD in the places your people know; let familiar geography preach his power.

Psalms 29:9

9The voice of the LORD makes the deer give birth and strips the forests bare, and in his temple all cry, "Glory!"

Arminian

The voice of the LORD makes the deer give birth and strips the forests bare, and in his temple all cry, "Glory!" Wesley: To calve—Through the terror it causes, which hastens the birth. Discovereth—Maketh bare of its trees, which it breaks or strips of their leaves. Glory—Having shewed the terrible effects of God's power in other places, he now shews the blessed privilege of God's people, that are praising God in his temple, when the rest of the world are trembling under the tokens of his displeasure.

Arminian pastoral: shared—terror outside and "Glory!" inside belong to one LORD. The temple company praises while the forests strip and the world trembles. Seek that privilege: worship, not only weather-watching.

Reformed

Calvin: it is worse than irrational that men are not moved at God's voice when it so influences wild beasts; unusual works that compel beasts should teach wisdom. In his temple every one speaketh of his praise: God's voice fills the world, yet his glory is celebrated only in his church, where he speaks intelligibly and gently allures the faithful. The doctrine of salvation alone cheers hearts and opens mouths in his praises by revealing his grace.

Reformed pastoral: shared—"Glory!" is the church's answer when creation shakes. Thunder alone leaves many deaf or fleeing; the fatherly word in his temple teaches how to praise aright.

Key contrast Both hear storm-terror outside and "Glory!" in the temple as one LORD's work—not a genre fight over theophany. Arminians typically stress Wesley's privilege contrast: God's people praising in the temple while the rest of the world trembles under tokens of displeasure. Reformed readers typically stress Calvin's further point: the world hears a terrifying voice, but true praise rises where God speaks intelligibly in his church and the doctrine of salvation opens the mouth.

Psalms 29:10

10The LORD sits enthroned over the flood; the LORD sits enthroned as king forever.

Arminian

The LORD sits enthroned over the flood; the LORD sits enthroned as king forever. Wesley: The flood—The most violent waters, which sometimes fall from the clouds upon the earth, companions of great thunders. As he sends terrible tempests and thunders, so he also restrains and over-rules them. Sitteth—He doth sit, and will sit as king for ever, sending such tempests when it pleaseth him.

Arminian pastoral: shared—enthronement over the flood means rule, not panic. The same King who sends also restrains. Forever names lasting reign; tempests do not unseat him.

Reformed

Calvin: some see allusion to the flood that drowned the world and testified that he is judge; he agrees in part but extends the sense—floods that still threaten destruction are controlled by providence so that he alone governs all things at all times. For explanation he adds: God sits King for ever.

Reformed pastoral: shared—enthroned over the flood and forever. Judgment and ongoing rule meet; when elements seem confounded, trust the King who moderates from his throne.

Key contrast Both confess the LORD enthroned over violent waters and seated as king forever. Arminians typically hear Wesley's storm-companions sense: cloud-floods with thunder, sent and yet restrained and overruled by the forever King. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin's widened frame: Noah's flood as judgment remembered, and present floods still ruled by the same providence of the King forever.

Psalms 29:11

11May the LORD give strength to his people! May the LORD bless his people with peace!

Arminian

May the LORD give strength to his people! May the LORD bless his people with peace! The psalm that began with ascribing glory ends in prayer for the people: strength and peace from the same LORD whose voice shook the earth.

Arminian pastoral: shared—care for the church crowns the theophany. Ask strength and peace for his people, not private immunity from every storm. The God of glory is also the blesser of his own.

Reformed

Calvin: though God exhibits visible power to the whole world, he exerts it peculiarly for his elect. He describes him differently here—not as one who overwhelms with dread, but as one who upholds, cherishes, and strengthens. Strength means the whole condition of life depending on grace; he blesses with peace those treated liberally so that nothing is wanting to complete happiness. Stand in awe of his majesty, yet hope from him all necessary to prosperity; his infinite power is an invincible fortress.

Reformed pastoral: shared—strength and peace for his people after the storm-voice. Awe and hope belong together; the elect are cherished by the same Lord the world only hears in thunder.

Key contrast Both end with the LORD's strength and peace for his people after the display of glory. Arminians typically hear the pastoral turn Wesley's outline names: dominion over the world joined to care over the church—pray strength and peace for the people who worship while storms rage. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin's election-and-grace accent: power shown to all, yet peculiarly for the elect; strength as life wholly dependent on grace; peace as liberal blessing; awe held together with hope in an invincible fortress.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on ascribing glory to the LORD, the thunder-voice that shakes cedars and wilderness, the temple cry of glory, the King enthroned over the flood, and strength and peace for his people—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 29:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“It is probable David wrote this psalm during a storm of thunder, lightning, and rain. He calls on the great to give glory to God; observes his power in thunder and lightning; his dominion over the world and care over the church. Ye—Ye potentates and rulers of the earth. Glory—By an humble and thankful acknowledgment of it. Give—The honour which he deserves: own him as the Almighty, and the only true God. Holiness—Or, in his holy and beautiful house. The waters—Above in the clouds; the Divine power displays itself in those high places, far above the reach of all earthly potentates. Glory—Having shewed the terrible effects of God's power in other places, he now shews the blessed privilege of God's people, that are praising God in his temple, when the rest of the world are trembling under the tokens of his displeasure. The flood—The most violent waters; as he sends terrible tempests and thunders, so he also restrains and over-rules them. Sitteth—He doth sit, and will sit as king for ever, sending such tempests when it pleaseth him.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament, Psalm 29

Reformed Notable voices

“David's design is to lead all to worship God; expressly he addresses the sons of the mighty—princes intoxicated with pride who lift horns against God. Give strength unto Jehovah: deluded, they think power is supplied from some other quarter than heaven; lay aside haughtiness and glorify God as he deserves. By the brightness of his sanctuary is meant the tabernacle of the covenant, that men, bidding adieu to superstition, betake themselves to the pure worship of God. The voice of Jehovah is upon the waters: he selects works that awaken the torpid and drag them, as it were in spite of themselves, humbly to adore him; thunders and violent tempests smite hearts with dread. In his temple every one speaketh of his praise: God's voice fills the world, but his glory is celebrated only in his church, where he speaks intelligibly and gently allures the faithful; the doctrine of salvation alone cheers hearts and opens mouths in his praises. Jehovah sitteth upon the flood: floods that still threaten are controlled by providence so that he alone governs all things at all times; he sits King for ever. Jehovah will give strength to his people and bless them with peace: though power is visible to the whole world, he exerts it peculiarly for his elect—not overwhelming them with dread, but upholding, cherishing, and strengthening them; every thing necessary to the life of the godly depends upon his grace.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms, Psalm 29
