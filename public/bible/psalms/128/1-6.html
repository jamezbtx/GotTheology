Passage

Psalms 128

Verses 1–6 · ESV · Voices

A Song of Ascents of household blessing under the fear of the LORD: the one who walks in His ways eats the fruit of labor, flourishes with wife and children at the table, and is blessed from Zion with Jerusalem’s good and children’s children—peace upon Israel. Domestic joy and Zion’s welfare are named together as gifts of reverent obedience.

Psalms 128:1

1Blessed is everyone who fears the LORD, who walks in his ways!

Arminian

Blessed is everyone who fears the LORD and walks in His ways. Reverence and obedient walking are joined as the path of real happiness.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere call: fear and walk, and blessing is truly offered. Grace enables the fear; the pilgrim still must take up the ways of the LORD.

Reformed

Blessed is everyone who fears the LORD—who walks in His ways. Fear and obedience mark those God names as blessed under His care.

Reformed accent: fear and walking are inseparable fruit. God does not disappoint those who serve Him; blessing rests where His grace works reverence into a life.

Key contrast Both join fear of the LORD with walking in His ways as the shape of blessing. Arminians typically stress a real call to fear and walk that pilgrims must answer under enabling grace; Reformed readers typically stress fear and obedience as inseparable marks of those God blesses and keeps.

Psalms 128:2

2You shall eat the fruit of the labor of your hands; you shall be blessed, and it shall be well with you.

Arminian

Eat the fruit of your hands’ labor—blessed, and it shall be well. Honest work under God is not vanity but a pathway of ordinary joy.

Arminian pastoral: labor on; receive the fruit with gratitude. Well-being here is not idle luxury but the goodness of work God prospers for those who fear Him.

Reformed

You shall eat the fruit of your hands’ labor; blessed, and it shall be well. God as foster-father sustains His servants through their own toil.

Reformed comfort: happiness is not defined by ease and heaped wealth. Contentment under God’s hand, fed by labor He blesses, is already mercy on the pilgrim road.

Psalms 128:3

3Your wife will be like a fruitful vine within your house; your children will be like olive shoots around your table.

Arminian

Wife as fruitful vine within the house; children as olive shoots around the table. Domestic flourishing is pictured as living, gathered joy.

Classical Arminians receive family blessing as gift to steward with care. Vine and olive shoots invite gratitude and faithful nurture, not presumption that fear of God needs no daily love.

Reformed

Fruitful vine within the house; olive shoots around the table. God Himself grants household fruitfulness as a preferred blessing above many riches.

Reformed note: wife and children are boons from His hand. Temporal gifts are ladders toward heavenly life, not chains that keep the heart from Zion.

Psalms 128:4

4Behold, thus shall the man be blessed who fears the LORD.

Arminian

Behold—thus is the man blessed who fears the LORD. The household picture is summarized as the portion of reverent faith.

Arminian reading: “thus” points to a pattern you may pursue. Fear God sincerely; look for His good in the ordinary rooms of home and table.

Reformed

Behold—surely thus shall the God-fearer be blessed. The demonstrative seals the doctrine against the world’s contrary verdict.

Reformed accent: outward blessing may not always shine equally, yet God does not waste the service of those who fear Him. “Behold” calls faith to see His favor where carnal reason denies it.

Psalms 128:5

5The LORD bless you from Zion! May you see the prosperity of Jerusalem all the days of your life!

Arminian

The LORD bless you from Zion; see Jerusalem’s prosperity all your days. Private household good is tied to the public good of God’s city.

Classical Arminians urge prayerful love for the church’s flourishing. Blessing from Zion widens the heart beyond one’s own walls toward Jerusalem’s peace.

Reformed

Blessing from Zion; prosperity of Jerusalem all one’s days. Domestic gifts and the church’s welfare are joined under God’s covenant care.

Reformed pastoral: do not sever home blessing from Zion. God blesses from the place of His name; the godly desire the church’s good as chief among earthly joys.

Key contrast Both bind household blessing to Zion’s good. Arminians typically emphasize a willing love and prayer for Jerusalem’s prosperity; Reformed readers typically emphasize God as the author who blesses from Zion and joins private welfare to the church’s flourishing.

Psalms 128:6

6May you see your children's children! Peace be upon Israel!

Arminian

See children’s children—and peace upon Israel. Longevity of family joy meets a closing prayer for the people’s shalom.

Arminian pastoral: ask God for generational mercy and for Israel’s peace. The Ascents song ends not in private comfort alone but in blessing sought for the whole people.

Reformed

Children’s children in view; peace upon Israel. Generational hope and corporate peace close the blessing under God’s hand.

Reformed comfort: peace upon Israel is God’s gift to name and seek. The fearer’s household story is gathered into the larger story of a people kept for shalom.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on fearing the LORD, walking in His ways, and receiving household blessing from Zion—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 128:1–6.

Arminian Notable voices

“Thou shalt not be exempted from labor. Thou shalt work: But God will bless and prosper that work, and thou and thy family shall eat of it. Ye shall all live on the produce of your own labor, and the hand of violence shall not be permitted to deprive you of it. Thus, Happy shalt thou be, and it shall be well with thee—Thou shalt have prosperity. Thy children, in every corner and apartment of thy house, shall be the evidences of the fruitfulness of thy wife, as bunches of grapes on every bough of the vine are the proofs of its being in a healthy thriving state. Thus shall the man be blessed that feareth the Lord—A great price for a small consideration. Fear God, and thou shalt have as much domestic good as may be useful to thee. The Lord shall bless thee out of Zion—In all thy approaches to him in his house by prayer, by sacrifice, and by offering, thou shalt have his especial blessing. Thou shalt see the good of Jerusalem—Thou shalt see the cause of God flourish in thy lifetime, and his Church in great prosperity. Peace upon Israel—May God favor his own cause, and bless all his people!”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Psalm 128)

Reformed Notable voices

“In the preceding Psalm it was stated that prosperity in all human affairs is to be hoped for exclusively from the grace of God; and now the Prophet admonishes us that those who desire to be partakers of the blessing of God must with sincerity of heart devote themselves wholly to him; for he will never disappoint those who serve him. The maxim that those are blessed who fear God, especially in the present life, is so much at variance with the common opinion of men that very few will give it their assent. We see how the most depraved, with no less pride than audacity, boast of fearing God. The Prophet therefore requires the attestation of the life as to this; for these two things, the fear of God and the keeping of his law, are inseparable. He recalls God’s servants to the practice of moderation. How few are to be found who would desire to live by their own labor; yea, who would account it a singular benefit to do so! The Prophet therefore bids the fearers of God be content with this one thing—with the assurance that having God for their foster-father, they shall be suitably maintained by the labor of their own hands. Jehovah shall bless thee from Zion: the benefits recounted are to be ascribed to God as their author. What is added concerning the good of Jerusalem enjoins upon the godly the duty of having it as chief desire to see the Church of God in a flourishing condition.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 128)
