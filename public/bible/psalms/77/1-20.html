Passage

Psalms 77

Verses 1–20 · WEB · Voices

Of Asaph. The psalmist cries aloud to God in trouble, stretches out his hand by night, refuses comfort, and finds even the memory of God a cause of moaning. Sleepless and speechless, he considers the days of old, searches his spirit, and asks whether the Lord will spurn forever, whether steadfast love and promise have ceased, whether grace and compassion are shut in anger. He then appeals to the years when the Most High stretched out his right hand, remembers the LORD's deeds and wonders, confesses God's holy way, and recounts the Exodus: redeemed people, trembling waters, storm and lightning, a path through the sea with unseen footprints, and a flock led by Moses and Aaron.

Psalms 77:1

1My cry goes to God! Indeed, I cry to God for help, and for him to listen to me.

Arminian

I cry to God, and he will hear me. The psalm opens in confident crying. Both traditions hear: both camps hear earnest prayer that expects a hearing.

Wesley's text stresses voice unto God and that he gave ear. Cry aloud; do not mute trouble before the LORD.

Reformed

Calvin treats the loud cry as the believer's vent under sorrow—true believers sometimes sigh and complain, sometimes fall silent as if choked. The opening anticipates both voice and later silence (v. 4).

Cry to God. The faithful are not uniform in affliction; voice and silence both belong to piety under pressure.

Psalms 77:2

2In the day of my trouble I sought the Lord. My hand was stretched out in the night, and didn’t get tired. My soul refused to be comforted.

Arminian

In the day of my trouble I sought the Lord; night hand stretched out without wearying; soul refuses comfort. Wesley: Night—which to others was a time of rest. Relentless seeking in sleepless trouble.

Refuse false comforts; keep seeking. Night prayer is not theatrical; it is the sore that will not cease.

Reformed

Calvin: stretched hand by night continually; sleep denied is an aggravation of distress. The soul that refuses comfort is not yet healed by easy words.

Day of trouble drives seeking. Affliction that removes sleep presses the soul toward God alone.

Psalms 77:3

3I remember God, and I groan. I complain, and my spirit is overwhelmed. Selah.

Arminian

When I remember God, and I groan. I complain, and my spirit faints. Wesley: Troubled—thoughts of God were now trouble because he was angry; Overwhelmed—so far from finding relief. Common reading: even remembering God can sting under sensed anger.

He is honest that his spirit is overwhelmed. Do not pretend meditation always soothes on the first pass.

Reformed

Calvin notes that remembering God's benefits can, under Satan's craft, deepen the wound of their present want. Memory and meditation may at first increase grief before they heal.

Moan and faint are true. The Selah marks the depth before the turn toward ancient days.

Psalms 77:4

4You hold my eyelids open. I am so troubled that I can’t speak.

Arminian

You hold my eyelids open. I am so troubled that I can’t speak. Wesley: Waking—by continual grief. Both confess: sleepless trouble that chokes speech.

When words fail, the God who holds the eyes still hears the cry of v. 1. Silence is not abandonment of faith.

Reformed

Calvin: overwhelmed and choked with calamities so as to be unable to utter a word. Believers alternate between venting and silence. God may hold the brows so sleep will not interrupt sorrow.

His trouble goes beyond words. Silence under God is still addressed to him.

Psalms 77:5

5I have considered the days of old, the years of ancient times.

Arminian

I have considered the days of old, the years of ancient times. Wesley: The days—the mighty works of God in former times. Relief sought in remembering former works.

Turn the mind to what God has done. Ancient years are medicine for present night.

Reformed

Calvin: he tried to assuage grief by remembrance of former joy, yet relief was not quickly obtained. Days of old and years of ancient times may comprehend many ages—the Church should recall blessings of every age, beginning from personal experience.

Recount the days of old. Personal and corporate memory both belong to the cure.

Psalms 77:6

6I remember my song in the night. I consider in my own heart; my spirit diligently inquires:

Arminian

I remember my song in the night; I meditate in my heart, and my spirit searches diligently. Wesley: My song—mercies that obliged me to sing praises even by night. Night song remembered; heart searched.

Commune with your heart; search diligently. Memory of praise is not nostalgia only—it is a call back to thanksgiving.

Reformed

Calvin: song denotes thanksgiving in prosperity; night solitude engenders more cares. Communing with the heart happens when none restrain us. The spirit searches causes and issue of calamities—profitable within due bounds, lest overmuch sorrow swallow us.

Diligent search of the spirit. Keep bounds; do not try to sound the unfathomable depth of judgments so as to drown.

Psalms 77:7

7“Will the Lord reject us forever? Will he be favorable no more?

Arminian

Will the Lord reject us forever? Will he be favorable? Wesley: Cut off—his peculiar people. Either reading: the hard question of lasting rejection.

The questions that follow are the voice of tempted faith, not settled doctrine. Bring them into the open before God.

Reformed

Calvin: these statements form part of his searchings; long calamity nearly made him unable to hope God would again be favorable. He does not properly find fault with God but reasons with himself from God's nature.

Spurn-forever is the temptation's edge. Argue from who God is, not from the length of the night alone.

Psalms 77:8

8Has his loving kindness vanished forever? Does his promise fail for generations?

Arminian

Has his loving kindness vanished forever? Does his promise fail for all time? Hesed and promise under question in the night.

The plain anguish stands. Steadfast love and promise are the very things faith must not surrender.

Reformed

Calvin: can God break off fatherly favor when he cannot divest himself of his own nature? By argument from goodness he repels temptation. When the oracle seems to fail, consolation is scarce—yet God tries his people wonderfully.

Steadfast love and promise cannot finally fail. The fear that they have ended is the assault; God's nature is the answer.

Psalms 77:9

9Has God forgotten to be gracious? Has he, in anger, withheld his compassion?” Selah.

Arminian

Has God forgotten to be gracious? Has he, in anger, withheld his compassion? Selah. Grace and compassion questioned under anger.

Selah after the cascade of questions. Pause before the turn of v. 10. Do not baptize despair as piety.

Reformed

Calvin continues the same searching: forgotten mercy and shut compassions are the climax of the temptation's questions, still answered by God's fatherly nature and remembered works.

Anger may be felt; forgotten grace is the lie to be refused. Selah, then appeal to the right hand.

Psalms 77:10

10Then I thought, “I will appeal to this: the years of the right hand of the Most High.”

Arminian

Then I thought, I will appeal to this: the years of the right hand of the Most High. Wesley (on KJV This is my infirmity): these suspicions of God's faithfulness proceed from the weakness of my faith; the years—wherein God has done great works, often ascribed to his right hand.

Name the infirmity; then remember the right hand's years. Weak faith's suspicions are confessed, not crowned.

Reformed

Calvin: after prolonged calamity he reasons that God who once showed fatherly favor cannot break its course; he appeals to the years of the right hand / wonders that sustain faith when special oracles feel distant.

The turn is theological and historical: God's power and past deeds, not a revised god who learns to care.

Key contrast Arminian readings, with Wesley, typically take the turning line as confession that suspicions of God's faithfulness are my infirmity—weak faith—and then as resolve to remember the years of the right hand's great works. Reformed readings, with Calvin, typically frame the same turn as reasoning from God's fatherly nature under long calamity: he who once embraced with favor will not break it off, and the appeal is to the years of the right hand / remembered wonders that answer the temptation. Leave the spurn-forever questions; cling to the Most High's right-hand years and deeds.

Psalms 77:11

11I will remember the LORD’s deeds; for I will remember your wonders of old.

Arminian

I will remember the LORD’s deeds of the LORD; yes, your wonders of old. Both sides agree: deliberate remembering of wonders.

Takeaway: will remember—twice. Make memory an act of faith after the infirmity of v. 10.

Reformed

Calvin: remembering the Lord's works and wonders of old is the path out of the gulf when promises feel far. The Church sets before her eyes blessings of every age.

Deeds and wonders of old. Memory is obedience under night.

Psalms 77:12

12I will also meditate on all your work, and consider your doings.

Arminian

I will also meditate on all your work, and consider your mighty deeds. Both traditions see: meditation replaces the earlier fainting meditation of v. 3.

Talk of his doings (Wesley). Ponder until the heart sings again.

Reformed

Calvin: meditation on mighty deeds continues the cure begun in remembering. Diligent thought about God's works replaces fruitless searching into despair.

Ponder and meditate. The same faculties that fainted now feed on mighty deeds.

Psalms 77:13

13Your way, God, is in the sanctuary. What god is great like God?

Arminian

Your way, God, is in the sanctuary. What god is great like God? Wesley: In holiness—God is holy, just, and true in all his works. Both note: holy way; incomparable greatness.

Sanctuary/holiness answers the night's suspicion that God has changed. His way is holy still.

Reformed

Calvin stresses the holiness and incomparability of God's way as the ground of trust after the questions. No god is great like ours.

God’s way is holy, and he is the great God. Attributes steady the soul that asked whether favor had ended.

Psalms 77:14

14You are the God who does wonders. You have made your strength known among the peoples.

Arminian

You are the God who does wonders. You have made your strength known among the peoples. God works wonders and makes his strength known publicly.

Wonders are not private only—they are declared among peoples. Faith rehearses public might.

Reformed

Calvin: God who does wonders has declared strength among the peoples. The Church's memory is missionary as well as consoling.

God works wonders and makes his might known among the peoples. The God who saved Israel shows strength beyond one night of trouble.

Psalms 77:15

15You have redeemed your people with your arm, the sons of Jacob and Joseph. Selah.

Arminian

You have redeemed your people with your arm, the sons of Jacob and Joseph. Selah. Redemption by the arm; Jacob and Joseph named.

Historic redemption of the people anchors personal night. Selah after the redeeming arm.

Reformed

Calvin: redeemed people—sons of Jacob and Joseph—recalls the Exodus-shaped salvation that answers vv. 7–9. The arm that redeemed still defines God's way.

Redeemed people by the arm. Corporate redemption underwrites the individual's appeal.

Psalms 77:16

16The waters saw you, God. The waters saw you, and they writhed. The depths also convulsed.

Arminian

When the waters saw you, and they were afraid; the deep trembled. Wesley: Afraid—and stood still, as men astonished. Common confession: creation fears its Maker at the sea crossing.

The waters are pictured as astonished witnesses. The night's God is the Red Sea's God.

Reformed

Calvin reads the trembling deep as creation's awe at God's approach in the Exodus theophany. Nature yields when God leads his people through.

Both readings hold: Waters afraid.

Psalms 77:17

17The clouds poured out water. The skies resounded with thunder. Your arrows also flashed around.

Arminian

The clouds poured out water; skies gave thunder; your arrows flashed. Wesley: Poured—when Israel passed the sea; Arrows—hail-stones or lightnings. Both agree: storm theophany at the crossing.

Arrows of God, not of the enemy. The same God who broke arrows in Psalm 76 sends his own in deliverance.

Reformed

Calvin: clouds, thunder, and arrows (lightnings) attend the mighty passage. Theophany language magnifies the Redeemer of v. 15.

Storm as God's escort of his people. Mighty deeds made visible.

Psalms 77:18

18The voice of your thunder was in the whirlwind. The lightnings lit up the world. The earth trembled and shook.

Arminian

Thunder in the whirlwind; lightnings lighted the world; earth trembled. Cosmic shaking at God's voice.

The spectacle stands. The world lit by lightning is the stage of redemption.

Reformed

Calvin continues the theophany: thunder, lightning, trembling earth—creation responds to the God who redeems.

Earthquake and storm serve the flock's passage. Fear of God, not chaos, fills the scene.

Psalms 77:19

19Your way was through the sea, your paths through the great waters. Your footsteps were not known.

Arminian

Your way was through the sea, your paths through great waters. Your footsteps were unseen. Wesley: Not known—because the water returned and covered them.

God leads where tracks vanish. Faith follows a path the waters erase behind.

Reformed

Calvin: path in great waters with footsteps unknown—God's guidance may be true yet inscrutable in its traces. The Church is led even when it cannot map every print.

Way through the sea, footprints unseen. Providence without a visible trail still leads.

Psalms 77:20

20You led your people like a flock, by the hand of Moses and Aaron.

Arminian

You led your people like a flock by the hand of Moses and Aaron. Wesley: Leddest—through sea and wilderness with singular care, as a shepherd his sheep. Shared close: shepherding through appointed servants.

Flock language ends the night with tenderness. Moses and Aaron are hands; the Shepherd is God.

Reformed

Calvin: led like a flock by Moses and Aaron—care and order in redemption's march. The God who was questioned in vv. 7–9 is the Shepherd who brought them through.

People as flock, led by the hand. Night questions yield to historic shepherding.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Older voices from both traditions on Psalm 77:1–20.

Arminian Notable voices

“Night—Which to others was a time of rest and quietness. Troubled—the thoughts of God were now a matter of trouble, because he was angry with me. Overwhelmed—so far was I from finding relief. Waking—By continual grief. The days—The mighty works of God in former times. My song—The mercies of God which have obliged me to sing his praises, not only in the day, but also by night. Cut off—His peculiar people. I said—These suspicions of God's faithfulness proceed from the weakness of my faith. The years—wherein God hath done great and glorious works, often ascribed to God's right-hand. In holiness—God is holy and just, and true in all his works. Afraid—And stood still, as men astonished. Poured—When the Israelites passed over the sea. Arrows—Hail-stones or lightnings. Not known—Because the water returned and covered them. Leddest—First through the sea, and afterwards through the wilderness, with singular care and tenderness, as a shepherd doth his sheep.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament, Psalm 77

Reformed Notable voices

“True believers, overwhelmed with sorrow, sometimes give vent to sighs and sometimes are silent as if choked. He sought relief in remembrance of former joy, yet it did not come quickly; days of old may comprehend many ages of the Church's blessings. Night solitude engenders more cares; communing with the heart and diligent search into causes and issue of calamities are profitable within due bounds. Will the Lord cast off forever?—he reasons from God's nature that it is impossible for him not to continue free favor toward those to whom he has once shown himself a father. Remember the works and wonders of old; God who works wonders redeemed his people. The waters were afraid; his way was through the sea, footprints unseen; he led his people like a flock by Moses and Aaron.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms, Psalm 77
