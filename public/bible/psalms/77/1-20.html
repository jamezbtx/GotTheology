Passage

Psalms 77

Verses 1–20 · ESV · Voices

Of Asaph. The psalmist cries aloud to God in trouble, stretches out his hand by night, refuses comfort, and finds even the memory of God a cause of moaning. Sleepless and speechless, he considers the days of old, searches his spirit, and asks whether the Lord will spurn forever, whether steadfast love and promise have ceased, whether grace and compassion are shut in anger. He then appeals to the years of the right hand of the Most High, remembers the LORD's deeds and wonders, confesses God's holy way, and recounts the Exodus: redeemed people, trembling waters, storm and lightning, a path through the sea with unseen footprints, and a flock led by Moses and Aaron.

Psalms 77:1

1I cry aloud to God, aloud to God, and he will hear me.

Arminian

I cry aloud to God, and he will hear me. The psalm opens in confident crying. Shared: both camps hear earnest prayer that expects a hearing.

Arminian pastoral: Wesley's text stresses voice unto God and that he gave ear. Cry aloud; do not mute trouble before the LORD.

Reformed

Calvin treats the loud cry as the believer's vent under sorrow—true believers sometimes sigh and complain, sometimes fall silent as if choked. The opening anticipates both voice and later silence (v. 4).

Reformed pastoral: shared—cry to God. The faithful are not uniform in affliction; voice and silence both belong to real piety under pressure.

Psalms 77:2

2In the day of my trouble I seek the Lord; in the night my hand is stretched out without wearying; my soul refuses to be comforted.

Arminian

In the day of my trouble I seek the Lord; night hand stretched out without wearying; soul refuses comfort. Wesley: Night—which to others was a time of rest. Shared: relentless seeking in sleepless trouble.

Arminian pastoral: refuse false comforts; keep seeking. Night prayer is not theatrical; it is the sore that will not cease.

Reformed

Calvin: stretched hand by night continually; sleep denied is an aggravation of distress. The soul that refuses comfort is not yet healed by easy words.

Reformed pastoral: shared—day of trouble drives seeking. Affliction that removes sleep presses the soul toward God alone.

Psalms 77:3

3When I remember God, I moan; when I meditate, my spirit faints. Selah

Arminian

When I remember God, I moan; when I meditate, my spirit faints. Wesley: Troubled—thoughts of God were now trouble because he was angry; Overwhelmed—so far from finding relief. Shared: even remembering God can sting under sensed anger.

Arminian pastoral: honesty about overwhelmed spirit. Do not pretend meditation always soothes on the first pass.

Reformed

Calvin notes that remembering God's benefits can, under Satan's craft, deepen the wound of their present want. Memory and meditation may at first increase grief before they heal.

Reformed pastoral: shared—moan and faint are real. The Selah marks the depth before the turn toward ancient days.

Psalms 77:4

4You hold my eyelids open; I am so troubled that I cannot speak.

Arminian

You hold my eyelids open; I am so troubled that I cannot speak. Wesley: Waking—by continual grief. Shared: sleepless trouble that chokes speech.

Arminian pastoral: when words fail, the God who holds the eyes still hears the cry of v. 1. Silence is not abandonment of faith.

Reformed

Calvin: overwhelmed and choked with calamities so as to be unable to utter a word. Believers alternate between venting and silence. God may hold the brows so sleep will not interrupt sorrow.

Reformed pastoral: shared—trouble beyond speech. Silence under God is still addressed to him.

Psalms 77:5

5I consider the days of old, the years long ago.

Arminian

I consider the days of old, the years long ago. Wesley: The days—the mighty works of God in former times. Shared: relief sought in remembering former works.

Arminian pastoral: turn the mind to what God has done. Ancient years are medicine for present night.

Reformed

Calvin: he tried to assuage grief by remembrance of former joy, yet relief was not quickly obtained. Days of old and years of ancient times may comprehend many ages—the Church should recall blessings of every age, beginning from personal experience.

Reformed pastoral: shared—recount the days of old. Personal and corporate memory both belong to the cure.

Psalms 77:6

6I said, “Let me remember my song in the night; let me meditate in my heart.” Then my spirit made a diligent search:

Arminian

Let me remember my song in the night; meditate in my heart; spirit made diligent search. Wesley: My song—mercies that obliged me to sing praises even by night. Shared: night song remembered; heart searched.

Arminian pastoral: commune with your heart; search diligently. Memory of praise is not nostalgia only—it is a call back to thanksgiving.

Reformed

Calvin: song denotes thanksgiving in prosperity; night solitude engenders more cares. Communing with the heart happens when none restrain us. The spirit searches causes and issue of calamities—profitable within due bounds, lest overmuch sorrow swallow us.

Reformed pastoral: shared—diligent search of the spirit. Keep bounds; do not try to sound the unfathomable depth of judgments so as to drown.

Psalms 77:7

7“Will the Lord spurn forever, and never again be favorable?

Arminian

Will the Lord spurn forever, and never again be favorable? Wesley: Cut off—his peculiar people. Shared: the hard question of lasting rejection.

Arminian pastoral: the questions that follow are the voice of tempted faith, not settled doctrine. Bring them into the open before God.

Reformed

Calvin: these statements form part of his searchings; long calamity nearly made him unable to hope God would again be favorable. He does not properly find fault with God but reasons with himself from God's nature.

Reformed pastoral: shared—spurn-forever is the temptation's edge. Argue from who God is, not from the length of the night alone.

Psalms 77:8

8Has his steadfast love forever ceased? Are his promises at an end for all time?

Arminian

Has his steadfast love forever ceased? Are his promises at an end for all time? Shared: hesed and promise under question in the night.

Arminian pastoral: Wesley has no extra lemma; the plain anguish stands. Steadfast love and promise are the very things faith must not surrender.

Reformed

Calvin: can God break off fatherly favor when he cannot divest himself of his own nature? By argument from goodness he repels temptation. When the oracle seems to fail, consolation is scarce—yet God tries his people wonderfully.

Reformed pastoral: shared—steadfast love and promise cannot finally fail. The fear that they have ended is the assault; God's nature is the answer.

Psalms 77:9

9Has God forgotten to be gracious? Has he in anger shut up his compassion?” Selah

Arminian

Has God forgotten to be gracious? Has he in anger shut up his compassion? Selah. Shared: grace and compassion questioned under anger.

Arminian pastoral: Selah after the cascade of questions. Pause before the turn of v. 10. Do not baptize despair as piety.

Reformed

Calvin continues the same searching: forgotten mercy and shut compassions are the climax of the temptation's questions, still answered by God's fatherly nature and remembered works.

Reformed pastoral: shared—anger may be felt; forgotten grace is the lie to be refused. Selah, then appeal to the right hand.

Psalms 77:10

10Then I said, “I will appeal to this, to the years of the right hand of the Most High.”

Arminian

Then I said, I will appeal to this, to the years of the right hand of the Most High. Wesley (on KJV This is my infirmity): these suspicions of God's faithfulness proceed from the weakness of my faith; the years—wherein God has done great works, often ascribed to his right hand. Shared turn: from despairing questions to the years of the right hand.

Arminian pastoral: name the infirmity; then remember the right hand's years. Weak faith's suspicions are confessed, not crowned.

Reformed

Calvin: after prolonged calamity he reasons that God who once showed fatherly favor cannot break its course; he appeals to the years of the right hand / wonders that sustain faith when special oracles feel distant.

Reformed pastoral: shared—appeal to the Most High's right-hand years. The turn is theological and historical: God's power and past deeds, not a revised god who learns to care.

Key contrast Arminian readings, with Wesley, typically take the turning line as confession that suspicions of God's faithfulness are my infirmity—weak faith—and then as resolve to remember the years of the right hand's great works. Reformed readings, with Calvin, typically frame the same turn as reasoning from God's fatherly nature under long calamity: he who once embraced with favor will not break it off, and the appeal is to the years of the right hand / remembered wonders that answer the temptation. Shared: leave the spurn-forever questions; cling to the Most High's right-hand years and deeds.

Psalms 77:11

11I will remember the deeds of the LORD; yes, I will remember your wonders of old.

Arminian

I will remember the deeds of the LORD; yes, your wonders of old. Shared: deliberate remembering of wonders.

Arminian pastoral: will remember—twice. Make memory an act of faith after the infirmity of v. 10.

Reformed

Calvin: remembering the Lord's works and wonders of old is the path out of the gulf when promises feel far. The Church sets before her eyes blessings of every age.

Reformed pastoral: shared—deeds and wonders of old. Memory is obedience under night.

Psalms 77:12

12I will ponder all your work, and meditate on your mighty deeds.

Arminian

I will ponder all your work, and meditate on your mighty deeds. Shared: meditation replaces the earlier fainting meditation of v. 3.

Arminian pastoral: talk of his doings (Wesley). Ponder until the heart sings again.

Reformed

Calvin: meditation on mighty deeds continues the cure begun in remembering. Diligent thought about God's works replaces fruitless searching into despair.

Reformed pastoral: shared—ponder and meditate. The same faculties that fainted now feed on mighty deeds.

Psalms 77:13

13Your way, O God, is holy. What god is great like our God?

Arminian

Your way, O God, is holy. What god is great like our God? Wesley: In holiness—God is holy, just, and true in all his works. Shared: holy way; incomparable greatness.

Arminian pastoral: sanctuary/holiness answers the night's suspicion that God has changed. His way is holy still.

Reformed

Calvin stresses the holiness and incomparability of God's way as the ground of trust after the questions. No god is great like ours.

Reformed pastoral: shared—holy way, great God. Attributes steady the soul that asked whether favor had ended.

Psalms 77:14

14You are the God who works wonders; you have made known your might among the peoples.

Arminian

You are the God who works wonders; you have made known your might among the peoples. Shared: wonder-working God publicly known.

Arminian pastoral: wonders are not private only—they are declared among peoples. Faith rehearses public might.

Reformed

Calvin: God who does wonders has declared strength among the peoples. The Church's memory is missionary as well as consoling.

Reformed pastoral: shared—wonders and known might. The God who saved Israel shows strength beyond one night of trouble.

Psalms 77:15

15You with your arm redeemed your people, the children of Jacob and Joseph. Selah

Arminian

You with your arm redeemed your people, the children of Jacob and Joseph. Selah. Shared: redemption by the arm; Jacob and Joseph named.

Arminian pastoral: historic redemption of the people anchors personal night. Selah after the redeeming arm.

Reformed

Calvin: redeemed people—sons of Jacob and Joseph—recalls the Exodus-shaped salvation that answers vv. 7–9. The arm that redeemed still defines God's way.

Reformed pastoral: shared—redeemed people by the arm. Corporate redemption underwrites the individual's appeal.

Psalms 77:16

16When the waters saw you, O God, when the waters saw you, they were afraid; indeed, the deep trembled.

Arminian

When the waters saw you, they were afraid; the deep trembled. Wesley: Afraid—and stood still, as men astonished. Shared: creation fears its Maker at the sea crossing.

Arminian pastoral: waters as astonished witnesses. The night's God is the Red Sea's God.

Reformed

Calvin reads the trembling deep as creation's awe at God's approach in the Exodus theophany. Nature yields when God leads his people through.

Reformed pastoral: shared—waters afraid. Historic sea, not myth as camp fight.

Psalms 77:17

17The clouds poured out water; the skies gave forth thunder; your arrows flashed on every side.

Arminian

The clouds poured out water; skies gave thunder; your arrows flashed. Wesley: Poured—when Israel passed the sea; Arrows—hail-stones or lightnings. Shared: storm theophany at the crossing.

Arminian pastoral: arrows of God, not of the enemy. The same God who broke arrows in Psalm 76 sends his own in deliverance.

Reformed

Calvin: clouds, thunder, and arrows (lightnings) attend the mighty passage. Theophany language magnifies the Redeemer of v. 15.

Reformed pastoral: shared—storm as God's escort of his people. Mighty deeds made visible.

Psalms 77:18

18The crash of your thunder was in the whirlwind; your lightnings lighted up the world; the earth trembled and shook.

Arminian

Thunder in the whirlwind; lightnings lighted the world; earth trembled. Shared: cosmic shaking at God's voice.

Arminian pastoral: Wesley has no extra lemma; the spectacle stands. The world lit by lightning is the stage of redemption.

Reformed

Calvin continues the theophany: thunder, lightning, trembling earth—creation responds to the God who redeems.

Reformed pastoral: shared—earthquake and storm serve the flock's passage. Fear of God, not chaos, fills the scene.

Psalms 77:19

19Your way was through the sea, your path through the great waters; yet your footprints were unseen.

Arminian

Your way was through the sea, your path through great waters; yet your footprints were unseen. Wesley: Not known—because the water returned and covered them. Shared: way in the sea; footprints hidden.

Arminian pastoral: God leads where tracks vanish. Faith follows a path the waters erase behind.

Reformed

Calvin: path in great waters with footsteps unknown—God's guidance may be real yet inscrutable in its traces. The Church is led even when it cannot map every print.

Reformed pastoral: shared—way through the sea, footprints unseen. Providence without a visible trail still leads.

Psalms 77:20

20You led your people like a flock by the hand of Moses and Aaron.

Arminian

You led your people like a flock by the hand of Moses and Aaron. Wesley: Leddest—through sea and wilderness with singular care, as a shepherd his sheep. Shared close: shepherding through appointed servants.

Arminian pastoral: flock language ends the night with tenderness. Moses and Aaron are hands; the Shepherd is God.

Reformed

Calvin: led like a flock by Moses and Aaron—care and order in redemption's march. The God who was questioned in vv. 7–9 is the Shepherd who brought them through.

Reformed pastoral: shared—people as flock, led by the hand. Night questions yield to historic shepherding.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on night trouble, refused comfort, questions of lasting favor, the appeal to the right hand of the Most High, remembered wonders, and the flock led through the sea—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 77:1–20.

Arminian Notable voices

“Night—Which to others was a time of rest and quietness. Troubled—the thoughts of God were now a matter of trouble, because he was angry with me. Overwhelmed—so far was I from finding relief. Waking—By continual grief. The days—The mighty works of God in former times. My song—The mercies of God which have obliged me to sing his praises, not only in the day, but also by night. Cut off—His peculiar people. I said—These suspicions of God's faithfulness proceed from the weakness of my faith. The years—wherein God hath done great and glorious works, often ascribed to God's right-hand. In holiness—God is holy and just, and true in all his works. Afraid—And stood still, as men astonished. Poured—When the Israelites passed over the sea. Arrows—Hail-stones or lightnings. Not known—Because the water returned and covered them. Leddest—First through the sea, and afterwards through the wilderness, with singular care and tenderness, as a shepherd doth his sheep.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament, Psalm 77

Reformed Notable voices

“True believers, overwhelmed with sorrow, sometimes give vent to sighs and sometimes are silent as if choked. He sought relief in remembrance of former joy, yet it did not come quickly; days of old may comprehend many ages of the Church's blessings. Night solitude engenders more cares; communing with the heart and diligent search into causes and issue of calamities are profitable within due bounds. Will the Lord cast off forever?—he reasons from God's nature that it is impossible for him not to continue free favor toward those to whom he has once shown himself a father. Remember the works and wonders of old; God who works wonders redeemed his people. The waters were afraid; his way was through the sea, footprints unseen; he led his people like a flock by Moses and Aaron.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Psalms, Psalm 77
