Passage

Psalms 103

Verses 1–22 · ESV · Voices

Of David: the soul is summoned to bless the LORD and forget none of his benefits—forgiveness, healing, redemption from the pit, crowning steadfast love, renewal like the eagle's—then mercy like a father's, sins removed as far as east from west, everlasting love on those who fear him and keep covenant, the throne established in the heavens, and all angels, hosts, and works called to join the soul's praise.

Psalms 103:1

1Bless the LORD, O my soul, and all that is within me, bless his holy name!

Arminian

Bless the LORD, O my soul—and all that is within me, his holy name. Wesley's frame: the psalmist stirs himself to praise and will call all works to assist. Inner life is commandeered for worship.

Arminian hearing: self-exhortation is real agency under grace. The whole inward self must bless; sluggishness is to be corrected, not excused.

Reformed

Calvin: by stirring himself to gratitude he gives every man a lesson. Our sloth needs continual incitement; even the fervent prophet confesses the malady. He addresses mind and heart and all faculties, as if removed from men and examining himself before God.

Reformed accent: the Spirit upbraids our undiligence and points the remedy—descend into yourself and awaken praise.

Psalms 103:2

2Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits,

Arminian

Bless again; forget not all his benefits. Memory is the fuel of praise. Ingratitude forgets; blessing remembers.

Arminian pastoral: practice recollection. List benefits; refuse the slide into spiritual amnesia. Grace restores memory; you must still remember.

Reformed

Calvin: God is not deficient in furnishing matter for praise; our ingratitude hinders. He deals liberally that we may celebrate his praise. Shameful forgetfulness buries innumerable benefits; the sole prohibition is not to forget them.

Reformed accent: benefits are objectively piled high; the fault is our burying them, not God's meanness.

Psalms 103:3

3who forgives all your iniquity, who heals all your diseases,

Arminian

Who forgives all your iniquity; who heals all your diseases. Pardon leads the list of benefits. Healing may include body and the deeper cure of guilt's wounds.

Arminian gospel: all iniquity forgiven is the mercy this psalm sings for those who bless his name. Receive by faith; let the soul answer pardon with praise.

Reformed

Calvin: he begins with pardoning mercy—reconciliation the fountain of all other blessings. Forgiveness freely blots out sins and receives us into favor. Healing diseases: not restricted to the body only; the medicine respects blotting out guilt and curing corruptions by the Spirit of regeneration.

Reformed accent: until the heavenly Physician succors us, we nourish many deaths within. Pardon is first for those who taste this goodness in Christ.

Psalms 103:4

4who redeems your life from the pit, who crowns you with steadfast love and mercy,

Arminian

Redeems life from the pit; crowns with steadfast love and mercy. From near-death to crowned kindness.

Arminian reading: redemption and crowning are gifts to be answered with blessing. The pit was real; so is the crown of hesed.

Reformed

Calvin: previous to curing, we are dead and adjudged to the grave. Mercy delivers from death—what room for self-gloriation? He adds compassions in the plural: before, behind, on all sides, grace presents itself in immeasurable abundance.

Reformed accent: crowning love surrounds those God redeems; the first step of spiritual life is resurrection from the grave by mercy.

Psalms 103:5

5who satisfies you with good so that your youth is renewed like the eagle's.

Arminian

Wesley: The eagles—which lives long in great strength and vigour. Satisfied with good; youth renewed.

Arminian pastoral: God fills the mouth with good and renews vigor. Expect renewal without denying aging bodies; spiritual strength can be young while years advance.

Reformed

Calvin: God constantly infused new vigor so strength continued—secret influence by which the godly retain unimpaired strength. Though the outward man decays, we are renewed to a better life and sustained by the Spirit.

Reformed comfort: eagle renewal images grace's secret vigor. Satisfaction is from God's bounty to overflowing.

Psalms 103:6

6The LORD works righteousness and justice for all who are oppressed.

Arminian

The LORD works righteousness and justice for all the oppressed. From personal benefits to public character of God.

Arminian hearing: oppression is answered by God's justice. Plead for the wronged; trust the LORD who works righteousness.

Reformed

Calvin: passing from personal benefits, he declares God the succorer of the oppressed—including himself under many persecutions. It is God's ordinary work to succor his servants when injuriously treated. Exercise patience; God takes vengeance and covers with the shield of his justice.

Reformed accent: ordinary providence for the wronged among his servants—righteousness as God's habit, not rare exception.

Psalms 103:7

7He made known his ways to Moses, his acts to the people of Israel.

Arminian

Ways to Moses; acts to Israel. Revelation in history and law. The exodus God is the praising soul's God.

Arminian reading: made known means truly shown. Israel saw acts; Moses received ways. Thanksgiving includes Scripture's story, not private mood only.

Reformed

Calvin: he speaks in the name of the chosen people. Ways shown to Moses were deliverance until the promised land—God's righteousness in succoring the oppressed. He made known his ways first to Moses the messenger, then to all the people. Thanksgiving for adoption and the law's light.

Reformed accent: true knowledge of God matches what faith finds in the written Word—not cold speculation into secret essence.

Psalms 103:8

8The LORD is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love.

Arminian

Merciful, gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love—Exodus 34's name of God on the psalmist's lips.

Arminian gospel: this character is why sinners hope. Slow anger and abounding love meet the penitent. Both camps confess this Name together before they differ on the scope of "those who fear him."

Reformed

Calvin: allusion to Moses' exclamation in Exodus 34:6. David applies those terms: Israel continued in relation to God by sheer goodness though they forfeited it by wickedness. God rises to assert his mercy by which alone we stand. Synonyms meet our doubting: merciful, gracious, patient, abundant in mercy.

Reformed accent: the Name corrects arrogance that robs God of praise and trembling that will not trust his grace.

Psalms 103:9

9He will not always chide, nor will he keep his anger forever.

Arminian

Not always chide; anger not kept forever. Rebuke has an end in mercy for his people.

Arminian pastoral: chastening is real and parental; it is not endless rage. Turn under the rod; hope in the Name of v. 8.

Reformed

Calvin: when God has been offended he will not be irreconcilable—inclined by nature to forgive. He institutes action to humble sinners, then recedes when he sees them subdued. He does not keep anger as men cherish secret revenge. This does not describe his mind toward all mankind without distinction; it sets forth a special privilege of the Church—passing by unbelievers under everlasting wrath.

Reformed accent: tender pardon for his children (Isaiah 54), not a denial of vengeance on the unbelieving.

Psalms 103:10

10He does not deal with us according to our sins, nor repay us according to our iniquities.

Arminian

Not dealt with according to sins; not repaid according to iniquities. Forbearance beyond desert.

Arminian hearing: every believer must confess this. Examine your life; continual provocation met by non-repayment is sheer mercy that should melt resistance.

Reformed

Calvin: proves from experience the divine character—only wonderful forbearance preserved Israel. Each should inquire how often we provoke wrath; yet he not only forbears but bountifully maintains those he might justly destroy.

Reformed accent: non-repayment is grace to a people who deserved ruin—evidence, not wage.

Psalms 103:11

11For as high as the heavens are above the earth, so great is his steadfast love toward those who fear him;

Arminian

Wesley: So great—so much above their deserts and expectations. Heaven-high hesed toward those who fear him.

Arminian reading: fear of the LORD is the living reverence grace enables and we must exercise. The love is vast above desert; "those who fear him" names the path of recipients.

Reformed

Calvin: God's mercy toward us is infinite—compared to the vast extent of the world. He does not treat generally of what God is toward the whole world, but of his character toward the faithful. One mercy restores strangers to life; another continually follows those embraced with fatherly love.

Reformed accent: heaven-high steadfast love is on those who fear him—the Church's privilege, daily pardoning those already adopted.

Key contrast Both confess steadfast love higher than the heavens, far above deserts. Arminians typically hear "those who fear him" as the sincere path of reverent faith grace enables and people must walk. Reformed readers typically hear the phrase as naming the faithful toward whom fatherly mercy is specially shown—covenant love that restores and then daily follows the adopted, not a general bare attribute measured alike to all.

Psalms 103:12

12as far as the east is from the west, so far does he remove our transgressions from us.

Arminian

East from west: transgressions removed that far. Measureless pardon matches heaven-high love.

Arminian gospel: removal is real forgiveness. Believe the distance; stop digging up what God has removed.

Reformed

Calvin: that mercy may reach us, the obstacle of guilt is taken away—sins removed as far as east from west. He speaks of mercy poured on the faithful, continually following those in fatherly love—not a one-moment remission after which righteousness is kept by meritorious works.

Reformed accent: daily pardon to the close of life; against both a once-for-all-plus-satisfactions scheme and perfectionist denial of ongoing need.

Psalms 103:13

13As a father shows compassion to his children, so the LORD shows compassion to those who fear him.

Arminian

Father to children; LORD to those who fear him. Compassion is parental, not mechanical.

Arminian pastoral: fear and childlike trust belong together. Draw near as children; the Father's pity meets frailty without licensing presumption.

Reformed

Calvin: he assigns the cause—God is a father. Having freely and sovereignly adopted us, he continually pardons; draw hope of forgiveness from that fountain. No one adopted on his own merit; sins freely pardoned. He is thus favorable to sincere worshippers—the free imputation of righteousness offered to those who devote themselves to so bountiful a Father.

Reformed accent: fatherly compassion rests on sovereign adoption; attainments still fall short, so goodness remains the only secure pillar.

Psalms 103:14

14For he knows our frame; he remembers that we are dust.

Arminian

Wesley: Knoweth—the weakness and mortality of our natures, and the frailty of our condition, so that if he should let loose his hand upon us, we should be irrecoverably destroyed. Dust remembered stays the hand.

Arminian hearing: God's knowledge of frailty is mercy's motive in the text. Humility and trust rise together—no room for pride, no need for despair.

Reformed

Calvin: he annihilates worth men arrogate; the consideration of our misery alone moves God to patience. The more wretched our condition, the more inclined is God to show mercy. Remembrance that we are clay incites him to do us good.

Reformed accent: dust is not leverage we claim; it is what God sees when he chooses compassion.

Psalms 103:15

15As for man, his days are like grass; he flourishes like a flower of the field;

Arminian

Days like grass; flourishes like a field flower. Brief beauty, real and passing.

Arminian pastoral: number your days. Flourish without pretending permanence; bless the God whose love outlasts the flower (v. 17).

Reformed

Calvin: all excellency withers like a fading flower at the first blast. Man is said to flourish only loosely; he grants the grass image rather than calling man mere vapor—yet life is a show that passes, dependent on another's will.

Reformed accent: brevity of life is why God mercifully pardons (cf. Psalm 78:39).

Psalms 103:16

16for the wind passes over it, and it is gone, and its place knows it no more.

Arminian

Wind passes; it is gone; place knows it no more. Erasure of the flower's spot.

Arminian reading: human places empty quickly. Invest praise in the everlasting Name, not in a spot on the field.

Reformed

As soon as the wind passes, it is gone; its place knows it no more. Calvin: our life is phantom; if God withdrew grace the soul itself would be a blast and the body dust.

Reformed accent: total frailty drives the contrast of the next verse's everlasting steadfast love.

Psalms 103:17

17But the steadfast love of the LORD is from everlasting to everlasting on those who fear him, and his righteousness to children's children,

Arminian

But—everlasting to everlasting hesed on those who fear him; righteousness to children's children. The hinge against grass and wind.

Arminian reading: fear of God remains the living description of recipients across ages. Promise to children's children invites household faithfulness; it does not baptize presumption in ungodly offspring.

Reformed

Calvin: leaves nothing to rely on but God's mercy. Even the regenerate have no intrinsic excellence; God is the inexhaustible fountain. Goodness everlasting lets the faithful boast of eternal salvation even in death. Righteousness here is God's faithful protection of his saints, extending to children's children (Deuteronomy 7:9)—associating offspring in adoptive mercy.

Reformed accent: everlasting love on those who fear him secures empty people by God's fountain, including seed by hereditary grace of adoption.

Psalms 103:18

18to those who keep his covenant and remember to do his commandments.

Arminian

To those who keep his covenant and remember to do his commandments. Fear of God (v. 17) is expounded as covenant-keeping obedience.

Arminian hearing: remembrance and doing are grace-enabled duties we must actually take up. Hypocrites and degenerate children must not steal the promise; keep and do.

Reformed

Calvin: by way of exception—God is merciful only to those who keep his covenant, which the unbelieving make void. Keeping the covenant is put for fear of God: true worshippers reverently obey his Word. The covenant begins with promise of grace, so faith and prayer are required above all. Remember statutes—or forgetfulness extinguishes truth; remembrance invigorates when men do them.

Reformed accent: the condition-language describes the marked people of mercy, not a meritorious bargain that purchases everlasting love.

Key contrast Both require covenant-keeping and doing commandments with the fear of the LORD, and both shut out hypocrisy. Arminians typically hear keep-and-do as living conditions grace enables and believers must fulfill, without which the promise is not theirs. Reformed readers typically hear the same words as the description of those on whom everlasting mercy rests—obedience flowing from adoption and the covenant's gracious beginning, not a wage that causes God's love.

Psalms 103:19

19The LORD has established his throne in the heavens, and his kingdom rules over all.

Arminian

Throne established in the heavens; kingdom rules over all. Praise widens from personal benefits to cosmic rule.

Arminian sovereignty: God truly reigns over all. Creaturely agency remains under that rule; the soul blesses a King whose dominion is not theatrical.

Reformed

Calvin: having recounted benefits to each and to the Church, he extols infinite glory. Ascend above the world; do not measure his power by man's. His majesty transcends the heavens; all kingdoms are under his control.

Reformed accent: established throne means effectual rule over all—not a title without sway.

Psalms 103:20

20Bless the LORD, O you his angels, you mighty ones who do his word, obeying the voice of his word!

Arminian

Wesley: His hosts (v. 21 context)—angels in vast numbers, mighty power, unanimous concurrence, exquisite order. Mighty ones who do his word, obeying his voice.

Arminian hearing: angels model prompt obedience. Join their blessing; let created might serve the word, not self.

Reformed

Calvin: he addresses angels to teach that advancing divine glory is their highest end. Clothed with strength, they hang on God's word—nothing more honorable than to obey. They execute commandments and are always ready.

Reformed accent: angelic readiness shames our sloth; we exhort them for our awakening, not because they lag.

Psalms 103:21

21Bless the LORD, all his hosts, his ministers, who do his will!

Arminian

Wesley: Ministers—this Hebrew word is commonly used of the highest and most honourable sort of servants. Hosts and ministers who do his will.

Arminian pastoral: honor is measured by doing his will. Bless with the hosts; aim at the same obedience.

Reformed

Calvin: hosts are not the stars here but myriads who stand ready before the throne. Ministers who do his pleasure—appointed as our guardians, not idle spectators. Pleasure expresses joyful, cheerful obedience.

Reformed accent: the honor of ministry is glad doing of God's pleasure.

Psalms 103:22

22Bless the LORD, all his works, in all places of his dominion. Bless the LORD, O my soul!

Arminian

All works in all places of his dominion—then back to my soul. Cosmic chorus returns to the personal summons of v. 1.

Arminian pastoral: if mute works re-echo praise, how can the awakened soul stay silent? End where you began: bless the LORD, O my soul.

Reformed

Calvin: he addresses all creatures—though without speech, they ought to re-echo the Creator's praises—so we learn no corner of heaven or earth is without his praise. Express mention of all places of dominion stirs the faithful: if even lands where his voice is unheard ought not be mute, how can we be silent to whom he opens his mouth?

Reformed accent: the design of recounting benefits and empire was to animate the soul again—Bless Jehovah, O my soul.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on blessing the LORD for pardon, fatherly compassion, and everlasting steadfast love on those who fear him—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Psalm 103:1–22.

Arminian Notable voices

“The psalmist stirs up himself to praise God for all his benefits, and calls upon all his works to assist him therein. The eagles—which lives long in great strength and vigour. So great—so much above their deserts and expectations. Knoweth—the weakness and mortality of our natures, and the frailty of our condition, so that if he should let loose his hand upon us, we should be irrecoverably destroyed. His hosts—a title often given to the angels, in regard of their vast numbers, mighty power, unanimous concurrence, and exquisite order. Ministers—the highest and most honourable sort of servants.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Psalm 103)

Reformed Notable voices

“Every godly man is taught to give thanks for mercies bestowed upon himself and for the grace vouchsafed to all his chosen ones, by making a covenant of salvation with them. The Psalmist magnifies the mercy by which God sustains his people—not for any merit of theirs, for they only deserve severe punishment, but because he compassionates their frailty. Bless Jehovah, O my soul, and forget not any of his benefits: who forgiveth all thine iniquities. He will not always chide; he hath not dealt with us after our sins; as far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions. His goodness is from everlasting to everlasting upon them that fear him—to those who keep his covenant. Jehovah hath established his throne in the heavens; his kingdom ruleth over all.”
John Calvin · Commentary on the Book of Psalms (on Psalm 103)
