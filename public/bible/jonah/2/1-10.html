Passage

Jonah 2

Verses 1–10 · WEB

From the belly of the fish Jonah prays. The prayer is one psalm-like unit of thanksgiving, full of the language of the Psalms: distress, the waters of the deep, being cast out of God’s sight, looking toward the temple, and the LORD bringing life up from the pit. It closes with a vow and the confession “Salvation belongs to the LORD.” At the LORD’s word the fish vomits Jonah onto dry land.

Jonah 2:1

1Then Jonah prayed to the LORD, his God, out of the fish’s belly.

Arminian

Jonah finally prays, to the LORD, his God, from inside the fish. Arminian readers see the turning point: the man who would not speak to God on the ship now calls on him in distress. God’s discipline has brought his servant back to fellowship without forcing a mechanical response.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the covenant phrase his God: even in rebellion Jonah remains the LORD’s, and the LORD remains his. The prayer is the fruit of God’s gracious dealing, as distress drives a wayward child back to his Father.

Jonah 2:2

2He said, “I called because of my affliction to the LORD. He answered me. Out of the belly of Sheol I cried. You heard my voice.

Arminian

The prayer opens as thanksgiving for an answer already given: I called…He answered me. Out of the belly of Sheol uses the Psalms’ language for the brink of death, as in Psalm 18:4–6 and 30:3. Arminian readers note that rescue from drowning is itself the answer, and the fish is the means of that deliverance.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the prayer is answered grace, not a bargain. God hears the cry of the afflicted from the very edge of death, as the Psalms repeatedly confess. The prayer is cast as a psalm because Jonah borrows the language of Israel’s worship.

Jonah 2:3

3For you threw me into the depths, in the heart of the seas. The flood was all around me. All your waves and your billows passed over me.

Arminian

You threw me into the depths: Jonah sees God’s hand behind the sailors’ act. All your waves and your billows passed over me echoes Psalm 42:7. Arminian readers note that he receives the ordeal as God’s just discipline rather than blaming the sailors or bad fortune.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress Jonah’s recognition of primary causation. The sailors threw him, but God cast him into the deep, so human agents act within God’s governing hand. The waves are your waves, his to send and to still.

Jonah 2:4

4I said, ‘I have been banished from your sight; yet I will look again toward your holy temple.’

Arminian

I have been banished from your sight answers Jonah’s own flight from the LORD’s presence: what he sought now feels like judgment. Yet I will look again toward your holy temple expresses renewed hope, as in Psalm 31:22. Arminian readers see repentance turning him back toward the place of God’s mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faith reviving in the darkest place: despair is answered by looking to God’s appointed place of mercy and atonement. Solomon’s prayer in 1 Kings 8 asks God to hear prayers directed toward the temple from afar, and Jonah prays in that hope.

Jonah 2:5

5The waters surrounded me, even to the soul. The deep was around me. The weeds were wrapped around my head.

Arminian

The waters surrounded me, even to the soul vividly depicts drowning, with weeds wrapped around his head. Arminian readers note that the prayer remembers the full terror of the danger before the rescue. Honest lament is part of faithful prayer.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the poetic intensity and the Psalms’ imagery of the deep as a picture of death and judgment, as in Psalm 69:1–2. Jonah’s experience shows how low God may let his children go before he lifts them up.

Jonah 2:6

6I went down to the bottoms of the mountains. The earth barred me in forever; yet you have brought my life up from the pit, LORD my God.

Arminian

Jonah goes down to the roots of the mountains, where the earth barred him in forever. This is the low point of the downward movement that began at Joppa. Yet you have brought my life up from the pit, LORD my God marks the turn. Arminian readers see God’s deliverance answering the cry of a returning servant.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the heart of the gospel pattern: down to the pit, then raised by God. The word yet sets God’s saving act against the hopelessness of Jonah’s condition. Many Christian readers connect the language with Christ’s descent and resurrection, following Matthew 12:40.

Jonah 2:7

7“When my soul fainted within me, I remembered the LORD. My prayer came in to you, into your holy temple.

Arminian

When my soul fainted within me, I remembered the LORD. Arminian readers note that remembering is an act of turning: at the end of his strength Jonah calls the LORD to mind and prays, and the prayer reaches God’s holy temple.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the prayer reaches into your holy temple, God’s heavenly dwelling, as in Psalm 18:6. Fainting human strength is the occasion for God’s grace. Even remembering God in distress is itself a work of grace in the believer.

Jonah 2:8

8Those who regard vain idols forsake their own mercy.

Arminian

Those who regard vain idols forsake their own mercy. Arminian readers see a contrast between idolaters and the one who trusts the true God. Some note the irony that the pagan sailors have just turned from their gods to the LORD, while Jonah himself had forsaken mercy by fleeing.

Reformed

Reformed readers read mercy here as hesed, God’s steadfast covenant love, which idolaters abandon. The verse contrasts false worship with the true God’s faithfulness. Many note Jonah’s possible blind spot: he speaks of idolaters with confidence, yet 4:2 shows he resents God’s mercy to Gentiles.

Jonah 2:9

9But I will sacrifice to you with the voice of thanksgiving. I will pay that which I have vowed. Salvation belongs to the LORD.”

Arminian

Jonah vows thanksgiving and sacrifice, echoing the sailors in 1:16. Salvation belongs to the LORD is the climax. Arminian readers affirm that deliverance comes from God alone and cannot be earned. Jonah receives it and answers with grateful worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers often treat Salvation belongs to the LORD as a compact summary of the Bible’s teaching on salvation: deliverance is God’s work from first to last, as in Psalm 3:8. Jonah’s rescue is wholly God’s initiative and power.

Key contrast Both affirm that salvation is God’s gift and not human achievement. Arminian readers typically see the line as consistent with grace that enables and is freely received. Reformed readers often read it more broadly as confessing God’s sovereign, effectual work in salvation from beginning to end.

Jonah 2:10

10Then the LORD spoke to the fish, and it vomited out Jonah on the dry land.

Arminian

The LORD speaks to the fish, and it vomits Jonah onto dry land. Arminian readers note the brief, almost comic close. God commands the creatures, and his servant is returned to the starting point for a second chance.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress God’s word commanding creation, the same word that appointed the fish. The crude verb vomited undercuts any heroism: Jonah is delivered by God’s power, not his own virtue. The way is now open for the recommissioning in chapter 3.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
