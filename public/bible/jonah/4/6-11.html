Passage

Jonah 4

Verses 6–11 · WEB

The LORD God appoints a vine to shade Jonah, and Jonah is very glad. Then God appoints a worm that kills the vine, and a scorching east wind, and Jonah again asks to die. God asks whether his anger over the vine is right, and Jonah insists it is. The LORD contrasts Jonah’s concern for a plant he did not grow with his own concern for Nineveh. It holds more than 120,000 people who cannot tell their right hand from their left, and many animals. The book ends on that unanswered question.

Jonah 4:6

6The LORD God prepared a vine and made it to come up over Jonah, that it might be a shade over his head to deliver him from his discomfort. So Jonah was exceedingly glad because of the vine.

Arminian

The LORD God prepared a vine, another appointment, to shade Jonah and deliver him from his discomfort. Jonah is exceedingly glad, the only time he is happy in the book. Arminian readers note that God’s kindness extends even to his sulking prophet, and God uses that kindness to teach him.

Reformed

Reformed readers again stress God’s sovereign appointment of a creature for his purpose. The vine is a providential object lesson. The word discomfort can also mean evil, a play on Jonah’s inner state that God is addressing.

Jonah 4:7

7But God prepared a worm at dawn the next day, and it chewed on the vine so that it withered.

Arminian

But God prepared a worm at dawn, which destroys the vine. Arminian readers see God deliberately removing comfort as discipline aimed at Jonah’s heart, not at harming him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God appoints small things as well as great ones, a worm as surely as a great fish. Providence governs the minute details of creation, and God uses loss to teach his children.

Jonah 4:8

8When the sun arose, God prepared a sultry east wind; and the sun beat on Jonah’s head, so that he was faint and requested for himself that he might die. He said, “It is better for me to die than to live.”

Arminian

God prepares a scorching east wind, and the sun beats on Jonah until he faints and again asks to die. Arminian readers note that the repeated wish to die shows how far Jonah’s feelings are out of line with God’s purpose. God is pressing him toward a change of heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the third appointment completing the lesson, as God brings Jonah to the end of himself. Jonah’s despair over personal discomfort contrasts with his indifference to the destruction of a city.

Jonah 4:9

9God said to Jonah, “Is it right for you to be angry about the vine?” He said, “I am right to be angry, even to death.”

Arminian

God repeats the question, now about the vine, and Jonah insists he is right to be angry, even to death. Arminian readers see stubborn resistance continuing. God does not force agreement but keeps reasoning with him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the persistence of sin in the believer and the patience of God in confronting it. Jonah’s defiant answer sets up God’s final argument.

Jonah 4:10

10The LORD said, “You have been concerned for the vine, for which you have not labored, neither made it grow; which came up in a night and perished in a night.

Arminian

The LORD points out that Jonah cares for a vine he did not plant or grow, which came up and perished in a night. Arminian readers see God appealing to Jonah’s own feelings in order to lead him to understand divine compassion.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the contrast between Jonah’s small, self-interested pity and God’s great, sovereign mercy. Jonah had no claim on the vine, yet he grieves its loss. God is Creator and Lord of Nineveh, and his concern is greater and rightful.

Jonah 4:11

11Shouldn’t I be concerned for Nineveh, that great city, in which are more than one hundred twenty thousand persons who can’t discern between their right hand and their left hand, and also many animals?”

Arminian

Shouldn’t I be concerned for Nineveh? God’s compassion extends to 120,000 people who can’t discern right from left, and to many animals. Arminian readers see here the wideness of God’s mercy toward the nations and his desire that none perish, as 2 Peter 3:9 says. The question is left open for Jonah and for every reader to answer.

Reformed

Reformed readers equally affirm God’s compassion for Nineveh and his care for all creatures, as Psalm 145:9 says. They stress that this mercy is God’s sovereign prerogative, freely exercised. The book ends without Jonah’s answer, pressing readers to rejoice in, rather than resent, God’s mercy to others.

Key contrast Both affirm God’s genuine compassion for Nineveh and the wideness of his mercy to the nations. Arminian readers often connect the verse with God’s universal saving desire for all people. Reformed readers often stress God’s sovereign freedom to show mercy to whom he will. Many also distinguish God’s general compassion for all creatures from his particular saving purpose.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
