Passage

Jonah 4

Verses 1–5 · WEB

Nineveh’s rescue displeases Jonah greatly. He prays in anger and says this is why he fled. He knew the LORD is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, abundant in loving kindness, and one who relents of harm, echoing Exodus 34:6–7. He asks to die. The LORD asks whether his anger is right. Jonah goes out east of the city, builds a booth, and waits to see what will happen.

Jonah 4:1

1But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was angry.

Arminian

But it displeased Jonah exceedingly. The Hebrew uses the same word for evil that Nineveh turned from: God turned from evil, and Jonah takes it as evil. Arminian readers see the shocking reversal of a prophet angered by mercy. It exposes how one can preach God’s word while resenting God’s heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that a believer may still harbor deep sin and narrowness of heart. Jonah’s anger is a sin of resentment toward God’s grace to others. The book does not hide its prophet’s failings, which keeps the focus on God’s character rather than human virtue.

Jonah 4:2

2He prayed to the LORD, and said, “Please, LORD, wasn’t this what I said when I was still in my own country? Therefore I hurried to flee to Tarshish, for I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness, and you relent of doing harm.

Arminian

Jonah reveals why he fled: he knew God is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, abundant in loving kindness, and relenting of harm. That is the creed of Exodus 34:6–7. Arminian readers see this as the key to the book: God’s merciful character, which desires that sinners turn and live, as Ezekiel 33:11 says.

Reformed

Reformed readers equally affirm this confession of God’s character and note the irony of Jonah using Israel’s creed as a complaint. They stress that God’s mercy is free and sovereign, his to show to whom he will, as Exodus 33:19 says. Jonah objects to precisely that freedom.

Jonah 4:3

3Therefore now, LORD, take, I beg you, my life from me, for it is better for me to die than to live.”

Arminian

Jonah asks to die, which echoes Elijah in 1 Kings 19:4, though here the cause is wounded pride rather than exhaustion. Arminian readers see the depth of his resentment: he would rather die than live in a world where God spares his enemies.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the self-centered despair of a believer whose will is opposed to God’s. God does not grant the request but patiently reasons with him, which shows his mercy to the prophet as well as to Nineveh.

Jonah 4:4

4The LORD said, “Is it right for you to be angry?”

Arminian

Is it right for you to be angry? God answers with a question rather than a rebuke. Arminian readers see divine patience and an invitation for Jonah to examine his heart and freely come to share God’s compassion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s gentle dealing with his child: he exposes sin by asking. Jonah does not answer, and God will press the question again through the vine. God is patient in sanctifying his servants.

Jonah 4:5

5Then Jonah went out of the city and sat on the east side of the city, and there made himself a booth and sat under it in the shade, until he might see what would become of the city.

Arminian

Jonah leaves the city, builds a booth on the east side, and waits to see what will happen, perhaps hoping the forty days will still bring judgment. Arminian readers note his refusal to accept God’s decision, as he sets himself up as a spectator.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the stubbornness of a heart that still hopes for judgment after God has shown mercy. Some suggest the verse looks back to the time between 3:4 and 3:10, but most read it in sequence after Jonah’s complaint.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
