Passage

Jonah 3

Verses 5–10 · WEB

The people of Nineveh believe God and fast in sackcloth, from greatest to least. The king leaves his throne for sackcloth and ashes and decrees a fast for people and animals. He calls everyone to cry to God and turn from evil and violence, hoping God may turn and relent. God sees their works and relents of the disaster he had announced, and he does not do it.

Jonah 3:5

5The people of Nineveh believed God; and they proclaimed a fast and put on sackcloth, from their greatest even to their least.

Arminian

The people of Nineveh believed God. Their response is immediate and widespread, from the greatest to the least. Arminian readers see genuine faith responding to God’s word as people freely turn in repentance. Jesus later holds up the Ninevites’ repentance as an example in Matthew 12:41.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree the faith and repentance are real, as Jesus’ commendation shows. They would see such a widespread response to a brief warning as evidence of God’s gracious work among the people. Commentators across both traditions differ on whether this was saving faith in the full sense or a real but more limited turning in fear of judgment.

Jonah 3:6

6The news reached the king of Nineveh, and he arose from his throne, took off his royal robe, covered himself with sackcloth, and sat in ashes.

Arminian

The news reaches the king, who leaves his throne, removes his robe, and sits in sackcloth and ashes. Arminian readers note that the most powerful man in the city humbles himself fully. Repentance is a free and costly response to God’s warning.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the king’s humility as the fruit of God’s word working through the city. When God humbles the proud he can reach the throne itself, and outward signs of mourning express inward submission.

Jonah 3:7

7He made a proclamation and published through Nineveh by the decree of the king and his nobles, saying, “Let neither man nor animal, herd nor flock, taste anything; let them not feed, nor drink water;

Arminian

The king and his nobles decree a fast for both man and animal. Arminian readers see a whole-city response that includes even the animals, which shows how seriously the city takes the threat.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the solemnity of the decree. Including animals reflects ancient Near Eastern mourning customs and anticipates God’s own concern for the animals in 4:11.

Jonah 3:8

8but let them be covered with sackcloth, both man and animal, and let them cry mightily to God. Yes, let them turn everyone from his evil way and from the violence that is in his hands.

Arminian

The decree goes beyond ritual: let them cry mightily to God and turn everyone from his evil way and from the violence that is in his hands. Arminian readers stress that true repentance includes ethical change and turning from specific sins, not only outward signs.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that true repentance turns from evil in deed as well as in feeling. The phrase the violence that is in his hands names Nineveh’s characteristic sin, which the prophets elsewhere associate with Assyria, as in Nahum 3:1.

Jonah 3:9

9Who knows whether God will not turn and relent, and turn away from his fierce anger, so that we might not perish?”

Arminian

Who knows whether God will not turn and relent? The king hopes but does not presume. Arminian readers see this as a fitting posture of repentance: God is free to show mercy, and human response genuinely matters to how he acts. The same uncertain hope appears in Joel 2:14.

Reformed

Reformed readers also commend the king’s humble uncertainty, since repentance does not put God in debt. They read the hope as reliance on God’s revealed character as merciful, not on any claim the Ninevites have. The relenting the king hopes for is the same mercy described in Exodus 34:6–7.

Jonah 3:10

10God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way. God relented of the disaster which he said he would do to them, and he didn’t do it.

Arminian

God saw their works, that they turned, and relented of the disaster. Arminian readers take this as a real, responsive change in God’s action toward Nineveh. God relates to people in time and responds to their repentance. That is consistent with his unchanging character and stated principle in Jeremiah 18:7–10. Some Arminian theologians speak of God’s plans as truly affected by creaturely choices, while others stress that he foresaw the response.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm that God truly spared Nineveh in response to repentance and that the narrative means it. They understand relenting as God carrying out his unchanging purpose to show mercy to the repentant, revealed in the conditional warning. The repentance itself is ordained as the means. Classic Reformed theology describes such language as accommodated to human understanding: God is not altered within himself, but his dealings with people truly change as they turn.

Key contrast Both affirm that God genuinely spared Nineveh in response to its repentance and that he is faithful to his own character. Arminian readers typically treat the relenting as God’s real response to free human choices within his consistent character. Reformed readers typically read it as the outworking, in time, of God’s unchanging decree, which includes the repentance as its ordained means, and they describe the language of relenting as accommodated speech.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
