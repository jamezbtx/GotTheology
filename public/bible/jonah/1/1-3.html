Passage

Jonah 1

Verses 1–3 · WEB

The LORD’s word comes to Jonah son of Amittai, the prophet named in 2 Kings 14:25, with a summons to preach against Nineveh, the great Assyrian city. Jonah rises, but to flee toward Tarshish in the opposite direction. He goes down to Joppa, pays the fare, and boards a ship to escape the presence of the LORD.

Jonah 1:1

1Now the LORD’s word came to Jonah the son of Amittai, saying,

Arminian

The book opens with the formula used for the prophets: the LORD’s word came. Jonah son of Amittai is the prophet of 2 Kings 14:25, who foretold the restoration of Israel’s borders under Jeroboam II. Arminian readers note that the call comes as a word to be heard and answered, which sets up the book’s drama of a prophet who can refuse what God plainly asks.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the initiative is wholly God’s. Jonah does not volunteer; the word comes to him. The same sovereign word that sends him will pursue him through storm and fish, so the opening line already anticipates that God’s purpose for Nineveh will not be overturned by the prophet’s reluctance.

Jonah 1:2

2“Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and preach against it, for their wickedness has come up before me.”

Arminian

God sends an Israelite prophet to Israel’s great enemy, the capital of Assyria. Their wickedness has come up before me echoes the language of Genesis 18:20–21 about Sodom. Arminian readers see God’s concern reaching beyond Israel to the nations. The warning itself carries an implied opportunity, since a God who sends a warning often has in view turning rather than only ruin.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Nineveh’s evil is real and that God is the righteous Judge of all nations, not Israel alone. Sending a preacher is the ordinary means God uses to accomplish his purposes among a people. Whether this message ends in judgment or mercy rests on God’s own counsel, which the rest of the book will unfold.

Jonah 1:3

3But Jonah rose up to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD. He went down to Joppa, and found a ship going to Tarshish; so he paid its fare, and went down into it, to go with them to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD.

Arminian

Jonah rises, but to flee. Tarshish lies far to the west, the opposite direction from Nineveh, and the repeated phrase from the presence of the LORD shows deliberate refusal of a prophetic office. He goes down to Joppa and down into the ship, the first steps of a downward movement. Arminian readers see here genuine resistance: a called man can truly disobey God’s revealed command.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Jonah’s flight is real sin and fully his own. They also note the irony that no one can actually escape the presence of the God of heaven, as Psalm 139:7–10 confesses. Jonah’s rebellion sets the stage for God’s sovereign pursuit; it does not frustrate God’s purpose but becomes the occasion for displaying it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
