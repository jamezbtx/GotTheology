Passage

Jonah 1

Verses 4–10 · WEB

The LORD hurls a great wind on the sea, and the ship is close to breaking up. The pagan sailors pray to their gods and throw cargo overboard while Jonah sleeps below deck. The captain wakes him to pray. Lots fall on Jonah, and under questioning he confesses that he is a Hebrew who fears the LORD, maker of sea and dry land. The sailors are terrified once they learn he is fleeing from that God.

Jonah 1:4

4But the LORD sent out a great wind on the sea, and there was a mighty storm on the sea, so that the ship was likely to break up.

Arminian

But the LORD answers Jonah’s flight with action: he sends out, literally hurls, a great wind on the sea. Arminian readers take this as God’s active discipline of a disobedient servant, aimed at bringing him back. The storm is personal and purposeful, and it is directed at Jonah rather than being a random disaster.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God governing wind and sea by his providence, as Psalm 135:6–7 says. The storm is a sovereign means to recover his prophet and fulfill his word. Both traditions agree the LORD is Lord of creation here; the narrative contrasts the God who made the sea with a man trying to use the sea to escape him.

Jonah 1:5

5Then the mariners were afraid, and every man cried to his god. They threw the cargo that was in the ship into the sea to lighten the ship. But Jonah had gone down into the innermost parts of the ship and he was laying down, and was fast asleep.

Arminian

The mariners are afraid and every man cries to his god. They do what they can, praying and lightening the ship, while the prophet of the true God has gone down into the innermost part of the ship and is fast asleep. Arminian readers note the sharp irony: pagans pray while the believer sleeps in disobedience, unaware of the danger his choice has brought on others.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the irony and the dulling effect of sin, which can leave a believer insensible while others suffer for his rebellion. The sailors’ many gods cannot calm the sea. Their prayers show the universal sense of the divine that Romans 1 describes, which still needs the true God’s revelation.

Jonah 1:6

6So the ship master came to him, and said to him, “What do you mean, sleeper? Arise, call on your God! Maybe your God will notice us, so that we won’t perish.”

Arminian

The ship master rebukes the sleeper and orders him to call on his God, in words that echo God’s own command, “Arise.” Maybe your God will notice us expresses the pagan’s uncertain hope. Arminian readers see God using even an unbeliever to rouse his servant. They also note that the captain’s humble maybe anticipates the Ninevite king’s “Who knows?” in 3:9.

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear the irony of a heathen calling the prophet to prayer. The captain’s maybe shows the uncertainty of religion that lacks God’s covenant word. Yet God uses the captain’s rebuke as part of his providential means of bringing Jonah to account.

Jonah 1:7

7They all said to each other, “Come! Let’s cast lots, that we may know who is responsible for this evil that is on us.” So they cast lots, and the lot fell on Jonah.

Arminian

The sailors cast lots to find out who is responsible, and the lot falls on Jonah. Arminian readers note that God directs the outcome while the sailors act in good faith with the means they know. The account exposes Jonah publicly without overriding anyone’s choices along the way.

Reformed

Reformed readers often cite Proverbs 16:33: the lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD. The fall of the lot is not chance but God’s exact governance of a seemingly random event, bringing hidden sin to light.

Key contrast Both agree God guided the lot to Jonah. Arminian readers usually describe this as God’s particular intervention in a specific case. Reformed readers usually treat it as an example of meticulous providence over every event, appealing to Proverbs 16:33.

Jonah 1:8

8Then they asked him, “Tell us, please, for whose cause this evil is on us. What is your occupation? Where do you come from? What is your country? Of what people are you?”

Arminian

The sailors press Jonah with rapid questions about his cause, occupation, origin, country, and people. Arminian readers see a fair and humane inquiry. The pagans seek the truth before acting and treat Jonah more justly than he has treated his commission.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the questions force Jonah to confess his identity before the very nations he was sent to serve. God brings his fleeing servant to bear witness after all, even if only by accusation, so the providence that pursued Jonah now makes him speak.

Jonah 1:9

9He said to them, “I am a Hebrew, and I fear the LORD, the God of heaven, who has made the sea and the dry land.”

Arminian

Jonah’s confession is orthodox: he is a Hebrew who fears the LORD, the God of heaven, maker of sea and dry land. Arminian readers point out the gap between creed and conduct. He confesses the Maker of the sea while running across it, a warning that right belief does not guarantee obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the contradiction of right confession and wrong life. Yet they see God’s word going out even through a disobedient man’s mouth: the sailors hear of the one true Creator. The confession sets up their own turning to the LORD in 1:16.

Jonah 1:10

10Then the men were exceedingly afraid, and said to him, “What have you done?” For the men knew that he was fleeing from the presence of the LORD, because he had told them.

Arminian

The men become exceedingly afraid once they understand whom Jonah is fleeing. What have you done? is a reproach that the prophet should have felt himself. Arminian readers see pagans quickly grasping the seriousness of resisting the Creator, while the prophet seems slow to repent.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the sailors’ fear grows as their knowledge of God grows: the knowledge of who God is brings dread of his judgment. Jonah’s own account of his flight condemns him, and the sailors now see the storm as the hand of Jonah’s God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
