Passage

Jonah 1

Verses 11–17 · WEB

With the storm rising, Jonah tells the sailors to throw him into the sea. They row hard for land first, then cry to the LORD not to hold this man’s blood against them. When Jonah goes overboard the sea falls calm. The sailors fear the LORD, offer sacrifice, and make vows. The LORD appoints a huge fish to swallow Jonah, and he is inside it three days and three nights. In WEB and English numbering this is 1:17; Hebrew Bibles number it 2:1.

Jonah 1:11

11Then they said to him, “What shall we do to you, that the sea may be calm to us?” For the sea grew more and more stormy.

Arminian

The sailors ask Jonah what they should do with him, since he knows his God. The sea grows more and more stormy as they talk. Arminian readers see the sailors seeking a way of responding rightly to Jonah’s God rather than simply acting on fear.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the worsening storm presses the question and keeps the matter in God’s hand. The pagans recognize that the remedy must come from the God who sent the storm, and they turn to his prophet to learn it.

Jonah 1:12

12He said to them, “Take me up, and throw me into the sea. Then the sea will be calm for you; for I know that because of me this great storm is on you.”

Arminian

Jonah tells them to throw him into the sea and takes responsibility: because of me this great storm is on you. Readers debate his motive. Some see self-sacrifice to save the sailors, others a wish to die rather than go to Nineveh, since 4:3 and 4:8 show that longing. Arminian readers often stress that Jonah owns his guilt, while noting he still does not pray for himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Jonah owning his sin and its consequences, a first step toward submission. Many note the uncertainty about his inner motive and resist reading too much into it. Christian interpreters have often seen a typological shadow here of one man cast out so that others may be saved.

Jonah 1:13

13Nevertheless the men rowed hard to get them back to the land; but they could not, for the sea grew more and more stormy against them.

Arminian

The men row hard to bring the ship back to land rather than sacrifice Jonah. Arminian readers admire the pagans’ compassion, which contrasts with Jonah’s later lack of concern for Nineveh’s people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that human effort cannot succeed against God’s purpose. The sea grows more stormy against them, showing that God has determined the means of deliverance and no rowing will change it.

Jonah 1:14

14Therefore they cried to the LORD, and said, “We beg you, LORD, we beg you, don’t let us die for this man’s life, and don’t lay on us innocent blood; for you, LORD, have done as it pleased you.”

Arminian

The sailors now pray to the LORD by name, asking not to be held guilty of innocent blood. For you, LORD, have done as it pleased you acknowledges God’s hand in the storm and the lot. Arminian readers see sincere prayer from Gentiles who have begun to know the true God through Jonah’s confession.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the sailors confessing God’s sovereign good pleasure, in the spirit of Psalm 115:3. Yet they remain conscientious about the moral weight of their own act. Sovereignty and responsibility stand together in their prayer, and both traditions affirm that pairing.

Jonah 1:15

15So they took up Jonah and threw him into the sea; and the sea ceased its raging.

Arminian

They throw Jonah into the sea, and the sea ceases its raging. Arminian readers see the immediate calm as God’s confirmation that the sailors acted rightly and that the storm was aimed at Jonah. Both traditions recall Jesus stilling the sea in the Gospels as a parallel display of the same divine authority.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s precise control of creation. The storm stops at the moment his purpose is served, which vindicates the sailors and shows that nothing in the sea moves outside his command.

Jonah 1:16

16Then the men feared the LORD exceedingly; and they offered a sacrifice to the LORD and made vows.

Arminian

The men fear the LORD exceedingly, offer sacrifice, and make vows. Their fear has turned from terror of the storm to reverence for the LORD. Arminian readers see Gentiles responding to revelation and turning to God, a preview of Nineveh and evidence that God’s mercy reaches the nations.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Gentiles brought to worship the true God, even through a disobedient prophet. Commentators differ on whether this marks full conversion or a pagan addition of one more god, and the text does not settle it. God’s purposes of grace advance despite Jonah’s failures.

Jonah 1:17

17The LORD prepared a huge fish to swallow up Jonah, and Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights.

Arminian

The LORD prepared, or appointed, a huge fish. This is the first of four appointments in the book: fish, vine, worm, and wind. The fish is God’s rescue, not his punishment, saving Jonah from drowning. Jesus cites Jonah’s three days and three nights as a sign of his own death and resurrection in Matthew 12:40. In WEB and English numbering this verse is 1:17; Hebrew Bibles number it 2:1.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the sovereign appointment: God commands creatures to serve his purpose. The fish shows both judgment and mercy, since Jonah is brought down into death-like darkness yet preserved. Reformed and Arminian readers alike follow Jesus in seeing a sign of the Son of Man’s burial and rising.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
