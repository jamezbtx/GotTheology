Passage

Numbers 26

Verses 52–65 · WEB

The LORD now gives the principle for dividing the land: larger tribes receive larger inheritances, yet the land is to be divided by lot. The text does not say exactly how the two principles work together. A common suggestion is that the lot fixes each tribe’s location while size governs its extent, but this is an interpretation. The Levites are counted separately, from a month old and upward, because they receive no land inheritance. Their total of 23,000 compares with 22,000 in Num 3:39. The genealogy recalls Amram and Jochebed, the parents of Aaron, Moses, and Miriam, and the deaths of Nadab and Abihu (Leviticus 10). The chapter closes by noting that none of those counted in the first census remained except Caleb and Joshua. This is the fulfillment of the LORD’s word in Num 14:29–30. Both traditions read the closing verses as a solemn witness that God keeps both his warnings and his promises.

Numbers 26:52

52The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses about how the counted people will receive the land.

Arminian readers note that the census was taken with the inheritance in view.

Reformed

God himself sets the terms for dividing the land.

Reformed readers stress that the inheritance is God’s gift, distributed by his instruction. Both traditions read the verse as introducing the rules that follow.

Numbers 26:53

53“To these the land shall be divided for an inheritance according to the number of names.

Arminian

The land is to be divided “according to the number of names.”

Arminian readers see a fair principle that gives each tribe what it needs.

Reformed

The census figures now serve God’s gift of land.

Reformed readers see God’s promise to Abraham moving toward fulfillment. Both traditions agree that the counting was for inheritance, not for pride in numbers.

Numbers 26:54

54To the more you shall give the more inheritance, and to the fewer you shall give the less inheritance. To everyone according to those who were counted of him shall his inheritance be given.

Arminian

Larger tribes receive more, smaller tribes less, each according to its count.

Arminian readers note the fairness and practical wisdom of the arrangement.

Reformed

The principle provides for every family according to its size.

Reformed readers stress God’s justice and care in distributing his gifts. Both traditions agree that the rule guards against favoritism.

Numbers 26:55

55Notwithstanding, the land shall be divided by lot. According to the names of the tribes of their fathers they shall inherit.

Arminian

“Notwithstanding, the land shall be divided by lot.” Inheritance is by the names of the ancestral tribes. How the lot and the size principle combine is not spelled out.

Arminian readers note that the lot removes human manipulation from the process. They also affirm that God directs the outcome (compare Prov 16:33).

Reformed

The lot places the decision in God’s hands.

Reformed readers stress God’s sovereign disposal of the inheritance (compare Prov 16:33). Both traditions agree that God governs the lot, and that the arrangement combines fairness with dependence on God.

Numbers 26:56

56According to the lot shall their inheritance be divided between the more and the fewer.”

Arminian

The lot decides between the larger and the smaller tribes.

Arminian readers see a process that the whole people could accept as fair.

Reformed

The lot expresses God’s decision for each tribe.

Reformed readers see the inheritance received as a gift from God rather than seized by strength. Both traditions read the verse as completing the instructions on dividing the land.

Numbers 26:57

57These are those who were counted of the Levites after their families: of Gershon, the family of the Gershonites; of Kohath, the family of the Kohathites; of Merari, the family of the Merarites.

Arminian

The Levites are counted by their clans: Gershonites, Kohathites, and Merarites.

Arminian readers note that the Levites are counted separately because of their distinct calling.

Reformed

The three Levitical clans correspond to their duties described in Numbers 3–4.

Reformed readers see God’s order for his servants continuing into the new generation. Both traditions agree that the Levites’ separate count reflects their service.

Numbers 26:58

58These are the families of Levi: the family of the Libnites, the family of the Hebronites, the family of the Mahlites, the family of the Mushites, and the family of the Korahites. Kohath became the father of Amram.

Arminian

Five Levitical families are named, including the Korahites. The list does not give every family named in Num 3:18–20. Kohath’s son Amram is mentioned to introduce Moses and Aaron.

Arminian readers note that the Korahites remain among the Levitical families despite Korah’s rebellion.

Reformed

The families of Levi continue.

Reformed readers see God’s mercy in preserving the Korahites (26:11). Both traditions agree that the list is selective and leads into the family of Moses and Aaron.

Numbers 26:59

59The name of Amram’s wife was Jochebed, the daughter of Levi, who was born to Levi in Egypt. She bore to Amram Aaron and Moses, and Miriam their sister.

Arminian

Amram’s wife was Jochebed, “the daughter of Levi,” who bore Aaron, Moses, and Miriam. How this fits the chronology of the stay in Egypt is discussed by interpreters. Many take “daughter of Levi” to mean a descendant of Levi.

Arminian readers note that the family of Israel’s deliverers is named with care, including Miriam.

Reformed

The verse names the family God used to lead Israel out of Egypt.

Reformed readers see God’s providence in preparing this family for its role. Both traditions agree that the genealogical question is open and that the verse’s main point is to identify Moses, Aaron, and Miriam.

Numbers 26:60

60To Aaron were born Nadab and Abihu, Eleazar and Ithamar.

Arminian

Aaron’s sons were Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar.

Arminian readers note the priestly family whose story is part of Israel’s history.

Reformed

The priestly line is traced.

Reformed readers see God’s choice of Aaron’s house for the priesthood. Both traditions read the verse as leading to the note about Nadab and Abihu.

Numbers 26:61

61Nadab and Abihu died when they offered strange fire before the LORD.

Arminian

Nadab and Abihu “died when they offered strange fire before the LORD” (Lev 10:1–2).

Arminian readers see a warning that privilege does not excuse disobedience in worship.

Reformed

Their deaths show God’s holiness in worship.

Reformed readers stress that God is to be worshiped as he commands. Both traditions agree that the reminder fits a chapter that recalls past judgments as warnings for the new generation.

Numbers 26:62

62Those who were counted of them were twenty-three thousand, every male from a month old and upward; for they were not counted among the children of Israel, because there was no inheritance given them among the children of Israel.

Arminian

The Levites number 23,000, every male from a month old and upward. They were counted separately because they received no inheritance in the land.

Arminian readers note that the Levites’ portion was the LORD and his service (compare Num 18:20–24).

Reformed

The figure compares with 22,000 in Num 3:39.

Reformed readers stress that the Levites’ inheritance was the LORD himself. Both traditions agree that their separate count reflects their unique calling.

Numbers 26:63

63These are those who were counted by Moses and Eleazar the priest, who counted the children of Israel in the plains of Moab by the Jordan at Jericho.

Arminian

Moses and Eleazar conducted this census “in the plains of Moab by the Jordan at Jericho.”

Arminian readers note that the new generation is counted at the threshold of the land.

Reformed

The verse closes the census by naming those who took it and where.

Reformed readers stress God’s faithfulness in bringing his people to this point. Both traditions read the verse as framing the census with its setting in 26:3.

Numbers 26:64

64But among these there was not a man of them who were counted by Moses and Aaron the priest, who counted the children of Israel in the wilderness of Sinai.

Arminian

Not one of those counted by Moses and Aaron in the wilderness of Sinai remained among them.

Arminian readers often take this as a real warning that belonging to God’s people outwardly does not by itself guarantee entering his promise. They connect it with the warnings in 1 Cor 10 and Hebrews 3–4.

Reformed

The verse records the fulfillment of God’s judgment on the generation that refused to enter the land.

Reformed readers also read these warnings as real. They often stress that God uses them as means to keep his people, and they distinguish outward membership from saving faith. Both traditions agree that the warning is to be heeded.

Numbers 26:65

65For the LORD had said of them, “They shall surely die in the wilderness.” There was not a man left of them, except Caleb the son of Jephunneh, and Joshua the son of Nun.

Arminian

The LORD had said, “They shall surely die in the wilderness,” and none was left except Caleb son of Jephunneh and Joshua son of Nun (Num 14:29–30).

Arminian readers stress that Caleb and Joshua trusted God when others did not. Their faithfulness was real and their reward sure.

Reformed

God’s word of judgment and his promise to Caleb and Joshua were both fulfilled exactly.

Reformed readers stress God’s faithfulness to his word and his grace in preserving the two who followed him fully. Both traditions agree that God keeps both his warnings and his promises, and that the new generation stands ready to enter the land.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
