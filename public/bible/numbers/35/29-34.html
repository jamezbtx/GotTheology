Passage

Numbers 35

Verses 29–34 · WEB

The chapter closes with lasting principles: murder requires more than one witness, no ransom can be taken for a murderer or to shorten an accidental killer’s stay in refuge, and bloodshed pollutes the land, which can be cleansed only by the blood of the one who shed it. The land must remain undefiled because the LORD dwells among Israel. Arminian and Reformed readers treat these verses plainly and as shared ground about the sanctity of life and the holiness of God’s presence.

Numbers 35:29

29“ ‘These things shall be for a statute and ordinance to you throughout your generations in all your dwellings.

Arminian

These rules are a statute for all generations in all their dwellings. They are not temporary.

Arminian readers see lasting principles of justice for Israel’s life in the land. Every generation is to keep them.

Reformed

The law is meant to endure. Its principles shape Israel’s communities.

Reformed readers see God’s law giving stable foundations for justice. Both traditions read this as shared ground, while recognizing that Christians apply Israel’s civil laws through their underlying principles rather than as a direct code.

Numbers 35:30

30“ ‘Whoever kills any person, the murderer shall be slain based on the testimony of witnesses; but one witness shall not testify alone against any person so that he dies.

Arminian

A murderer may be put to death only on the testimony of witnesses; one witness is not enough. Evidence must be solid.

Arminian readers see protection against false accusation. Justice requires care.

Reformed

The two-witness principle appears also in Deuteronomy 17:6 and 19:15, and Jesus and Paul draw on it (Matthew 18:16; 2 Corinthians 13:1).

Reformed readers see God’s concern for truth in judgment. Both traditions share this reading.

Numbers 35:31

31“ ‘Moreover you shall take no ransom for the life of a murderer who is guilty of death. He shall surely be put to death.

Arminian

No ransom may be taken for a murderer’s life. Wealth cannot buy exemption from justice.

Arminian readers see equality before the law. The rich cannot escape what the poor would face.

Reformed

The life of the victim cannot be priced. Money cannot satisfy the demand of justice for murder.

Reformed readers see the sanctity of life made in God’s image. Both traditions read this as shared ground, and both note that it guards against corruption in judgment.

Numbers 35:32

32“ ‘You shall take no ransom for him who has fled to his city of refuge, that he may come again to dwell in the land before the death of the priest.

Arminian

No ransom may be taken to let the accidental killer return home before the priest’s death. The refuge rules cannot be bought out.

Arminian readers see fairness: release comes by God’s appointed means, not by payment.

Reformed

Only the priest’s death ends the confinement. No other price is accepted.

Reformed readers see an emphasis that many Christians link cautiously with the uniqueness of Christ’s death as the way of release. Both traditions share this reading without pressing it beyond the text.

Numbers 35:33

33“ ‘So you shall not pollute the land where you live; for blood pollutes the land. No atonement can be made for the land for the blood that is shed in it, but by the blood of him who shed it.

Arminian

Israel must not pollute the land, for blood pollutes it, and no atonement can be made for the land except by the blood of the one who shed it. Bloodshed is a defilement, not only a crime.

Arminian readers see the seriousness of murder before God.

Reformed

The land itself is defiled by unpunished murder. Justice is also a matter of holiness.

Reformed readers see the moral order of creation upheld by God. Both traditions read this plainly as shared ground; Christians often recall Abel’s blood crying from the ground (Genesis 4:10).

Numbers 35:34

34You shall not defile the land which you inhabit, where I dwell; for I, the LORD, dwell among the children of Israel.’ ”

Arminian

Israel must not defile the land where the LORD dwells, for he dwells among them. God’s presence is the reason for holiness.

Arminian readers see justice flowing from relationship with the God who lives among his people.

Reformed

The LORD’s dwelling in the land makes its purity a matter of worship. Justice and holiness are joined.

Reformed readers see God’s presence as the center of Israel’s life. Both traditions share this reading and see it fulfilled in God dwelling among his people in Christ and by his Spirit.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
