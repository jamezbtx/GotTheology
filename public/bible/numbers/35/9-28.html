Passage

Numbers 35

Verses 9–28 · WEB

God establishes six cities of refuge, three on each side of the Jordan, for anyone, Israelite or foreigner, who kills a person unintentionally. The law distinguishes murder from accidental killing by weapons, hostility, and intent, and requires the congregation to judge between the killer and the avenger of blood. An accidental killer stays in the refuge city until the high priest dies, then may go home. Both traditions read this as shared ground about justice and mercy, and many cautiously see Christ as refuge and as the High Priest whose death brings release.

Numbers 35:9

9The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses again, introducing the law of the cities of refuge. A new section begins.

Arminian readers see God addressing the hard realities of violence and accident in a fallen world.

Reformed

God’s word now turns to how Israel must deal with killing. Justice must be ordered by his command.

Reformed readers see God’s law restraining evil and protecting life. Both traditions read this introduction as shared ground, with parallels in Deuteronomy 19 and Joshua 20.

Numbers 35:10

10“Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘When you pass over the Jordan into the land of Canaan,

Arminian

The law applies once Israel crosses the Jordan into Canaan. It is for settled life.

Arminian readers see God preparing his people to live justly in the land he gives.

Reformed

The instruction looks ahead to life after the crossing. God plans for justice in the new setting.

Reformed readers see the land as a gift that brings responsibilities. Both traditions share this reading: possessing the land means living under God’s law within it, including in how life is protected.

Numbers 35:11

11then you shall appoint for yourselves cities to be cities of refuge for you, that the man slayer who kills any person unwittingly may flee there.

Arminian

Cities of refuge are appointed for anyone who kills a person unwittingly. Accidental killing is distinguished from murder.

Arminian readers see God’s law taking intention seriously. Moral responsibility depends on what a person meant to do.

Reformed

The law distinguishes guilt by intention. An accidental killer is not treated as a murderer.

Reformed readers see God’s justice as discerning, not crude. Both traditions agree that this distinction is central to the passage and is shared ground.

Numbers 35:12

12The cities shall be for your refuge from the avenger, that the man slayer not die until he stands before the congregation for judgment.

Arminian

The cities protect the killer from the avenger until he stands before the congregation for judgment. Refuge leads to a fair hearing.

Arminian readers see protection from vengeance and a process for truth. The refuge is not a way to escape justice.

Reformed

The avenger cannot act before a trial. The congregation must judge.

Reformed readers see God restraining private vengeance through public justice. Both traditions read this as shared ground about due process.

Numbers 35:13

13The cities which you shall give shall be for you six cities of refuge.

Arminian

There will be six cities of refuge. The number is fixed.

Arminian readers see God providing enough refuge so that no one is too far from safety. Mercy is planned, not improvised.

Reformed

Six cities are set apart for this purpose. The provision is deliberate.

Reformed readers see God’s orderly planning for mercy. Both traditions share this reading; Deuteronomy 19:3 adds that roads should be prepared so the killer can reach a city, showing the same concern for access to refuge.

Numbers 35:14

14You shall give three cities beyond the Jordan, and you shall give three cities in the land of Canaan. They shall be cities of refuge.

Arminian

Three cities lie east of the Jordan and three in Canaan. Refuge is spread across the land.

Arminian readers see access to safety for all the tribes, wherever they settle.

Reformed

The distribution ensures that refuge is within reach. Joshua 20 names the six cities.

Reformed readers see God’s care in making mercy available to every part of his people. Both traditions read this as shared ground about the reach of refuge.

Numbers 35:15

15These six cities shall be refuge for the children of Israel, for the stranger, and for the foreigner living among them, that everyone who kills any person unwittingly may flee there.

Arminian

The cities shelter Israelites, strangers, and foreigners alike. Refuge is not limited to Israel.

Arminian readers see God’s mercy extending to outsiders, a theme Christians connect with the gospel’s reach to all nations.

Reformed

The law includes resident foreigners equally. Protection does not depend on ancestry.

Reformed readers see God’s justice applied impartially. Both traditions read this inclusion as shared ground, and many see it as anticipating the gospel’s welcome to the nations.

Numbers 35:16

16“ ‘But if he struck him with an instrument of iron, so that he died, he is a murderer. The murderer shall surely be put to death.

Arminian

Striking someone with an iron instrument so that they die is murder, and the murderer must be put to death. The weapon shows intent.

Arminian readers see the law using objective evidence to judge intention.

Reformed

The use of a deadly instrument marks the act as murder. The penalty is death.

Reformed readers see the sanctity of human life, grounded in God’s image (Genesis 9:6). Both traditions read these examples soberly and as shared ground about the seriousness of murder.

Numbers 35:17

17If he struck him with a stone in the hand, by which a man may die, and he died, he is a murderer. The murderer shall surely be put to death.

Arminian

Striking someone with a stone that could kill is also murder. The same penalty applies.

Arminian readers see the law giving practical guidance to judges facing real cases. The circumstances of an act help reveal what the person intended.

Reformed

The examples build a pattern: deadly means imply intent to harm. The verdict follows.

Reformed readers see God’s law providing principles rather than exhaustive rules. Both traditions share this reading; the focus is on discerning intent from the circumstances of the act.

Numbers 35:18

18Or if he struck him with a weapon of wood in the hand, by which a man may die, and he died, he is a murderer. The murderer shall surely be put to death.

Arminian

A wooden weapon that could kill likewise marks a murderer. The list of weapon examples ends here.

Arminian readers see consistency in how the law treats deliberate killing. The type of weapon matters less than what its use reveals.

Reformed

Iron, stone, or wood, the question is whether the weapon could kill. The law is clear.

Reformed readers see justice applied consistently whatever the means. Both traditions read this as shared ground, illustrating the same principle in a third example.

Numbers 35:19

19The avenger of blood shall himself put the murderer to death. When he meets him, he shall put him to death.

Arminian

The avenger of blood himself puts the murderer to death. The Hebrew term is the same word used for a kinsman-redeemer, suggesting a family member responsible for justice.

Arminian readers see family responsibility within a community system of justice, limited by the trial process.

Reformed

The avenger acts only against a proven murderer. The congregation’s judgment frames his role.

Reformed readers see God’s justice working through appointed means in Israel’s setting. Both traditions read this in its ancient context and note that it is restrained by the refuge system.

Numbers 35:20

20If he shoved him out of hatred, or hurled something at him while lying in wait, so that he died,

Arminian

Shoving out of hatred or throwing something while lying in wait, causing death, also counts as murder. Hatred and ambush reveal intent.

Arminian readers see the law weighing the heart behind the act.

Reformed

Intent is shown by hatred and planning. The law looks beyond the method to the motive.

Reformed readers see God’s concern with the heart, which Jesus deepens in Matthew 5:21–22. Both traditions share this reading of the law’s attention to motive.

Numbers 35:21

21or in hostility struck him with his hand, so that he died, he who struck him shall surely be put to death. He is a murderer. The avenger of blood shall put the murderer to death when he meets him.

Arminian

Striking with the hand in hostility, causing death, is murder; the avenger may put him to death. Hostility is decisive.

Arminian readers see consistent judgment based on motive. The law is careful, not arbitrary.

Reformed

Even without a weapon, hostile intent makes the act murder. The verdict is the same.

Reformed readers see God’s justice focused on the heart’s intent. Both traditions read this as shared ground, completing the description of deliberate killing before turning to accidental death.

Numbers 35:22

22“ ‘But if he shoved him suddenly without hostility, or hurled on him anything without lying in wait,

Arminian

If someone shoves another suddenly without hostility, or throws something without lying in wait, the case is different. Accident is now described.

Arminian readers see the law protecting those who did not intend harm.

Reformed

The contrast with verses 20–21 is deliberate. The same actions without hostility are judged differently.

Reformed readers see God’s justice distinguishing degrees of guilt. Both traditions share this reading; motive and circumstances matter in God’s law.

Numbers 35:23

23or with any stone, by which a man may die, not seeing him, and cast it on him so that he died, and he was not his enemy and not seeking his harm,

Arminian

If a stone falls on someone unseen, and the killer was not an enemy or seeking harm, it is accidental. The law gives a clear example.

Arminian readers see mercy for those who cause harm unintentionally.

Reformed

The example stresses lack of enmity and intent. The killer is not a murderer.

Reformed readers see God’s law as just and merciful together. Both traditions read this as shared ground, and Deuteronomy 19:5 gives a similar example of an axe head flying off.

Numbers 35:24

24then the congregation shall judge between the striker and the avenger of blood according to these ordinances.

Arminian

The congregation judges between the killer and the avenger according to these ordinances. Justice is public.

Arminian readers see a community responsible for discerning truth fairly. The avenger’s grief does not decide the case; the hearing does.

Reformed

The congregation acts as judge, using God’s ordinances. Personal vengeance is restrained.

Reformed readers see God’s law guiding communal judgment. Both traditions agree that the process protects both the innocent and the cause of justice, and read the verse as shared ground.

Numbers 35:25

25The congregation shall deliver the man slayer out of the hand of the avenger of blood, and the congregation shall restore him to his city of refuge, where he had fled. He shall dwell therein until the death of the high priest, who was anointed with the holy oil.

Arminian

The congregation delivers the accidental killer and returns him to his city of refuge until the death of the high priest. The high priest’s death marks release.

Arminian readers, with many Christians, cautiously see Christ as the refuge and his death as bringing release.

Reformed

The high priest’s death ends the killer’s confinement. Why it does so is not explained; some interpreters see an atoning significance in it.

Reformed readers likewise see a possible picture of Christ the High Priest whose death brings freedom. Both traditions share this cautious typology without overclaiming.

Numbers 35:26

26“ ‘But if the man slayer shall at any time go beyond the border of his city of refuge where he flees,

Arminian

If the killer leaves the city’s border, he loses its protection. Refuge requires remaining within it.

Arminian readers often apply this as a call to remain in Christ, the refuge, while recognizing that the text is about a legal provision.

Reformed

The protection is real but bounded. Leaving the city exposes the killer to danger.

Reformed readers likewise draw cautious application about abiding in Christ. Both traditions agree the verse’s first meaning is legal, and that any spiritual application should not be pressed too far.

Numbers 35:27

27and the avenger of blood finds him outside of the border of his city of refuge, and the avenger of blood kills the man slayer, he shall not be guilty of blood,

Arminian

If the avenger finds him outside the city and kills him, the avenger is not guilty. The boundary matters.

Arminian readers see real consequences for leaving refuge. The killer’s choice to leave affects the outcome.

Reformed

The law protects the avenger who acts outside the city. Responsibility rests on the one who left.

Reformed readers see the seriousness of the boundaries God sets. Both traditions read this as part of a legal system rather than a direct statement about salvation.

Numbers 35:28

28because he should have remained in his city of refuge until the death of the high priest. But after the death of the high priest, the man slayer shall return into the land of his possession.

Arminian

The killer should have stayed until the high priest’s death; afterward he may return home. Release comes at a fixed point.

Arminian readers see hope written into the law. Confinement is not forever.

Reformed

The high priest’s death brings freedom to return to one’s possession. Restoration is promised.

Reformed readers see a picture many Christians connect with Christ, whose death opens the way to our inheritance. Both traditions share this cautious typology, with Hebrews 6:18 speaking of believers who have fled for refuge.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
