Passage

Numbers 35

Verses 1–8 · WEB

Near the end of Numbers, in the plains of Moab before entering Canaan, God commands Israel to give the Levites forty-eight cities with pasture lands, since Levi receives no tribal territory. Six of these will be cities of refuge. Each tribe gives in proportion to its inheritance. The passage is practical and largely shared ground: God provides for those who serve him through the generosity of his people, and the Levites are spread throughout Israel to serve the whole nation.

Numbers 35:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses in the plains of Moab by the Jordan at Jericho, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses in the plains of Moab, by the Jordan at Jericho. Israel stands at the edge of the land, and the laws now prepare for settled life.

Arminian readers see God preparing his people ahead of time for the responsibilities of life in the land.

Reformed

The setting recalls the closing chapters of Numbers, given on the threshold of Canaan. God’s word orders the future before it arrives.

Reformed readers see God’s providence planning for his people’s life. Both traditions read this introduction as shared ground.

Numbers 35:2

2“Command the children of Israel to give to the Levites cities to dwell in out of their inheritance. You shall give pasture lands for the cities around them to the Levites.

Arminian

Israel must give the Levites cities and surrounding pasture lands out of their own inheritance. The Levites had no tribal territory of their own.

Arminian readers see generosity built into the people’s possession of the land. What God gives them they share.

Reformed

The Levites’ portion is the LORD himself (Numbers 18:20, 23–24), yet they still need homes. The tribes provide for them.

Reformed readers see God providing for those who serve him through the gifts of his people. Both traditions share this reading.

Numbers 35:3

3They shall have the cities to dwell in. Their pasture lands shall be for their livestock, and for their possessions, and for all their animals.

Arminian

The cities are for living in, and the pasture lands for livestock and possessions. The provision is practical and ordinary.

Arminian readers see God caring for the everyday needs of those set apart for service. Holiness and daily life belong together.

Reformed

The Levites receive homes and grazing land, not wealth or territory. Their needs are met modestly.

Reformed readers see God’s care for his servants expressed in concrete provision. Both traditions read this as shared ground about supporting those who serve God’s people.

Numbers 35:4

4“The pasture lands of the cities, which you shall give to the Levites, shall be from the wall of the city and outward one thousand cubits around it.

Arminian

The pasture lands extend a thousand cubits from the city wall. The WEB footnotes a cubit as about 18 inches or 46 centimeters.

Arminian readers see clear measurements that prevent disputes. Fairness is built into the law.

Reformed

The precise boundary protects both the Levites and their neighbors. God’s law is concrete.

Reformed readers see order in God’s provision. Both traditions read the detail as practical; how verse 4’s thousand cubits relates to verse 5’s two thousand is explained differently by interpreters.

Numbers 35:5

5You shall measure outside of the city for the east side two thousand cubits, and for the south side two thousand cubits, and for the west side two thousand cubits, and for the north side two thousand cubits, the city being in the middle. This shall be the pasture lands of their cities.

Arminian

Two thousand cubits are measured on each side, with the city in the middle. The pasture land surrounds the city.

Arminian readers see a careful, generous allotment. The Levites are given space to live and work.

Reformed

The measurements frame the city on every side. Several explanations of the relation to verse 4 have been offered, and the text does not settle the question.

Reformed readers likewise hold such details lightly. Both traditions agree the point is secure provision for the Levites.

Numbers 35:6

6“The cities which you shall give to the Levites, they shall be the six cities of refuge, which you shall give for the man slayer to flee to. Besides them you shall give forty-two cities.

Arminian

Six of the Levites’ cities will be cities of refuge for anyone who kills someone; forty-two more are added. Refuge is placed in priestly hands.

Arminian readers see mercy and justice joined. Those who serve at God’s house also protect the vulnerable.

Reformed

The cities of refuge belong to the Levites, linking protection to the priestly tribe. Justice is tied to worship.

Reformed readers see God’s holiness and mercy working together in Israel’s institutions. Both traditions share this reading of the refuge cities’ setting.

Numbers 35:7

7All the cities which you shall give to the Levites shall be forty-eight cities together with their pasture lands.

Arminian

The Levites receive forty-eight cities in all, with pasture lands. Joshua 21 later records their distribution.

Arminian readers see the Levites spread throughout Israel, serving the whole people rather than one region.

Reformed

Forty-eight cities scatter the Levites among the tribes. Genesis 49:7 had spoken of Levi being scattered in Israel; here that scattering becomes a place of service.

Reformed readers see God turning a judgment into a blessing. Both traditions share this reading.

Numbers 35:8

8Concerning the cities which you shall give of the possession of the children of Israel, from the many you shall take many, and from the few you shall take few. Everyone according to his inheritance which he inherits shall give some of his cities to the Levites.”

Arminian

Larger tribes give more cities, smaller tribes fewer. Each gives according to its inheritance.

Arminian readers see proportional giving as fair. No tribe is burdened beyond its means, and every tribe shares in supporting the Levites.

Reformed

The rule is proportional: from the many, many; from the few, few. Responsibility matches resources.

Reformed readers see wisdom and equity in God’s law. Both traditions read this as shared ground, and Christians often compare it with the New Testament principle of giving according to what one has (2 Corinthians 8:12).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
