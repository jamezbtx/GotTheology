Passage

Numbers 10

Verses 29–36 · WEB

Moses urges Hobab, his Midianite relative, to come with Israel and share in the good the LORD has promised. Hobab first declines, and Moses presses him again, saying he can be Israel’s “eyes” in the wilderness. The text does not record Hobab’s final answer, though Judges 1:16 and 4:11 place his descendants among Israel. The chapter closes with the ark going ahead to seek a resting place, the cloud overhead, and Moses’ words when the ark set out and rested; Psalm 68:1 echoes 10:35. Both traditions see human wisdom and divine guidance working together, not as rivals. In the Hebrew manuscript tradition, verses 35–36 are set off by special scribal marks, whose purpose is debated.

Numbers 10:29

29Moses said to Hobab, the son of Reuel the Midianite, Moses’ father-in-law, “We are journeying to the place of which the LORD said, ‘I will give it to you.’ Come with us, and we will treat you well; for the LORD has spoken good concerning Israel.”

Arminian

Moses invites Hobab, son of Reuel the Midianite, to come with Israel, promising to share the good the LORD has spoken. The Hebrew term for “father-in-law” can refer to Reuel or Hobab, and Judges 4:11 links it with Hobab.

Arminian readers see the invitation as an example of God’s blessing offered to outsiders. Moses extends welcome freely.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses’ confidence in God’s promise to Israel and his desire that others share in it. The good is grounded in God’s word.

Both traditions see here an early picture of outsiders invited into God’s people.

Numbers 10:30

30He said to him, “I will not go; but I will depart to my own land, and to my relatives.”

Arminian

Hobab declines: he will return to his own land and family. The invitation is not automatically accepted.

Arminian readers note that Hobab’s response is genuinely his own; a sincere invitation can be refused, and God does not override his choice here.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Hobab’s initial refusal does not end the story, and that God’s purposes often work through further persuasion and changed hearts.

Both traditions read the verse as a real human decision about ties to land and family, without building a doctrine of grace on it.

Numbers 10:31

31Moses said, “Don’t leave us, please; because you know how we are to encamp in the wilderness, and you can be our eyes.

Arminian

Moses urges him to stay: Hobab knows how to camp in the wilderness and can be Israel’s eyes. Human skill is valued.

Arminian readers see Moses using every legitimate means to persuade. God’s guidance and human wisdom work together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence working through ordinary means, including human knowledge of the land. The cloud’s guidance does not cancel practical wisdom.

Both traditions agree that seeking human help is not a failure of faith.

Numbers 10:32

32It shall be, if you go with us—yes, it shall be—that whatever good the LORD does to us, we will do the same to you.”

Arminian

If Hobab comes, Israel will share with him whatever good the LORD does for them. The promise is generous.

Arminian readers see the gospel-like pattern of shared blessing offered to those who join God’s people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the blessing grounded in what the LORD does, not in Israel’s own resources.

Both traditions note that Judges 1:16 and 4:11 together place Hobab’s descendants among Israel, suggesting he came, though Numbers does not record his answer.

Numbers 10:33

33They set forward from the Mount of the LORD three days’ journey. The ark of the LORD’s covenant went before them three days’ journey, to seek out a resting place for them.

Arminian

They set out from the mountain of the LORD three days’ journey, with the ark going ahead to seek a resting place. God himself scouts the way.

Arminian readers see God’s guidance as active and caring, preparing the way for his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the ark, the symbol of God’s throne, leading the way: God goes before his people. Many readers connect the “resting place” with the theme of rest developed in Hebrews 3–4.

Both traditions see God’s presence leading his people toward rest.

Numbers 10:34

34The cloud of the LORD was over them by day, when they set forward from the camp.

Arminian

The cloud of the LORD is over them by day as they set out. God’s presence covers the march.

Arminian readers see God’s protection accompanying a people who are walking in obedience, and they note how quickly that privilege will be tested in the following chapters.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithful covering over his people, given not because of their merit but because of his promise.

Both traditions recall Paul’s reflection that all Israel was “under the cloud” (1 Corinthians 10:1), together with his warning that privilege does not guarantee faithfulness (10:5–12).

Numbers 10:35

35When the ark went forward, Moses said, “Rise up, LORD, and let your enemies be scattered! Let those who hate you flee before you!”

Arminian

When the ark set out, Moses said, “Rise up, LORD, and let your enemies be scattered!” Psalm 68:1 echoes these words.

Arminian readers see prayer that calls on God to act. The people’s journey is accompanied by calling on God’s help.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the warrior-king who leads his people and defeats his enemies. The prayer acknowledges that victory is his.

Both traditions note that verses 35–36 are set off in Hebrew manuscripts by special scribal marks, whose meaning interpreters debate.

Numbers 10:36

36When it rested, he said, “Return, LORD, to the ten thousands of the thousands of Israel.”

Arminian

When the ark rested, Moses said, “Return, LORD, to the ten thousands of the thousands of Israel.” God’s presence settles among his people.

Arminian readers see the prayer as an invitation for God to dwell among his people in rest, as a relationship of fellowship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God returning to dwell with his people as their covenant Lord. Psalm 132:8 uses similar language about the ark and God’s resting place.

Both traditions see the prayer as a fitting close to the departure: God goes before his people and dwells among them.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
