Passage

Numbers 36

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Numbers closes by revisiting the case of Zelophehad’s daughters, who in chapter 27 received their father’s inheritance because he had no sons. Leaders of their clan in Manasseh now point out that if the daughters marry outside the tribe, the land will pass permanently to another tribe. At the LORD’s word, Moses rules that heiresses may marry whom they choose within their father’s tribe, and the daughters comply. Both traditions read this plainly as shared ground about justice, the land as God’s gift, and faithful obedience.

Numbers 36:1

1The heads of the fathers’ households of the family of the children of Gilead, the son of Machir, the son of Manasseh, of the families of the sons of Joseph, came near and spoke before Moses and before the princes, the heads of the fathers’ households of the children of Israel.

Arminian

The family heads of Gilead, of the tribe of Manasseh, come before Moses and the leaders. They raise a question following the earlier ruling for Zelophehad’s daughters in Numbers 27:1–11.

Arminian readers see God’s people bringing real concerns openly to their leaders.

Reformed

The clan leaders approach through the proper channels. The case continues a story begun in chapter 27.

Reformed readers see God’s law being applied to new circumstances under his guidance. Both traditions read this as shared ground about orderly community decision-making.

Numbers 36:2

2They said, “The LORD commanded my lord to give the land for inheritance by lot to the children of Israel. My lord was commanded by the LORD to give the inheritance of Zelophehad our brother to his daughters.

Arminian

They recall that the LORD commanded the land be given by lot, and that Zelophehad’s inheritance go to his daughters. They accept the earlier ruling.

Arminian readers see that the clan does not challenge the daughters’ right; they seek to apply it well.

Reformed

The leaders affirm God’s command about the daughters. Their concern is a consequence, not the ruling itself.

Reformed readers see respect for God’s earlier word. Both traditions agree that the daughters’ inheritance is upheld, and that the question is how to protect tribal allotments.

Numbers 36:3

3If they are married to any of the sons of the other tribes of the children of Israel, then their inheritance will be taken away from the inheritance of our fathers, and will be added to the inheritance of the tribe to which they shall belong. So it will be taken away from the lot of our inheritance.

Arminian

If the daughters marry outside the tribe, their land would pass to another tribe. Manasseh’s allotment would shrink.

Arminian readers see a reasonable concern for preserving what God allotted to each tribe.

Reformed

The issue is the permanence of the tribal inheritance given by lot. Marriage could move land between tribes.

Reformed readers see the importance of the land as God’s gift distributed by his decision. Both traditions read this plainly as a practical legal question and as shared ground.

Numbers 36:4

4When the jubilee of the children of Israel comes, then their inheritance will be added to the inheritance of the tribe to which they shall belong. So their inheritance will be taken away from the inheritance of the tribe of our fathers.”

Arminian

Even the jubilee would not restore the land, since it would count as the other tribe’s inheritance. The loss would be permanent.

Arminian readers see the leaders thinking carefully about how God’s laws interact.

Reformed

The jubilee of Leviticus 25 returned sold land, but inherited land passing by marriage would not revert. The concern is precise.

Reformed readers see careful reasoning within God’s law. Both traditions share this reading and note that the jubilee aimed to keep families attached to their God-given inheritance.

Numbers 36:5

5Moses commanded the children of Israel according to the LORD’s word, saying, “The tribe of the sons of Joseph speak what is right.

Arminian

Moses, at the LORD’s word, says the tribe of Joseph speaks rightly. Their concern is affirmed.

Arminian readers see God responding to his people’s questions and refining the application of his law.

Reformed

Moses gives the ruling according to the LORD’s word. The leaders’ concern is judged valid.

Reformed readers see God’s word as the final authority for resolving disputes. Both traditions read this as shared ground, as in chapter 27, where a new case leads to a fresh word from God.

Numbers 36:6

6This is the thing which the LORD commands concerning the daughters of Zelophehad, saying, ‘Let them be married to whom they think best, only they shall marry into the family of the tribe of their father.

Arminian

The daughters may marry whom they think best, but within their father’s tribe. Freedom is given within a limit.

Arminian readers see real personal choice preserved. The women decide whom to marry, within the tribe.

Reformed

The ruling balances the daughters’ freedom with the tribe’s inheritance. Both concerns are honored.

Reformed readers see God’s wisdom in combining liberty and order. Both traditions read this as shared ground, noting that the daughters are not forced to marry a particular man.

Numbers 36:7

7So shall no inheritance of the children of Israel move from tribe to tribe; for the children of Israel shall all keep the inheritance of the tribe of his fathers.

Arminian

No inheritance will move from tribe to tribe; each keeps its fathers’ inheritance. The principle is stated generally.

Arminian readers see stability for every tribe in the land God gave.

Reformed

The ruling becomes a general principle for Israel. Tribal boundaries are to remain intact.

Reformed readers see the land’s distribution as God’s settled gift. Both traditions share this reading of the law’s purpose: each family keeps its place in the promised land.

Numbers 36:8

8Every daughter who possesses an inheritance in any tribe of the children of Israel shall be wife to one of the family of the tribe of her father, that the children of Israel may each possess the inheritance of his fathers.

Arminian

Every daughter who inherits must marry within her father’s tribe so each family keeps its inheritance. The rule applies widely.

Arminian readers see a fair, general solution arising from one family’s case.

Reformed

The ruling is extended to all heiresses. The specific case becomes a standing law.

Reformed readers see God’s law addressing the community as a whole. Both traditions read this plainly as shared ground, and both note that it applies only to daughters who actually inherit land.

Numbers 36:9

9So shall no inheritance move from one tribe to another tribe; for the tribes of the children of Israel shall each keep his own inheritance.’ ”

Arminian

No inheritance will move between tribes; each tribe keeps its own. The principle is repeated for emphasis.

Arminian readers see the importance of preserving what God has entrusted to each tribe.

Reformed

The repetition underlines the ruling’s seriousness. The tribal structure is protected.

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to the allotment he made by lot. Both traditions share this reading of the inheritance as a lasting gift that the community must guard.

Numbers 36:10

10The daughters of Zelophehad did as the LORD commanded Moses:

Arminian

The daughters of Zelophehad do as the LORD commanded Moses. They obey willingly.

Arminian readers see faithful response to God’s word. Their obedience closes the story well, showing trust in the God who first granted their claim.

Reformed

The daughters comply with the ruling. Their story, begun with boldness in chapter 27, ends in obedience.

Reformed readers see God’s word fulfilled in their response. Both traditions read this as shared ground, admiring both their earlier courage and their faithful acceptance of the clarified law.

Numbers 36:11

11for Mahlah, Tirzah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Noah, the daughters of Zelophehad, were married to their father’s brothers’ sons.

Arminian

Mahlah, Tirzah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Noah marry their father’s brothers’ sons, their cousins. Their names are recorded.

Arminian readers see the women honored by name in the narrative, as in chapter 27.

Reformed

The daughters are named again, and they marry within the family. The ruling is followed exactly.

Reformed readers see the text dignifying these women by recording their names. Both traditions share this reading and note that the order of names differs slightly from 27:1.

Numbers 36:12

12They were married into the families of the sons of Manasseh the son of Joseph. Their inheritance remained in the tribe of the family of their father.

Arminian

They marry within Manasseh’s families, and their inheritance stays in their father’s tribe. The concern is resolved.

Arminian readers see a peaceful outcome in which justice for the daughters and stability for the tribe are both kept.

Reformed

The inheritance remains where God placed it. The case ends satisfactorily.

Reformed readers see God’s law achieving its purpose. Both traditions read this as shared ground: the daughters’ right is preserved, and the tribal inheritance is protected.

Numbers 36:13

13These are the commandments and the ordinances which the LORD commanded by Moses to the children of Israel in the plains of Moab by the Jordan at Jericho.

Arminian

These are the commandments and ordinances given by Moses in the plains of Moab by the Jordan at Jericho. The book of Numbers closes.

Arminian readers see God’s people poised to enter the land with his word to guide them.

Reformed

The closing summary frames the final chapters as instruction for life in Canaan. The setting matches 35:1.

Reformed readers see God faithfully bringing his people to the threshold of the promise. Both traditions read this ending as shared ground about God’s faithfulness and his word.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
