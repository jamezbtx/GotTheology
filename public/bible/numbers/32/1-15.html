Passage

Numbers 32

Verses 1–15 · WEB

Reuben and Gad, rich in livestock, see that the conquered land east of the Jordan suits their herds and ask Moses to give it to them instead of an inheritance across the river. Moses hears this as a repeat of the unbelief at Kadesh Barnea, when the spies discouraged Israel and a whole generation died in the wilderness (Numbers 13–14). He warns that turning back now could bring the same judgment on the whole people.

Numbers 32:1

1Now the children of Reuben and the children of Gad had a very great multitude of livestock. They saw the land of Jazer, and the land of Gilead. Behold, the place was a place for livestock.

Arminian

Reuben and Gad have great herds and see that Jazer and Gilead are good pastureland. Arminian readers note that the request grows from a practical observation. Both traditions agree that the verse connects naturally with the livestock gained in chapter 31.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the tribes’ prosperity becomes the occasion for a test of faithfulness. Shared ground: there is nothing wrong in noticing good land, but the narrative will show how the request could become a temptation to abandon their brothers.

Numbers 32:2

2Then the children of Gad and the children of Reuben came and spoke to Moses, and to Eleazar the priest, and to the princes of the congregation, saying,

Arminian

Gad and Reuben approach Moses, Eleazar, and the leaders. Arminian readers note that they follow proper channels rather than acting on their own. Both traditions agree that the formal approach shows respect for Israel’s leadership.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the orderly presentation of the request to the recognized authorities. Shared ground: the tribes seek approval rather than simply seizing the land, which allows the matter to be resolved within the community.

Numbers 32:3

3“Ataroth, Dibon, Jazer, Nimrah, Heshbon, Elealeh, Sebam, Nebo, and Beon,

Arminian

The tribes list the towns they want: Ataroth, Dibon, Jazer, Nimrah, Heshbon, Elealeh, Sebam, Nebo, and Beon. Arminian readers note the specific, concrete request. Both traditions agree that several names reappear in 32:34–38, sometimes in slightly different forms.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the towns lie in territory recently taken from Sihon and Og (21:21–35). Shared ground: the list ties the request to real places and prepares for the account of their rebuilding later in the chapter.

Numbers 32:4

4the land which the LORD struck before the congregation of Israel, is a land for livestock; and your servants have livestock.”

Arminian

They describe the land as one the LORD struck before Israel, suitable for livestock. Arminian readers note that they acknowledge God’s role in the conquest. Both traditions agree that their reasoning is partly theological and partly practical.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the tribes recognize the land as given by God’s victory. Shared ground: their words affirm God’s action, yet the question remains whether their request honors the larger purpose of entering Canaan together.

Numbers 32:5

5They said, “If we have found favor in your sight, let this land be given to your servants for a possession. Don’t bring us over the Jordan.”

Arminian

They ask for the land as a possession and add, “Don’t bring us over the Jordan.” Arminian readers note that this phrase raises Moses’ alarm. Both traditions agree that the request, as first stated, seems to withdraw from the shared task of conquest.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the final request the seed of a possible unbelief or self-interest. Shared ground: the wording sounds like separation from Israel’s mission, and Moses’ reaction in the next verses shows how seriously it was taken.

Numbers 32:6

6Moses said to the children of Gad, and to the children of Reuben, “Shall your brothers go to war while you sit here?

Arminian

Moses asks whether their brothers should go to war while they sit here. Arminian readers see a strong appeal to fairness and solidarity. Both traditions agree that Moses’ concern is the unity and shared burden of Israel’s tribes.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Moses frames the issue as a matter of covenant obligation among brothers. Shared ground: no tribe may enjoy God’s gifts while leaving others to bear the cost of obedience.

Numbers 32:7

7Why do you discourage the heart of the children of Israel from going over into the land which the LORD has given them?

Arminian

Moses warns that their request could discourage Israel from entering the land. Arminian readers note the influence that one group’s choices can have on the faith of others. Both traditions agree that discouragement was the root problem at Kadesh.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the danger of a request that weakens the community’s resolve to follow God’s promise. Shared ground: Moses is concerned with the spiritual effect on all Israel, not only with territorial arrangements.

Numbers 32:8

8Your fathers did so when I sent them from Kadesh Barnea to see the land.

Arminian

Moses recalls that their fathers did the same when sent from Kadesh Barnea to see the land. Arminian readers note the appeal to history as a warning. Both traditions agree that Numbers 13–14 is the background for Moses’ rebuke.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses interpreting the present in light of past unbelief. Shared ground: remembering God’s past judgments helps the new generation avoid repeating them, a pattern found throughout Scripture (compare 1 Corinthians 10:6–11).

Numbers 32:9

9For when they went up to the valley of Eshcol, and saw the land, they discouraged the heart of the children of Israel, that they should not go into the land which the LORD had given them.

Arminian

The spies went up to the valley of Eshcol, saw the land, and discouraged Israel. Arminian readers note the irony: they saw the land’s goodness yet still discouraged the people. Both traditions agree that the issue was not the land but unbelief (13:23–33).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the tragedy of seeing God’s gift and refusing to trust him. Shared ground: the spies’ report turned the people away from what God had promised, and Moses fears a similar outcome now.

Numbers 32:10

10The LORD’s anger burned in that day, and he swore, saying,

Arminian

The LORD’s anger burned, and he swore an oath. Arminian readers see God’s genuine response to the people’s rebellion. Both traditions agree that the oath recalls Numbers 14:21–23. His oath is not arbitrary; it answers a refusal to trust after repeated signs of his care.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the oath as a solemn declaration of God’s judgment. Shared ground: God’s anger at unbelief is real, and his oath shows the seriousness with which he treats rejection of his promises.

Numbers 32:11

11‘Surely none of the men who came up out of Egypt, from twenty years old and upward, shall see the land which I swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob; because they have not wholly followed me,

Arminian

No one twenty years or older who came out of Egypt would see the land, because they did not follow God wholly. Arminian readers stress that the judgment came from their failure to follow fully. Both traditions agree that this repeats Numbers 14:29–30.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the land was sworn to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, yet the unbelieving generation forfeited their part in it. Shared ground: God’s promise stands, but those who refuse to follow him do not enjoy it.

Numbers 32:12

12except Caleb the son of Jephunneh the Kenizzite, and Joshua the son of Nun, because they have followed the LORD completely.’

Arminian

Caleb and Joshua are the exceptions, because they followed the LORD completely. Arminian readers see faithfulness honored. Both traditions note the text’s description of Caleb as a Kenizzite, as in Joshua 14:6, and honor his wholehearted loyalty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Caleb and Joshua as examples of wholehearted faith sustained by God. Shared ground: complete devotion distinguishes them from their generation, and both enter the land as God promised.

Numbers 32:13

13The LORD’s anger burned against Israel, and he made them wander back and forth in the wilderness forty years, until all the generation who had done evil in the LORD’s sight was consumed.

Arminian

The LORD made Israel wander forty years until the evil generation was consumed. Arminian readers note the long consequence of unbelief. Both traditions agree that the wilderness years were both judgment and preparation for the next generation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s justice and patience together: judgment falls, yet the nation continues and the promise endures. Shared ground: the forty years are a sober reminder of the cost of unbelief, and Hebrews 3:16–19 uses them as a warning.

Numbers 32:14

14“Behold, you have risen up in your fathers’ place, an increase of sinful men, to increase the fierce anger of the LORD toward Israel.

Arminian

Moses calls the tribes “an increase of sinful men” risen up in their fathers’ place. Arminian readers see a sharp warning against repeating past sin. Both traditions agree that Moses fears the new generation is following the old pattern.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses’ strong language as a prophetic rebuke. Shared ground: the warning is meant to provoke repentance, not to condemn the tribes irreversibly, as their response in the next section shows.

Numbers 32:15

15For if you turn away from after him, he will yet again leave them in the wilderness; and you will destroy all these people.”

Arminian

Moses warns that if they turn away, God will leave Israel in the wilderness again and they will destroy the whole people. Arminian readers note the communal consequences of individual choices. Both traditions agree that Moses’ concern is for all Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the warning as highlighting the corporate nature of covenant faithfulness. Shared ground: one group’s unfaithfulness can bring harm on the whole community, so the stakes of the request are high.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
