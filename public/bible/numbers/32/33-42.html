Passage

Numbers 32

Verses 33–42 · WEB

Moses assigns the former kingdoms of Sihon and Og to Gad, Reuben, and half of Manasseh. The tribes rebuild and rename towns, and clans of Manasseh, Machir, Jair, and Nobah, take territory in Gilead and Bashan. The settlement east of the Jordan is recorded as land given by Moses on terms later fulfilled when these tribes fought alongside their brothers (Joshua 22:1–4).

Numbers 32:33

33Moses gave to them, even to the children of Gad, and to the children of Reuben, and to the half-tribe of Manasseh the son of Joseph, the kingdom of Sihon king of the Amorites, and the kingdom of Og king of Bashan; the land, according to its cities and borders, even the cities of the surrounding land.

Arminian

Moses gives the kingdoms of Sihon and Og to Gad, Reuben, and the half-tribe of Manasseh. Arminian readers note that Manasseh appears here for the first time in this arrangement. Both traditions agree that these lands were taken in Numbers 21:21–35.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the allotment as God’s gift through Moses, fulfilling the conditional agreement in advance. Shared ground: the half-tribe of Manasseh’s inclusion is introduced without explanation, and Deuteronomy 3:12–17 gives a parallel account.

Numbers 32:34

34The children of Gad built Dibon, Ataroth, Aroer,

Arminian

Gad builds Dibon, Ataroth, and Aroer. Arminian readers note that “built” likely means rebuilt or fortified existing towns. Both traditions agree that the tribes begin settling their land. Dibon and Aroer reappear in later biblical history as towns east of the Jordan.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the building as taking possession of God’s gift. Shared ground: the towns become homes for Gad’s families while their men prepare to fight in Canaan. Building becomes a visible act of settling into what God has given.

Numbers 32:35

35Atroth-shophan, Jazer, Jogbehah,

Arminian

Gad also builds Atroth-shophan, Jazer, and Jogbehah. Arminian readers note the continuing list of settlements. Both traditions agree that Jazer was among the towns originally requested (32:3). The names give the account a concrete, verifiable setting.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the expansion of Gad’s settlements. Shared ground: the list records the tribe’s establishment in the land, giving later readers a geographic record. Each name marks a place where Israelite families would now live.

Numbers 32:36

36Beth Nimrah, and Beth Haran: fortified cities and folds for sheep.

Arminian

Beth Nimrah and Beth Haran are fortified cities with sheepfolds. Arminian readers note the combination of protection for families and provision for flocks. Both traditions agree that this fulfills the plan of 32:16–17.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fortified cities as the promised protection for the little ones. Shared ground: the tribes do as they said, building both cities and folds. Words and actions now match.

Numbers 32:37

37The children of Reuben built Heshbon, Elealeh, Kiriathaim,

Arminian

Reuben builds Heshbon, Elealeh, and Kiriathaim. Arminian readers note that Heshbon had been Sihon’s capital (21:26). Both traditions agree that Reuben’s settlements are listed separately from Gad’s. The change of rule from an Amorite king to Israelite tribes is recorded without fanfare.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the former royal city now belongs to Israel. Shared ground: the record shows the transfer of power from Sihon to the tribes of Israel by God’s gift.

Numbers 32:38

38Nebo, and Baal Meon, (their names being changed), and Sibmah. They gave other names to the cities which they built.

Arminian

Reuben builds Nebo, Baal Meon, and Sibmah, changing their names. Arminian readers note that Nebo and Baal were names of pagan deities, which may explain the renaming, though the text does not say. Both traditions agree that the renaming marks new ownership.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the renaming as possibly rejecting pagan associations. The text only notes that names were changed. Shared ground: the tribes give the cities new names, marking them as Israel’s.

Numbers 32:39

39The children of Machir the son of Manasseh went to Gilead, took it, and dispossessed the Amorites who were therein.

Arminian

The descendants of Machir, son of Manasseh, take Gilead and dispossess the Amorites. Arminian readers note the active role of the clan. Both traditions agree that this explains how part of Manasseh came to settle east of the Jordan.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the conquest of Gilead as God’s provision for Manasseh. Shared ground: Machir’s descendants take the land, and the narrative records their initiative. The clan’s action is recorded as part of the larger allotment Moses confirms in the next verse.

Numbers 32:40

40Moses gave Gilead to Machir the son of Manasseh; and he lived therein.

Arminian

Moses gives Gilead to Machir, who lives there. Arminian readers note that Moses formally recognizes what Machir’s clan took. Both traditions agree that the gift confirms their possession. Gift and conquest are presented together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses’ gift as legitimating the possession under God’s authority. Shared ground: Machir’s inheritance is established, and later texts mention Gilead as Manasseh’s portion (Joshua 17:1). The narrative presents human initiative and divine gift side by side.

Numbers 32:41

41Jair the son of Manasseh went and took its villages, and called them Havvoth Jair.

Arminian

Jair, a descendant of Manasseh, takes villages and names them Havvoth Jair. Arminian readers note the personal naming. Both traditions agree that the name is commonly understood to mean villages of Jair, and it is mentioned also in Deuteronomy 3:14.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the naming as a memorial of conquest. Shared ground: Jair’s villages become part of Manasseh’s territory, and the narrative records the clan’s achievements. Names in the land become a record of who settled where.

Numbers 32:42

42Nobah went and took Kenath and its villages, and called it Nobah, after his own name.

Arminian

Nobah takes Kenath and its villages and names it after himself. Arminian readers note that the chapter ends with individual initiative within the tribal framework. Both traditions agree that the settlement east of the Jordan is now complete in this account.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the conclusion of the chapter as recording God’s gifts through human action. Shared ground: the eastern tribes are settled, awaiting the fulfillment of their pledge in Canaan. The chapter ends with land given, towns renamed, and a pledge still to be kept.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
