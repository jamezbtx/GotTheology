Passage

Numbers 9

Verses 1–14 · WEB

In the first month of the second year after the exodus, still at Sinai, Israel keeps its first Passover since leaving Egypt. Some men are unclean through contact with a dead body and cannot take part. Instead of simply excluding them, Moses brings their question to the LORD, who appoints a second Passover one month later for the unclean and for those far away on a journey. The LORD also warns that anyone able to keep the feast who neglects it will be cut off, and he opens the Passover to the foreigner who wishes to keep it under the same statute. Both traditions see God’s mercy and his seriousness held together here. Christians in both read the Passover in light of Christ, “our Passover” (1 Corinthians 5:7), and note that John 19:36 applies the unbroken-bone rule of 9:12 to Jesus.

Numbers 9:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses in the wilderness of Sinai, in the first month of the second year after they had come out of the land of Egypt, saying,

Arminian

The date anchors the command: the first month of the second year, still at Sinai, a year after the exodus. God speaks before Israel moves on.

Arminian readers see God renewing his covenant relationship with a people who must keep choosing to walk in it. The memory of redemption is set before them again so that their trust can be renewed, not presumed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God taking the initiative to order his people’s worship before they leave the mountain. The God who redeemed them now shapes their life by his word.

Both traditions read the timing as showing that worship grounded in God’s saving act comes first, and travel second.

Numbers 9:2

2“Let the children of Israel keep the Passover in its appointed season.

Arminian

Israel is to keep the Passover “in its appointed season.” The feast is not optional or movable at will; God sets its time.

Arminian readers note that the command addresses the whole people as responsible worshipers. Remembering God’s deliverance is a covenant duty that each generation must take up for itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the language of appointment: God fixes the times of his worship. They often connect this with the conviction that worship should follow what God has commanded rather than human invention.

Both traditions see in the Passover a memorial that points ahead to Christ, “our Passover” (1 Corinthians 5:7).

Numbers 9:3

3On the fourteenth day of this month, at evening, you shall keep it in its appointed season. You shall keep it according to all its statutes and according to all its ordinances.”

Arminian

The details are fixed: the fourteenth day, at evening, according to all its statutes and ordinances. The original Passover regulations of Exodus 12 still govern.

Arminian readers emphasize faithful obedience as the fitting response of a redeemed people. The care required is not legalism but love expressed in attentive worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the means of grace are given by God, not designed by worshipers. The details protect the meaning of the feast and keep its focus on God’s deliverance.

Both traditions see careful obedience in worship as honoring the God who acted in history.

Numbers 9:4

4Moses told the children of Israel that they should keep the Passover.

Arminian

Moses tells the people to keep the Passover. God’s word reaches Israel through the mediator he appointed.

Arminian readers note that Moses relays the command and the people must hear and respond. Faithful leadership calls others to obedience without coercing them.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God ordinarily works through appointed ministers who speak his word to his people. Moses’ role anticipates the ministry of the word in the church.

Both traditions honor Moses as a faithful servant in God’s house (compare Hebrews 3:5), pointing beyond himself to Christ.

Numbers 9:5

5They kept the Passover in the first month, on the fourteenth day of the month at evening, in the wilderness of Sinai. According to all that the LORD commanded Moses, so the children of Israel did.

Arminian

Israel keeps the Passover exactly as the LORD commanded. The verse records full and willing obedience.

Arminian readers see a moment of genuine covenant faithfulness. The later failures in Numbers show that such obedience can be lost, which makes this moment an example to imitate.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience as the fruit of God’s grace at work among his redeemed people. The record also serves as a standard against which later unbelief is measured.

Both traditions read the verse as a bright spot that the rest of Numbers will contrast with grumbling and rebellion.

Numbers 9:6

6There were certain men who were unclean because of the dead body of a man, so that they could not keep the Passover on that day, and they came before Moses and Aaron on that day.

Arminian

Some men are unclean through contact with a dead body and cannot keep the feast. They come to Moses and Aaron that very day.

Arminian readers note that the men act on their desire to worship rather than accepting exclusion. Their earnest approach is met with God’s mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the purity laws (compare Numbers 19) are not lightly set aside. Holiness matters, and these men know their condition bars them from the feast.

Both traditions stress that corpse impurity is ritual, not moral guilt; caring for the dead was a duty, not a sin.

Numbers 9:7

7Those men said to him, “We are unclean because of the dead body of a man. Why are we kept back, that we may not offer the offering of the LORD in its appointed season among the children of Israel?”

Arminian

The men ask why they should be kept from offering the LORD’s offering among Israel. Their question is a plea to belong, not a complaint against the law.

Arminian readers hear in their question a heart that longs for communion with God. God honors such longing and opens a way.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that they ask within the framework of God’s law, not against it. They seek a word from God rather than making their own exception.

Both traditions take pastoral encouragement here: those who feel shut out of worship may bring their case to God.

Numbers 9:8

8Moses answered them, “Wait, that I may hear what the LORD will command concerning you.”

Arminian

Moses replies, “Wait, that I may hear what the LORD will command.” He does not improvise a ruling.

Arminian readers see humble leadership that refuses to presume on God’s will. Moses models dependence on God for decisions not covered by existing instruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sufficiency and authority of God’s word: new questions are settled by revelation, not human opinion. Moses waits for God to speak.

Both traditions commend Moses’ patience as a model for leaders facing hard cases.

Numbers 9:9

9The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses. God answers the question raised by the unclean men.

Arminian readers note that God responds to the real situation of his people. His instructions are not abstract; they meet actual needs as they arise.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that revelation here is given progressively, as God chooses, and that it carries his authority. The answer comes as a statute for future generations, not only for these men.

Both traditions see God as attentive and responsive to his people’s needs.

Numbers 9:10

10“Say to the children of Israel, ‘If any man of you or of your generations is unclean by reason of a dead body, or is on a journey far away, he shall still keep the Passover to the LORD.

Arminian

Anyone unclean through a dead body or far away on a journey may still keep the Passover. The provision extends to future generations.

Arminian readers see God’s gracious will to include rather than exclude. The provision reflects his desire that all his people share in remembering redemption.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to his covenant people, providing a way for those hindered by circumstance to receive the sign of his deliverance.

Both traditions rejoice that God makes room for the unclean and the distant, a theme fulfilled when Christ welcomes those far off (Ephesians 2:13).

Numbers 9:11

11In the second month, on the fourteenth day at evening they shall keep it; they shall eat it with unleavened bread and bitter herbs.

Arminian

The second Passover falls one month later, on the fourteenth day at evening, with unleavened bread and bitter herbs. The same feast is kept at a different time.

Arminian readers note that the delay does not lessen the feast. God’s grace adapts to human weakness without lowering its meaning.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the bitter herbs and unleavened bread keep the original meaning intact. The substance of the sign does not change.

Both traditions note that Hezekiah later kept a Passover in the second month (2 Chronicles 30:2–3, 15), a sign that this provision was remembered.

Numbers 9:12

12They shall leave none of it until the morning, nor break a bone of it. According to all the statute of the Passover they shall keep it.

Arminian

None of the lamb may be left until morning, and no bone may be broken. The full Passover statute applies.

Arminian readers note that the second Passover is not a lesser rite. Grace offers a second opportunity, but it is the same feast with the same holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that John 19:36 applies the unbroken-bone rule to Jesus at the cross (compare Exodus 12:46). The detail points beyond itself to Christ.

Both traditions affirm this New Testament reading of the Passover lamb as fulfilled in Christ.

Numbers 9:13

13But the man who is clean, and is not on a journey, and fails to keep the Passover, that soul shall be cut off from his people. Because he didn’t offer the offering of the LORD in its appointed season, that man shall bear his sin.

Arminian

A person who is clean and not traveling, yet fails to keep the Passover, will be cut off and bear his sin. Mercy for the hindered is not license for the indifferent.

Arminian readers stress that God’s grace can be refused, and that refusing it has real consequences. Neglect of God’s appointed worship is a serious matter.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that despising the signs of God’s covenant is a serious offense. The warning protects the covenant community from treating grace lightly.

Both traditions hold God’s kindness to the unclean and his warning to the careless together; the exact meaning of “cut off” is debated.

Numbers 9:14

14“ ‘If a foreigner lives among you and desires to keep the Passover to the LORD, then he shall do so according to the statute of the Passover, and according to its ordinance. You shall have one statute, both for the foreigner and for him who is born in the land.’ ”

Arminian

A foreigner who lives among Israel and wishes to keep the Passover may do so under the same statute. There is one law for the foreigner and the native-born.

Arminian readers see God’s openness to outsiders who desire him, a foreshadowing of the gospel offered to all nations.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant people defined by God’s call rather than by ethnicity alone. Exodus 12:48 adds that males must be circumcised to take part, which the verse assumes.

Both traditions see here an early sign of God’s purpose to gather people from every nation (compare Galatians 3:8).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
