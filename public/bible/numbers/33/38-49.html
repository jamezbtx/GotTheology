Passage

Numbers 33

Verses 38–49 · WEB

The itinerary pauses for Aaron’s death on Mount Hor in the fortieth year, at age one hundred twenty-three (compare Numbers 20:22–29). It notes the king of Arad hearing of Israel’s approach (compare Numbers 21:1–3), then traces the final stages around Edom and Moab to the plains of Moab by the Jordan opposite Jericho. Some places, such as Dibon, Nebo, and the plains of Moab, are widely identified in the region east of the Jordan; many others remain uncertain. Both traditions see the end of an old generation and the arrival of a new one at the edge of the promise, sustained by God throughout, as Deuteronomy 8:2–4 later reflects.

Numbers 33:38

38Aaron the priest went up into Mount Hor at the commandment of the LORD and died there, in the fortieth year after the children of Israel had come out of the land of Egypt, in the fifth month, on the first day of the month.

Arminian

Aaron went up Mount Hor at the LORD’s command and died there in the fortieth year, on the first day of the fifth month. This is the only death date the itinerary records.

Arminian readers note that Aaron, though he shared Moses’ failure at Meribah, died in God’s care and was honored with a precise memorial.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priesthood passed to Eleazar (Numbers 20:25–28), showing that God’s provision for his people did not end with one man. Hebrews 7:23–24 contrasts priests who die with Christ, who holds his priesthood permanently.

Both traditions see God’s faithfulness outlasting his servants.

Numbers 33:39

39Aaron was one hundred twenty-three years old when he died in Mount Hor.

Arminian

Aaron was one hundred twenty-three years old when he died. The record honors a long life of service.

Arminian readers note that Aaron’s life included both faithfulness and serious failure, such as the golden calf, yet God sustained him in his office to the end.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s grace preserved Aaron in his calling despite his sins, a picture of God’s faithfulness to the servants he appoints.

Both traditions honor Aaron as a real man whose life points beyond itself to the perfect high priest.

Numbers 33:40

40The Canaanite king of Arad, who lived in the South in the land of Canaan, heard of the coming of the children of Israel.

Arminian

The Canaanite king of Arad heard of Israel’s approach. Numbers 21:1–3 records the battle that followed.

Arminian readers note that Israel’s approach provoked opposition, and that the people responded with a vow and prayer to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God gave Israel victory in answer to their vow, a foretaste of the conquest to come.

Both traditions read the verse as marking a shift: the wilderness is giving way to the conflicts of entering the land.

Numbers 33:41

41They traveled from Mount Hor, and encamped in Zalmonah.

Arminian

From Mount Hor they camped at Zalmonah. The site is unidentified.

Arminian readers recall that on this stretch the people grew impatient and spoke against God and Moses, and God sent the bronze serpent as a remedy for those who looked (Numbers 21:4–9).

Reformed

Reformed readers recall Jesus’ use of the bronze serpent as a picture of his being lifted up (John 3:14–15). The list does not name the event, but the stage is the same.

Both traditions see grace provided in the midst of judgment.

Numbers 33:42

42They traveled from Zalmonah, and encamped in Punon.

Arminian

From Zalmonah they camped at Punon. Punon is often linked with a known copper-mining area south of the Dead Sea, though the identification is not certain.

Arminian readers note that the people were moving steadily closer to the land, despite failure along the way.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s promise drew his people onward. Every station brought the fulfillment nearer. He was keeping his word even as the people murmured along the way.

Both traditions treat the identification as tentative and focus on the movement toward promise.

Numbers 33:43

43They traveled from Punon, and encamped in Oboth.

Arminian

From Punon they camped at Oboth. Numbers 21:10 also mentions Oboth.

Arminian readers note that the new generation was learning to trust God in the same kinds of places their parents had failed. Arminian readers see grace giving a second generation a real opportunity to respond differently.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s leading was continuous, linking the narrative of chapter 21 with this summary record.

Both traditions see the itinerary and the narrative together as testimony to God’s guidance in the final year.

Numbers 33:44

44They traveled from Oboth, and encamped in Iye Abarim, in the border of Moab.

Arminian

From Oboth they camped at Iye Abarim, on the border of Moab. The people now approach the region east of the Jordan.

Arminian readers note that reaching the border of Moab was a turning point after decades of wandering, calling for renewed faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God brought his people to the threshold of the land exactly as he had promised, despite every failure.

Both traditions see this as a moment of hope: the long road was ending.

Numbers 33:45

45They traveled from Iyim, and encamped in Dibon Gad.

Arminian

From Iyim they camped at Dibon Gad. Iyim appears to be a short form of Iye Abarim, and Dibon is widely identified with a site in central Moab.

Arminian readers note that the people now passed through territory that would later be assigned to Gad, a sign of the inheritance taking shape.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God was already preparing the land east of the Jordan for the tribes who would ask for it (Numbers 32).

Both traditions treat the identification as probable but not beyond question.

Numbers 33:46

46They traveled from Dibon Gad, and encamped in Almon Diblathaim.

Arminian

From Dibon Gad they camped at Almon Diblathaim. The site is uncertain.

Arminian readers note that the people moved through lands they had not conquered by their own strength, depending on God’s help. They were learning that the land would be received, not seized by their own power.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s victories over Sihon and Og (Numbers 21:21–35) had opened the way. The record reflects ground that God had given.

Both traditions see in these final stages God’s faithfulness in clearing a path for his people.

Numbers 33:47

47They traveled from Almon Diblathaim, and encamped in the mountains of Abarim, before Nebo.

Arminian

From Almon Diblathaim they camped in the mountains of Abarim, before Nebo. Nebo is the mountain from which Moses would later view the land (Deuteronomy 34:1).

Arminian readers note the poignancy of the location: Moses would see the land but not enter it, a consequence of his own failure.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s discipline of Moses was real, yet he showed him the land in grace. God’s purposes for his servant ended in mercy.

Both traditions read Nebo as a place of both judgment and grace.

Numbers 33:48

48They traveled from the mountains of Abarim, and encamped in the plains of Moab by the Jordan at Jericho.

Arminian

From the mountains of Abarim they camped in the plains of Moab by the Jordan at Jericho. The journey reaches its final camp.

Arminian readers note that the people now stood where the next step would be entering the land, a moment calling for decisive faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God had brought his people to the place he intended. The book of Deuteronomy and the crossing of the Jordan follow from here.

Both traditions see the plains of Moab as the threshold of promise fulfilled.

Numbers 33:49

49They encamped by the Jordan, from Beth Jeshimoth even to Abel Shittim in the plains of Moab.

Arminian

They camped by the Jordan from Beth Jeshimoth to Abel Shittim in the plains of Moab. The camp stretched along the river.

Arminian readers recall that at Shittim the people fell into sin with Baal Peor (Numbers 25), showing that the danger of unfaithfulness remained even at the edge of the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers recall that God preserved his people despite that failure, and that the new generation was counted again (Numbers 26).

Both traditions see the final camp as a place of both warning and hope.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
