Passage

Numbers 33

Verses 50–56 · WEB

At the Jordan, the LORD commands Israel to drive out the inhabitants of Canaan, destroy their idols and molten images, demolish their high places, and divide the land by lot. If they fail, those who remain will trouble them, and God will do to Israel what he planned to do to the nations. This is a specific command given in a particular covenant-historical setting, centered on removing idolatry from the land God was giving his people (compare Deuteronomy 7:1–6; 9:4–5). Both traditions read it soberly and deny that it licenses violence against any people today. The church’s warfare is spiritual, not by the sword (2 Corinthians 10:3–5; Ephesians 6:12), though Christians still take the warning against idolatry seriously.

Numbers 33:50

50The LORD spoke to Moses in the plains of Moab by the Jordan at Jericho, saying,

Arminian

The LORD spoke to Moses in the plains of Moab by the Jordan at Jericho. The setting is the threshold of the land.

Arminian readers note that God’s instructions come at the moment when the people must decide how they will live in the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God speaks to prepare his people for what he is about to give. His word precedes their entry.

Both traditions read what follows as covenant instruction for a specific moment in Israel’s history.

Numbers 33:51

51Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them, “When you pass over the Jordan into the land of Canaan,

Arminian

Moses is to tell Israel what to do when they cross the Jordan into Canaan. The command anticipates entry.

Arminian readers note that the instruction assumes the people will act, and that their obedience will shape their future in the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the crossing itself is assumed as certain because God has promised it. His word frames their future.

Both traditions read the command within Israel’s unique role in God’s plan, not as a general rule for all nations.

Numbers 33:52

52then you shall drive out all the inhabitants of the land from before you, destroy all their stone idols, destroy all their molten images, and demolish all their high places.

Arminian

Israel is to drive out the inhabitants, destroy their stone idols and molten images, and demolish their high places. The focus falls on removing idolatry from the land.

Arminian readers note that the command concerns a specific historical setting and the danger of idolatry corrupting Israel’s worship (compare Deuteronomy 7:4–5). They deny that it licenses violence today.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s judgment on the nations was tied to their wickedness (compare Genesis 15:16; Deuteronomy 9:4–5), and that the command was unique to this redemptive-historical moment. They likewise deny that it licenses violence today.

Both traditions apply the passage spiritually to the call to forsake idols (compare 1 John 5:21).

Numbers 33:53

53You shall take possession of the land, and dwell therein; for I have given the land to you to possess it.

Arminian

Israel is to take possession of the land and live in it, for God has given it to them. The land is gift before it is task.

Arminian readers note that receiving the gift requires active obedience. God gives, and his people must take hold of what he gives.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the gift rests on God’s promise and initiative. Israel possesses the land because God has given it, not because they earned it.

Both traditions read the verse as holding gift and responsibility together, a pattern Christians apply to receiving God’s grace.

Numbers 33:54

54You shall inherit the land by lot according to your families; to the larger groups you shall give a larger inheritance, and to the smaller you shall give a smaller inheritance. Wherever the lot falls to any man, that shall be his. You shall inherit according to the tribes of your fathers.

Arminian

The land is to be divided by lot according to families, with larger groups receiving more and smaller groups less. Wherever the lot falls, that portion belongs to the family.

Arminian readers see fairness and proportion in the distribution, reflecting God’s concern for justice among his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence in the lot (compare Proverbs 16:33). The outcome is his decision, not human chance.

Both traditions agree that the distribution expresses God’s care for every family, so that each has a share in the inheritance (compare Numbers 26:52–56).

Numbers 33:55

55“But if you do not drive out the inhabitants of the land from before you, then those you let remain of them will be like pricks in your eyes and thorns in your sides. They will harass you in the land in which you dwell.

Arminian

If Israel fails to drive out the inhabitants, those who remain will be like pricks in their eyes and thorns in their sides. The warning is vivid.

Arminian readers note that the covenant’s blessings are conditioned on faithfulness, and that failure brings real consequences, as Judges 2:1–3 later shows.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the warning exposes the danger of compromise with idolatry, which later history confirms (compare Joshua 23:12–13).

Both traditions apply the verse to the danger of tolerating sin in one’s life, without applying it to any human group today.

Numbers 33:56

56It shall happen that as I thought to do to them, so I will do to you.”

Arminian

God warns that what he planned to do to the nations, he will do to Israel. Covenant privilege does not exempt from judgment.

Arminian readers see the conditional nature of covenant blessing. Israel’s position in the land depends on continued faithfulness, as the later exile shows (compare 2 Kings 17).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s holiness applied to his own people. He does not show partiality, and his warnings are meant to drive his people to faithfulness.

Both traditions read the verse as a sober warning, fulfilled in the exile, and as a call to take God’s holiness seriously.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
