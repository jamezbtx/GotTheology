Passage

Numbers 33

Verses 16–37 · WEB

This section covers the long middle of the journey, from Sinai to Mount Hor on the edge of Edom. It begins with Kibroth Hattaavah and Hazeroth, known from Numbers 11–12, and includes Kadesh in the wilderness of Zin, where the people refused to enter the land (compare Numbers 13–14; 20). Most of the stations between Hazeroth and Ezion Geber are mentioned only here, and their locations are unidentified. Ezion Geber is generally placed near the head of the Gulf of Aqaba, and Kadesh in the region south of Canaan, though exact sites are debated. Both traditions see in this sparse list the years of wandering that followed Israel’s unbelief: judgment was real, yet God kept leading, feeding, and preserving the next generation.

Numbers 33:16

16They traveled from the wilderness of Sinai, and encamped in Kibroth Hattaavah.

Arminian

From the wilderness of Sinai they camped at Kibroth Hattaavah, the graves of craving (compare Numbers 11:34). The name itself preserves a memory of judgment.

Arminian readers see the danger of desire that turns from gratitude to demand. The people’s craving had consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the heart’s tendency to idolize comfort, and God’s holy response to it. The station stands as a lasting warning (compare 1 Corinthians 10:6).

Both traditions read the name as a call to contentment and trust in God’s provision.

Numbers 33:17

17They traveled from Kibroth Hattaavah, and encamped in Hazeroth.

Arminian

From Kibroth Hattaavah they camped at Hazeroth, where Miriam and Aaron spoke against Moses (Numbers 12). The itinerary does not retell the story.

Arminian readers note that even leaders closest to Moses fell into envy, showing that privilege does not remove responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God defended his chosen servant and showed that leadership among his people is his appointment.

Both traditions draw the lesson that grumbling against God’s servants is serious, and that God is gracious in restoring those who repent, as Miriam was restored.

Numbers 33:18

18They traveled from Hazeroth, and encamped in Rithmah.

Arminian

From Hazeroth they camped at Rithmah. From here to Ezion Geber, most stations are named only in this chapter, and their locations are unknown.

Arminian readers note that the list now enters years about which Scripture says little, the long consequence of the refusal at Kadesh (Numbers 14:33–34).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that even years of discipline are recorded. God did not abandon his people during the wandering, though a generation would die in the wilderness.

Both traditions avoid guessing at locations and focus on God’s faithfulness through judgment.

Numbers 33:19

19They traveled from Rithmah, and encamped in Rimmon Perez.

Arminian

From Rithmah they camped at Rimmon Perez. The name is unknown outside this list.

Arminian readers see in the repeated pattern of setting out and camping a people still being invited to trust, even under discipline.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s patient preservation of a people under judgment, sustaining them for the sake of his promise to the coming generation.

Both traditions read the long middle of the list as testimony that God’s discipline is not abandonment (compare Hebrews 12:5–11).

Numbers 33:20

20They traveled from Rimmon Perez, and encamped in Libnah.

Arminian

From Rimmon Perez they camped at Libnah. A town named Libnah appears later in Judah, but it is not clear that it is the same place.

Arminian readers note that the wilderness years were not wasted; they were the setting in which God shaped a new generation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s purposes are not halted by human failure. He was preparing the people who would enter the land.

Both traditions treat the identification cautiously and draw the theological point rather than a geographical one.

Numbers 33:21

21They traveled from Libnah, and encamped in Rissah.

Arminian

From Libnah they camped at Rissah, another unidentified station. The repetition continues.

Arminian readers hear in the monotony a call to perseverance. Faith is often worked out in repeated, ordinary obedience. Perseverance, in this reading, is a continuing response to grace rather than a single past decision.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the steady rhythm of providence. God’s care is as present in the routine as in the miraculous.

Both traditions draw from the repetition the pastoral lesson that faithfulness in the long middle of life matters to God, even when nothing seems to happen.

Numbers 33:22

22They traveled from Rissah, and encamped in Kehelathah.

Arminian

From Rissah they camped at Kehelathah. The site is unknown.

Arminian readers note that each move required the people to rise and follow again, renewing their response to God’s leading. The people’s part was genuine, and the record honors it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the cloud and the word of the LORD governed every departure (compare Numbers 9:17–23). The list is the record of that guidance.

Both traditions see the itinerary as the outworking of chapter 9’s pattern: God leads, and his people follow, stage after stage.

Numbers 33:23

23They traveled from Kehelathah, and encamped in Mount Shepher.

Arminian

From Kehelathah they camped at Mount Shepher. The mountain is not otherwise known.

Arminian readers note that the journey included heights as well as plains, a picture of the varied terrain of faith. Height and lowland alike were part of the road God set before them.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s presence is not tied to famous places. A mountain unknown to history was known to him.

Both traditions avoid speculation and simply affirm that God accompanied his people wherever they went.

Numbers 33:24

24They traveled from Mount Shepher, and encamped in Haradah.

Arminian

From Mount Shepher they camped at Haradah. The name is unknown outside this list.

Arminian readers note that the record holds the whole journey, not only its highlights, and so calls readers to value faithful endurance.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Scripture’s record is selective but purposeful. These names testify to real places and real days in a real history.

Both traditions read the entry as a witness to God’s faithfulness in seasons that left little mark on later memory.

Numbers 33:25

25They traveled from Haradah, and encamped in Makheloth.

Arminian

From Haradah they camped at Makheloth. The site is unidentified.

Arminian readers see the wilderness generation living under the consequence of their unbelief, yet still within God’s care and still able to teach their children.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God preserving a covenant line through discipline. The children who would inherit the land were growing up in these camps.

Both traditions draw hope from this: God’s promises outlast the failures of one generation.

Numbers 33:26

26They traveled from Makheloth, and encamped in Tahath.

Arminian

From Makheloth they camped at Tahath. The location is unknown.

Arminian readers note that God’s patience gave the people time and opportunity to return to faithfulness, even within judgment. Judgment on one generation did not close the door of mercy for their children.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s patience is part of his covenant faithfulness. He bears with his people for the sake of his name.

Both traditions read the wilderness years as both judgment and mercy, without separating the two.

Numbers 33:27

27They traveled from Tahath, and encamped in Terah.

Arminian

From Tahath they camped at Terah. The name is not otherwise known as a place.

Arminian readers note that the journey was real and physical, involving labor, waiting, and endurance. Faith in this sense is not escape from ordinary life but trust exercised within it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that faith is lived out in history and geography, not in abstractions. God works through real times and places.

Both traditions see the importance of the concrete in Scripture: God’s dealings with his people happen in the world he made.

Numbers 33:28

28They traveled from Terah, and encamped in Mithkah.

Arminian

From Terah they camped at Mithkah. The site is unknown.

Arminian readers note that each stage was another opportunity for trust, and that the cumulative record shows a people sustained day by day. Arminian readers hear in this an invitation to daily, renewed dependence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s provision in the wilderness included clothing that did not wear out and feet that did not swell (compare Deuteronomy 8:4).

Both traditions see the wilderness as a school in which God taught his people to depend on him.

Numbers 33:29

29They traveled from Mithkah, and encamped in Hashmonah.

Arminian

From Mithkah they camped at Hashmonah. The location is unidentified.

Arminian readers note that the people’s journey shaped their character. Endurance in the wilderness was formative, not merely punitive. God used even consequences to train a people for faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God humbled and tested his people to show what was in their hearts (compare Deuteronomy 8:2). His discipline aimed at their good.

Both traditions read the wilderness as formation, a theme that Christians apply to seasons of trial in their own lives.

Numbers 33:30

30They traveled from Hashmonah, and encamped in Moseroth.

Arminian

From Hashmonah they camped at Moseroth. Deuteronomy 10:6 mentions Moserah in connection with Aaron’s death, which interpreters harmonize with Mount Hor in different ways.

Arminian readers note the textual question and hold it with patience rather than forcing a solution.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the relationship between Moserah and Mount Hor is debated, with several proposed explanations.

Both traditions treat such questions as part of careful reading, not as a threat to Scripture’s trustworthiness.

Numbers 33:31

31They traveled from Moseroth, and encamped in Bene Jaakan.

Arminian

From Moseroth they camped at Bene Jaakan. Deuteronomy 10:6 also mentions Bene Jaakan, though in a different order.

Arminian readers note that the variation in sequence is one of several details that show the itinerary was preserved in more than one form of tradition.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that interpreters offer different explanations for the order, including the possibility of repeated visits to the same region.

Both traditions hold the question with humility and do not build theology on the sequence.

Numbers 33:32

32They traveled from Bene Jaakan, and encamped in Hor Haggidgad.

Arminian

From Bene Jaakan they camped at Hor Haggidgad. The site is unknown.

Arminian readers note that the people kept following, even after decades of wandering, and that this continued obedience was itself meaningful. Late obedience is still obedience, and God received it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God kept his people moving toward the promise, even through a long and circuitous route.

Both traditions draw from this a lesson of hope: the long way is still God’s way when he leads it.

Numbers 33:33

33They traveled from Hor Haggidgad, and encamped in Jotbathah.

Arminian

From Hor Haggidgad they camped at Jotbathah. Deuteronomy 10:7 describes Jotbathah as a land of streams of water.

Arminian readers see God’s provision of water in the wilderness as a sign of care that invites gratitude.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God supplying his people according to his promise, even during years of discipline.

Both traditions take comfort that God provides refreshment along the way, and that his gifts in hard seasons are evidence of his faithfulness.

Numbers 33:34

34They traveled from Jotbathah, and encamped in Abronah.

Arminian

From Jotbathah they camped at Abronah. The location is unidentified.

Arminian readers note that each step brought the people closer to the edge of the land, though they could not see it yet. Arminian readers see hope offered to those who keep walking.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s timing governed the end of the wandering. The generation under judgment would pass, and the new one would enter.

Both traditions read the slow approach as evidence that God’s purposes move forward even when progress is hard to see.

Numbers 33:35

35They traveled from Abronah, and encamped in Ezion Geber.

Arminian

From Abronah they camped at Ezion Geber. Ezion Geber is generally placed near the head of the Gulf of Aqaba (compare 1 Kings 9:26), though the exact site is debated.

Arminian readers note that the people had travelled far south, a reminder of how long the way had become.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the route around Edom, rather than through it, reflects both Edom’s refusal and God’s direction (compare Numbers 20:14–21).

Both traditions see God guiding his people around obstacles, not only through them.

Numbers 33:36

36They traveled from Ezion Geber, and encamped at Kadesh in the wilderness of Zin.

Arminian

From Ezion Geber they camped at Kadesh in the wilderness of Zin. Kadesh is where the spies returned and the people refused to enter the land (Numbers 13–14), and where Miriam died and the rock was struck (Numbers 20).

Arminian readers see Kadesh as a place of decisive human choice, where unbelief cost a generation its inheritance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Kadesh as a place where God’s holiness and patience were both displayed, even toward Moses and Aaron (Numbers 20:12).

Both traditions hear the warning of Hebrews 3–4 against hardening one’s heart.

Numbers 33:37

37They traveled from Kadesh, and encamped in Mount Hor, in the edge of the land of Edom.

Arminian

From Kadesh they camped at Mount Hor, on the edge of Edom. The traditional site near Petra is debated.

Arminian readers note that the new generation now approached the land from a different direction, having learned from their parents’ failure.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God brought his people to the borders of Edom without conflict, keeping his word to Esau’s descendants (compare Deuteronomy 2:4–6).

Both traditions see the approach to Mount Hor as the beginning of the end of the wilderness years.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
