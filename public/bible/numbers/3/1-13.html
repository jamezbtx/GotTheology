Passage

Numbers 3

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Numbers 3:1–13 introduces the family of Aaron and Moses, recalls the deaths of Nadab and Abihu (Leviticus 10:1–2), and sets the Levites beside Aaron as assistants given to the priests. God then explains why: he claimed every firstborn of Israel when he struck Egypt’s firstborn, and he now takes the Levites in their place. Arminian and Reformed readers share the main themes: God orders who may draw near, holiness is guarded, and redemption creates a people who belong to him.

Numbers 3:1

1Now this is the history of the generations of Aaron and Moses in the day that the LORD spoke with Moses in Mount Sinai.

Arminian

‘The generations of Aaron and Moses’ introduces the priestly family. Arminians note that God works through real families and history, calling particular people to particular service at Sinai.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the formula (toledot) connects Numbers to Genesis’s family histories and to God’s covenant purposes. Both traditions agree that the verse grounds Israel’s worship in a real lineage and a real moment of revelation.

Numbers 3:2

2These are the names of the sons of Aaron: Nadab the firstborn, and Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar.

Arminian

Aaron’s four sons are named, with Nadab the firstborn. Arminians note that being in the chosen family brought great privilege but, as the next verses show, did not remove personal accountability.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters note that God appointed the priesthood to Aaron’s line by his own choice. Both traditions agree that the verse names the priests God set apart and introduces the warning of verse 4.

Numbers 3:3

3These are the names of the sons of Aaron, the priests who were anointed, whom he consecrated to minister in the priest’s office.

Arminian

These sons were anointed and consecrated to serve as priests (Leviticus 8). Arminians see consecration as God’s gracious setting apart, which calls for faithful response.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that consecration was God’s act through Moses, not a status the priests claimed for themselves (compare Hebrews 5:4). Both traditions agree that priestly office comes by divine appointment.

Numbers 3:4

4Nadab and Abihu died before the LORD when they offered strange fire before the LORD in the wilderness of Sinai, and they had no children. Eleazar and Ithamar ministered in the priest’s office in the presence of Aaron their father.

Arminian

Nadab and Abihu died for offering ‘strange fire’ (Leviticus 10:1–2). Arminians see a sober warning that even those anointed for service can act presumptuously and face judgment, so privilege calls for humility.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters stress that God must be worshiped as he commands, a text often cited in support of the regulative principle. Both traditions agree on the warning, and both note that the priesthood continued through Eleazar and Ithamar under Aaron.

Numbers 3:5

5The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

A divine speech introduces the Levites’ role. Arminians note again that God himself defines who serves and how, so the arrangement is his gracious design.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the Levites’ role rests on God’s word, not Israel’s invention. Both traditions read the formula as marking the following commands as revelation.

Numbers 3:6

6“Bring the tribe of Levi near, and set them before Aaron the priest, that they may minister to him.

Arminian

The Levites are brought near and set before Aaron to serve him. Arminians see the Levites as honored helpers whose service supports the priests and blesses the whole people.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters note the ordered relation: Levites assist, priests officiate. Both traditions see a pattern of distinct roles within one ministry, and neither reads the verse as ranking people’s worth before God.

Numbers 3:7

7They shall keep his requirements, and the requirements of the whole congregation before the Tent of Meeting, to do the service of the tabernacle.

Arminian

They keep the duties of Aaron and of the whole congregation at the Tent of Meeting. Arminians note that the Levites serve on behalf of all Israel, so their work is a shared offering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the Levites act as representatives for the congregation. Both traditions agree that the verse frames tabernacle service as service done for the whole people.

Numbers 3:8

8They shall keep all the furnishings of the Tent of Meeting, and the obligations of the children of Israel, to do the service of the tabernacle.

Arminian

They guard the furnishings and Israel’s obligations for the tabernacle service. Arminians see faithful care of holy things as a real form of worship and stewardship.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and note that ‘keep’ includes guarding the sanctuary from encroachment (compare 1:53). Both traditions agree on the meaning of the verse.

Numbers 3:9

9You shall give the Levites to Aaron and to his sons. They are wholly given to him on the behalf of the children of Israel.

Arminian

The Levites are ‘wholly given’ to Aaron on Israel’s behalf. Arminians see a picture of whole-hearted dedication to God’s service.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same language and see the Levites as a gift from God to the priesthood (compare 8:16–19). Both traditions read the verse as teaching full dedication and agree on it.

Numbers 3:10

10You shall appoint Aaron and his sons, and they shall keep their priesthood, but the stranger who comes near shall be put to death.”

Arminian

Aaron and his sons keep the priesthood, and an unauthorized outsider who comes near is to die. Arminians see the severity as teaching that access to God is on his terms.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that approach to the holy requires God’s appointment. Both traditions read the warning as fulfilled in Christ, through whom all believers now have access (Hebrews 10:19–22).

Numbers 3:11

11The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

A new divine speech explains why God takes the Levites. Arminians note that God gives reasons for his commands, inviting understanding rather than bare compliance.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that God explains his arrangement, rooting it in redemptive history. Both traditions agree on the function of this introduction.

Numbers 3:12

12“Behold, I have taken the Levites from among the children of Israel instead of all the firstborn who open the womb among the children of Israel; and the Levites shall be mine,

Arminian

God takes the Levites ‘instead of’ Israel’s firstborn. Arminians see substitution: one group stands in for another by God’s gracious appointment.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree on the substitutionary pattern. Both traditions see it as an anticipation of redemption through a substitute, though they note the Levites serve rather than die in place of the firstborn.

Numbers 3:13

13for all the firstborn are mine. On the day that I struck down all the firstborn in the land of Egypt I made holy to me all the firstborn in Israel, both man and animal. They shall be mine. I am the LORD.”

Arminian

‘All the firstborn are mine’, claimed when God struck Egypt’s firstborn (Exodus 13:2, 11–15). Arminians see that God’s claim on Israel arises from his redeeming act, and redemption calls for a grateful life.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that God’s ownership rests on Passover redemption. Both traditions connect this with believers being bought with a price (1 Corinthians 6:19–20) and with Christ as the firstborn (Colossians 1:15–18).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
