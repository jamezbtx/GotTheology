Passage

Numbers 11

Verses 16–30 · WEB

God answers Moses by sharing the Spirit on him with seventy elders and by promising meat for a whole month, a gift that will expose the people’s rejection of him. When Moses doubts, God asks whether his hand has grown short. The elders prophesy as a sign, and Eldad and Medad prophesy in the camp; Moses rebukes Joshua’s jealousy and wishes all the LORD’s people were prophets. This is shared ground about the Spirit, leadership, and God’s faithfulness to his word.

Numbers 11:16

16The LORD said to Moses, “Gather to me seventy men of the elders of Israel, whom you know to be the elders of the people and officers over them; and bring them to the Tent of Meeting, that they may stand there with you.

Arminian

God tells Moses to gather seventy recognized elders and officers at the Tent of Meeting. The answer to Moses’ burden is shared leadership.

Arminian readers see God working through people already known for their responsibility.

Reformed

God chooses the remedy: seventy elders who stand with Moses. The plan recalls the earlier sharing of judicial work in Exodus 18.

Reformed readers see God’s provision of ordered leadership. Both traditions read this as shared ground about God equipping many to serve his people.

Numbers 11:17

17I will come down and talk with you there. I will take of the Spirit which is on you, and will put it on them; and they shall bear the burden of the people with you, that you don’t bear it yourself alone.

Arminian

God will come down, take of the Spirit on Moses, and put it on the elders so they share the burden. The Spirit equips for service.

Arminian readers see the Spirit given for ministry, not hoarded by one leader.

Reformed

The Spirit on Moses is shared without being diminished in purpose. Leadership depends on God’s gift.

Reformed readers stress that God alone gives the Spirit and the ability to serve. Both traditions agree that Spirit-empowered service is a gift, not an achievement.

Numbers 11:18

18“Say to the people, ‘Sanctify yourselves in preparation for tomorrow, and you will eat meat; for you have wept in the ears of the LORD, saying, “Who will give us meat to eat? For it was well with us in Egypt.” Therefore the LORD will give you meat, and you will eat.

Arminian

The people must sanctify themselves, for tomorrow they will eat meat. They wept, saying it was well with them in Egypt.

Arminian readers see God answering the demand, but in a way that exposes it. Getting what one craves can itself be a judgment.

Reformed

God grants the request while naming its root: preferring Egypt to him. The gift carries warning.

Reformed readers see God giving people over to their desires as judgment, a theme also found in Psalm 106:15. Both traditions read this as a sobering, shared lesson.

Numbers 11:19

19You will not eat just one day, or two days, or five days, or ten days, or twenty days,

Arminian

Not one day, or two, or five, or ten, or twenty. The rhetoric builds.

Arminian readers see God’s answer matching and exceeding their demand, so that they learn its emptiness.

Reformed

The counting escalates toward a whole month. The surplus will become burdensome.

Reformed readers see God’s sovereignty in shaping how the request is fulfilled. Both traditions agree that the excess is meant to teach rather than simply to satisfy.

Numbers 11:20

20but a whole month, until it comes out at your nostrils, and it is loathsome to you; because you have rejected the LORD who is among you, and have wept before him, saying, “Why did we come out of Egypt?” ’ ”

Arminian

They will eat a whole month until it is loathsome, because they rejected the LORD among them and asked why they left Egypt. The real issue is named.

Arminian readers see rejection of God, not hunger, as the heart of the sin.

Reformed

The craving was a rejection of the LORD who is among them. Wanting Egypt meant rejecting redemption.

Reformed readers see unbelief as the root of grumbling. Both traditions read this as shared ground: discontent with God’s provision is ultimately discontent with God himself.

Numbers 11:21

21Moses said, “The people, among whom I am, are six hundred thousand men on foot; and you have said, ‘I will give them meat, that they may eat a whole month.’

Arminian

Moses notes there are six hundred thousand men on foot, and God has promised meat for a month. The numbers seem impossible.

Arminian readers see Moses honestly struggling to believe. Faith and doubt mix in a real leader.

Reformed

Moses measures the promise by human resources. The scale overwhelms him.

Reformed readers see even great servants of God needing their faith strengthened. Both traditions agree that the question prepares for God’s answer about the strength of his hand.

Numbers 11:22

22Shall flocks and herds be slaughtered for them, to be sufficient for them? Shall all the fish of the sea be gathered together for them, to be sufficient for them?”

Arminian

Moses asks whether all flocks, herds, or fish would be enough. He cannot imagine how God will do it.

Arminian readers see sincere bewilderment rather than defiance. God answers the question rather than punishing it.

Reformed

Moses’ question is practical and doubtful. He calculates rather than trusts.

Reformed readers see God patiently addressing his servant’s weakness. Both traditions note a similar pattern in the disciples’ questions before Jesus fed the crowds, as shared ground.

Numbers 11:23

23The LORD said to Moses, “Has the LORD’s hand grown short? Now you will see whether my word will happen to you or not.”

Arminian

God answers, “Has the LORD’s hand grown short?” Moses will see whether God’s word comes true.

Arminian readers see God inviting trust by pointing to his power. The coming event will prove his word.

Reformed

God’s rhetorical question rebukes doubt. His word will be fulfilled.

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to his word as the ground of confidence. Both traditions share this reading; Isaiah 50:2 and 59:1 later use similar language about the LORD’s hand.

Numbers 11:24

24Moses went out, and told the people the LORD’s words; and he gathered seventy men of the elders of the people, and set them around the Tent.

Arminian

Moses tells the people God’s words and gathers the seventy elders around the Tent. He obeys.

Arminian readers see Moses responding faithfully despite his doubts. Obedience does not wait for every question to be settled; he acts on God’s word.

Reformed

Moses carries out the instruction. The elders are positioned around the Tent.

Reformed readers see obedience following God’s reassurance. Both traditions read this as shared ground: Moses does what God commands, and the scene is set for the gift of the Spirit.

Numbers 11:25

25The LORD came down in the cloud, and spoke to him, and took of the Spirit that was on him, and put it on the seventy elders. When the Spirit rested on them, they prophesied, but they did so no more.

Arminian

The LORD comes down in the cloud and puts the Spirit on the seventy; they prophesy, but do so no more. The sign is temporary.

Arminian readers see the prophesying as a visible confirmation of the elders’ appointment.

Reformed

The prophesying authenticates the elders’ calling. That it did not continue shows its purpose was a sign.

Reformed readers see God marking his chosen leaders publicly. Both traditions agree the main point is the Spirit’s equipping for shared leadership, not ongoing prophetic office.

Numbers 11:26

26But two men remained in the camp. The name of one was Eldad, and the name of the other Medad; and the Spirit rested on them. They were of those who were written, but had not gone out to the Tent; and they prophesied in the camp.

Arminian

Eldad and Medad, listed among the seventy but still in the camp, also receive the Spirit and prophesy there. The Spirit is not bound to the Tent.

Arminian readers see God’s freedom in giving his Spirit beyond expected boundaries.

Reformed

The two were registered but absent. God still gives them the Spirit.

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign freedom in the Spirit’s work. Both traditions read this as shared ground; the reason they stayed in the camp is not explained, and interpreters should not guess too confidently.

Numbers 11:27

27A young man ran, and told Moses, and said, “Eldad and Medad are prophesying in the camp!”

Arminian

A young man runs to tell Moses that Eldad and Medad are prophesying in the camp. The news seems irregular.

Arminian readers see the community trying to make sense of unexpected gifts.

Reformed

The report raises the question of order and authority. Who may prophesy and where?

Reformed readers see the tension between God’s freedom and orderly leadership. Both traditions agree the scene prepares for Moses’ generous answer and is not a dispute about salvation.

Numbers 11:28

28Joshua the son of Nun, the servant of Moses, one of his chosen men, answered, “My lord Moses, forbid them!”

Arminian

Joshua, Moses’ servant and one of his chosen men, asks Moses to forbid them. He is protective of Moses’ authority.

Arminian readers see sincere zeal that is misdirected. Moses will correct Joshua gently, showing that loyalty to a leader must not hinder God’s work.

Reformed

Joshua’s loyalty is real but narrow. He fears a challenge to Moses.

Reformed readers see a faithful servant who still needs to learn. Both traditions note a parallel when the disciples wanted to stop someone casting out demons in Jesus’ name (Mark 9:38–40).

Numbers 11:29

29Moses said to him, “Are you jealous for my sake? I wish that all the LORD’s people were prophets, that the LORD would put his Spirit on them!”

Arminian

Moses answers, “Are you jealous for my sake? I wish that all the LORD’s people were prophets.” He welcomes the Spirit’s wider work.

Arminian readers see a longing for the Spirit to be given broadly. Christians connect it with Joel 2:28 and Pentecost (Acts 2:17).

Reformed

Moses rejects jealousy for his position. His wish looks forward to wider outpouring of the Spirit.

Reformed readers likewise see its fulfilment in the Spirit poured on all God’s people in Christ. Both traditions treat this as clear shared ground about humility and the Spirit’s gift.

Numbers 11:30

30Moses went into the camp, he and the elders of Israel.

Arminian

Moses and the elders return to the camp. The shared leadership is now in place.

Arminian readers see the episode resolved peacefully. Moses’ generosity sets the tone, and the elders now stand with him in the work.

Reformed

Leaders return together to serve the people. The burden is now shared.

Reformed readers see God’s provision completed. Both traditions read this as a quiet close to the elders’ story before the narrative returns to the quail and its consequences.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
