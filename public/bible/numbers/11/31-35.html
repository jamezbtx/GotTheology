Passage

Numbers 11

Verses 31–35 · WEB

God sends quail on a wind in overwhelming quantity, the people gather greedily, and a plague strikes them while the meat is still in their mouths. The place is named Kibroth Hattaavah, “graves of lust,” and the people move on to Hazeroth. Later Scripture recalls the episode as a warning against craving evil things. Both traditions read it soberly as judgment on a rejection of God, not on eating meat as such.

Numbers 11:31

31A wind from the LORD went out and brought quails from the sea, and let them fall by the camp, about a day’s journey on this side, and a day’s journey on the other side, around the camp, and about two cubits above the surface of the earth.

Arminian

A wind from the LORD brings quail from the sea, piled around the camp for a day’s journey and about two cubits deep. The WEB footnotes a cubit as about 18 inches.

Arminian readers see God keeping his word in overwhelming abundance.

Reformed

The wind is from the LORD; providence uses natural means. The quantity fulfils God’s promise.

Reformed readers see God governing creation to accomplish his word. Both traditions agree that the abundance is both answer and test, as Psalm 78:26–29 later recalls.

Numbers 11:32

32The people rose up all that day, and all of that night, and all the next day, and gathered the quails. He who gathered least gathered ten homers; and they spread them all out for themselves around the camp.

Arminian

The people gather quail for two days and a night; even the least gathers ten homers. The WEB footnotes a homer as about 220 liters.

Arminian readers see craving revealed in excessive gathering. The people’s response exposes their hearts.

Reformed

The gathering is relentless and greedy. Ten homers is a vast amount for one person.

Reformed readers see unrestrained desire taking hold. Both traditions read this as shared ground and contrast it with the manna, gathered only for each day’s need.

Numbers 11:33

33While the meat was still between their teeth, before it was chewed, the LORD’s anger burned against the people, and the LORD struck the people with a very great plague.

Arminian

While the meat is still in their mouths, the LORD’s anger burns and he strikes them with a great plague. Judgment comes swiftly.

Arminian readers see judgment on a persistent rejection of God, not on eating meat itself.

Reformed

The plague falls on those who craved. God’s holiness meets their rebellion.

Reformed readers see God’s righteous judgment on unbelief. Both traditions note that 1 Corinthians 10:6 uses these events as warnings not to crave evil things, and both handle the severity soberly.

Numbers 11:34

34The name of that place was called Kibroth Hattaavah, because there they buried the people who lusted.

Arminian

The place is named Kibroth Hattaavah, which the WEB footnote explains as meaning “graves of lust,” because there they buried those who craved. Memory is fixed in a name.

Arminian readers see a lasting warning about desire that turns from God.

Reformed

The name records both sin and judgment. Future generations would remember.

Reformed readers see God teaching his people through history. Both traditions read this as shared ground: craving that rejects God brings death, while gratitude receives his gifts rightly.

Numbers 11:35

35From Kibroth Hattaavah the people traveled to Hazeroth; and they stayed at Hazeroth.

Arminian

The people travel from Kibroth Hattaavah to Hazeroth and stay there. The journey continues.

Arminian readers see God continuing to lead his people despite their failure. Judgment does not end the journey; the people are still invited to follow and trust.

Reformed

The itinerary moves on. God has not abandoned Israel.

Reformed readers see God’s covenant faithfulness persisting through judgment. Both traditions share this reading, and Hazeroth becomes the setting for the next conflict, involving Miriam and Aaron.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
