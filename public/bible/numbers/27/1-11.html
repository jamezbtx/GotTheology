Passage

Numbers 27

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Numbers 27:1–11 records the appeal of Zelophehad’s five daughters, Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah, of the tribe of Manasseh. Their father died in the wilderness without sons, and they ask that his name and land not disappear from the family. Moses takes the case to the LORD, who declares that they ‘speak right’ and sets a general law of inheritance. Numbers 36 adds a marriage condition, and Joshua 17:3–6 records the land being granted. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that the passage shows God’s justice, his care for the vulnerable, and his willingness to hear a faithful appeal.

Numbers 27:1

1Then the daughters of Zelophehad, the son of Hepher, the son of Gilead, the son of Machir, the son of Manasseh, of the families of Manasseh the son of Joseph came near. These are the names of his daughters: Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah.

Arminian

The daughters of Zelophehad come forward, named one by one, with their lineage traced to Joseph. Arminians note that Scripture records these women by name, honoring their initiative and faith in God’s promise of land.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the careful genealogy, which ties the daughters to the tribal allotment God promised to Manasseh. Both traditions agree that the verse presents these women as rightful heirs of the promise who act on it.

Numbers 27:2

2They stood before Moses, before Eleazar the priest, and before the princes and all the congregation, at the door of the Tent of Meeting, saying,

Arminian

They stand before Moses, Eleazar, the leaders, and the whole congregation at the Tent of Meeting. Arminians admire their courage in bringing a just claim openly and through the proper channels.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters note the same public, orderly appeal before God’s appointed leaders at his sanctuary. Both traditions see an example of seeking justice through the means God has provided.

Numbers 27:3

3“Our father died in the wilderness. He was not among the company of those who gathered themselves together against the LORD in the company of Korah, but he died in his own sin. He had no sons.

Arminian

Their father ‘died in his own sin’, not as part of Korah’s rebellion. Arminians read this as meaning he died among the unbelieving wilderness generation for his own failings, without the extra guilt of open revolt.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the phrase separates him from Korah’s rebellion, whose families were judged (chapter 16), and places him among the generation that died in the wilderness. Both traditions agree that the daughters are not claiming their father was sinless, only that his line should not be cut off.

Numbers 27:4

4Why should the name of our father be taken away from among his family, because he had no son? Give to us a possession among the brothers of our father.”

Arminian

‘Why should the name of our father be taken away?’ Arminians see faith in God’s promise: the daughters want a share in the land God has pledged to give.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and see confidence in God’s covenant promise of land to every family. Both traditions note the concern for preserving a family’s name and inheritance in Israel.

Numbers 27:5

5Moses brought their cause before the LORD.

Arminian

Moses brings their case before the LORD. Arminians note that Moses does not decide on his own authority but seeks God’s will, a model of humble leadership.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree and see the verse as showing that God’s word, not human custom, settles the question. Both traditions note similar cases where Moses seeks a ruling (Leviticus 24:12; Numbers 9:8; 15:34).

Numbers 27:6

6The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD answers Moses. Arminians see God’s responsiveness to his people’s petitions, a God who hears and acts. The ruling comes directly from God rather than from Moses’ own judgment.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that God graciously answers and reveals his will. Both traditions read the verse as introducing a divine ruling and agree on its meaning.

Numbers 27:7

7“The daughters of Zelophehad speak right. You shall surely give them a possession of an inheritance among their father’s brothers. You shall cause the inheritance of their father to pass to them.

Arminian

‘The daughters of Zelophehad speak right.’ Arminians see God affirming their request and granting them an inheritance, which shows his care for those left vulnerable by the law’s usual patterns.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that God vindicates the daughters and extends his provision. Both traditions see God’s justice and compassion here, and neither reads the verse as overturning Israel’s family structure, which Numbers 36 also protects.

Numbers 27:8

8You shall speak to the children of Israel, saying, ‘If a man dies, and has no son, then you shall cause his inheritance to pass to his daughter.

Arminian

If a man has no son, his inheritance passes to his daughter. Arminians see a single case becoming a general law, showing God’s concern for every family in Israel.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the ruling becomes a lasting statute. Both traditions see the law protecting the family line and the land God assigned to it.

Numbers 27:9

9If he has no daughter, then you shall give his inheritance to his brothers.

Arminian

If there is no daughter, the inheritance goes to his brothers. Arminians see the law keeping land within the family God gave it to.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the order of succession keeps the allotment within the clan. Both traditions agree on the meaning of the verse.

Numbers 27:10

10If he has no brothers, then you shall give his inheritance to his father’s brothers.

Arminian

If there are no brothers, it goes to his father’s brothers. Arminians see the careful, ordered provision continuing outward through the family.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree. Both traditions read the verse as part of the orderly succession that preserves tribal inheritance, and they agree on it.

Numbers 27:11

11If his father has no brothers, then you shall give his inheritance to his kinsman who is next to him of his family, and he shall possess it. This shall be a statute and ordinance for the children of Israel, as the LORD commanded Moses.’ ”

Arminian

If there are no uncles, the nearest kinsman inherits, ‘a statute and ordinance’ as the LORD commanded. Arminians see God providing for every possibility so no inheritance is lost.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree and note that the ruling is given the force of permanent law. Both traditions see this as God’s faithfulness to the land promise across generations.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
