Passage

Numbers 19

Verses 11–22 · WEB

Contact with a corpse, bone, or grave makes a person unclean for seven days, and death in a tent defiles those inside and open vessels. A clean person sprinkles the ash-water with hyssop on the third and seventh days, while refusing purification defiles the sanctuary and brings being cut off. Both traditions read these laws as addressing ritual impurity from death rather than moral guilt, and share the conviction that God provides the cleansing his people cannot supply themselves.

Numbers 19:11

11“He who touches the dead body of any man shall be unclean seven days.

Arminian

Whoever touches a human corpse is unclean seven days. Arminian readers see death treated as the most serious source of defilement, opposed to the life of God’s presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the contrast between death and the holiness of God. Both traditions agree that the law addresses ritual impurity rather than moral guilt for caring for the dead.

Numbers 19:12

12He shall purify himself with water on the third day, and on the seventh day he shall be clean; but if he doesn’t purify himself the third day, then the seventh day he shall not be clean.

Arminian

The unclean person purifies with water on the third and seventh days to become clean. Arminian readers see a clear path to restoration that requires the person’s participation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God appointing the means by which cleansing is received. Both traditions agree that failing to use the provided remedy leaves the person unclean, as the next verse states.

Numbers 19:13

13Whoever touches a dead person, the body of a man who has died, and doesn’t purify himself, defiles the LORD’s tabernacle; and that soul shall be cut off from Israel; because the water for impurity was not sprinkled on him, he shall be unclean. His uncleanness is yet on him.

Arminian

Anyone who touches a corpse and refuses purification defiles the tabernacle and is cut off. Arminian readers see the seriousness of neglecting God’s appointed remedy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the danger of defiling God’s dwelling through disregard. Both traditions agree the verse teaches that uncleanness persists until the prescribed water is applied.

Numbers 19:14

14“This is the law when a man dies in a tent: everyone who comes into the tent, and everyone who is in the tent, shall be unclean seven days.

Arminian

When someone dies in a tent, all who enter or are inside become unclean seven days. Arminian readers see how death’s impurity spreads through ordinary living spaces.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the pervasive reach of death’s defilement. Both traditions agree the law teaches Israel to take seriously the presence of death in daily life.

Numbers 19:15

15Every open vessel, which has no covering bound on it, is unclean.

Arminian

Every open vessel in the tent becomes unclean. Arminian readers see even household items affected, showing the thoroughness of the purity regulations. Ordinary life is touched by death’s reach.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the detail as part of a comprehensive system. Both traditions agree that covered vessels are protected, illustrating careful attention to the boundaries of impurity.

Numbers 19:16

16“Whoever in the open field touches one who is slain with a sword, or a dead body, or a bone of a man, or a grave, shall be unclean seven days.

Arminian

Touching a slain person, a corpse, a bone, or a grave in the open field causes seven days of uncleanness. Arminian readers see death’s defilement extending beyond the home.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see contact with death in any form requiring purification. Both traditions agree the inclusion of bones and graves shows how widely the law applies.

Numbers 19:17

17“For the unclean, they shall take of the ashes of the burning of the sin offering; and running water shall be poured on them in a vessel.

Arminian

Ashes of the sin offering are mixed with running water in a vessel. Arminian readers see the stored provision put to use for those who need cleansing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the appointed means prepared for purification. Both traditions note that Hebrews 9:13 refers to the sprinkling of these ashes as sanctifying for the cleansing of the flesh.

Numbers 19:18

18A clean person shall take hyssop, dip it in the water, and sprinkle it on the tent, on all the vessels, on the persons who were there, and on him who touched the bone, or the slain, or the dead, or the grave.

Arminian

A clean person sprinkles the water with hyssop on the tent, vessels, and people. Arminian readers see cleansing applied to every person and object affected by death.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the purification reaching all that was defiled. Both traditions agree that the clean person acts on behalf of the unclean, who cannot cleanse themselves.

Numbers 19:19

19The clean person shall sprinkle on the unclean on the third day, and on the seventh day. On the seventh day, he shall purify him. He shall wash his clothes and bathe himself in water, and shall be clean at evening.

Arminian

The sprinkling occurs on the third and seventh days, followed by washing and cleanness at evening. Arminian readers see a process with defined steps leading to full restoration.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s ordered means bringing the unclean back to purity. Both traditions agree the timing and washing complete the purification described earlier.

Numbers 19:20

20But the man who shall be unclean, and shall not purify himself, that soul shall be cut off from among the assembly, because he has defiled the sanctuary of the LORD. The water for impurity has not been sprinkled on him. He is unclean.

Arminian

The unclean person who refuses purification is cut off for defiling the sanctuary. Arminian readers see a warning that rejecting God’s provision has serious consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the gravity of despising the appointed cleansing. Both traditions agree that the repetition from verse 13 emphasizes the importance of the remedy.

Numbers 19:21

21It shall be a perpetual statute to them. He who sprinkles the water for impurity shall wash his clothes, and he who touches the water for impurity shall be unclean until evening.

Arminian

This is a perpetual statute; the sprinkler and anyone touching the water become unclean until evening. Arminian readers see once more the paradox that the cleansing water renders handlers unclean.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note this feature of the ritual. Both traditions agree that it underlines the seriousness of impurity and the holiness of what deals with it.

Numbers 19:22

22“Whatever the unclean person touches shall be unclean; and the soul that touches it shall be unclean until evening.”

Arminian

Whatever the unclean person touches becomes unclean until evening. Arminian readers see the contagious nature of impurity, requiring vigilance throughout the community. Uncleanness does not stay put.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see impurity spreading through contact. Both traditions agree the chapter ends by reinforcing the need for the cleansing that God provided. The problem is ongoing, and so is the remedy.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
