Passage

Numbers 19

Verses 1–10 · WEB

God prescribes the red heifer: an unblemished, never-yoked animal slaughtered outside the camp, its blood sprinkled toward the Tent of Meeting, and its body burned with cedar, hyssop, and scarlet. The ashes are stored for the water of cleansing, and everyone who handles them becomes unclean until evening. Both traditions share the typology of Hebrews 9:13–14, which contrasts these ashes with the greater cleansing of Christ’s blood, and avoid detailed allegory beyond what Scripture supplies.

Numbers 19:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses and to Aaron, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses and Aaron, introducing a statute for cleansing. Arminian readers see God providing his people a remedy for the defilement that death brings into their camp.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God taking the initiative to address impurity among his people. Both traditions agree the ritual that follows is God’s provision rather than a human invention to manage uncleanness.

Numbers 19:2

2“This is the statute of the law which the LORD has commanded. Tell the children of Israel to bring you a red heifer without spot, in which is no defect, and which was never yoked.

Arminian

Israel must bring a red heifer without defect that has never been yoked. Arminian readers see the call to bring God an animal set apart and unspoiled for this special purpose.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God prescribing the offering’s fitness in detail. Both traditions share the typological reading of Hebrews 9:13–14, which compares the heifer’s ashes with the greater cleansing of Christ’s blood.

Numbers 19:3

3You shall give her to Eleazar the priest, and he shall bring her outside of the camp, and one shall kill her before his face.

Arminian

The heifer is given to Eleazar, taken outside the camp, and slaughtered before him. Arminian readers see the ritual set apart from the sanctuary, with the priest overseeing its proper performance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the sacrifice carried out under priestly supervision. Both traditions often link the location outside the camp with Hebrews 13:12, where Jesus suffers outside the gate to sanctify the people.

Numbers 19:4

4Eleazar the priest shall take some of her blood with his finger, and sprinkle her blood toward the front of the Tent of Meeting seven times.

Arminian

Eleazar sprinkles the blood toward the front of the Tent of Meeting seven times. Arminian readers see the ritual connected to God’s dwelling even though performed outside the camp.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the blood directed toward the sanctuary as the place of God’s presence. Both traditions agree the sevenfold sprinkling stresses completeness without requiring speculation about further symbolism.

Numbers 19:5

5One shall burn the heifer in his sight; her skin, and her meat, and her blood, with her dung, shall he burn.

Arminian

The whole heifer, including skin, flesh, blood, and dung, is burned. Arminian readers see total consumption producing the ashes needed for cleansing the community. Nothing of the animal is held back.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a complete burning that sets this sacrifice apart from ordinary offerings. Both traditions agree that the purpose is to create ashes for the water of purification described later.

Numbers 19:6

6The priest shall take cedar wood, hyssop, and scarlet, and cast it into the middle of the burning of the heifer.

Arminian

Cedar wood, hyssop, and scarlet are thrown into the fire. Arminian readers see materials also used in other cleansing rites, such as the cleansing of skin disease in Leviticus 14.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the same elements connecting this ritual to other purification laws. Both traditions avoid assigning detailed allegorical meanings beyond what Scripture itself provides.

Numbers 19:7

7Then the priest shall wash his clothes, and he shall bathe his flesh in water, and afterward he shall come into the camp, and the priest shall be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

The priest washes his clothes and body and remains unclean until evening. Arminian readers see the paradox that the one handling the means of cleansing becomes unclean himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that those who prepare the purification are rendered unclean. Both traditions agree this feature underlines the seriousness of dealing with death and impurity before a holy God.

Numbers 19:8

8He who burns her shall wash his clothes in water, and bathe his flesh in water, and shall be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

The one who burns the heifer must also wash and remains unclean until evening. Arminian readers see that everyone involved in the ritual is affected by its contact with impurity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the purification requirements applied to each participant. Both traditions agree that the repeated washings show how carefully Israel was taught to treat holiness and uncleanness.

Numbers 19:9

9“A man who is clean shall gather up the ashes of the heifer, and lay them up outside of the camp in a clean place; and it shall be kept for the congregation of the children of Israel for use in water for cleansing impurity. It is a sin offering.

Arminian

A clean man stores the ashes outside the camp for the water of cleansing; it is a sin offering. Arminian readers see God providing a lasting supply for the community’s ongoing needs.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a provision kept for repeated use among God’s people. Both traditions agree that calling it a sin offering connects ritual cleansing with the broader sacrificial system.

Numbers 19:10

10He who gathers the ashes of the heifer shall wash his clothes, and be unclean until the evening. It shall be to the children of Israel, and to the stranger who lives as a foreigner among them, for a statute forever.

Arminian

The gatherer of the ashes washes and is unclean until evening; the statute applies to Israel and resident foreigners forever. Arminian readers see the remedy offered to all who live among God’s people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the inclusion of foreigners under the same statute. Both traditions agree that the provision reaches beyond native Israelites to everyone dwelling in the camp.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
