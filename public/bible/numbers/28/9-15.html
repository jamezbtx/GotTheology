Passage

Numbers 28

Verses 9–15 · WEB

Numbers 28:9–15 adds the Sabbath and new-moon offerings to the daily ones. On each Sabbath two extra lambs are offered, and at each new month two bulls, a ram, seven lambs, their grain and drink offerings, and a male goat as a sin offering. Both traditions read these as marking holy time, week by week and month by month, with worship that belongs to God, and both see the festival calendar as a shadow fulfilled in Christ (Colossians 2:16–17).

Numbers 28:9

9“ ‘On the Sabbath day, you shall offer two male lambs a year old without defect, and two tenths of an ephah of fine flour for a meal offering mixed with oil, and its drink offering:

Arminian

On the Sabbath two extra lambs with grain and drink offerings are offered. Arminians see the Sabbath as a special day of rest and worship that deserves greater devotion.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree on the Sabbath’s significance. Christians in both traditions have differed among themselves over how the Sabbath relates to the Lord’s Day, and that question does not divide neatly along Arminian and Reformed lines.

Numbers 28:10

10this is the burnt offering of every Sabbath, in addition to the continual burnt offering and its drink offering.

Arminian

The Sabbath offering is added to the continual one. Arminians see that special days add to daily worship rather than replacing it.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the daily offering continues as the foundation. Both traditions agree on the verse. The pattern of adding to the daily offering, rather than replacing it, continues through the rest of the calendar.

Numbers 28:11

11“ ‘In the beginnings of your months, you shall offer a burnt offering to the LORD: two young bulls, one ram, seven male lambs a year old without defect,

Arminian

At the start of each month, two bulls, a ram, and seven lambs are offered. Arminians see the new moon as a regular reminder of God’s faithfulness through time.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree. Both traditions note that new moons appear elsewhere as times of worship (1 Samuel 20:5; Isaiah 66:23) and agree on the verse.

Numbers 28:12

12and three tenths of an ephah of fine flour for a meal offering mixed with oil, for each bull; and two tenth parts of fine flour for a meal offering mixed with oil, for the one ram;

Arminian

Three tenths of flour for each bull and two tenths for the ram. Arminians see that the grain offering grows with the size of the animal.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree on the graded proportions. Both traditions read the verse as procedural and agree on it. The same proportions of three tenths, two tenths, and one tenth recur at every festival in chapters 28–29.

Numbers 28:13

13and one tenth part of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering to every lamb, as a burnt offering of a pleasant aroma, an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

One tenth for each lamb, as a pleasant aroma. Arminians see God delighting in his people’s ordered, wholehearted worship.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and connect ‘pleasant aroma’ with Christ’s offering (Ephesians 5:2). Both traditions agree on the verse.

Numbers 28:14

14Their drink offerings shall be half a hin of wine for a bull, the third part of a hin for the ram, and the fourth part of a hin for a lamb. This is the burnt offering of every month throughout the months of the year.

Arminian

Wine offerings are scaled by animal: half, a third, and a quarter hin. Arminians see the careful proportion as reflecting God’s order.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Both traditions read the verse as completing the monthly offering and agree on it. The closing words make clear that this monthly pattern runs through the whole year.

Numbers 28:15

15Also, one male goat for a sin offering to the LORD shall be offered in addition to the continual burnt offering and its drink offering.

Arminian

A male goat is offered as a sin offering at each new month. Arminians see that ongoing worship includes regular acknowledgment of sin and cleansing.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and see the repeated sin offerings as pointing to the need for a final sacrifice (Hebrews 10:1–4, 11–14). Both traditions agree on the verse.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
