Passage

Numbers 5

Verses 11–31 · WEB

This section gives the “law of jealousy” (5:29), a procedure for a case the community could not otherwise settle: a husband suspects his wife of adultery, but there is no witness and she has not been caught (5:13). Instead of leaving the matter to the husband, the case is brought to the priest and set “before the LORD.” It involves a grain offering, holy water mixed with dust from the tabernacle floor, a sworn oath with a conditional curse, and the drinking of the “water of bitterness.” The outcome is left to God: if she is innocent she is “free” and will conceive (5:28); if guilty she bears her iniquity (5:31). The exact meaning of some terms, especially the physical effects in 5:21–22 and 5:27, is uncertain, and interpreters differ on them. This passage is unique in the Law, and the text gives no example of the procedure being carried out.

Numbers 5:11

11The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

A new instruction begins. The case that follows is sensitive, and the text handles it as a matter to be settled before God, not by private action.

Arminian readers approach it as part of God’s provision for justice within Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers also read it as revealed law for Israel’s covenant life under the old administration.

Both traditions read it carefully within its setting rather than applying it directly to the church.

Numbers 5:12

12“Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them: ‘If any man’s wife goes astray and is unfaithful to him,

Arminian

The case concerns a wife suspected of being unfaithful. Marriage faithfulness is treated as a matter that concerns the whole covenant community.

Arminian readers note that the law takes seriously both the possibility of real wrong and the danger of false suspicion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the seriousness Scripture gives to marriage and adultery.

Both traditions agree that the passage assumes adultery is a grave covenant breach, and that the procedure exists because the truth is unknown.

Numbers 5:13

13and a man lies with her carnally, and it is hidden from the eyes of her husband and this is kept concealed, and she is defiled, there is no witness against her, and she isn’t taken in the act;

Arminian

The key condition is that the act, if it happened, is hidden: “there is no witness against her, and she isn’t taken in the act.” Normally a matter required two or three witnesses (Deut 19:15).

Arminian readers see the law addressing a case ordinary courts could not decide.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the absence of evidence. When human judgment reaches its limit, the case is referred to God, before whom nothing is hidden (cf. Heb 4:13).

This is shared ground.

Numbers 5:14

14and the spirit of jealousy comes on him, and he is jealous of his wife and she is defiled; or if the spirit of jealousy comes on him, and he is jealous of his wife and she isn’t defiled;

Arminian

The verse explicitly allows for both possibilities: the husband may be jealous and she defiled, or jealous and she “isn’t defiled.” Suspicion alone does not establish guilt.

Arminian readers note that the law does not assume the woman is guilty.

Reformed

Reformed readers also stress that the text keeps both outcomes open. “The spirit of jealousy” describes the husband’s suspicion, which may or may not be justified.

Many interpreters in both traditions read the procedure as protecting a wife from a husband’s unchecked suspicion. That is an interpretive inference, but it fits this verse’s two-sided wording.

Numbers 5:15

15then the man shall bring his wife to the priest, and shall bring her offering for her: one tenth of an ephah of barley meal. He shall pour no oil on it, nor put frankincense on it, for it is a meal offering of jealousy, a meal offering of memorial, bringing iniquity to memory.

Arminian

The husband brings his wife to the priest with an offering: a tenth of an ephah of barley meal, with no oil or frankincense. It is called a “meal offering of jealousy” that brings “iniquity to memory.” (The WEB footnote gives an ephah as about 22 liters.)

Arminian readers note that the matter is taken out of the husband’s hands and brought before God through the priest.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that the lack of oil and frankincense sets the offering apart from joyful grain offerings; it is plain and serious.

Both traditions note that the text says it brings iniquity “to memory,” that is, it places the matter before the LORD; it does not presume the woman’s guilt.

Numbers 5:16

16The priest shall bring her near, and set her before the LORD.

Arminian

The priest sets the woman “before the LORD.” This is the central idea of the procedure: the case is placed in God’s presence.

Arminian readers see God himself as the judge of what no human can know.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize that judgment belongs to God, who sees in secret. The priest sets the woman before the LORD, not merely before himself, so the case is taken out of private accusation and placed at the sanctuary.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 5:17

17The priest shall take holy water in an earthen vessel; and the priest shall take some of the dust that is on the floor of the tabernacle and put it into the water.

Arminian

The priest mixes holy water in a clay vessel with dust from the tabernacle floor. The text does not explain the symbolism of the dust, and interpreters offer different suggestions.

Arminian readers emphasize that both the water and the dust come from the sanctuary, tying the procedure to God’s presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same observation and caution against explaining more than the text says.

Both traditions agree that the materials are ordinary. Nothing in the text suggests that the water itself has power to harm; the outcome is attributed to the LORD (5:21).

Numbers 5:18

18The priest shall set the woman before the LORD, and let the hair of the woman’s head go loose, and put the meal offering of memorial in her hands, which is the meal offering of jealousy. The priest shall have in his hand the water of bitterness that brings a curse.

Arminian

The priest loosens the woman’s hair, places the offering in her hands, and holds the “water of bitterness that brings a curse.” WEB uses similar wording for the leper’s hair in Leviticus 13:45; the meaning of the gesture here is not explained and is debated.

Arminian readers note the solemnity of the scene without assuming her guilt.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the loosened hair as part of a solemn public setting before the LORD; whether it signals shame, mourning, or something else is uncertain.

Both traditions agree not to build doctrine on the uncertain meaning of the gesture.

Numbers 5:19

19The priest shall cause her to take an oath and shall tell the woman, “If no man has lain with you, and if you haven’t gone aside to uncleanness, being under your husband’s authority, be free from this water of bitterness that brings a curse.

Arminian

The oath begins with the possibility of innocence: if she has not gone astray, she is to “be free” from the water. The first words spoken over her declare her innocence possible.

Arminian readers note how the oath protects an innocent woman.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the conditional form: the oath clears the innocent as clearly as it exposes the guilty.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 5:20

20But if you have gone astray, being under your husband’s authority, and if you are defiled, and some man has lain with you besides your husband—”

Arminian

The oath then states the other possibility: if she has been unfaithful. The sentence breaks off with a dash in WEB, and the curse follows in the next verse.

Arminian readers see that the woman is not condemned beforehand; everything depends on what is actually true.

Reformed

Reformed readers again note the conditional wording. The law does not assume what it cannot know.

Both traditions agree on this reading.

Numbers 5:21

21then the priest shall cause the woman to swear with the oath of cursing, and the priest shall tell the woman, “May the LORD make you a curse and an oath among your people, when the LORD allows your thigh to fall away, and your body to swell;

Arminian

The curse is spoken in the LORD’s name: if guilty, the LORD will make her “a curse and an oath” among her people, with her “thigh” falling away and her body swelling. The precise physical meaning is uncertain. Some interpreters, reading 5:28 as its contrast, take it to refer to loss of fertility; this is an interpretive view.

Arminian readers stress that the result, if any, is the LORD’s act, not the priest’s or the husband’s.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize that the outcome is in the hands of the sovereign God, who alone knows the truth.

Both traditions caution against speculating about the medical details the text does not define.

Numbers 5:22

22and this water that brings a curse will go into your bowels, and make your body swell, and your thigh fall away.” The woman shall say, “Amen, Amen.”

Arminian

The woman answers, “Amen, Amen.” She formally accepts the oath and places herself under God’s judgment.

Arminian readers note that she takes part in the procedure; she is not merely acted upon.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the double “Amen” as solemn agreement that the LORD should judge truly.

Both traditions agree that the response shows the oath’s seriousness.

Numbers 5:23

23“ ‘The priest shall write these curses in a book, and he shall wipe them into the water of bitterness.

Arminian

The priest writes the curses and wipes them into the water. The spoken words of the oath are literally joined to what she will drink.

Arminian readers see a vivid sign that the word of God goes with the act.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same observation: the written word and the sign are joined.

Both traditions avoid assigning a fuller symbolic meaning than the text gives.

Numbers 5:24

24He shall make the woman drink the water of bitterness that causes the curse; and the water that causes the curse shall enter into her and become bitter.

Arminian

The woman drinks the water “that causes the curse.” Verse 26 places the drinking after the offering, so this verse may summarize the step before the details are given.

Arminian readers again stress that any effect comes from the LORD, as 5:21 says.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the water as a means God uses in this case, not as a substance with power of its own.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 5:25

25The priest shall take the meal offering of jealousy out of the woman’s hand, and shall wave the meal offering before the LORD, and bring it to the altar.

Arminian

The priest takes the offering from her hands, waves it before the LORD, and brings it to the altar. The whole procedure takes place within worship.

Arminian readers note that the case is handled as a sacred matter, not a private quarrel.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the offering places the matter formally before God at his altar.

Both traditions agree on this.

Numbers 5:26

26The priest shall take a handful of the meal offering, as its memorial portion, and burn it on the altar, and afterward shall make the woman drink the water.

Arminian

A handful of the offering is burned as a memorial portion, and then she drinks. The order shows that the whole case is first presented to the LORD.

Arminian readers see the sequence as emphasizing that God judges, not the ritual itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the memorial portion brings the matter “to memory” before God (5:15).

Shared ground.

Numbers 5:27

27When he has made her drink the water, then it shall happen, if she is defiled and has committed a trespass against her husband, that the water that causes the curse will enter into her and become bitter, and her body will swell, and her thigh will fall away; and the woman will be a curse among her people.

Arminian

If she is guilty, the curse takes effect and she becomes “a curse among her people.” As in 5:21, the precise nature of the physical effect is uncertain.

Arminian readers note that hidden sin is not finally hidden from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see that God brings hidden things to light.

Both traditions treat this as a unique provision of the Mosaic covenant and note that the New Testament does not carry it over into the church. Christians in both traditions also read the curse of the law in light of Christ, who became “a curse for us” (Gal 3:13).

Numbers 5:28

28If the woman isn’t defiled, but is clean; then she shall be free, and shall conceive offspring.

Arminian

If she is innocent, she “shall be free, and shall conceive offspring” (WEB footnote: “or, seed”). The law ends with vindication and a positive promise.

Arminian readers stress how fully the innocent woman is cleared; she is not left under suspicion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that God himself vindicates the innocent.

Both traditions agree that the promise of offspring is a sign of blessing and restoration, though the exact relation to 5:21 and 5:27 is debated.

Numbers 5:29

29“ ‘This is the law of jealousy, when a wife, being under her husband, goes astray, and is defiled,

Arminian

The summary names this “the law of jealousy,” covering the case of a wife who has gone astray.

Arminian readers note that the law is presented as a defined, limited procedure, not a general license for suspicion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the formal closing typical of Levitical and Numbers laws. The heading “the law of jealousy” here names the case of a wife who has gone astray; the next verse will add the case of jealousy without proof, so the law is bounded and formal, not an open license for suspicion.

Shared ground.

Numbers 5:30

30or when the spirit of jealousy comes on a man, and he is jealous of his wife; then he shall set the woman before the LORD, and the priest shall execute on her all this law.

Arminian

The summary repeats the second case: a husband is jealous, whether or not there was unfaithfulness. In either case, he must bring her before the LORD and the priest must carry out this law.

Arminian readers again see that suspicion is handled by God’s ordained process, not by the husband’s own judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the summary repeats both possibilities from 5:14. Even when only “the spirit of jealousy” comes on the husband, he must set the woman before the LORD, and the priest must execute this law rather than leave the matter to a private ordeal.

Shared ground.

Numbers 5:31

31The man shall be free from iniquity, and that woman shall bear her iniquity.’ ”

Arminian

The husband is “free from iniquity” for bringing the case, and if she is guilty, the woman “shall bear her iniquity.” Each person answers for their own actions. The law addresses only a husband’s suspicion, not a wife’s, which reflects the marriage customs of its ancient setting.

Arminian readers stress personal accountability in this verse.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see that responsibility is placed where it belongs.

Both traditions note that the verse states the legal outcome only; it does not describe any human punishment beyond what the LORD brings.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
