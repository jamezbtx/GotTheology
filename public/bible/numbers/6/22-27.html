Passage

Numbers 6

Verses 22–27 · WEB

The section on Israel’s camp and its holiness (Numbers 1–6) closes with the priestly blessing, often called the Aaronic blessing. The LORD gives Aaron and his sons the exact words with which to bless Israel: three lines, each naming the LORD, asking that he would bless and keep, make his face shine and be gracious, and lift up his face and give peace. The final verse explains what it means: by these words the priests “put my name on the children of Israel,” and the LORD himself promises, “I will bless them.” The blessing is shared ground for every Christian tradition, and its language is echoed in Psalms 4:6 and 67:1.

Numbers 6:22

22The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD gives the next instruction to Moses, now concerning the blessing that closes this section.

Arminian readers note that the blessing begins with God’s initiative.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see that the words of blessing are given by God, not composed by the priests. The LORD speaks to Moses so that Aaron and his sons may put a blessing of God’s own wording on the people, not a formula of their devising.

Shared ground.

Numbers 6:23

23“Speak to Aaron and to his sons, saying, ‘This is how you shall bless the children of Israel.’ You shall tell them,

Arminian

Aaron and his sons are told: “This is how you shall bless the children of Israel.” Blessing is part of the priests’ appointed ministry.

Arminian readers see God’s desire to bless his people expressed in a regular, spoken form.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the priests speak words God has given, so the blessing carries his authority.

Both traditions agree, and many churches in both traditions use this blessing as a benediction.

Numbers 6:24

24‘The LORD bless you, and keep you.

Arminian

“The LORD bless you, and keep you.” Blessing includes God’s goodness and provision; keeping includes his protection.

Arminian readers see the LORD’s faithful care for his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s preserving care for his people.

This is shared ground. The verse is a prayer and blessing over the whole people and does not itself address debates about perseverance.

Numbers 6:25

25The LORD make his face to shine on you, and be gracious to you.

Arminian

“The LORD make his face to shine on you, and be gracious to you.” A shining face pictures favor and delight (cf. Ps 4:6; 67:1).

Arminian readers stress that grace is God’s free favor, offered warmly to his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress grace as God’s unearned favor.

Both traditions treat this as shared ground: the people’s good comes from God’s gracious face turned toward them.

Numbers 6:26

26The LORD lift up his face toward you, and give you peace.’

Arminian

“The LORD lift up his face toward you, and give you peace.” Peace (shalom) means wholeness and well-being, not only the absence of conflict.

Arminian readers see the climax of the blessing in restored, whole life with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see peace as the full fruit of God’s favor.

Both traditions read the triple naming of the LORD in verses 24–26 as emphatic. Some Christian readers also find it fitting in light of later Trinitarian blessings (cf. 2 Cor 13:14); that is a later Christian reading, not the text’s plain claim.

Numbers 6:27

27“So they shall put my name on the children of Israel; and I will bless them.”

Arminian

“So they shall put my name on the children of Israel; and I will bless them.” The priests speak, but the LORD himself blesses.

Arminian readers see the promise as God’s own commitment to bless his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize that the blessing’s power rests on God’s promise, not on the priest.

This is shared ground: to bear the LORD’s name is to belong to him and be under his blessing.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
