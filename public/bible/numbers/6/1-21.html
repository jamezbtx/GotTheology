Passage

Numbers 6

Verses 1–21 · WEB

Numbers 6:1–21 gives the law of the Nazirite (from a Hebrew root meaning “to separate”), a voluntary vow open to “either man or woman” (6:2) by which a person is set apart to the LORD for a period. The vow has three marks: no product of the grapevine, no razor on the head, and no contact with a corpse, even for close family. The law covers what to do if the vow is accidentally defiled (vv. 9–12) and the offerings that complete it (vv. 13–21). Elsewhere Scripture mentions lifelong Nazirites such as Samson (Judges 13:5), and Acts 21:23–26 describes Paul joining men completing a vow in the temple.

Numbers 6:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

A new instruction begins, now concerning voluntary dedication.

Arminian readers note that, after laws about uncleanness and wrongdoing, God now provides a way for ordinary Israelites to devote themselves to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same formula of revelation. Even voluntary devotion is regulated by God’s word.

Shared ground.

Numbers 6:2

2“Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them: ‘When either man or woman shall make a special vow, the vow of a Nazirite, to separate himself to the LORD,

Arminian

The Nazirite vow is open to “either man or woman.” It is a “special vow” to separate oneself to the LORD.

Arminian readers highlight the freely chosen nature of the vow and its availability to anyone in Israel, not only priests.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the vow is voluntary, but once made it binds the one who makes it.

Both traditions see the Nazirite as a layperson set apart in a way that resembles the priests’ holiness.

Numbers 6:3

3he shall separate himself from wine and strong drink. He shall drink no vinegar of wine, or vinegar of fermented drink, neither shall he drink any juice of grapes, nor eat fresh grapes or dried.

Arminian

The first mark of the vow is total abstinence from wine, strong drink and every grape product.

Arminian readers see visible self-denial expressing devotion to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that wine is not condemned elsewhere in the law; the abstinence is a sign of special consecration, not a general rule.

Both traditions agree on this. Luke 1:15 says John the Baptist would drink no wine nor strong drink.

Numbers 6:4

4All the days of his separation he shall eat nothing that is made of the grapevine, from the seeds even to the skins.

Arminian

Even the seeds and skins are excluded: “nothing that is made of the grapevine.” The rule is thorough.

Arminian readers note that consecration is meant to be wholehearted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the thoroughness as marking the vow clearly off from ordinary life. From the seeds even to the skins, every product of the grapevine is barred for the whole period of separation, so the Nazirite’s table is visibly different from that of other Israelites.

Shared ground.

Numbers 6:5

5“ ‘All the days of his vow of separation no razor shall come on his head, until the days are fulfilled in which he separates himself to the LORD. He shall be holy. He shall let the locks of the hair of his head grow long.

Arminian

The second mark is uncut hair. The Nazirite “shall be holy” for the vow’s duration, and the long hair is a visible sign of that.

Arminian readers see an outward sign of an inward commitment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note similar language about no razor in Judges 13:5 (Samson) and 1 Samuel 1:11 (Hannah’s vow for Samuel).

Both traditions agree that the sign has meaning because of God’s word, not because of any power in the hair.

Numbers 6:6

6“ ‘All the days that he separates himself to the LORD he shall not go near a dead body.

Arminian

The third mark is avoiding any dead body for the whole period.

Arminian readers note that the Nazirite, like the high priest, is set apart from the defilement of death.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same connection: the Nazirite’s holiness resembles priestly holiness (cf. Lev 21:11 for the high priest).

Shared ground.

Numbers 6:7

7He shall not make himself unclean for his father, or for his mother, for his brother, or for his sister, when they die, because his separation to God is on his head.

Arminian

Not even for father, mother, brother or sister may the Nazirite become unclean, “because his separation to God is on his head.” (WEB footnote: “God” here translates Elohim.)

Arminian readers note the cost of the vow: devotion to God takes priority even over family obligations.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the priority of God’s claim over even the closest human bonds.

Both traditions agree on this. The “separation… on his head” refers to the uncut hair as the visible sign of the vow.

Numbers 6:8

8All the days of his separation he is holy to the LORD.

Arminian

The summary: “All the days of his separation he is holy to the LORD.”

Arminian readers see holiness here as belonging to the LORD for a set time by a freely made commitment.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that “holy to the LORD” means set apart for God’s use and claim. The phrase summarizes the whole period of the vow—the hair, the drink, and the corpse rules—as a temporary consecration, not a claim about the person’s standing in every other respect.

Shared ground.

Numbers 6:9

9“ ‘If any man dies very suddenly beside him, and he defiles the head of his separation, then he shall shave his head in the day of his cleansing. On the seventh day he shall shave it.

Arminian

If someone dies suddenly beside the Nazirite, the vow is defiled even though the Nazirite did nothing deliberate. The hair is shaved on the seventh day, the day of cleansing.

Arminian readers note that the law deals with accidental defilement as well as chosen actions.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that ritual uncleanness can come without moral intent, as in 5:2, and still require cleansing.

Both traditions agree on this.

Numbers 6:10

10On the eighth day he shall bring two turtledoves or two young pigeons to the priest, to the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

On the eighth day the Nazirite brings two birds, the inexpensive offering, to the priest at the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian readers see a merciful, affordable way to restore the vow.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that restoration comes through God’s appointed means at the sanctuary. On the eighth day the Nazirite brings two turtledoves or two young pigeons, the same inexpensive pair used in other restorations, to the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Shared ground.

Numbers 6:11

11The priest shall offer one for a sin offering, and the other for a burnt offering, and make atonement for him, because he sinned by reason of the dead, and shall make his head holy that same day.

Arminian

One bird is a sin offering and the other a burnt offering, and the priest makes atonement “because he sinned by reason of the dead.” Here “sinned” covers unintended defilement.

Arminian readers note that atonement is provided even for unintentional failures.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that even unintended defilement needs atonement before God.

Both traditions agree on this, and that the head is made holy again “that same day.”

Numbers 6:12

12He shall separate to the LORD the days of his separation, and shall bring a male lamb a year old for a trespass offering; but the former days shall be void, because his separation was defiled.

Arminian

The vow begins again, with a lamb for a trespass offering, and “the former days shall be void.” The time already completed does not count.

Arminian readers see that a vow, once defiled, must be fully restarted, which underlines its seriousness.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point and see the strictness of the vow.

Both traditions read the verse as ritual law about this particular vow, not as a statement about the security of salvation.

Numbers 6:13

13“ ‘This is the law of the Nazirite: when the days of his separation are fulfilled, he shall be brought to the door of the Tent of Meeting,

Arminian

Now the law describes how the vow is completed when its days are fulfilled. The Nazirite comes to the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian readers note that the vow has an end; it is a period of devotion, not necessarily lifelong.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that the completion, like the vow itself, takes place before God at the sanctuary.

Shared ground.

Numbers 6:14

14and he shall offer his offering to the LORD: one male lamb a year old without defect for a burnt offering, one ewe lamb a year old without defect for a sin offering, one ram without defect for peace offerings,

Arminian

The offerings are substantial: a burnt offering, a sin offering and peace offerings, all without defect.

Arminian readers see the completion as an act of worship, with sin offering, dedication and fellowship together.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that even after faithful service, the Nazirite brings a sin offering.

Both traditions agree that devotion does not earn standing before God; atonement and thanksgiving go together.

Numbers 6:15

15a basket of unleavened bread, cakes of fine flour mixed with oil, and unleavened wafers anointed with oil with their meal offering and their drink offerings.

Arminian

Unleavened bread, cakes, wafers, meal offerings and drink offerings accompany the sacrifices.

Arminian readers see generous worship marking a completed commitment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the details follow God’s prescriptions, not personal invention. The unleavened cakes and wafers with oil, together with the meal and drink offerings, accompany the animal sacrifices in the pattern already given for grain offerings.

Shared ground.

Numbers 6:16

16The priest shall present them before the LORD, and shall offer his sin offering and his burnt offering.

Arminian

The priest presents the offerings before the LORD and offers the sin offering and burnt offering.

Arminian readers note the priest’s mediating role.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize priestly mediation, which Christians in both traditions see fulfilled in Christ’s priesthood (cf. Heb 7:25).

Shared ground.

Numbers 6:17

17He shall offer the ram for a sacrifice of peace offerings to the LORD, with the basket of unleavened bread. The priest shall offer also its meal offering and its drink offering.

Arminian

The ram is offered as a peace offering with the bread, and the priest also offers its meal and drink offerings.

Arminian readers see the peace offering as celebrating fellowship with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the peace offering as expressing communion between God and the worshiper.

Shared ground.

Numbers 6:18

18The Nazirite shall shave the head of his separation at the door of the Tent of Meeting, take the hair of the head of his separation, and put it on the fire which is under the sacrifice of peace offerings.

Arminian

The Nazirite shaves the consecrated hair at the Tent and puts it on the fire under the peace offering. The sign of the vow is given to God.

Arminian readers note that the hair is not kept as a souvenir; it belongs to the LORD.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the burning of the hair as marking the vow’s completion before God.

Both traditions agree. Acts 18:18 mentions Paul shaving his head because of a vow, though the text does not say it was a Nazirite vow.

Numbers 6:19

19The priest shall take the boiled shoulder of the ram, one unleavened cake out of the basket, and one unleavened wafer, and shall put them on the hands of the Nazirite after he has shaved the head of his separation;

Arminian

The priest places the boiled shoulder of the ram, a cake and a wafer in the Nazirite’s hands.

Arminian readers note the Nazirite’s active participation in the closing worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe the careful order of the rite: what has been offered to God is placed in the worshiper’s hands and then presented by the priest.

Both traditions read this as shared ground, marking the vow’s close as an act of fellowship before the LORD.

Numbers 6:20

20and the priest shall wave them for a wave offering before the LORD. They are holy for the priest, together with the breast that is waved and the thigh that is offered. After that the Nazirite may drink wine.

Arminian

The priest waves them before the LORD; they become the priest’s portion. “After that the Nazirite may drink wine.” The vow ends and ordinary life resumes.

Arminian readers note that the restriction was for a set time, not a lasting judgment that wine is wrong.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see that the vow’s end is marked clearly by God’s word. After the wave offering, the holy portions belong to the priest, and “the Nazirite may drink wine” again; the restriction is lifted as plainly as it was imposed.

Shared ground.

Numbers 6:21

21“ ‘This is the law of the Nazirite who vows and of his offering to the LORD for his separation, in addition to that which he is able to afford. According to his vow which he vows, so he must do after the law of his separation.’ ”

Arminian

The summary adds that a Nazirite may offer more than required, “in addition to that which he is able to afford,” and must do whatever he vowed.

Arminian readers stress the integrity of keeping one’s own word to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the binding nature of vows made to God.

Both traditions agree that a vow, once made, must be kept. Amos 2:11–12 later rebukes Israel for making Nazirites drink wine.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
