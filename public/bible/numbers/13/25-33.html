Passage

Numbers 13

Verses 25–33 · WEB

After forty days the spies return to Kadesh and report. They confirm that the land flows with milk and honey, but they dwell on its strong people and fortified cities. Caleb urges the people to go up at once, but the other spies insist Israel cannot win and spread an evil report, describing giants who made them feel like grasshoppers.

Numbers 13:25

25They returned from spying out the land at the end of forty days.

Arminian

The spies return after forty days. The number will return in 14:34, where each day becomes a year of wandering. A limited scouting trip becomes, by their unbelief, the measure of a generation’s judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same link. The forty days of scouting become the measure of judgment, a correspondence God himself will state when the sentence is given.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:26

26They went and came to Moses, to Aaron, and to all the congregation of the children of Israel, to the wilderness of Paran, to Kadesh; and brought back word to them and to all the congregation. They showed them the fruit of the land.

Arminian

They report to Moses, Aaron, and the whole congregation at Kadesh in the wilderness of Paran, and show the fruit. The report is public, so its effect will be national.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The rebellion that follows at Kadesh, with its forty years in the wilderness, becomes a lasting warning in later Scripture (Psalm 95:10–11, quoted in Hebrews 3).

Numbers 13:27

27They told him, and said, “We came to the land where you sent us. Surely it flows with milk and honey, and this is its fruit.

Arminian

The spies confirm God’s word: the land “flows with milk and honey.” Their testimony agrees with the promise in every respect except its trustworthiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the irony. The spies confess the goodness of the gift while about to doubt the Giver. This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:28

28However, the people who dwell in the land are strong, and the cities are fortified and very large. Moreover, we saw the children of Anak there.

Arminian

“However” turns the report. The people are strong, the cities fortified, and the descendants of Anak are there. The facts may be accurate, but they are presented as reasons to despair.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see unbelief not in noticing difficulty but in weighing it without reference to God. This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:29

29Amalek dwells in the land of the South. The Hittite, the Jebusite, and the Amorite dwell in the hill country. The Canaanite dwells by the sea, and along the side of the Jordan.”

Arminian

The spies list the nations: Amalek, Hittites, Jebusites, Amorites, and Canaanites. The list adds to the sense of overwhelming opposition. Most of them are among the peoples God had already promised to drive out (Exodus 3:8).

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same contrast with God’s earlier promise. Both traditions see the report as forgetting what God had said.

Numbers 13:30

30Caleb stilled the people before Moses, and said, “Let’s go up at once, and possess it; for we are well able to overcome it!”

Arminian

Caleb quiets the people and urges them to go up at once, confident they can overcome. His confidence rests on God’s promise, not on Israel’s strength. Arminian readers see a model of faith freely exercised against majority opinion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Caleb’s courage as faith produced by God’s grace (compare 14:24). Both traditions treat Caleb as an example to imitate.

Numbers 13:31

31But the men who went up with him said, “We aren’t able to go up against the people; for they are stronger than we.”

Arminian

The other spies contradict Caleb: “We aren’t able to go up against the people; for they are stronger than we.” Their conclusion leaves God out entirely and answers Caleb’s confidence with a comparison of human strength alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The heart of the failure is unbelief, as Hebrews 3:19 later states. “They are stronger than we” treats the contest as if the LORD were not the one who had promised the land.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:32

32They brought up an evil report of the land which they had spied out to the children of Israel, saying, “The land, through which we have gone to spy it out, is a land that eats up its inhabitants; and all the people who we saw in it are men of great stature.

Arminian

The report becomes an “evil report”: the land “eats up its inhabitants.” The spies now distort even the land’s goodness. Unbelief drifts from fear into misrepresentation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same progression. Sin grows when unbelief is not checked. The “evil report” now claims that the land “eats up its inhabitants,” distorting even the goodness the spies themselves had seen and displayed.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:33

33There we saw the Nephilim, the sons of Anak, who come from the Nephilim. We were in our own sight as grasshoppers, and so we were in their sight.”

Arminian

They claim to have seen the Nephilim, linking the Anakites with the figures of Genesis 6:4, and say they looked like grasshoppers. Interpreters differ on how literally to take the claim; in context it is part of an exaggerated report. Their view of themselves shapes their view of God’s promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise treat the claim as part of an evil report and note the debated meaning of “Nephilim.” Both traditions stress that seeing oneself as a grasshopper reflects forgetting the God who had defeated Egypt.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
