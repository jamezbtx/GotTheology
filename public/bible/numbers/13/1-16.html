Passage

Numbers 13

Verses 1–16 · WEB

At the LORD’s command, Moses sends twelve leaders, one from each tribe, from the wilderness of Paran to spy out Canaan, the land God has promised to give Israel. The list of names includes Caleb of Judah and Hoshea of Ephraim, whom Moses renames Joshua.

Numbers 13:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The episode begins with God’s word to Moses. What follows is a test of whether Israel will trust the God who has already brought them out of Egypt.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the mission starts with God’s initiative. The God who speaks here is the God who has promised the land.

Numbers 13:2

2“Send men, that they may spy out the land of Canaan, which I give to the children of Israel. Of every tribe of their fathers, you shall send a man, every one a prince among them.”

Arminian

God commands the mission and calls Canaan the land “which I give to the children of Israel.” The gift is already certain from God’s side; the scouting is about how Israel will enter. Deuteronomy 1:22 recalls that the people first proposed sending men, and Moses agreed; the two accounts give complementary angles.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the land is promised as a gift before any report comes back. The spies are not sent to decide whether the promise is good but to see what God is giving. Both traditions note the Deuteronomy 1 account.

Numbers 13:3

3Moses sent them from the wilderness of Paran according to the commandment of the LORD. All of them were men who were heads of the children of Israel.

Arminian

Moses obeys, sending leaders who were heads of their tribes. The men are chosen for their standing, which makes their later failure more serious, since their report sways the whole nation.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same observation. Responsibility rises with leadership. These were “heads of the children of Israel,” sent from the wilderness of Paran at the LORD’s command, so the later evil report is a failure of official witnesses, not of casual travelers.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:4

4These were their names: Of the tribe of Reuben, Shammua the son of Zaccur.

Arminian

The list begins with Reuben, the firstborn. Each tribe is represented, so the whole nation shares in what the spies see and report.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the representatives act on behalf of their tribes, which is why the outcome affects everyone. Little divergence arises here.

Numbers 13:5

5Of the tribe of Simeon, Shaphat the son of Hori.

Arminian

Shaphat the son of Hori represents Simeon. His name appears only here; like most of the twelve, he is otherwise unknown. The roll call underlines that every tribe had a witness to the goodness of the land, so later unbelief cannot be blamed on having no representative of its own.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The list functions as a record of accountability. Simeon’s named leader went with the others and saw the land, so the tribe later shared in a report its own head had been sent to verify.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:6

6Of the tribe of Judah, Caleb the son of Jephunneh.

Arminian

Caleb of Judah is named here. He will stand out later for his faith (13:30; 14:24). Arminian readers note that individual faithfulness is possible even when the majority falls into unbelief.

Reformed

Reformed readers also highlight Caleb, whose “other spirit” (14:24) they read as the fruit of God’s grace working in him. Both traditions honor him as an example.

Numbers 13:7

7Of the tribe of Issachar, Igal the son of Joseph.

Arminian

Igal represents Issachar. Most of these names appear nowhere else in Scripture, and the ten unfaithful spies are remembered only for their report.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same contrast between the forgotten ten and the remembered two. Igal the son of Joseph stands for Issachar and, like most of this list, is not mentioned again; Scripture remembers him only as one of those who brought back the report.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:8

8Of the tribe of Ephraim, Hoshea the son of Nun.

Arminian

Hoshea of Ephraim is introduced; verse 16 records that Moses called him Joshua. He will become Moses’ successor and will lead the next generation into the land, one of the two spies who trust God’s promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. His presence here prepares for his later role in bringing the next generation into the land. Naming him now, before the name change in verse 16, ties the coming conquest to this mission and to Ephraim’s representative.

Numbers 13:9

9Of the tribe of Benjamin, Palti the son of Raphu.

Arminian

Palti the son of Raphu represents Benjamin. The list continues with each tribe’s leader, and this name, like most of the ten who later fail, appears nowhere else. Benjamin too had its own witness to the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the list the same way. Palti stands among the official heads sent from Paran; no theological difference is at stake in the naming, but the verse keeps Benjamin inside the same accountable mission as the other tribes.

Numbers 13:10

10Of the tribe of Zebulun, Gaddiel the son of Sodi.

Arminian

Gaddiel the son of Sodi represents Zebulun. The names show that the mission was a formal, national undertaking, not a private scouting trip, and Zebulun’s leader is recorded with the rest.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Gaddiel is otherwise unknown, yet his name stands in the official list so that Zebulun’s later response can be weighed against the testimony its own head was sent to bring back.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:11

11Of the tribe of Joseph, of the tribe of Manasseh, Gaddi the son of Susi.

Arminian

Gaddi represents Manasseh, described as part of the tribe of Joseph. Joseph’s descendants form two tribes, Ephraim and Manasseh, so Levi is not among the twelve here.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same tribal arrangement. Levi, set apart for sanctuary service, is not counted among the tribes that will receive territory.

Numbers 13:12

12Of the tribe of Dan, Ammiel the son of Gemalli.

Arminian

Ammiel the son of Gemalli represents Dan. The roll call continues through the northern tribes, and Dan, soon to be the largest camp in chapter 2, also sends a named head to see the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers read this the same way. Ammiel’s name, like most on the list, does not return in later narrative; the verse simply records that Dan too was represented among the twelve.

No divergence arises.

Numbers 13:13

13Of the tribe of Asher, Sethur the son of Michael.

Arminian

Sethur the son of Michael represents Asher. The name is otherwise unknown, but it places Asher among the tribes that had a firsthand witness of the land before the later report was given.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise treat the verse as part of the formal list. Sethur stands as Asher’s official representative, so the tribe is not left without a named witness when the report is later made.

Numbers 13:14

14Of the tribe of Naphtali, Nahbi the son of Vophsi.

Arminian

Nahbi the son of Vophsi represents Naphtali. Like most of the twelve, he is mentioned only here, yet his name completes Naphtali’s place in the official mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The verse records Naphtali’s representative alongside the others, so every remaining tribe except Gad has now been named in the same accountable list.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:15

15Of the tribe of Gad, Geuel the son of Machi.

Arminian

Geuel the son of Machi represents Gad, completing the twelve. Levi is not listed, as in the military census, and Joseph is represented through Ephraim and Manasseh already named. Every tribe that will enter the conflict has had a man of its own on the mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the list’s completeness: every tribe will have heard firsthand testimony from its own representative. Geuel closes the roll, and the mission can now proceed as a national act rather than as the errand of a few.

Numbers 13:16

16These are the names of the men who Moses sent to spy out the land. Moses called Hoshea the son of Nun Joshua.

Arminian

Moses renames Hoshea as Joshua. Hoshea means something like “salvation”; Joshua adds the divine name, meaning roughly “the LORD saves.” The Greek form of Joshua is the same as the name Jesus, which is why Hebrews 4:8 can refer to Joshua by that name.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same meaning and the same Greek link. Many in both traditions see a typological connection: Joshua leads the people into the land, while Jesus brings a greater rest (Hebrews 4:8–9). The text itself does not explain the renaming.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
