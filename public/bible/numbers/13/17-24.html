Passage

Numbers 13

Verses 17–24 · WEB

Moses gives the spies their instructions: go through the Negev into the hill country and observe the land, its people, its cities, and its fruitfulness. They travel from the wilderness of Zin to Rehob near Hamath, see the descendants of Anak at Hebron, and carry back a huge cluster of grapes from the valley of Eshcol.

Numbers 13:17

17Moses sent them to spy out the land of Canaan, and said to them, “Go up this way by the South, and go up into the hill country.

Arminian

The spies are sent through the South, the Negev, into the hill country. The mission is practical reconnaissance for entering a land God has already promised.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that prudent planning is not opposed to trusting God’s promise. Means and promise go together. This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:18

18See the land, what it is; and the people who dwell therein, whether they are strong or weak, whether they are few or many;

Arminian

Moses asks about the people: strong or weak, few or many. These are fair questions, but the answers will become the occasion for unbelief when they are weighed apart from God’s promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same thing. Gathering information is not sin; reasoning from it as if God were absent is. Moses asks whether the people are strong or weak, few or many—fair questions, which the spies will later treat as if the LORD had given no promise.

Numbers 13:19

19and what the land is that they dwell in, whether it is good or bad; and what cities they are that they dwell in, whether in camps, or in strongholds;

Arminian

The spies are to assess the land and its cities, whether open camps or strongholds. The details prepare for the report in verse 28.

Reformed

Reformed readers read this the same way. The spies are also to notice whether the cities are camps or strongholds, details that prepare for the report in verse 28. No theological difference is at stake in the questions themselves; the issue will be how the answers are used.

Numbers 13:20

20and what the land is, whether it is fertile or poor, whether there is wood therein, or not. Be courageous, and bring some of the fruit of the land.” Now the time was the time of the first-ripe grapes.

Arminian

Moses tells them to be courageous and to bring back fruit. It is the season of first-ripe grapes. The fruit will be tangible evidence that God’s promise of a good land is true.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fruit as a visible pledge of the promise. The call to courage anticipates the test of faith to come.

Numbers 13:21

21So they went up, and spied out the land from the wilderness of Zin to Rehob, to the entrance of Hamath.

Arminian

The spies cover the land from the wilderness of Zin in the south to near Hamath in the north. They see the whole of what God is giving.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same breadth. The spies’ firsthand knowledge leaves them without excuse for their later report.

Numbers 13:22

22They went up by the South, and came to Hebron; and Ahiman, Sheshai, and Talmai, the children of Anak, were there. (Now Hebron was built seven years before Zoan in Egypt.)

Arminian

At Hebron they see Ahiman, Sheshai, and Talmai, descendants of Anak. The note that Hebron was built seven years before Zoan in Egypt marks Hebron’s antiquity. Hebron is also where Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob were buried (Genesis 23; 49:29–31), so it ties the land to the patriarchal promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers also notice the link to Abraham at Hebron. The place of the patriarchs’ burial now stands as a reminder of God’s promise, yet it is also where the spies see the giants that frighten them.

Numbers 13:23

23They came to the valley of Eshcol, and cut down from there a branch with one cluster of grapes, and they bore it on a staff between two. They also brought some of the pomegranates and figs.

Arminian

The cluster of grapes is so large that two men carry it on a pole, along with pomegranates and figs. The land really is as good as God said.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the fruit as evidence of God’s faithfulness to his word. The cluster is so large that two men carry it on a staff, with pomegranates and figs besides, so the land’s richness is not a rumor but something the people can see.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 13:24

24That place was called the valley of Eshcol, because of the cluster which the children of Israel cut down from there.

Arminian

The valley is named Eshcol, meaning “cluster,” after the grapes the Israelites cut there. The name preserves the memory of the land’s abundance, so later fear has to stand beside a place-name that testifies to what they actually found.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The naming of the valley after the cluster fixes the evidence in Israel’s memory; the land’s goodness is not left as a passing impression.

Little divergence arises here.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
