Passage

Numbers 7

Verses 48–83 · WEB

The second six days record the identical gifts of Ephraim, Manasseh, Benjamin, Dan, Asher, and Naphtali, completing the twelve tribes in the order of the camp in Numbers 2. The unchanged wording again gives each tribe equal standing before the LORD at his altar. Readers in both traditions find in the list a picture of a whole people, tribe by tribe, dedicating worship according to God’s order.

Numbers 7:48

48On the seventh day Elishama the son of Ammihud, prince of the children of Ephraim,

Arminian

Ephraim, the younger son of Joseph, heads the western camp (Numbers 2:18) and offers seventh. Arminian readers recall Jacob blessing Ephraim before Manasseh (Genesis 48:14–20). Both traditions note that the second half of the list begins with the Joseph tribes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Ephraim’s position as reflecting God’s freedom in blessing, the younger before the elder, as in Genesis 48. Shared ground: Elishama’s gift is the same as every other leader’s.

Numbers 7:49

49gave his offering: one silver platter, the weight of which was one hundred thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

Ephraim’s platter and bowl match all the others, beginning the second six days. Arminian readers see that the generosity of the first half continues unbroken. Both traditions agree that the sanctuary shekel ensured a uniform and honest standard.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the honest standard a reflection of God’s justice (compare Leviticus 19:36). Shared ground: the meal offering dedicates daily provision, and the weights are set by the sanctuary standard.

Numbers 7:50

50one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

Ephraim’s gold ladle of incense begins the second half. Arminian readers see the continuity of worship. Both traditions note that gold ladles served the holy place, where incense was burned (Exodus 30:7–8).

Reformed

Reformed readers note the connection with the altar of incense and its daily morning and evening use. Shared ground: incense represents worship and prayer offered according to God’s command, and Ephraim’s gift equals the rest.

Numbers 7:51

51one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

Ephraim’s burnt offering of bull, ram, and lamb begins the second half. Arminian readers see the continuing devotion of the tribes. Both traditions agree that the burnt offering was wholly consumed, giving all to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the burnt offering was accepted on the worshiper’s behalf (Leviticus 1:4). Shared ground: Ephraim’s offering pictures complete devotion, and both traditions see the burnt offerings fulfilled in Christ.

Numbers 7:52

52one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

Ephraim’s sin offering begins the second half. Arminian readers see the continuing need for atonement even after six days of offerings, since each tribe must bring its own. Both traditions agree that the sin offering addresses defilement.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sin offering as pointing to Christ, who was made sin for us (2 Corinthians 5:21). Shared ground: no tribe approaches God’s altar without atonement, and Ephraim’s prominence does not exempt it.

Numbers 7:53

53and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old. This was the offering of Elishama the son of Ammihud.

Arminian

Ephraim’s peace offerings begin the second half. Arminian readers see continuing fellowship. Both traditions agree that the peace offering is a shared meal of joy. The verse closes by naming Elishama.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the peace offering as a sign of covenant fellowship, in which the worshiper shares a meal before God. Shared ground: Elishama is named, and the peace offering celebrates communion with the LORD.

Numbers 7:54

54On the eighth day Gamaliel the son of Pedahzur, prince of the children of Manasseh,

Arminian

Manasseh, Joseph’s firstborn, offers eighth through Gamaliel, after Ephraim. Arminian readers note that Joseph’s two sons each count as full tribes, as Jacob declared (Genesis 48:5). Both traditions see the inclusion as a fulfillment of Jacob’s blessing.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Manasseh follows Ephraim, consistent with Jacob’s crossed hands (Genesis 48:14). Shared ground: both Joseph tribes stand equally before the altar, each with a full offering recorded in identical words.

Numbers 7:55

55gave his offering: one silver platter, the weight of which was one hundred thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

Manasseh’s silver vessels with flour and oil are recorded word for word. Arminian readers note that repetition honors each giver without favoritism. Both traditions agree that the record avoids summary to give Manasseh its own full account.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the full record as reflecting God’s attention to each gift. Shared ground: the meal offering is accepted on the same terms from every tribe, and both traditions read it as worship ordered by God.

Numbers 7:56

56one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

Manasseh’s incense ladle is identical to the others. Arminian readers see equal access to the privilege of worship. Both traditions agree that the precious gold reflects the honor due to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the identical gift as an expression of covenant unity among the tribes. Shared ground: incense symbolizes the people’s prayers, the ladles serve the sanctuary’s worship, and Manasseh’s gift equals Ephraim’s.

Numbers 7:57

57one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

Manasseh’s burnt offering matches Ephraim’s in every detail. Arminian readers see equal devotion across the two Joseph tribes, neither outdoing the other. Both traditions agree that the offering signifies total dedication to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the offering as part of the covenant worship God established. Shared ground: the whole burnt offering is given to the LORD, and both traditions read it in light of Hebrews.

Numbers 7:58

58one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

Manasseh’s sin offering of a male goat matches Ephraim’s. Arminian readers see equal need and equal provision: the same sacrifice suffices for each tribe. Both traditions agree that the sin offering is God’s gracious provision.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sin offering as part of covenant worship, where God provides the means of cleansing before fellowship. Shared ground: atonement is required, and God supplies it to Manasseh as to every other tribe.

Numbers 7:59

59and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old. This was the offering of Gamaliel the son of Pedahzur.

Arminian

Manasseh’s peace offerings match Ephraim’s. Arminian readers see equal fellowship for both Joseph tribes. Both traditions agree that the peace offering celebrates communion with God. The verse closes by naming Gamaliel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the peace offering as part of covenant worship, a celebration that follows atonement. Shared ground: Gamaliel is named personally, and the peace offering is shared before the LORD.

Numbers 7:60

60On the ninth day Abidan the son of Gideoni, prince of the children of Benjamin,

Arminian

Benjamin, Jacob’s youngest son, offers ninth with the western camp (Numbers 2:22). Arminian readers note that the smallest or youngest is not overlooked. Both traditions agree that Abidan’s gift receives the same complete record as the rest.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Benjamin, sharing Rachel as mother with Joseph, completes the western camp. Shared ground: youngest or eldest, each tribe’s offering is recorded in full before the LORD.

Numbers 7:61

61gave his offering: one silver platter, the weight of which was one hundred thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

Benjamin’s grain offering in silver vessels shows that the youngest tribe gives as fully as the eldest. Arminian readers see equality of opportunity in worship. Both traditions agree the meal offering expresses thanksgiving and dedication.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the identical gift reflects equal standing in the covenant. Shared ground: the meal offering acknowledges God as the giver of every harvest and is presented according to his instructions.

Numbers 7:62

62one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

Benjamin’s gold ladle of incense shows the youngest tribe sharing fully in worship. Arminian readers note God’s welcome of all who come as he directs. Both traditions agree that incense represents prayer and devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the equal gift as reflecting God’s impartial acceptance of each tribe. Shared ground: the incense offering is presented according to God’s order and pictures worship rising to him.

Numbers 7:63

63one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

Benjamin’s burnt offering shows the youngest tribe giving as fully as the eldest. Arminian readers see total devotion offered by all. Both traditions agree that the burnt offering expressed complete surrender to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the burnt offering as pointing to Christ’s perfect obedience and sacrifice, the complete offering no animal could provide. Shared ground: the whole offering belongs to God. Benjamin’s gift is no smaller.

Numbers 7:64

64one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

Benjamin’s sin offering shows the youngest tribe also needing atonement. Arminian readers see that sin is universal and that God’s provision reaches everyone. Both traditions agree that the sin offering addresses this shared need.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sin offering as pointing to Christ’s atoning sacrifice, which accomplishes what the repeated animal offerings could only picture. Shared ground: every tribe needs and receives atonement.

Numbers 7:65

65and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old. This was the offering of Abidan the son of Gideoni.

Arminian

Benjamin’s peace offerings show the youngest tribe sharing fully in fellowship. Arminian readers see God’s welcome. Both traditions agree that the peace offering expresses joy and communion. The verse closes by naming Abidan.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the peace offering as a covenant meal of fellowship, celebrated after the sin and burnt offerings. Shared ground: Abidan is named, and Benjamin’s fellowship with God is celebrated as fully as Judah’s.

Numbers 7:66

66On the tenth day Ahiezer the son of Ammishaddai, prince of the children of Dan,

Arminian

Dan heads the northern camp (Numbers 2:25) and offers tenth through Ahiezer. Arminian readers note Dan’s later role as rear guard on the march (Numbers 10:25). Both traditions agree that Dan, son of Rachel’s servant Bilhah, has an equal share.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Dan’s position completes the camp’s order on the north. Shared ground: the tribe receives the same full record, and its gift is accepted at God’s altar alongside the others.

Numbers 7:67

67gave his offering: one silver platter, the weight of which was one hundred thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

Dan’s platter and bowl with flour and oil continue the pattern. Arminian readers see faithful participation regardless of a tribe’s later history. Both traditions agree that the offering’s value lies in its conformity to God’s command.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the offering as an act of covenant faithfulness ordered by God. Shared ground: the meal offering presents the labor of the land to the LORD, and its silver vessels serve the sanctuary.

Numbers 7:68

68one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

Dan’s ladle of incense continues the pattern. Arminian readers see sincere devotion offered by each tribe. Both traditions agree that the incense offering belongs to the holy worship of the sanctuary.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the importance of offering incense only as God commanded, a lesson Leviticus 10 had made vivid. Shared ground: incense symbolizes prayer, and Dan’s gold ladle honors God’s presence.

Numbers 7:69

69one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

Dan’s burnt offering repeats the bull, ram, and lamb. Arminian readers see sincere devotion from each tribe, including those at the edges of the camp. Both traditions agree that the burnt offering is a gift wholly given to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the burnt offering as part of the system that foreshadowed Christ’s complete self-giving. Shared ground: the offering symbolizes complete dedication and is accepted by God from Dan as from Judah.

Numbers 7:70

70one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

Dan’s sin offering repeats the pattern. Arminian readers see God’s provision of atonement extended to all, so that no tribe is left without a way to approach. Both traditions agree that the sin offering purifies the worshiper.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sin offering as part of the system fulfilled in Christ, whose single offering replaced the many (Hebrews 10:12). Shared ground: atonement precedes dedication. Dan, like every tribe, comes by way of the sin offering.

Numbers 7:71

71and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old. This was the offering of Ahiezer the son of Ammishaddai.

Arminian

Dan’s peace offerings repeat the pattern of seventeen animals. Arminian readers see fellowship offered to all tribes. Both traditions see in this tenth peace offering the same joyful communion with God. The verse closes by naming Ahiezer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the peace offering as part of the system fulfilled in Christ, through whom believers have peace with God. Shared ground: Ahiezer is named, and the peace offering is shared.

Numbers 7:72

72On the eleventh day Pagiel the son of Ochran, prince of the children of Asher,

Arminian

Asher, camped with Dan on the north (Numbers 2:27), offers eleventh through Pagiel. Arminian readers note the persistence of the pattern even late in the sequence. Both traditions agree that repetition here emphasizes equal honor rather than wasting words.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Asher, a son of Zilpah, again shows that the servant women’s sons share equal standing. Shared ground: the eleventh day is recorded as carefully as the first.

Numbers 7:73

73gave his offering: one silver platter, the weight of which was one hundred thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

Asher’s silver vessels and grain offering match the rest. Arminian readers note that late in the sequence the gift is neither reduced nor inflated. Both traditions agree that consistency in giving honors God’s order.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the sameness a reminder that worship is not competition between tribes. Shared ground: each tribe gives the same, and the meal offering dedicates daily provision to God.

Numbers 7:74

74one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

Asher’s gold ladle of incense is recorded as fully as the others. Arminian readers see consistency in worship. Both traditions agree that the repetition emphasizes each tribe’s equal share in the privilege of prayer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gift as part of worship ordered by God, not invented by the giver. Shared ground: incense pictures the prayers of God’s people, offered through his appointed means, and Asher’s share is equal.

Numbers 7:75

75one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

Asher’s burnt offering is recorded identically to every other. Arminian readers see consistency in devotion even on the eleventh day. Both traditions agree that the burnt offering expresses entire dedication to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repetition as emphasizing faithful conformity to God’s command rather than variety for its own sake. Shared ground: the whole offering belongs to God. Asher’s offering is complete.

Numbers 7:76

76one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

Asher’s sin offering is recorded identically. Arminian readers see consistent acknowledgment of sin late in the sequence, without any sense that later tribes need less. Both traditions agree that the sin offering is necessary for worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sin offering as reflecting God’s holiness, which every worshiper must reckon with. Shared ground: every tribe brings a sin offering, and Asher is no exception. The pattern holds.

Numbers 7:77

77and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old. This was the offering of Pagiel the son of Ochran.

Arminian

Asher’s peace offerings are recorded identically. Arminian readers see consistent fellowship offered by God to every tribe. Both traditions agree that the peace offering expresses communion with God. The verse closes by naming Pagiel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the peace offering as celebrating covenant fellowship after atonement. Shared ground: Pagiel is named personally, and fellowship with the LORD is celebrated. The eleventh day ends as the first did, with a shared meal.

Numbers 7:78

78On the twelfth day Ahira the son of Enan, prince of the children of Naphtali,

Arminian

Naphtali, the last tribe of the northern camp (Numbers 2:29), offers on the twelfth day through Ahira. Arminian readers note that the final tribe is honored as fully as the first. Both traditions agree that the sequence is now complete.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the completion of the twelve as the whole covenant people represented at the altar. Shared ground: from Judah to Naphtali every tribe brings an identical, complete offering.

Numbers 7:79

79gave his offering: one silver platter, the weight of which was one hundred thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

Naphtali’s platter and bowl complete the twelve meal offerings. Arminian readers see the whole people’s generosity brought to completion. Both traditions agree that the twelve sets of vessels, totaled in 7:85, served the altar.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the completed set as a picture of the whole people’s dedication. Shared ground: the final meal offering matches the first, and all twelve are accepted on equal terms by the LORD.

Numbers 7:80

80one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

Naphtali’s ladle completes the twelve incense offerings. Arminian readers see the whole people united in worship. Both traditions agree that the total of twelve gold ladles (7:86) honors God’s sanctuary.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the completed set as a picture of the whole covenant people at prayer. Shared ground: incense symbolizes worship, and Naphtali’s gift equals Judah’s. The twelfth ladle completes the set totaled in 7:86.

Numbers 7:81

81one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

Naphtali’s burnt offering completes the twelve. Arminian readers see the whole people’s devotion brought to completion, last tribe as wholehearted as the first. Both traditions agree that the twelve burnt offerings (7:87) represent complete dedication.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Naphtali’s burnt offering, the last of twelve, as rounding out the whole people’s consecration to God. Shared ground: the burnt offering belongs to God and is fulfilled in Christ’s offering of himself.

Numbers 7:82

82one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

Naphtali’s sin offering completes the twelve. Arminian readers see the whole people acknowledging sin. Both traditions agree that the twelve sin offerings (7:87) together show that every tribe approached the altar through atonement.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the final sin offering means every tribe, from the royal tribe to the last, has acknowledged its need for atonement. Shared ground: the sin offering is fulfilled in Christ.

Numbers 7:83

83and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old. This was the offering of Ahira the son of Enan.

Arminian

Naphtali’s peace offerings complete the twelve. Arminian readers see the whole people united in fellowship. Both traditions agree that the peace offerings (totaled in 7:88) express communion. The verse closes by naming Ahira.

Reformed

With this last peace offering, Reformed readers see the twelve days ending in the whole people’s fellowship with God. Shared ground: the giver is named, and the peace offering is fulfilled in Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
