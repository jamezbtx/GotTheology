Passage

Numbers 7

Verses 84–89 · WEB

The chapter closes by totaling the twelve leaders’ gifts: silver and gold vessels by weight and the animals for each kind of offering. It then records Moses entering the Tent of Meeting and hearing the LORD’s voice from above the mercy seat between the cherubim. The dedicated altar and the speaking God belong together: the people bring their gifts, and God meets and speaks with his people’s mediator.

Numbers 7:84

84This was the dedication offering of the altar, on the day when it was anointed, by the princes of Israel: twelve silver platters, twelve silver bowls, twelve golden ladles;

Arminian

The summary totals the dedication gifts: twelve silver platters, twelve bowls, twelve gold ladles. Arminian readers see the combined generosity of the whole people. Both traditions note that the summary turns twelve individual records into one corporate act of worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the totals as presenting the gifts as one dedication of the whole covenant people. Shared ground: the altar is dedicated on the day of its anointing by gifts from every tribe, each recorded and then counted together.

Numbers 7:85

85each silver platter weighing one hundred thirty shekels, and each bowl seventy; all the silver of the vessels two thousand four hundred shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary;

Arminian

The silver totals 2,400 shekels, about 24 kilograms by the WEB footnote’s estimate of a shekel. Arminian readers see costly, willing generosity. Both traditions agree that the sanctuary shekel provides an honest standard of measure for what is given to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the arithmetic exactly matches twelve platters and bowls (12 × 200 shekels). Shared ground: the careful accounting reflects integrity in handling what is offered to God, a principle later echoed in Paul’s care with collected gifts (2 Corinthians 8:20–21).

Numbers 7:86

86the twelve golden ladles, full of incense, weighing ten shekels apiece, according to the shekel of the sanctuary; all the gold of the ladles weighed one hundred twenty shekels;

Arminian

The twelve gold ladles of incense total 120 shekels, about 1.2 kilograms. Arminian readers see worship and prayer represented by every tribe. Both traditions agree that the gold, though small in weight, honors the holy service of incense.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the precise total, consistent with twelve ladles of ten shekels. Shared ground: the incense offerings together picture the prayers of the whole people, and the gold reflects the honor due to God’s presence.

Numbers 7:87

87all the cattle for the burnt offering twelve bulls, the rams twelve, the male lambs a year old twelve, and their meal offering; and twelve male goats for a sin offering;

Arminian

The burnt offerings total twelve bulls, twelve rams, and twelve lambs with their meal offerings, plus twelve goats for sin offerings. Arminian readers see complete devotion and humble acknowledgment of sin. Both traditions agree that atonement and dedication belong together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the totals of burnt and sin offerings as underlining the need for atonement for the whole people. Shared ground: both traditions read these offerings as shadows fulfilled in Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice (Hebrews 10:1–10).

Numbers 7:88

88and all the cattle for the sacrifice of peace offerings: twenty-four bulls, sixty rams, sixty male goats, and sixty male lambs a year old. This was the dedication offering of the altar, after it was anointed.

Arminian

The peace offerings total twenty-four bulls and sixty each of rams, goats, and lambs, by far the largest group. Arminian readers see joyful fellowship as the culmination of the dedication. Both traditions agree that the altar’s dedication ends in celebration.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the abundance of peace offerings as celebrating fellowship with God after atonement. Shared ground: the dedication moves from atonement to communion, and both traditions see Christ as the one who makes peace with God (Romans 5:1).

Numbers 7:89

89When Moses went into the Tent of Meeting to speak with the LORD, he heard his voice speaking to him from above the mercy seat that was on the ark of the Testimony, from between the two cherubim; and he spoke to him.

Arminian

Moses enters the Tent of Meeting and hears God’s voice from above the mercy seat, between the cherubim, as God had promised (Exodus 25:22). Arminian readers see a God who speaks personally with his people’s mediator. Both traditions agree that the dedicated altar leads to divine communication.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s word as the climax: the people bring gifts, and God speaks. The mercy seat, where atonement blood was later sprinkled (Leviticus 16:14–15), is the place of God’s speaking. Shared ground: God meets his people at the place of atonement and makes himself known by his word.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
