Passage

Numbers 7

Verses 12–47 · WEB

The first six days of the altar’s dedication record the gifts of Judah, Issachar, Zebulun, Reuben, Simeon, and Gad, following the order of the camp in Numbers 2. Each leader brings exactly the same gift: silver vessels with a grain offering, a gold ladle of incense, and animals for burnt, sin, and peace offerings. The repetition gives every tribe the same full record, so that no tribe’s gift is summarized or passed over.

Numbers 7:12

12He who offered his offering the first day was Nahshon the son of Amminadab, of the tribe of Judah,

Arminian

Judah goes first, matching its place at the head of the eastern camp (Numbers 2:3). Nahshon later appears in David’s genealogy (Ruth 4:20). Arminian readers see leadership exercised by example. Both traditions note Judah’s prominence anticipated in Genesis 49:10.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Judah’s precedence fits God’s purposes for the royal line, and Matthew 1:4 places Nahshon among Jesus’ ancestors. Shared ground: the first gift comes from the tribe through which the promised King will come.

Numbers 7:13

13and his offering was: one silver platter, the weight of which was one hundred thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

The silver platter (about 1.3 kilograms, per the WEB footnote on the shekel) and bowl hold fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering (Leviticus 2). Arminian readers see costly devotion offered freely. Both traditions note the standard sanctuary shekel.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the vessels are weighed by the sanctuary shekel, a fixed standard set by God, not the giver. Shared ground: the meal offering presents the fruit of labor to God.

Numbers 7:14

14one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

The gold ladle, about 100 grams, is full of incense. Incense is linked with prayer elsewhere in Scripture (Psalm 141:2). Arminian readers see prayer and worship rising to God. Both traditions agree that incense was offered only as God prescribed.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that incense must follow God’s command, as Leviticus 10:1–2 showed. The gold marks its use near the holy place. Shared ground: incense symbolizes worship and prayer offered to God.

Numbers 7:15

15one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

A young bull, a ram, and a year-old lamb are offered as a burnt offering, wholly consumed on the altar (Leviticus 1). Arminian readers see total devotion freely given. Both traditions connect the burnt offering with the call to offer oneself to God (Romans 12:1).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the burnt offering as symbolizing complete consecration, with atonement in view (Leviticus 1:4). Shared ground: the whole offering belongs to God, and both traditions see it fulfilled in Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice (Hebrews 10:10).

Numbers 7:16

16one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

A male goat is the sin offering, matching the offering prescribed for a leader’s sin (Leviticus 4:22–23). Arminian readers see that even leaders need atonement. Both traditions agree that dedication begins with dealing with sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sin offering as purification required before fellowship with a holy God. Shared ground: leaders are not exempt, and both traditions see the sin offering fulfilled in Christ (Hebrews 9:26; 2 Corinthians 5:21).

Numbers 7:17

17and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old. This was the offering of Nahshon the son of Amminadab.

Arminian

The peace offerings (two cattle, five rams, five goats, five lambs) are the largest part of the gift. Part was eaten by the worshipers (Leviticus 7:15), so the dedication ends in shared fellowship. Arminian readers see joyful communion. This closes Nahshon’s offering.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the peace offering as celebrating restored fellowship after atonement. Both traditions see Christ as the one who makes peace (Ephesians 2:14). Shared ground: the closing formula names Nahshon, honoring his gift individually.

Numbers 7:18

18On the second day Nethanel the son of Zuar, prince of Issachar, gave his offering.

Arminian

Issachar offers on the second day, camped beside Judah on the east (Numbers 2:5). Arminian readers note that a less prominent tribe gives exactly what Judah gave. Both traditions agree that the order follows the camp arrangement, not rank of worth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the equal treatment of Issachar as a sign that standing before God’s altar does not depend on tribal prominence. Shared ground: Nethanel’s gift matches Nahshon’s, and each tribe is honored alike.

Numbers 7:19

19He offered for his offering: one silver platter, the weight of which was one hundred thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

Issachar’s silver vessels and grain offering exactly match Judah’s. Arminian readers see willing participation that does not try to outdo or underdo the others. Both traditions agree that the meal offering expresses dedication of daily provision to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the meal offering as acknowledging that daily bread comes from God. The identical weights stress equality. Shared ground: the flour and oil are gifts of ordinary life offered to the LORD.

Numbers 7:20

20one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

Issachar’s gold ladle of incense matches Judah’s. Arminian readers see equal access to the worship that incense represents. Both traditions note the connection between incense and the prayers of God’s people (Revelation 5:8).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gold ladle as a reminder that worship is costly and holy, offered with what is precious. Shared ground: incense pictures prayer, and Issachar brings exactly the same offering of devotion as Judah.

Numbers 7:21

21one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

Issachar’s burnt offering of bull, ram, and lamb matches Judah’s. Arminian readers see equal devotion from each tribe. Both traditions agree that the burnt offering expresses complete dedication to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the burnt offering was accepted to make atonement for the worshiper (Leviticus 1:4), so dedication and atonement are intertwined. Shared ground: the whole animal is given to God, picturing entire devotion.

Numbers 7:22

22one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

Issachar’s sin offering of a male goat matches Judah’s. Arminian readers see that all need atonement. Both traditions agree that the sin offering addresses the defilement of sin before worship can proceed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sin offering as God’s gracious provision, not a human achievement: God himself appoints the means by which sinful people are cleansed. Shared ground: atonement is necessary for approaching God, and Issachar’s leader brings it like Judah’s.

Numbers 7:23

23and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, five male lambs a year old. This was the offering of Nethanel the son of Zuar.

Arminian

Issachar’s seventeen animals for peace offerings match Judah’s. Arminian readers see fellowship with God offered to all tribes equally. Both traditions agree that the peace offering expresses communion and joy. The verse closes by naming Nethanel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the peace offering as a covenant meal. Shared ground: the closing formula names the giver, honoring each leader’s gift, and the peace offering celebrates fellowship with God.

Numbers 7:24

24On the third day Eliab the son of Helon, prince of the children of Zebulun,

Arminian

Zebulun, the third tribe of the eastern camp (Numbers 2:7), brings its gift through Eliab. Arminian readers note the steady participation of each tribe in turn. Both traditions agree that the recurring pattern underlines the unity of Israel’s worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Zebulun, like Issachar a son of Leah, receives a full and equal record. Shared ground: the orderly sequence reflects God’s arrangement of the camp around his dwelling.

Numbers 7:25

25gave his offering: one silver platter, the weight of which was a hundred and thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

Zebulun’s platter and bowl, filled with flour and oil, serve both as gifts to the sanctuary and as vessels for its service. Arminian readers note that the gifts remain in use for worship. Both traditions agree the silver honors God’s house.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that precious metal and simple grain are joined in one offering. Shared ground: the meal offering, described in Leviticus 2, is a gift of thanks and dedication, and the vessels serve the altar’s ongoing worship.

Numbers 7:26

26one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

Zebulun’s gold ladle of incense is small in weight but precious in material. Arminian readers note that devotion is measured by sincerity, not size. Both traditions agree the incense offering represents worship rising before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that gold, the most precious metal, is reserved for objects near God’s presence. Shared ground: the incense offering expresses reverent worship and is presented according to God’s prescription.

Numbers 7:27

27one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

Zebulun’s burnt offering repeats the three animals of descending size, from bull to ram to lamb. Arminian readers note the costliness of the gift for a pastoral people. Both traditions agree that the burnt offering signified surrender to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the offering as part of the sacrificial system that pointed forward to Christ. Shared ground: the burnt offering is wholly consumed, and its meaning is complete dedication.

Numbers 7:28

28one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

Zebulun’s leader brings the same male goat for a sin offering. Arminian readers see the humble acknowledgment that even a leader’s dedication must begin with confession. Both traditions agree that the sin offering purifies and restores fellowship with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sin offering as pointing beyond itself to Christ’s atoning work, since animal blood could not finally remove sin (Hebrews 10:4). Shared ground: no tribe approaches God’s altar without atonement, and Zebulun’s offering says so plainly.

Numbers 7:29

29and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old. This was the offering of Eliab the son of Helon.

Arminian

Zebulun’s peace offerings repeat the pattern. Arminian readers see the joy of fellowship after atonement. Both traditions agree that the peace offering is a shared meal before God. The verse closes by naming Eliab.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the order of offerings, sin and burnt before peace, as teaching that fellowship follows atonement. Shared ground: the peace offering celebrates communion, and the giver is named.

Numbers 7:30

30On the fourth day Elizur the son of Shedeur, prince of the children of Reuben,

Arminian

Reuben, Jacob’s firstborn, offers fourth as head of the southern camp (Numbers 2:10). Arminian readers note that Reuben still serves faithfully though he lost the preeminence (Genesis 49:3–4). Both traditions see God’s order, not birth order, setting the sequence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign ordering, which places Judah before Reuben, consistent with Genesis 49. Yet Reuben’s gift is identical. Shared ground: no tribe is excluded, and each brings the same complete offering.

Numbers 7:31

31gave his offering: one silver platter, the weight of which was one hundred thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

Reuben’s grain offering in silver vessels shows that the firstborn tribe gives the same as the others. Arminian readers note humility in accepting God’s order. Both traditions agree that the offering’s value is set by God’s standard, the sanctuary shekel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the identical offering as a picture of equal standing under God’s covenant. Shared ground: the meal offering represents dedication of the people’s labor and sustenance to the LORD.

Numbers 7:32

32one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

Reuben’s ladle of incense continues the pattern. Arminian readers see the incense as a picture of prayer offered by each tribe. Both traditions agree that the identical gift reflects equal participation in worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that incense in the sanctuary was accompanied by priestly mediation. Many in both traditions see Christ’s intercession foreshadowed (Hebrews 7:25), though this is a typological reading. Shared ground: prayer rises to God through his appointed means.

Numbers 7:33

33one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

Reuben’s burnt offering of bull, ram, and lamb is recorded fully. Arminian readers see willing surrender from the firstborn tribe. Both traditions agree that the burnt offering expressed devotion and dependence on God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the burnt offering as part of God’s provision for approaching him. Shared ground: the whole offering is given to the LORD, and both traditions read it in light of Christ’s sacrifice.

Numbers 7:34

34one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

Reuben’s sin offering of a male goat is recorded fully. Arminian readers see that the firstborn tribe also needs atonement. Both traditions agree that the sin offering deals with sin before dedication.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in Reuben’s sin offering a reminder that no human privilege, not even birthright, removes the need for atonement. Shared ground: every tribe, first or last, brings the same sin offering before it brings its peace offerings.

Numbers 7:35

35and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old. This was the offering of Elizur the son of Shedeur.

Arminian

Reuben’s peace offerings are recorded fully. Arminian readers see fellowship extended to the firstborn tribe. Both traditions agree that the peace offering expresses joy and communion. The verse closes by naming Elizur.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the peace offering as celebrating God’s covenant fellowship with a forgiven people, a meal eaten in his presence. Shared ground: the closing formula names Elizur personally, and the peace offering is shared joyfully.

Numbers 7:36

36On the fifth day Shelumiel the son of Zurishaddai, prince of the children of Simeon,

Arminian

Simeon, camped with Reuben on the south (Numbers 2:12), offers fifth through Shelumiel. Arminian readers note that Simeon, rebuked with Levi in Genesis 49:5–7, still participates fully. Both traditions see grace in Simeon’s inclusion at the altar.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Simeon’s earlier history does not bar it from bringing the same gift as the others. Shared ground: God receives each tribe at his altar on equal terms.

Numbers 7:37

37gave his offering: one silver platter, the weight of which was one hundred thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

Simeon’s offering repeats the silver platter and bowl with flour and oil. Arminian readers see consistency in devotion, each tribe bringing its full share. Both traditions note that the oil mixed with flour follows the meal offering instructions of Leviticus 2:1–4.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the repetition highlights faithful conformity to the pattern. Shared ground: the grain offering expresses gratitude and dedication, and both traditions see the whole system fulfilled in Christ’s offering of himself (Hebrews 10:10).

Numbers 7:38

38one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

Simeon’s gold ladle full of incense is recorded identically. Arminian readers see consistent devotion across the tribes. Both traditions agree that incense was a fragrant offering signifying worship acceptable to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the incense as part of worship ordered by God, offered in prescribed vessels and at prescribed times. Shared ground: the ladle and incense honor God, express the people’s prayers, and come from Simeon on equal terms.

Numbers 7:39

39one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

Simeon’s burnt offering matches the others. Arminian readers note that the year-old lamb recalls Passover. Many in both traditions see Christ as the Lamb foreshadowed in such offerings (John 1:29), a typological reading.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the burnt offering as pointing to Christ’s complete self-offering. Shared ground: the burnt offering expresses entire devotion to God, and it belongs to the system fulfilled in Christ.

Numbers 7:40

40one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

Simeon’s male goat for a sin offering matches the others. Arminian readers see that God provides atonement for every tribe without exception, including one with a troubled past. Both traditions agree that the sin offering is a gracious provision.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sin offering as part of the sacrificial system fulfilled in Christ, who suffered outside the gate (Hebrews 13:11–12). Shared ground: atonement is necessary and provided by God.

Numbers 7:41

41and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old: this was the offering of Shelumiel the son of Zurishaddai.

Arminian

Simeon’s seventeen animals for peace offerings match the others. Arminian readers see fellowship with God offered freely to every tribe. Both traditions agree that the peace offering celebrates communion and joy. The verse closes by naming Shelumiel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the peace offering as a covenant meal in which God’s people eat in his presence after atonement has been made. Shared ground: the giver is named, and the peace offering celebrates fellowship with God.

Numbers 7:42

42On the sixth day, Eliasaph the son of Deuel, prince of the children of Gad,

Arminian

Gad, the third tribe of the southern camp, offers through Eliasaph. Numbers 1:14 also reads Deuel, while the WEB of 2:14 reads Reuel, likely a spelling difference between similar Hebrew letters. Arminian readers note Gad’s equal place in worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Gad, a son of Leah’s servant Zilpah, receives the same honor as Leah’s sons. Shared ground: the record makes no distinction by mother or rank, and the first six days close with Gad’s identical gift.

Numbers 7:43

43gave his offering: one silver platter, the weight of which was one hundred thirty shekels, one silver bowl of seventy shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, both of them full of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering;

Arminian

Gad’s silver vessels with flour and oil complete the first six days’ meal offerings. Arminian readers note the steady generosity of the tribes. Both traditions agree that grain offerings accompanied other sacrifices as tokens of dedication.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the meal offering as part of a complete act of worship. Shared ground: the people’s gifts serve God’s altar, and the identical record shows that God values each tribe’s devotion equally.

Numbers 7:44

44one golden ladle of ten shekels, full of incense;

Arminian

Gad’s ladle of incense completes the first half of the list. Arminian readers note that each tribe joins in the same act of devotion. Both traditions agree that incense pictures prayer and worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repetition as reinforcing the orderliness of worship, in which nothing is improvised. Shared ground: incense belongs to the holy service of God, and Gad’s gift is accepted exactly as Judah’s was.

Numbers 7:45

45one young bull, one ram, one male lamb a year old, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

Gad’s burnt offering completes the first six days of burnt offerings. Arminian readers see devotion offered consistently, day after day. Both traditions agree that the three animals together form a costly and complete burnt offering.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the offering as an act of worship ordered by God’s law and repeated faithfully. Shared ground: the whole burnt offering belongs to God, symbolizes complete dedication, and anticipates Christ’s self-offering.

Numbers 7:46

46one male goat for a sin offering;

Arminian

Gad’s sin offering completes the first half of the list. Arminian readers see humility repeated in each tribe’s acknowledgment of sin, a reminder that confession is never finished business. Both traditions agree that the sin offering purifies the worshiper.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sin offering as an expression of both God’s holiness, which requires atonement, and his grace, which provides it. Shared ground: dedication requires atonement, and the sixth day’s offering repeats that lesson.

Numbers 7:47

47and for the sacrifice of peace offerings, two head of cattle, five rams, five male goats, and five male lambs a year old. This was the offering of Eliasaph the son of Deuel.

Arminian

Gad’s peace offerings complete the first half. Arminian readers see joy and fellowship. Both traditions agree that the peace offering expresses communion with God. The verse closes by naming Eliasaph.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the peace offering as pointing to the peace Christ makes through the blood of his cross (Colossians 1:20). Shared ground: Eliasaph is named personally, and fellowship with God is celebrated.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
