Passage

Numbers 7

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Numbers 7 steps back in time to the day Moses finished setting up and anointing the tabernacle (compare Exodus 40:17). The tribal leaders, the same men named in Numbers 1:5–15, bring six covered wagons and twelve oxen, which God tells Moses to give to the Levite clans according to their work; the Kohathites receive none because they carry the holy things on their shoulders. God then directs that each leader present his dedication offering for the altar on his own day.

Numbers 7:1

1On the day that Moses had finished setting up the tabernacle, and had anointed it and sanctified it with all its furniture, and the altar with all its vessels, and had anointed and sanctified them;

Arminian

The scene dates to the day the tabernacle was completed and anointed. Arminian readers see a people responding to God’s dwelling among them with willing gifts. Both traditions note that Numbers arranges material by theme, not strictly by date, since this day precedes the census of chapter 1.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the tabernacle is anointed and sanctified before gifts are brought: God’s consecrating action comes first. The flashback to Exodus 40 links the dedication with God’s glory filling the tent. Shared ground: worship begins with what God has established.

Numbers 7:2

2the princes of Israel, the heads of their fathers’ houses, gave offerings. These were the princes of the tribes. These are they who were over those who were counted;

Arminian

The givers are the tribal leaders who oversaw the census. Arminian readers see leadership expressed in generous example. Both traditions note that leaders who were counted among the people now lead them in giving.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the leaders acting as representatives of their tribes, so the gifts are corporate. Their names match Numbers 1:5–15. Shared ground: those entrusted with leadership carry a special responsibility to lead in worship and generosity.

Numbers 7:3

3and they brought their offering before the LORD, six covered wagons and twelve oxen; a wagon for every two of the princes, and for each one an ox. They presented them before the tabernacle.

Arminian

Six covered wagons and twelve oxen, one wagon per pair of leaders and one ox each, are practical gifts. Arminian readers note that the leaders’ offering anticipates the needs of a moving sanctuary. Both traditions see generosity shaped by real need.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the practical gift a reminder that ordinary means serve God’s purposes. The shared wagons show cooperation among tribes. Shared ground: gifts for transport and labor are as truly offered to the LORD as gifts for the altar.

Numbers 7:4

4The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

God speaks to Moses about what to do with the gifts. Arminian readers note that the leaders’ initiative receives God’s response and direction. Both traditions agree that offerings, however generous, are received and directed according to God’s word.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God governs the use of the gifts; the leaders give, but God assigns. Shared ground: Israel’s offerings are placed under God’s direction, so that worship stays ordered by his command rather than by the givers’ preferences.

Numbers 7:5

5“Accept these from them, that they may be used in doing the service of the Tent of Meeting; and you shall give them to the Levites, to every man according to his service.”

Arminian

Moses is to accept the gifts and give them to the Levites according to their service. Arminian readers see God honoring a freewill offering and putting it to good use. Both traditions agree that gifts are matched to the work each group must do.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s wisdom in distributing resources according to calling. The Levites’ service is defined by God, and the gifts serve it. Shared ground: God cares about the practical work of ministry and equips those who carry it out.

Numbers 7:6

6Moses took the wagons and the oxen, and gave them to the Levites.

Arminian

Moses obeys, giving the wagons and oxen to the Levites. Arminian readers note the simple obedience that follows God’s word. Both traditions agree the transfer shows the people’s gifts supporting those set apart for sanctuary service.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Moses’ faithful execution of God’s instruction. The Levites receive what the tribes provide. Shared ground: the whole people support the service of the sanctuary, a pattern later echoed in the support of ministry (1 Corinthians 9:13–14).

Numbers 7:7

7He gave two wagons and four oxen to the sons of Gershon, according to their service.

Arminian

The Gershonites receive two wagons and four oxen, according to their service of carrying the tabernacle’s curtains and coverings (Numbers 4:24–26). Arminian readers note that resources are matched to responsibility. Both traditions agree that the distribution fits the assigned tasks.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s ordering of work among the Levite clans. The Gershonites’ lighter loads receive fewer wagons. Shared ground: God’s distribution of resources is fair and fitted to each calling, not equal for its own sake.

Numbers 7:8

8He gave four wagons and eight oxen to the sons of Merari, according to their service, under the direction of Ithamar the son of Aaron the priest.

Arminian

The Merarites receive four wagons and eight oxen, since they carried the heavy frames, bars, pillars, and sockets (Numbers 4:31–32). Arminian readers note the heavier provision for heavier work. Both traditions agree that oversight by Ithamar keeps the work ordered under priestly direction.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the Merarites’ heavier tasks receive more help, and that they work under the priest Ithamar’s direction. Shared ground: service is organized, accountable, and supplied according to need.

Numbers 7:9

9But to the sons of Kohath he gave none, because the service of the sanctuary belonged to them; they carried it on their shoulders.

Arminian

The Kohathites receive no wagons because they carry the holy things on their shoulders (Numbers 4:15). Arminian readers see that some service cannot be made easier without losing reverence. Both traditions recall that later neglect of this rule had serious consequences (2 Samuel 6:3–7; 1 Chronicles 15:13–15).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s prescribed manner of handling holy things: the most sacred objects are carried by consecrated people, not by carts. The later episode with Uzzah confirms the point. Shared ground: holiness shapes even the method of service.

Numbers 7:10

10The princes gave offerings for the dedication of the altar in the day that it was anointed. The princes gave their offerings before the altar.

Arminian

The leaders bring offerings for the altar’s dedication on the day it is anointed. Arminian readers see joyful participation in the inauguration of worship. Both traditions note that the altar’s dedication is a public event involving all the tribes through their leaders.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the altar, already anointed, now receives the people’s dedication gifts. Shared ground: dedication involves both God’s consecration and the people’s offering, and the altar becomes the center of Israel’s worship.

Numbers 7:11

11The LORD said to Moses, “They shall offer their offering, each prince on his day, for the dedication of the altar.”

Arminian

God directs that each leader present his offering on his own day. Arminian readers see God giving each tribe its own moment of honor. Both traditions agree that the arrangement prevents any tribe from being overshadowed and gives the dedication an orderly, extended form.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s ordering of worship down to the schedule. Each tribe receives a day by divine command. Shared ground: God values each tribe’s participation and establishes the pattern that the following verses record in full.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
