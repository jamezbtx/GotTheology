Passage

Numbers 21

Verses 21–35 · WEB

Israel asks Sihon king of the Amorites for peaceful passage on the King’s Highway, but Sihon attacks, and Israel defeats him and takes his land from the Arnon to the Jabbok, including Heshbon, which Sihon had earlier taken from Moab, as an old taunt-song recalls. Israel then defeats Og king of Bashan at Edrei after the LORD tells Moses not to fear him. Both traditions read these victories soberly as God’s gift of the land and judgment on hostile kings, celebrated later in Israel’s worship (see Ps 136:17–22); the passage is largely shared ground.

Numbers 21:21

21Israel sent messengers to Sihon king of the Amorites, saying,

Arminian

Israel sends messengers to Sihon. Arminian readers note that Israel begins with diplomacy rather than force.

The parallel account in Deuteronomy 2:26 calls the request “words of peace.”

Reformed

Reformed readers also note the peaceful approach, which Deuteronomy 2:26 describes as “words of peace.” Israel does not seek conflict.

The outcome, however, rests with God, who has already purposed to give Sihon’s land to Israel (Deut 2:24).

Numbers 21:22

22“Let me pass through your land. We will not turn away into field or vineyard. We will not drink of the water of the wells. We will go by the king’s highway, until we have passed your border.”

Arminian

Israel promises to stay on the King’s Highway and not touch fields, vineyards, or wells. Arminian readers see a reasonable request that respects Sihon’s property.

The same offer had been made to Edom (Num 20:17).

Reformed

Reformed readers see Israel acting justly. The promise to take nothing from fields, vineyards, or wells leaves Sihon without any just reason to refuse.

The same restraint had been shown toward Edom (Num 20:17), so Israel’s conduct is consistent.

Numbers 21:23

23Sihon would not allow Israel to pass through his border, but Sihon gathered all his people together, and went out against Israel into the wilderness, and came to Jahaz. He fought against Israel.

Arminian

Sihon refuses and attacks Israel at Jahaz. Arminian readers stress Sihon’s own choice to refuse and to fight.

Deuteronomy 2:30 says the LORD hardened his spirit; Arminian readers usually read this as God confirming a hostility Sihon already chose.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Deuteronomy 2:30, where the LORD hardened Sihon’s spirit to give him into Israel’s hand. They see God’s sovereign purpose working through Sihon’s real and culpable choice.

Both traditions agree that Sihon was responsible and that God used his hostility to fulfill his promise.

Numbers 21:24

24Israel struck him with the edge of the sword, and possessed his land from the Arnon to the Jabbok, even to the children of Ammon; for the border of the children of Ammon was fortified.

Arminian

Israel defeats Sihon and takes his land from the Arnon to the Jabbok. Arminian readers note that Israel stops at the border of Ammon, respecting another boundary (cf. Deut 2:19, 37).

The victory is limited and defined.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God giving the land as he promised, as the fuller account in Deuteronomy 2:31–36 makes explicit.

The border with Ammon is respected (Deut 2:19, 37), which shows that God orders the boundaries of the nations as well as Israel’s.

Numbers 21:25

25Israel took all these cities. Israel lived in all the cities of the Amorites, in Heshbon, and in all its villages.

Arminian

Israel takes the cities and settles in Heshbon and its villages. Arminian readers note that this territory later becomes part of the inheritance east of the Jordan (Num 32).

Israel begins to dwell in land God gives.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the first fruits of the promise. Land east of the Jordan is given before the crossing, and it later becomes the inheritance of Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh (Num 32:33).

God’s faithfulness becomes visible in towns and villages Israel can live in.

Numbers 21:26

26For Heshbon was the city of Sihon the king of the Amorites, who had fought against the former king of Moab, and taken all his land out of his hand, even to the Arnon.

Arminian

Heshbon had been Sihon’s city, taken from Moab. Arminian readers note that the history matters for later disputes over the land (cf. Judg 11:12–27).

Israel took the land from Sihon, not from Moab.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see this history used later in Jephthah’s reply to the Ammonite king (Judg 11:19–26).

God’s providence governs the rise and fall of nations. Sihon took the land from Moab, and God then gave it to Israel.

Numbers 21:27

27Therefore those who speak in proverbs say, “Come to Heshbon. Let the city of Sihon be built and established;

Arminian

Those who speak in proverbs say, “Come to Heshbon.” Arminian readers note that the text quotes a song, perhaps originally an Amorite taunt against Moab.

Its origin and setting are discussed by interpreters.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an old poem quoted to confirm the history. Its call to rebuild Heshbon as Sihon’s city carries irony now that Sihon has fallen to Israel.

Whoever first composed it, the song now serves Israel’s account of what the LORD has done.

Numbers 21:28

28for a fire has gone out of Heshbon, a flame from the city of Sihon. It has devoured Ar of Moab, The lords of the high places of the Arnon.

Arminian

Fire went out from Heshbon and devoured Ar of Moab. Arminian readers note the imagery of conquest.

Jeremiah 48:45–46 echoes this poem in an oracle against Moab.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Jeremiah 48:45–46 reusing the language.

The poem recalls Sihon’s victory over Moab, which Israel’s victory now overturns.

Numbers 21:29

29Woe to you, Moab! You are undone, people of Chemosh! He has given his sons as fugitives, and his daughters into captivity, to Sihon king of the Amorites.

Arminian

Woe to Moab, people of Chemosh! Chemosh, Moab’s god (1 Kgs 11:7), could not protect his people. Arminian readers note the futility of idols.

Moab’s sons and daughters were scattered or captured by Sihon.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the contrast between Chemosh and the LORD, who gives Israel victory over Sihon.

The poem also shows that God judges nations by many instruments.

Numbers 21:30

30We have shot at them. Heshbon has perished even to Dibon. We have laid waste even to Nophah, Which reaches to Medeba.”

Arminian

Heshbon perished to Dibon; Nophah to Medeba. Arminian readers note that the Hebrew of this verse is difficult and translations differ.

The general sense is complete devastation.

Reformed

Reformed readers also acknowledge the difficulty of the Hebrew in this verse, and do not build doctrine on uncertain details.

The poem ends with a picture of devastation, which prepares for the summary that Israel now lived in the Amorite land.

Numbers 21:31

31Thus Israel lived in the land of the Amorites.

Arminian

“Thus Israel lived in the land of the Amorites.” Arminian readers note that the summary marks a turning point: the people who wandered now settle.

The territory taken here becomes a home before the Jordan is crossed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the promise beginning to be fulfilled. The Amorites, whose iniquity was not yet complete in Abraham’s day (Gen 15:16), are displaced.

The short statement underlines that the land is God’s gift.

Numbers 21:32

32Moses sent to spy out Jazer. They took its villages, and drove out the Amorites who were there.

Arminian

Moses sends spies to Jazer, and they take its villages. Arminian readers note the contrast with the spies of Numbers 13–14, whose report led to unbelief.

This time spying leads to action.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a reversal of the earlier failure of the spies in Numbers 13–14. This time the report leads to action rather than unbelief.

The new generation takes Jazer and its villages, showing renewed trust in God’s promise.

Numbers 21:33

33They turned and went up by the way of Bashan. Og the king of Bashan went out against them, he and all his people, to battle at Edrei.

Arminian

Og king of Bashan marches out against Israel at Edrei. Arminian readers note another unprovoked attack, which frames the battle as defensive.

The location of Edrei is uncertain, and no attempt is made at a firm identification.

Reformed

Reformed readers see another enemy whom God will give into Israel’s hand, as Deuteronomy 3:1–11 recounts more fully.

Og is later remembered as one of the last of a people of great stature (Deut 3:11), which magnifies God’s power in the victory.

Numbers 21:34

34The LORD said to Moses, “Don’t fear him, for I have delivered him into your hand, with all his people, and his land. You shall do to him as you did to Sihon king of the Amorites, who lived at Heshbon.”

Arminian

The LORD tells Moses not to fear Og, for he has already given him into Israel’s hand. Arminian readers note God’s encouragement before battle.

The promise is tied to what God has just done to Sihon, so past faithfulness grounds present courage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s promise preceding victory. The battle is the LORD’s.

“I have delivered him into your hand” speaks of the outcome as already settled by God, even though Israel must still go out and fight.

Numbers 21:35

35So they struck him, with his sons and all his people, until there were no survivors; and they possessed his land.

Arminian

Israel defeats Og and his people and possesses his land. Arminian readers read this soberly as judgment within the conquest.

Israel’s later worship recalls these victories (Ps 135:10–12; 136:17–22).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the psalms celebrating God’s faithfulness in defeating Sihon and Og.

Both traditions read the passage as a particular act of judgment in Israel’s history, not a general model for warfare.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
