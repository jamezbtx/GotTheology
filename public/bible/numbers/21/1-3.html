Passage

Numbers 21

Verses 1–3 · WEB

The Canaanite king of Arad attacks Israel and takes captives; Israel vows to devote the Canaanite cities to destruction if the LORD gives victory, and the LORD hears, so the place is named Hormah. The episode reverses Israel’s earlier defeat at Hormah after their failed attempt to enter the land (see Num 14:45). Both traditions read this ban on Canaanite cities soberly as a specific act of judgment within Israel’s conquest, not a pattern for today, and the passage is largely shared ground.

Numbers 21:1

1The Canaanite, the king of Arad, who lived in the South, heard that Israel came by the way of Atharim. He fought against Israel, and took some of them captive.

Arminian

The king of Arad attacks Israel as it moves toward the land and takes some captive. Arminian readers note that the first battle of this new generation begins with a setback, just as the previous generation was defeated in this region (Num 14:45; Deut 1:44).

The location of Arad and the meaning of “the way of Atharim” are not certain, so the notes avoid precise identifications.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the new generation facing the same enemies that defeated their parents. The question is whether they will now rely on the LORD.

The Canaanites strike first. The text presents Israel as responding to aggression, under God’s direction, rather than starting the conflict.

Numbers 21:2

2Israel vowed a vow to the LORD, and said, “If you will indeed deliver this people into my hand, then I will utterly destroy their cities.”

Arminian

Israel vows to devote the cities to destruction if the LORD gives them victory. Arminian readers note the contrast with Numbers 14, where Israel went up without the LORD and failed. This time they turn to him first.

The vow uses the language of the ban, devoting what is taken entirely to God (cf. Lev 27:28–29; Deut 7:1–2).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see dependence on God replacing the presumption of Numbers 14:40–45. Victory is asked for, not assumed.

The ban was a specific instrument of God’s judgment on the Canaanites in that period (Gen 15:16; Deut 20:16–18). It is not presented as a general model for warfare.

Numbers 21:3

3The LORD listened to the voice of Israel, and delivered up the Canaanites; and they utterly destroyed them and their cities. The name of the place was called Hormah.

Arminian

The LORD listens and gives the Canaanites into Israel’s hand, and the place is named Hormah, meaning “destruction” (WEB footnote). Arminian readers see God answering a people who now seek him.

The passage is sobering. Both traditions read the ban as God’s judgment within the conquest, not as permission for violence in other settings.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to his promise of the land and his judgment on Canaanite sin, worked through Israel.

The name reverses the earlier defeat at Hormah (Num 14:45). Judges 1:17 also connects a destruction with the name Hormah; how the two accounts relate is discussed by interpreters.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
