Passage

Numbers 21

Verses 10–20 · WEB

Israel journeys through a series of camps north from the wilderness toward the plains of Moab, crossing the Zered and the Arnon. The account quotes a fragment from the Book of the Wars of the LORD and a short song to a well where God gave water, ending at the top of Pisgah. Several stations cannot be located with confidence, and the passage is shared ground for both traditions as a record of God’s guidance and provision on the way.

Numbers 21:10

10The children of Israel traveled, and encamped in Oboth.

Arminian

Israel moves on and camps at Oboth. Arminian readers note that after the serpent episode the journey resumes; judgment and mercy do not stop God’s purpose.

The itinerary in Numbers 33:41–49 lists some stations differently, and interpreters discuss how the two lists relate.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God leading his people stage by stage toward the land.

The list of camps reminds readers that God’s guidance is concrete and covers ordinary steps as well as dramatic events.

Numbers 21:11

11They traveled from Oboth, and encamped at Iyeabarim, in the wilderness which is before Moab, toward the sunrise.

Arminian

They camp at Iyeabarim, east of Moab. Arminian readers note the movement along the edge of Moab’s territory rather than through it.

The exact location is uncertain.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Israel respecting boundaries that God had set for other peoples (cf. Deut 2:9).

The journey is guided, not aimless, even when the places are obscure to modern readers.

Numbers 21:12

12From there they traveled, and encamped in the valley of Zered.

Arminian

They camp in the valley of Zered. Arminian readers note that Deuteronomy marks crossing the Zered as the end of the thirty-eight years in which the earlier generation died (Deut 2:13–14).

A new stage of the journey begins.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Deuteronomy 2:13–14. God’s judgment on the unbelieving generation has run its course, and his promise to their children moves forward.

God’s purpose outlasts human failure.

Numbers 21:13

13From there they traveled, and encamped on the other side of the Arnon, which is in the wilderness that comes out of the border of the Amorites; for the Arnon is the border of Moab, between Moab and the Amorites.

Arminian

They camp beyond the Arnon, the border between Moab and the Amorites. Arminian readers note the careful attention to borders, which matters for the conflict with Sihon that follows.

Israel is now outside Moab’s territory (cf. Judg 11:18).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the setting prepared for God’s gift of the Amorite land.

The Arnon marks the point where Israel’s path turns from passing by to possessing (cf. Deut 2:24).

Numbers 21:14

14Therefore it is said in The Book of the Wars of the LORD, “Vaheb in Suphah, the valleys of the Arnon,

Arminian

The text quotes the Book of the Wars of the LORD, a source not otherwise preserved. Arminian readers note that Scripture sometimes cites other writings (cf. Josh 10:13; 2 Sam 1:18).

The fragment is difficult, and place names like Vaheb and Suphah are uncertain.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see inspired Scripture drawing on an older source. The title presents Israel’s battles as the LORD’s wars.

The quotation supports the statement about the Arnon border in verse 13.

Numbers 21:15

15the slope of the valleys that incline toward the dwelling of Ar, leans on the border of Moab.”

Arminian

The fragment continues about the slopes toward Ar and the border of Moab. Arminian readers note that its main function is geographical.

The Hebrew is obscure, and translations vary.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the quotation confirming the boundary.

Even fragmentary poetry is used to show that Israel stayed within the limits God set.

Numbers 21:16

16From there they traveled to Beer; that is the well of which the LORD said to Moses, “Gather the people together, and I will give them water.”

Arminian

At Beer, God gives water when the people gather. Arminian readers note the contrast with earlier water stories marked by complaint (Exod 17:1–7; Num 20:2–13).

This time God offers water before anyone grumbles.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s unprompted provision. He takes the initiative to gather the people and give them water.

The name Beer means “well”; the account is about God’s gift, not human ingenuity.

Numbers 21:17

17Then Israel sang this song: “Spring up, well! Sing to it,

Arminian

Israel sings a short song to the well: “Spring up, well!” Arminian readers note that joy and gratitude now replace the complaints of verse 5.

The song is brief and perhaps traditional, but its placement shows a people responding to provision with praise.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see praise answering grace.

The song shows a people learning to rejoice in God’s gifts. Christians sometimes recall Jesus’ promise of living water (John 4:14), as a reading.

Numbers 21:18

18the well, which the princes dug, which the nobles of the people dug, with the scepter, and with their poles.” From the wilderness they traveled to Mattanah;

Arminian

The princes and nobles dug the well with scepter and poles, and the journey continues to Mattanah. Arminian readers note that leaders take part in the work God blesses.

Human effort and divine gift go together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s provision coming through the labor of the people’s leaders, who dig with scepter and staff.

The means are human, but the gift is God’s, as verse 16 says. The verse then returns to the itinerary, moving the people on toward Mattanah.

Numbers 21:19

19and from Mattanah to Nahaliel; and from Nahaliel to Bamoth;

Arminian

The journey continues to Nahaliel and Bamoth. Arminian readers note steady progress toward the plains of Moab after the long years in the wilderness.

These places cannot be located with confidence, so no attempt is made to fix them on a map.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s guidance through each stage, including places whose names are all that survive.

The brief itinerary carries the story forward to the edge of Moab, where the next conflicts take place.

Numbers 21:20

20and from Bamoth to the valley that is in the field of Moab, to the top of Pisgah, which looks down on the desert.

Arminian

They reach the valley in Moab’s field and the top of Pisgah, overlooking the desert. Arminian readers note the significance of Pisgah, from which Moses later sees the land (Deut 34:1).

The journey nears its goal.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Pisgah as a place of vision toward the promise, the summit from which Moses will later view the land (Deut 34:1).

The wilderness journey is nearly over, and the land is coming into view.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
