Passage

Numbers 21

Verses 4–9 · WEB

Discouraged by the long detour around Edom, the people speak against God and Moses and despise the manna; the LORD sends venomous snakes, and many die. When the people confess their sin, Moses prays, and the LORD has him set a bronze serpent on a pole so that anyone bitten who looks at it lives, an image Jesus applies to his own lifting up and to believing in him (see John 3:14–15). Both traditions agree that God provides the remedy and that the power is his, not the bronze object’s (2 Kgs 18:4); they differ over the nature and source of the faith that receives the remedy.

Numbers 21:4

4They traveled from Mount Hor by the way to the Red Sea, to go around the land of Edom. The soul of the people was very discouraged because of the journey.

Arminian

Israel leaves Mount Hor, where Aaron died (Num 20:22–29), and takes the long way around Edom, which had refused them passage (Num 20:14–21). Arminian readers note that the people’s discouragement is understandable: the detour lengthened the journey just as they hoped to go forward.

Discouragement itself is not condemned, but it becomes the soil in which complaint grows.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a test of faith in the wilderness. The detour is part of God’s leading, even when it seems to move away from the goal.

The weariness of the journey exposes what is in the people’s hearts (cf. Deut 8:2).

Numbers 21:5

5The people spoke against God and against Moses: “Why have you brought us up out of Egypt to die in the wilderness? For there is no bread, there is no water, and our soul loathes this disgusting food!”

Arminian

The people speak against God and Moses and call the manna disgusting food. Arminian readers note that this repeats earlier complaints (Num 11:4–6) and treats God’s daily provision with contempt.

The complaint is a real choice to reject what God has given, not merely a mood.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ingratitude and unbelief. The manna, which Jesus later uses to point to himself as the bread from heaven (John 6:31–35), is despised.

Paul warns Christians against testing the Lord as Israel did here (1 Cor 10:9).

Numbers 21:6

6The LORD sent venomous snakes among the people, and they bit the people. Many people of Israel died.

Arminian

The LORD sends venomous snakes, and many die. Arminian readers see judgment that answers the people’s rebellion and is meant to bring them to repentance.

Deuteronomy 8:15 recalls the wilderness as a place of such snakes. The Hebrew term is traditionally rendered “fiery” in some English versions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s holy judgment on sin, which is just and also corrective.

Paul cites this event as a warning to the church (1 Cor 10:9–11). The judgment was real, and the deaths were the wages of rebellion (cf. Rom 6:23).

Numbers 21:7

7The people came to Moses, and said, “We have sinned, because we have spoken against the LORD and against you. Pray to the LORD, that he take away the serpents from us.” Moses prayed for the people.

Arminian

The people confess, “We have sinned,” and ask Moses to pray, and he does. Arminian readers note that repentance is a genuine response to God’s discipline.

Moses’ intercession shows grace toward the very people who spoke against him.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see true confession and the role of a mediator who prays for the people.

God hears and answers, but notably he does not simply remove the serpents. He provides a remedy that must be looked to.

Numbers 21:8

8The LORD said to Moses, “Make a venomous snake, and set it on a pole. It shall happen that everyone who is bitten, when he sees it, shall live.”

Arminian

God tells Moses to set a serpent on a pole, and everyone bitten who looks at it will live. Arminian readers stress the universal scope: “everyone who is bitten” may look and live, and Jesus applies this to “whoever believes” (John 3:14–16).

Looking is a real response that the bitten person makes. Arminian readers hold that God’s grace enables that response for all who hear, though it can be refused.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a remedy freely offered to all who are bitten, and they agree that looking is the person’s own act.

Reformed theology holds that saving faith, pictured by this look, is itself God’s gift, effectually given to those he draws (John 6:44; Eph 2:8–9; Phil 1:29). The offer is sincere to all, and those who look do so because God has opened their eyes.

Key contrast Both affirm that God alone provides the remedy, that the remedy is offered to everyone bitten, that looking (believing) receives rather than earns life, and that the power lies in God, not in the bronze object (2 Kgs 18:4). Arminian readers hold that God’s grace enables every hearer to look, so faith is a freely given response that may be refused. Reformed readers hold that faith is God’s gift effectually given to the elect, so those who look do so because God has made them willing.

Numbers 21:9

9Moses made a serpent of bronze, and set it on the pole. If a serpent had bitten any man, when he looked at the serpent of bronze, he lived.

Arminian

Moses makes a bronze serpent, and anyone bitten who looks at it lives. Arminian readers note the simplicity of the remedy: no work or payment, just looking to what God provided.

In John 3:14–15 Jesus says the Son of Man must be lifted up in the same way, so that whoever believes may have eternal life.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see salvation by looking away from oneself to God’s provision (cf. Isa 45:22). Christ, lifted up on the cross, bears the curse (John 3:14–15; Gal 3:13).

Later Israel turned the bronze serpent into an object of worship, and Hezekiah destroyed it (2 Kgs 18:4). The healing power was God’s, not the object’s.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
