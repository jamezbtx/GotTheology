Passage

Numbers 30

Verses 1–16 · WEB

Chapter 30 turns from the fixed offerings Israel owed to the promises an Israelite might freely make. 29:39 has just mentioned “your vows.” A vow or binding oath made to the LORD must be kept (30:2; compare Deut 23:21–23; Eccl 5:4–5). Most of the chapter deals with vows made by a woman who lived under the authority of a father or husband. In the ancient Israelite household, the father, and after marriage the husband, was responsible for the household and its property, so a woman’s vow could affect him. The law lets him confirm her vow by saying nothing, or cancel it on the day he hears of it. If he cancels it, “The LORD will forgive her.” A widow or divorced woman, who answers for herself, is bound by her own vow (30:9). The law describes that ancient household order; it does not discuss other arrangements. Versification: English 30:1–16 is Hebrew 30:2–17; this page keeps the WEB numbers.

Numbers 30:1

1Moses spoke to the heads of the tribes of the children of Israel, saying, “This is the thing which the LORD has commanded.

Arminian

Moses addresses “the heads of the tribes of the children of Israel.” The law that follows concerns responsibility within the household. In Hebrew Bibles this is 30:2.

Arminian readers note that the law is presented as God’s command, not as Moses’ own ruling.

Reformed

Reformed readers see “This is the thing which the LORD has commanded” as grounding the household laws in God’s authority.

Shared ground.

Numbers 30:2

2When a man vows a vow to the LORD, or swears an oath to bind his soul with a bond, he shall not break his word. He shall do according to all that proceeds out of his mouth.

Arminian

The basic rule is that a man who vows to the LORD or binds himself by oath must keep his word: “He shall do according to all that proceeds out of his mouth.” Deuteronomy adds that vowing is voluntary: “if you refrain from making a vow, it shall be no sin in you” (Deut 23:22).

Arminian readers stress that people truly bind themselves by their words and are fully accountable for keeping them.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that a vow is made “to the LORD,” so breaking it wrongs God himself (compare Eccl 5:4–5).

Shared ground: both traditions see here the seriousness of speech before God. Jesus later presses the same truthfulness: “let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes’ and your ‘No’ be ‘No’ ” (Matt 5:37).

Numbers 30:3

3“Also, when a woman vows a vow to the LORD and binds herself by a pledge, being in her father’s house, in her youth,

Arminian

The law turns to a young woman living “in her father’s house.” In that household the father was responsible for the family and its property, so her vow could affect him as well.

Arminian readers note that the woman’s vow is real and made to the LORD. The law takes her devotion seriously; women could also make the Nazirite vow (6:2).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law places her vow within the order of the household as it then existed.

Shared ground: the law protects both her vow and the household responsible for it.

Numbers 30:4

4and her father hears her vow and her pledge with which she has bound her soul, and her father says nothing to her, then all her vows shall stand, and every pledge with which she has bound her soul shall stand.

Arminian

If her father hears and says nothing, her vows stand. His silence counts as consent.

Arminian readers see that the woman’s vow is presumed to stand unless it is actively forbidden.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that the law gives the father clear responsibility, and that his silence has consequences.

Shared ground.

Numbers 30:5

5But if her father forbids her in the day that he hears, none of her vows or of her pledges with which she has bound her soul, shall stand. The LORD will forgive her, because her father has forbidden her.

Arminian

If the father forbids her on the day he hears, her vow does not stand, and “The LORD will forgive her, because her father has forbidden her.”

Arminian readers see God’s kindness: a woman who meant to keep her vow is not held guilty when someone else annuls it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that God himself recognizes the authority he gave in the household, and he releases her from the obligation.

Shared ground: both traditions see mercy here. The text puts the responsibility on the one who forbids, not on her.

Numbers 30:6

6“If she has a husband, while her vows are on her, or the rash utterance of her lips with which she has bound her soul,

Arminian

The case now concerns a woman who marries while a vow, or “the rash utterance of her lips,” is still on her. The law recognizes that some vows may be made hastily (compare Lev 5:4).

Arminian readers note that the law provides a remedy for rash words without making light of vows.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the realism of the law: it expects that people will sometimes speak rashly, and it provides a way to deal with such words rather than ignoring them.

Shared ground.

Numbers 30:7

7and her husband hears it, and says nothing to her in the day that he hears it; then her vows shall stand, and her pledges with which she has bound her soul shall stand.

Arminian

As with the father, the husband’s silence on the day he hears confirms her vows.

Arminian readers see the woman’s promises standing unless they are actually overruled.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the husband taking on responsibility for the household when he marries. His silence on the day he hears has the same confirming force as the father’s.

Shared ground.

Numbers 30:8

8But if her husband forbids her in the day that he hears it, then he makes void her vow which is on her and the rash utterance of her lips, with which she has bound her soul. The LORD will forgive her.

Arminian

If the husband forbids it on the day he hears, he “makes void” her vow, and “The LORD will forgive her.”

Arminian readers see that God does not hold her guilty for an obligation she was not free to keep.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same release grounded in the household order God recognized. If the husband forbids the vow on the day he hears it, the LORD forgives her.

Shared ground.

Numbers 30:9

9“But the vow of a widow, or of her who is divorced, everything with which she has bound her soul shall stand against her.

Arminian

A widow or divorced woman is bound by her own vow: it “shall stand against her.” She has no father or husband over her to annul it.

Arminian readers note that she is fully responsible for her own word. The rules for annulling are about household responsibility, not about any lesser standing before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the same person can be bound or released depending on her household position. The law follows that ancient household order.

Shared ground.

Numbers 30:10

10“If she vowed in her husband’s house or bound her soul by a bond with an oath,

Arminian

The law now considers a vow made by a wife while already living in her husband’s house.

Arminian readers see the law treating each situation separately and fairly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the careful sorting of cases typical of Israel’s laws. A vow made already in the husband’s house is treated as its own situation.

Shared ground.

Numbers 30:11

11and her husband heard it, and held his peace at her and didn’t disallow her, then all her vows shall stand, and every pledge with which she bound her soul shall stand.

Arminian

If her husband heard and “held his peace” without disallowing her, her vows stand.

Arminian readers again see the vow presumed valid unless actively annulled.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that a husband’s silence is treated as a decision, with real weight. If he heard and did not disallow the vow, it stands as confirmed.

Shared ground.

Numbers 30:12

12But if her husband made them null and void in the day that he heard them, then whatever proceeded out of her lips concerning her vows, or concerning the bond of her soul, shall not stand. Her husband has made them void. The LORD will forgive her.

Arminian

If he annulled them on the day he heard, they do not stand. “Her husband has made them void. The LORD will forgive her.”

Arminian readers see once more that God releases her from guilt she did not incur.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the repetition of “The LORD will forgive her” as the law’s steady assurance that annulment does not make her guilty.

Shared ground.

Numbers 30:13

13Every vow, and every binding oath to afflict the soul, her husband may establish it, or her husband may make it void.

Arminian

The husband may confirm or annul “every binding oath to afflict the soul,” language used for self-denial such as fasting (29:7; Ps 35:13). Such vows could affect the whole household.

Arminian readers note that even sincere self-denial was to be practiced with regard for one’s household responsibilities.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the husband’s authority in this setting reaching even to religious vows, under God’s law.

Shared ground.

Numbers 30:14

14But if her husband says nothing to her from day to day, then he establishes all her vows or all her pledges which are on her. He has established them, because he said nothing to her in the day that he heard them.

Arminian

If the husband says nothing “from day to day,” he confirms her vows. He cannot keep a vow hanging indefinitely.

Arminian readers see the time limit protecting the woman from uncertainty about whether she is bound.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that the law requires a timely decision and holds the husband to it. Silence day after day is itself a confirmation; he cannot reopen the matter later at will.

Shared ground.

Numbers 30:15

15But if he makes them null and void after he has heard them, then he shall bear her iniquity.”

Arminian

If he annuls them later, after he has heard them, “he shall bear her iniquity.” The guilt for the broken vow passes to him.

Arminian readers see personal accountability: the one who causes a vow to be broken answers for it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that authority in the household brings responsibility before God, not just privilege.

Shared ground: both traditions see that the law guards against arbitrary use of a husband’s authority.

Numbers 30:16

16These are the statutes which the LORD commanded Moses, between a man and his wife, between a father and his daughter, being in her youth, in her father’s house.

Arminian

The summary names these statutes as governing the relationship “between a man and his wife, between a father and his daughter.” In Hebrew Bibles this is 30:17.

Arminian readers see the law as regulating household relationships before God in Israel’s ancient setting.

Reformed

Reformed readers see these statutes as God’s ordering of household responsibility under the old covenant.

Shared ground: both traditions read this as civil and household law for ancient Israel, with the lasting lesson of keeping one’s word to God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
