Passage

Numbers 22

Verses 22–35 · WEB

As Balaam rides toward Moab, God’s anger burns and the angel of the LORD stands in the road with a drawn sword; the donkey sees the angel three times and turns aside, and Balaam beats her until the LORD opens her mouth to speak. Then the LORD opens Balaam’s eyes, the angel rebukes him, and Balaam is allowed to go on, but may speak only the word given to him. Both traditions read the episode as God’s sovereign rebuke of a seer blinded by reward, and some readers in both identify the angel of the LORD with the preincarnate Christ, though this is a reading rather than a consensus.

Numbers 22:22

22God’s anger burned because he went; and the LORD’s angel placed himself in the way as an adversary against him. Now he was riding on his donkey, and his two servants were with him.

Arminian

God’s anger burns because Balaam went, and the angel of the LORD stands in the way as an adversary. Arminian readers often explain the tension with verse 20 by Balaam’s heart: God permitted the journey, but Balaam went intending to profit or even curse.

The New Testament’s judgment on Balaam’s greed supports that reading (2 Pet 2:15; Jude 11). Other explanations have also been proposed.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s anger at Balaam’s motives rather than at the journey as such. God’s permission did not mean approval of his heart.

Some readers in both traditions identify the angel of the LORD with the preincarnate Christ, based on passages where the angel speaks as God (Gen 16:7–13; Exod 3:2–6). This is a reading, not a consensus.

Numbers 22:23

23The donkey saw the LORD’s angel standing in the way, with his sword drawn in his hand; and the donkey turned out of the path, and went into the field. Balaam struck the donkey, to turn her into the path.

Arminian

The donkey sees the angel with a drawn sword and turns into a field, and Balaam beats her. Arminian readers note the irony: the animal sees what the famous seer cannot.

Balaam’s spiritual blindness is exposed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God humbling the proud seer through a donkey (cf. 1 Cor 1:27).

The drawn sword shows the seriousness of Balaam’s danger.

Numbers 22:24

24Then the LORD’s angel stood in a narrow path between the vineyards, a wall being on this side, and a wall on that side.

Arminian

The angel stands in a narrow path between vineyards, with a wall on each side. Arminian readers note the narrowing space.

God gradually limits Balaam’s room to maneuver, giving him repeated chances to recognize that something is wrong.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God hemming Balaam in step by step.

The narrowing path mirrors Balaam’s narrowing escape. God’s warnings are patient but increasingly direct.

Numbers 22:25

25The donkey saw the LORD’s angel, and she thrust herself to the wall, and crushed Balaam’s foot against the wall. He struck her again.

Arminian

The donkey presses against the wall and crushes Balaam’s foot, and he strikes her again. Arminian readers note that Balaam’s pain does not lead to reflection.

He responds with anger rather than inquiry.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see blindness persisting despite warning. Pain does not open Balaam’s eyes.

God’s warnings come in increasingly forceful ways, yet only God’s direct act in verse 31 will make Balaam see.

Numbers 22:26

26The LORD’s angel went further, and stood in a narrow place, where there was no way to turn either to the right hand or to the left.

Arminian

The angel moves ahead to a place with no room to turn right or left. Arminian readers note that every way of escape is now closed.

The three stages build toward a climax in which Balaam must face what he has refused to see.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God closing every way of escape.

The progression from open field to narrow path to no way through shows God’s control over the encounter and his determination to confront Balaam.

Numbers 22:27

27The donkey saw the LORD’s angel, and she lay down under Balaam. Balaam’s anger burned, and he struck the donkey with his staff.

Arminian

The donkey lies down, and Balaam angrily strikes her with his staff. Arminian readers note that the seer who claims spiritual insight loses his temper.

His anger mirrors God’s anger in verse 22.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a contrast between God’s righteous anger (v. 22) and Balaam’s sinful rage.

Balaam beats the very animal that is saving his life. His anger reveals a heart out of tune with God’s purposes.

Numbers 22:28

28The LORD opened the mouth of the donkey, and she said to Balaam, “What have I done to you, that you have struck me these three times?”

Arminian

The LORD opens the donkey’s mouth, and she speaks. Arminian readers note that God can speak through any means.

Peter refers to the donkey rebuking Balaam’s madness (2 Pet 2:16).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereignty over all creation, which extends even to a donkey’s speech.

The miracle humbles a professional diviner. God chooses what is weak and foolish to shame the wise (1 Cor 1:27).

Numbers 22:29

29Balaam said to the donkey, “Because you have mocked me, I wish there were a sword in my hand, for now I would have killed you.”

Arminian

Balaam says he would kill the donkey if he had a sword. Arminian readers note the irony: the angel has a sword, and it is Balaam who is in danger.

Balaam’s anger blinds him to the absurdity of arguing with a donkey.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the irony: the man who wishes for a sword does not see the sword drawn against him.

Balaam threatens the donkey with death while he himself stands under the threat of death.

Numbers 22:30

30The donkey said to Balaam, “Am I not your donkey, on which you have ridden all your life long until today? Was I ever in the habit of doing so to you?” He said, “No.”

Arminian

The donkey reminds Balaam of her faithful service, and he admits she has never done this before. Arminian readers note that the donkey reasons more clearly than her rider.

Balaam is forced to admit something unusual is happening.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Balaam’s pride humbled as he admits that the donkey has never done this before.

His admission prepares for the revelation that follows, when God opens his eyes.

Numbers 22:31

31Then the LORD opened the eyes of Balaam, and he saw the LORD’s angel standing in the way, with his sword drawn in his hand; and he bowed his head, and fell on his face.

Arminian

The LORD opens Balaam’s eyes, and he sees the angel and falls on his face. Arminian readers note that God grants Balaam sight he lacked (cf. 2 Kgs 6:17).

Revelation leads to humility.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that Balaam sees only when the LORD opens his eyes. Spiritual sight is God’s gift.

Both traditions agree that the seer could not see until God acted.

Numbers 22:32

32The LORD’s angel said to him, “Why have you struck your donkey these three times? Behold, I have come out as an adversary, because your way is perverse before me.

Arminian

The angel rebukes Balaam for beating the donkey and says his way is perverse. Arminian readers note that God’s opposition is a response to Balaam’s wrong course.

The angel acts as an adversary to stop him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s judgment on Balaam’s heart. The angel says Balaam’s way is perverse before him.

The rebuke exposes what was hidden behind Balaam’s outwardly correct words in verse 18.

Numbers 22:33

33The donkey saw me, and turned away before me these three times. Unless she had turned away from me, surely now I would have killed you, and saved her alive.”

Arminian

The angel says the donkey saved Balaam’s life by turning aside. Arminian readers note God’s mercy in sparing Balaam through the donkey.

The donkey would have been spared, but Balaam killed.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see mercy in the warning. The donkey’s turning aside spared Balaam’s life.

God’s patience preserves Balaam for the purpose of blessing Israel, which God intends to bring about through him.

Numbers 22:34

34Balaam said to the LORD’s angel, “I have sinned; for I didn’t know that you stood in the way against me. Now therefore, if it displeases you, I will go back again.”

Arminian

Balaam says, “I have sinned,” and offers to go back. Arminian readers note the confession but also its limits: he says he did not know, and offers to return only if it displeases the angel.

Whether his confession reflects real repentance is unclear.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a confession that may fall short of true repentance.

Later Scripture remembers Balaam as one who did not change (Num 31:16; Rev 2:14).

Numbers 22:35

35The LORD’s angel said to Balaam, “Go with the men; but you shall only speak the word that I shall speak to you.” So Balaam went with the princes of Balak.

Arminian

The angel says to go, but speak only the word given. Arminian readers note that God restates the condition from verse 20.

Balaam continues, but under clear restraint.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s purpose unchanged: Balaam will speak only what God gives.

God uses even a compromised prophet to accomplish his will, as he later turns Balaam’s words into blessing (Deut 23:5).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
