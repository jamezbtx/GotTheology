Passage

Numbers 22

Verses 15–21 · WEB

Balak sends a larger and more honorable delegation with promises of great honor, and Balaam answers that he cannot go beyond the LORD’s word even for a house full of silver and gold, yet asks them to stay the night to see what more the LORD will say. God permits him to go with them on condition that he do only what God tells him, and Balaam sets out. Both traditions see God’s sovereign control over a hired seer, and they read Balaam’s outward correctness alongside the inward motives that later Scripture exposes (see 2 Pet 2:15).

Numbers 22:15

15Balak again sent princes, more, and more honorable than they.

Arminian

Balak sends more princes, more honorable than the first. Arminian readers note that Balak treats the first refusal as a bargaining position.

Temptation often returns with a larger offer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the pressure of status and reward increasing.

Balak assumes everyone has a price, which is part of what the story will test.

Numbers 22:16

16They came to Balaam, and said to him, “Balak the son of Zippor says, ‘Please let nothing hinder you from coming to me,

Arminian

The princes urge Balaam to let nothing hinder him. Arminian readers note the irony: God himself has already hindered him.

Balak’s plea sets human persuasion against God’s word.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the irony. The only real hindrance is the word of the LORD, which Balak does not take into account.

The story will show that nothing can override it.

Numbers 22:17

17for I will promote you to very great honor, and whatever you say to me I will do. Please come therefore, and curse this people for me.’ ”

Arminian

Balak promises great honor and to do whatever Balaam says. Arminian readers note the appeal to ambition and pride.

Balaam is offered status as well as money.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the lure of honor that later Scripture links with Balaam’s downfall (2 Pet 2:15; Jude 11).

The request is unchanged: curse this people.

Numbers 22:18

18Balaam answered the servants of Balak, “If Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold, I can’t go beyond the word of the LORD my God, to do less or more.

Arminian

Balaam says he could not go beyond the LORD’s word for a house full of silver and gold. Arminian readers note that his words sound faithful.

The New Testament, however, says Balaam loved the wages of wrongdoing (2 Pet 2:15), so his words and heart may not have matched.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a correct confession that does not reflect a faithful heart. Balaam knows he is bound by God’s word, but he still wants the reward.

His words are true even if his motives are not, a pattern also seen in Caiaphas’s unintended prophecy (John 11:49–52).

Numbers 22:19

19Now therefore please stay here tonight as well, that I may know what else the LORD will speak to me.”

Arminian

Balaam asks them to stay the night so he can learn what else the LORD will say. Arminian readers note that God has already answered (v. 12). Asking again suggests Balaam hopes for a different answer.

Persisting after a clear “no” can be a form of resistance.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Balaam reopening a question God had already settled (v. 12).

The request reveals the pull of the reward on his heart, even while his words in verse 18 sound faithful.

Numbers 22:20

20God came to Balaam at night, and said to him, “If the men have come to call you, rise up, go with them; but only the word which I speak to you, that you shall do.”

Arminian

God comes at night and tells Balaam that if the men have come to call him, he may go, but must do only what God says. Arminian readers note that God permits what Balaam wants while setting firm limits.

God sometimes allows a person to pursue a chosen course while still restraining its outcome.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign permission. Balaam may go, but he will only speak what God gives him.

God uses even Balaam’s desire to bring about blessing on Israel (cf. Deut 23:5).

Numbers 22:21

21Balaam rose up in the morning, and saddled his donkey, and went with the princes of Moab.

Arminian

Balaam saddles his donkey and goes with the princes of Moab. Arminian readers note that he goes eagerly.

The next verse reveals God’s anger, which suggests something wrong in Balaam’s heart or purpose.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Balaam’s readiness to go. He rises early, as one eager to set out.

The tension between God’s permission in verse 20 and God’s anger in verse 22 has been explained in several ways by interpreters; most connect it with Balaam’s motives.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
