Passage

Numbers 22

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Israel camps on the plains of Moab, and Balak king of Moab, terrified by Israel’s numbers and its defeat of the Amorites, sends elders of Moab and Midian with payment to hire Balaam son of Beor to curse Israel. God tells Balaam not to go or curse the people, because they are blessed, and Balaam sends the messengers home. Both traditions see God protecting his people from a hostile pagan seer, and they share the view that God’s blessing on Israel cannot be overturned by human curses (see Gen 12:3).

Numbers 22:1

1The children of Israel traveled, and encamped in the plains of Moab beyond the Jordan at Jericho.

Arminian

Israel camps on the plains of Moab across the Jordan from Jericho. Arminian readers note that this is where the rest of Numbers and Deuteronomy will take place.

Israel is on the threshold of the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Israel at the edge of the promise, a setting that frames the Balaam story.

The people are unaware of the threat developing against them.

Numbers 22:2

2Balak the son of Zippor saw all that Israel had done to the Amorites.

Arminian

Balak son of Zippor sees what Israel did to the Amorites. Arminian readers note that fear drives his decision.

News of God’s works spreads to the nations.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the nations reacting to God’s acts on Israel’s behalf (cf. Exod 15:14–16).

Balak’s fear is a response to the LORD’s victories.

Numbers 22:3

3Moab was very afraid of the people, because they were many. Moab was distressed because of the children of Israel.

Arminian

Moab is terrified because Israel is numerous. Arminian readers note the irony: Moab fears Israel, though Israel had not attacked Moab (Deut 2:9).

Fear leads to hostile action.

Reformed

Reformed readers recall the fear of Pharaoh at Israel’s numbers (Exod 1:9–12). The promise of a great people is visible.

Moab’s dread shows God fulfilling his promise to Abraham.

Numbers 22:4

4Moab said to the elders of Midian, “Now this multitude will lick up all that is around us, as the ox licks up the grass of the field.” Balak the son of Zippor was king of Moab at that time.

Arminian

Moab speaks to the elders of Midian about Israel licking up everything like an ox. Arminian readers note the alliance of fear.

Midian will play a role later (Num 25; 31).

Reformed

Reformed readers see enemies uniting against God’s people. Moab and Midian join forces out of fear.

The image of an ox licking up grass expresses dread of being consumed. Midian’s alliance here prepares for the later events at Peor (Num 25; 31:16).

Numbers 22:5

5He sent messengers to Balaam the son of Beor, to Pethor, which is by the River, to the land of the children of his people, to call him, saying, “Behold, there is a people who came out of Egypt. Behold, they cover the surface of the earth, and they are staying opposite me.

Arminian

Balak sends for Balaam son of Beor at Pethor by the River. Arminian readers note that Balaam is a foreign seer with a reputation, from far away (cf. Deut 23:4).

The River is usually understood as the Euphrates, though Pethor’s exact location is uncertain.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a pagan diviner summoned against God’s people.

The New Testament later remembers Balaam negatively (2 Pet 2:15; Jude 11; Rev 2:14).

Numbers 22:6

6Please come now therefore, and curse this people for me; for they are too mighty for me. Perhaps I shall prevail, that we may strike them, and that I may drive them out of the land; for I know that he whom you bless is blessed, and he whom you curse is cursed.”

Arminian

Balak asks Balaam to curse Israel, believing whoever he blesses is blessed and whoever he curses is cursed. Arminian readers note the irony: Balak thinks blessing and cursing lie in Balaam’s power.

God promised Abraham that he would bless those who bless him and curse those who curse him (Gen 12:3).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Genesis 12:3. God, not Balaam, controls blessing and cursing.

Balak’s assumption sets up the story’s central lesson.

Numbers 22:7

7The elders of Moab and the elders of Midian departed with the rewards of divination in their hand. They came to Balaam, and spoke to him the words of Balak.

Arminian

The elders bring the fee for divination. Arminian readers note that Balaam’s services were for hire.

The payment foreshadows the greed that later Scripture attributes to him (2 Pet 2:15).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the motif of payment that runs through the story.

Balaam’s love of reward is remembered in 2 Peter 2:15 and Jude 11.

Numbers 22:8

8He said to them, “Lodge here this night, and I will bring you word again, as the LORD shall speak to me.” The princes of Moab stayed with Balaam.

Arminian

Balaam asks them to stay while he seeks the LORD’s word. Arminian readers note that Balaam uses the name of the LORD.

How Balaam knew Israel’s God is not explained.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God speaking even through a pagan seer for his own purposes.

Balaam’s use of the divine name does not make him a faithful servant.

Numbers 22:9

9God came to Balaam, and said, “Who are these men with you?”

Arminian

God comes to Balaam and asks who these men are. Arminian readers note that God’s question invites Balaam to reflect, not because God lacks knowledge.

Compare God’s questions in Genesis 3:9 and 4:9.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a question meant to expose rather than inform, like God’s questions in Eden (Gen 3:9).

God takes the initiative in the encounter. Balaam does not summon him; God comes to Balaam.

Numbers 22:10

10Balaam said to God, “Balak the son of Zippor, king of Moab, has said to me,

Arminian

Balaam tells God that Balak son of Zippor, king of Moab, has sent to him. Arminian readers note that he answers plainly and names the king who sent for him.

Balaam does not yet hide anything. The question is what he will do with God’s answer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Balaam reporting accurately before God. Nothing is hidden from God, and Balaam’s account adds nothing new to God’s knowledge.

The narrative builds toward God’s answer, which settles the matter.

Numbers 22:11

11‘Behold, the people that has come out of Egypt covers the surface of the earth. Now, come curse them for me. Perhaps I shall be able to fight against them, and shall drive them out.’ ”

Arminian

Balaam repeats Balak’s request: Israel covers the land, so come and curse them. Arminian readers note that Balaam passes on the request in full.

Balak hopes that a curse will let him fight and drive Israel out, which shows that he trusts in curse more than in arms.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the enemy’s plan laid bare before God.

Balak’s goal is not only defense but driving Israel out of the region. The request will be refused because God has already blessed Israel (v. 12; Gen 12:3).

Numbers 22:12

12God said to Balaam, “You shall not go with them. You shall not curse the people, for they are blessed.”

Arminian

God says not to go or curse the people, for they are blessed. Arminian readers note the clarity of the divine word.

Israel’s blessing rests on God’s promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the decisive statement: God’s people are blessed, and no curse can reverse that.

This anticipates Balaam’s oracles in chapters 23–24.

Numbers 22:13

13Balaam rose up in the morning, and said to the princes of Balak, “Go to your land; for the LORD refuses to permit me to go with you.”

Arminian

Balaam tells the princes that the LORD refuses to let him go. Arminian readers note that he obeys outwardly.

He says only that he may not go, not that Israel is blessed.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Balaam reports the refusal but leaves out God’s reason, that the people are blessed.

Some interpreters see in this omission a heart still open to Balak’s offer, a reading later Scripture supports (2 Pet 2:15).

Numbers 22:14

14The princes of Moab rose up, and they went to Balak, and said, “Balaam refuses to come with us.”

Arminian

The princes report that Balaam refuses to come. Arminian readers note that the message shifts: Balaam refuses rather than the LORD refuses.

Balak takes it as negotiation.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the message altered on its way back: the LORD’s refusal becomes Balaam’s refusal.

Balak hears a negotiating position rather than a divine “no,” and responds by raising the offer.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
