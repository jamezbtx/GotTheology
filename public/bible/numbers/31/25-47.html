Passage

Numbers 31

Verses 25–47 · WEB

God directs Moses and Eleazar to count the plunder and divide it in half between the soldiers and the rest of the congregation. From the soldiers’ half, one in five hundred goes to Eleazar as the LORD’s portion; from the congregation’s half, one in fifty goes to the Levites. The text then gives the totals of sheep, cattle, donkeys, and captive girls, and the shares and tribute that came from each, recording that Moses did as the LORD commanded.

Numbers 31:25

25The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses about the spoil. Arminian readers note that God’s word governs even the division of plunder. Both traditions agree that this prevents the spoil from becoming a source of greed or conflict.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereignty extending to the economics of war. Shared ground: the division of spoil is regulated by God’s command, not left to the strongest or the first to seize it.

Numbers 31:26

26“Count the plunder that was taken, both of man and of animal, you, and Eleazar the priest, and the heads of the fathers’ households of the congregation;

Arminian

Moses, Eleazar, and the family heads must count the plunder, both people and animals. Arminian readers note that leaders share accountability for the count. Both traditions agree that the joint counting promotes fairness and transparency.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the involvement of civil, priestly, and family leaders as a check on abuse. Shared ground: accountability in handling shared resources is a biblical principle, echoed later in Paul’s care with collected gifts (2 Corinthians 8:20–21).

Numbers 31:27

27and divide the plunder into two parts: between the men skilled in war, who went out to battle, and all the congregation.

Arminian

The plunder is divided in half between the soldiers and the congregation. Arminian readers see a principle of sharing: those who stayed also benefit. Both traditions note that David later established a similar rule for those who guarded the baggage (1 Samuel 30:24–25).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the whole community as participants in God’s victory, since the soldiers fought as Israel’s representatives. Shared ground: the equal division reflects corporate solidarity and prevents the fighters from claiming everything for themselves.

Numbers 31:28

28Levy a tribute to the LORD of the men of war who went out to battle: one soul of five hundred; of the persons, of the cattle, of the donkeys, and of the flocks.

Arminian

A tribute for the LORD is taken from the soldiers’ half: one in five hundred of persons and animals. Arminian readers see gratitude expressed through giving. Both traditions agree that the tribute acknowledges God as the source of victory.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the tribute as recognizing that the spoil ultimately belongs to God. Shared ground: the small proportion still marks every category as owed to the LORD, and the soldiers give first from what they received.

Numbers 31:29

29Take it from their half, and give it to Eleazar the priest, for the LORD’s wave offering.

Arminian

The soldiers’ tribute goes to Eleazar the priest as the LORD’s wave offering. Arminian readers note that the priest receives it on God’s behalf. Both traditions agree that the offering supports the priesthood and honors God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the wave offering marks the tribute as dedicated to God before it serves the priest. Shared ground: what is given to God sustains those who serve at his sanctuary, a pattern seen throughout the law.

Numbers 31:30

30Of the children of Israel’s half, you shall take one drawn out of every fifty, of the persons, of the cattle, of the donkeys, and of the flocks, of all the livestock, and give them to the Levites, who perform the duty of the LORD’s tabernacle.”

Arminian

From the congregation’s half, one in fifty goes to the Levites who serve the tabernacle. Arminian readers note the higher rate, perhaps reflecting the Levites’ larger number compared with the priests, though the text does not explain it. Both traditions agree that the whole people support sanctuary service.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s provision for the Levites, who had no territorial inheritance (18:20–24). The text does not explain the ratio. Shared ground: the congregation, though it did not fight, gives from its share to support those who serve God.

Numbers 31:31

31Moses and Eleazar the priest did as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

Moses and Eleazar do as the LORD commanded. Arminian readers note the faithful execution of the instructions. Both traditions agree that the formula marks the division as obedient and complete. Leaders model the obedience they require of others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the familiar formula “as the LORD commanded” as framing the division within God’s will. Shared ground: the leaders carry out the instructions precisely, and the following verses record the results.

Numbers 31:32

32Now the plunder, over and above the booty which the men of war took, was six hundred seventy-five thousand sheep,

Arminian

The plunder includes 675,000 sheep. Arminian readers note the enormous scale, which signals Midian’s wealth. Both traditions acknowledge that some interpreters discuss whether such large numbers should be read literally, and they avoid settling it beyond what the text supports.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the phrase “over and above the booty” distinguishes this counted plunder from items soldiers kept individually (31:53). Shared ground: the sheep head the list because they are the most numerous, and the count prepares for the division.

Numbers 31:33

33seventy-two thousand head of cattle,

Arminian

There are 72,000 cattle. Arminian readers note that this abundance of livestock helps explain the next chapter, where Reuben and Gad seek grazing land (32:1). Both traditions agree that the plunder greatly increased Israel’s herds.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the cattle as part of God’s provision for Israel before entering Canaan. Shared ground: the livestock totals tie chapter 31 to chapter 32, where the wealth of the herds shapes the request of Reuben and Gad.

Numbers 31:34

34sixty-one thousand donkeys,

Arminian

There are 61,000 donkeys. Arminian readers note their value as pack animals for a people about to cross into Canaan. Both traditions agree that the list moves from flocks to herds to beasts of burden in a deliberate order.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the orderly categories of the count. Shared ground: the donkeys would serve Israel’s practical needs for transport, and their careful numbering reflects the chapter’s concern with fair and accountable division.

Numbers 31:35

35and thirty-two thousand persons in all, of the women who had not known man by lying with him.

Arminian

The captives total 32,000 girls who had not slept with a man. Arminian readers find the listing of human beings alongside animals painful and say so. Both traditions agree that these are the girls spared under 31:18, now counted for distribution.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the count connects to Moses’ command in 31:18. Shared ground: the text records their number without describing their lives afterward, and both traditions read the verse soberly, without speculating beyond what is written.

Numbers 31:36

36The half, which was the portion of those who went out to war, was in number three hundred thirty-seven thousand five hundred sheep;

Arminian

The soldiers’ half includes 337,500 sheep. Arminian readers note that the 12,000 fighters receive the same total as the whole congregation, a large share for a small group. Both traditions agree that the arithmetic exactly halves the total in 31:32.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the precision of the division, consistent with God’s command. Shared ground: the soldiers are rewarded generously for the risk they bore, while the larger community receives an equal half.

Numbers 31:37

37and the LORD’s tribute of the sheep was six hundred seventy-five.

Arminian

The LORD’s tribute from the sheep is 675, one five-hundredth of the soldiers’ share. Arminian readers note that even a small fraction is counted exactly, signifying faithful giving. Both traditions agree that the tribute follows the ratio of 31:28.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the exact figure as showing that what belongs to God is not estimated loosely. Shared ground: the soldiers’ tribute is calculated precisely, demonstrating integrity in fulfilling the command. Small sums matter when they are owed to God.

Numbers 31:38

38The cattle were thirty-six thousand, of which the LORD’s tribute was seventy-two.

Arminian

The soldiers’ cattle number 36,000, with 72 as the LORD’s tribute. Arminian readers note the consistency of the ratio across categories. Both traditions agree that each kind of plunder is treated by the same standard.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that no category is exempt from God’s portion. Shared ground: applying one rule to every kind of spoil shows that the tribute is a matter of principle, not convenience.

Numbers 31:39

39The donkeys were thirty thousand five hundred, of which the LORD’s tribute was sixty-one.

Arminian

The donkeys number 30,500, with 61 as tribute. Arminian readers note that the tribute is taken even from working animals that might seem more useful kept. Both traditions agree that the ratio is applied without exception.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the donkey tribute the same faithful accounting. Shared ground: God’s claim reaches even practical goods, and the leaders follow the instruction exactly in each category. Working animals are not exempt simply because they are useful.

Numbers 31:40

40The persons were sixteen thousand, of whom the LORD’s tribute was thirty-two persons.

Arminian

Of 16,000 captive girls in the soldiers’ half, 32 are the LORD’s tribute. Arminian readers note that the text does not say how these served at the sanctuary, and decline to guess. Both traditions agree that the verse should be read without speculation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the persons given to Eleazar are not described further. Shared ground: the text records only the number, and responsible reading does not fill in details about their lives that Scripture does not give.

Numbers 31:41

41Moses gave the tribute, which was the LORD’s wave offering, to Eleazar the priest, as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

Moses gives the tribute, the LORD’s wave offering, to Eleazar as commanded. Arminian readers note that Moses personally completes the transfer. Both traditions agree that the act closes the account of the soldiers’ share.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repeated phrase “as the LORD commanded Moses” as confirming obedience. Shared ground: the soldiers’ tribute reaches the priest exactly as instructed, completing their part of the division. Nothing is withheld from the priest’s share.

Numbers 31:42

42Of the children of Israel’s half, which Moses divided off from the men who fought

Arminian

The narrative turns to the congregation’s half, divided from the fighters’ portion. Arminian readers note that the congregation’s share is treated with the same care as the soldiers’. Both traditions agree that this verse introduces the parenthesis that follows.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the structure: the sentence pauses for the totals in 31:43–46 before resuming in 31:47. Shared ground: the congregation’s portion is recorded just as fully as the warriors’, underscoring equal treatment.

Numbers 31:43

43(now the congregation’s half was three hundred thirty-seven thousand five hundred sheep,

Arminian

The congregation’s half includes 337,500 sheep, matching the soldiers’. Arminian readers see grace in the equal share given to those who did not fight. Both traditions agree that the community as a whole benefits from the victory.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the shared benefit as reflecting that the victory belonged to the LORD and therefore to his whole people. Shared ground: the congregation receives an equal portion, showing solidarity within Israel.

Numbers 31:44

44thirty-six thousand head of cattle,

Arminian

The congregation receives 36,000 cattle. Arminian readers note that families who stayed in camp now have significant herds. Both traditions agree that this increase sets the stage for the livestock concerns of chapter 32.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the parallel with the soldiers’ cattle, again exactly half. Shared ground: the herds distributed here will help explain why Reuben and Gad, already rich in livestock, look for pasture east of the Jordan.

Numbers 31:45

45thirty thousand five hundred donkeys,

Arminian

The congregation receives 30,500 donkeys. Arminian readers note the practical help for families preparing to move into Canaan. Both traditions agree that the figure exactly mirrors the soldiers’ share. Families who did not fight still need animals to carry their goods into the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the matching number as evidence of an even, rule-governed division. Shared ground: the beasts of burden support the whole community’s coming journey, and the accounting shows no favoritism.

Numbers 31:46

46and sixteen thousand persons),

Arminian

The congregation’s half includes 16,000 persons. Arminian readers note again the discomfort of seeing people listed with property, and the importance of reading this in light of later laws that limit how captives could be treated (Deuteronomy 21:10–14). Both traditions read the verse carefully.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the parenthesis closes with the captive girls. Shared ground: the text records the distribution without describing individual outcomes, and both traditions affirm the dignity of every person made in God’s image while reading this record honestly.

Numbers 31:47

47even of the children of Israel’s half, Moses took one drawn out of every fifty, both of man and of animal, and gave them to the Levites, who performed the duty of the LORD’s tabernacle, as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

From the congregation’s half, Moses gives one in fifty to the Levites. Arminian readers note that the Levites’ rate is ten times the priests’ rate from the soldiers’ half. Both traditions agree that the community supports those who serve the tabernacle.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the completion of God’s instructions, again marked “as the LORD commanded Moses.” Shared ground: the Levites, who received no land inheritance, are provided for from the shared spoil. Their provision comes from the whole community’s share.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
