Passage

Numbers 31

Verses 13–24 · WEB

Moses meets the returning army and is angry that the women were spared, since, by the text’s account, they had led Israel into sin at Peor on Balaam’s advice. He orders the killing of the boys and of women who had slept with a man, while the girls are kept alive. The section then turns to purification: those who killed or touched the dead must stay outside the camp seven days, and captives, clothing, and metal must be cleansed by fire or water as Eleazar instructs.

Numbers 31:13

13Moses and Eleazar the priest, with all the princes of the congregation, went out to meet them outside of the camp.

Arminian

Moses, Eleazar, and the leaders meet the army outside the camp. Arminian readers note that the soldiers, having contacted the dead, cannot yet enter (compare 31:19). Both traditions agree that the meeting place reflects concern for the camp’s purity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the leaders coming out as recognition of the army’s service and of the need for purification before reentry. Shared ground: holiness governs the community’s life even after victory, and the camp is protected from defilement by death.

Numbers 31:14

14Moses was angry with the officers of the army, the captains of thousands and the captains of hundreds, who came from the service of the war.

Arminian

Moses is angry with the officers returning from battle. Arminian readers note that Moses holds leaders accountable for their decisions. Both traditions agree that his anger arises from the next verse’s concern, not from the victory itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses judging the officers by the standard of God’s purpose for the campaign. Shared ground: leaders bear responsibility for what happens under their command, and Moses’ anger signals that something has gone against the war’s stated aim.

Numbers 31:15

15Moses said to them, “Have you saved all the women alive?

Arminian

Moses asks whether they have kept all the women alive. Arminian readers note that the question exposes a decision contrary to the campaign’s purpose as Moses understood it. Both traditions acknowledge that this begins the most difficult part of the chapter for modern readers.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Moses’ question implies the soldiers’ choice conflicted with the judgment God intended. Shared ground: the text does not hide Moses’ anger, and both traditions read what follows soberly, without either excusing or exaggerating it.

Numbers 31:16

16Behold, these caused the children of Israel, through the counsel of Balaam, to commit trespass against the LORD in the matter of Peor, and so the plague was among the congregation of the LORD.

Arminian

Moses explains that these women, through Balaam’s counsel, led Israel into trespass at Peor, bringing the plague. Arminian readers see the link to Numbers 25, where 24,000 died (25:9). Both traditions note that Revelation 2:14 also attributes the stumbling block at Peor to Balaam.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this verse as the stated ground for the judgment: deliberate seduction into idolatry that brought death on Israel. Shared ground: the text itself gives the reason, Balaam’s counsel and the sin at Peor, and no speculative motives are added beyond it.

Numbers 31:17

17Now therefore kill every male among the little ones, and kill every woman who has known man by lying with him.

Arminian

Moses orders the killing of every male child and every woman who has slept with a man. Arminian readers state plainly that this is among the hardest commands in Scripture. Many hold it as a unique, limited judgment tied to Peor, not a pattern for any later people.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm God’s right as Judge over all life while acknowledging the command remains deeply troubling. They typically treat it as a unique act within Israel’s theocracy. Shared ground: both traditions reject any use of this text to justify violence today, and neither pretends it is easy.

Numbers 31:18

18But all the girls, who have not known man by lying with him, keep alive for yourselves.

Arminian

The girls who had not slept with a man are to be kept alive “for yourselves.” Arminian readers note that the text does not detail their status. Deuteronomy 21:10–14 later regulates marriage to captive women with certain protections. Both traditions refuse to speculate beyond the text.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the girls are spared because they were not involved at Peor, the stated ground of judgment. Their later status is not described here, though Deuteronomy 21:10–14 gives relevant regulations. Shared ground: the verse is difficult, and honesty requires neither sanitizing nor sensationalizing it.

Numbers 31:19

19“Encamp outside of the camp for seven days. Whoever has killed any person, and whoever has touched any slain, purify yourselves on the third day and on the seventh day, you and your captives.

Arminian

Those who killed or touched the dead must stay outside the camp seven days and purify themselves on the third and seventh days, along with their captives. Arminian readers note that even a commanded war leaves defilement. Both traditions see this as applying the corpse-purity law of Numbers 19:11–12.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the purification law applies regardless of the war’s divine sanction: contact with death defiles. Shared ground: the requirement reminds Israel that killing, even under command, is not a light matter, and the captives are included in the cleansing.

Numbers 31:20

20You shall purify every garment, and all that is made of skin, and all work of goats’ hair, and all things made of wood.”

Arminian

Garments, leather, goats’ hair work, and wooden items must be purified. Arminian readers note the thoroughness of the cleansing, extending to everyday objects. Both traditions agree that the rule reflects the seriousness with which death’s defilement was treated in Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the comprehensive scope of purification as reflecting God’s holiness touching every part of the camp’s life. Shared ground: possessions taken from the battlefield had to be cleansed before entering ordinary use in Israel.

Numbers 31:21

21Eleazar the priest said to the men of war who went to the battle, “This is the statute of the law which the LORD has commanded Moses.

Arminian

Eleazar tells the soldiers this is a statute the LORD commanded Moses. Arminian readers note the priest’s role in teaching purity law (compare Leviticus 10:10–11). Both traditions agree that the instruction carries divine authority, not merely priestly custom.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Eleazar grounds the rule in God’s command. Shared ground: the priest’s teaching role is exercised here as the law prescribes, and the soldiers receive clear instruction on how to return to the camp.

Numbers 31:22

22However the gold, and the silver, the bronze, the iron, the tin, and the lead,

Arminian

Gold, silver, bronze, iron, tin, and lead are listed. Arminian readers note the variety of metals taken as plunder. Both traditions agree that the list introduces a distinction between materials that can withstand fire and those that cannot.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the precise listing, which prepares for the purification method in the next verse. Shared ground: the metals are valuable and must be cleansed before use, showing that plunder did not bypass the law.

Numbers 31:23

23everything that may withstand the fire, you shall make to go through the fire, and it shall be clean; nevertheless it shall be purified with the water for impurity. All that doesn’t withstand the fire you shall make to go through the water.

Arminian

Fireproof items pass through fire and are also purified with the water for impurity; others pass through water. Arminian readers see practical wisdom matched to materials. Both traditions agree that “water for impurity” refers to the purification water of Numbers 19.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the combination of fire and water as thorough cleansing. Some readers draw symbolic connections, but the text presents it as practical ritual law. Shared ground: purification is required for everything brought into the camp.

Numbers 31:24

24You shall wash your clothes on the seventh day, and you shall be clean. Afterward you shall come into the camp.”

Arminian

Soldiers wash their clothes on the seventh day and are clean, then may enter the camp. Arminian readers see restoration available after defilement. Both traditions agree that the process ends with reintegration into the community.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that cleansing is completed on God’s schedule, not the soldiers’ preference. Shared ground: the law provides a path back into the camp, and the section closes with the warriors restored to the congregation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
