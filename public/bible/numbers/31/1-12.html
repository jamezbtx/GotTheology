Passage

Numbers 31

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Near the end of Moses’ life, the LORD commands Israel to take vengeance on Midian for the seduction into idolatry at Baal Peor, where a Midianite woman was involved and a plague struck the camp (Numbers 25:1–18). A thousand men from each tribe go out with Phinehas the priest and the sanctuary trumpets; they kill the men of Midian, its five kings, and Balaam, and bring back captives and plunder. Readers in every tradition find this chapter morally difficult, and the notes try to state what the text says plainly, without softening or embellishing it.

Numbers 31:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses, opening the last major campaign of his life. Arminian readers note that what follows is presented as God’s command, not Israel’s private revenge. Both traditions agree that the chapter must be read in light of Numbers 25, where its cause is set out.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the initiative belongs to God, the Judge of all the earth. Shared ground: the war is framed as a response to specific events at Peor, not as a general license for violence, and readers should not detach it from that setting.

Numbers 31:2

2“Avenge the children of Israel on the Midianites. Afterward you shall be gathered to your people.”

Arminian

God commands vengeance on Midian for Israel, then tells Moses he will be gathered to his people. Arminian readers see God’s justice responding to Midian’s deliberate attempt to draw Israel into idolatry (25:17–18). Both traditions agree that vengeance here is God’s judicial act, not personal vindictiveness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign right to judge nations for their sin, exercised at a particular point in redemptive history. The note of Moses’ coming death (compare 27:12–13) frames this as his final task. Shared ground: Scripture elsewhere reserves vengeance to God (Deuteronomy 32:35; Romans 12:19).

Numbers 31:3

3Moses spoke to the people, saying, “Arm men from among you for war, that they may go against Midian, to execute the LORD’s vengeance on Midian.

Arminian

Moses calls the campaign “the LORD’s vengeance on Midian.” Arminian readers note that the war is defined theologically, as judgment, rather than as conquest for land or wealth. Both traditions read it as a unique act of judgment in Israel’s history, not a model for believers today.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the identification of Israel’s action with the LORD’s own judgment, which limits it to God’s express command. Shared ground: Christians in both traditions hold that the church’s warfare is not with the sword (2 Corinthians 10:3–4; Ephesians 6:12).

Numbers 31:4

4You shall send one thousand out of every tribe, throughout all the tribes of Israel, to the war.”

Arminian

One thousand men are drawn from every tribe. Arminian readers see shared responsibility: every tribe takes part equally rather than leaving the task to a few. Both traditions observe that the force is relatively small, emphasizing that the outcome depends on God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the equal levy as expressing Israel’s unity as one covenant people under God. Shared ground: the whole nation is represented, so that the judgment is a corporate act carried out at God’s command, not a tribal feud.

Numbers 31:5

5So there were delivered, out of the thousands of Israel, a thousand from every tribe, twelve thousand armed for war.

Arminian

Twelve thousand men are delivered for war, a thousand from each tribe. Arminian readers note that the text records the levy as carried out exactly as commanded. Both traditions agree that the precision of the numbers emphasizes orderly obedience rather than military might.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the passive phrasing, the men “were delivered,” suggesting the community supplied them as Moses directed. Shared ground: the army is organized by tribe and number, presenting Israel as a disciplined people acting under God’s word.

Numbers 31:6

6Moses sent them, one thousand of every tribe, to the war with Phinehas the son of Eleazar the priest, to the war, with the vessels of the sanctuary and the trumpets for the alarm in his hand.

Arminian

Phinehas, whose zeal had ended the plague at Peor (25:7–13), goes with the sanctuary vessels and trumpets. Arminian readers note the priestly presence marking this as a holy war. Both traditions agree that the trumpets recall God’s promise to remember Israel in battle (10:9).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priest and sacred trumpets as signs that God himself directs the battle. Shared ground: Phinehas’s role ties the campaign directly to Peor, and the text presents the war as worship-bound obedience rather than ordinary warfare.

Numbers 31:7

7They fought against Midian, as the LORD commanded Moses. They killed every male.

Arminian

Israel fights “as the LORD commanded” and kills every male. Arminian readers acknowledge the severity and refuse to soften it. Readers in both traditions often note that Midianites appear again later (Judges 6), so “every male” likely refers to those of this group or region, not every Midianite everywhere.

Reformed

Reformed readers state plainly that the text presents this killing as commanded by God, and that this is hard for modern readers. Shared ground: the later reappearance of Midian in Judges shows the account concerns a specific campaign, and the verse should not be stretched beyond what it says.

Numbers 31:8

8They killed the kings of Midian with the rest of their slain: Evi, Rekem, Zur, Hur, and Reba, the five kings of Midian. They also killed Balaam the son of Beor with the sword.

Arminian

The five kings of Midian are killed, and Balaam son of Beor dies by the sword. Arminian readers note the tragedy of Balaam, who spoke God’s blessing yet later counseled Israel’s ruin (31:16). Both traditions agree that his end shows the danger of knowing truth while serving gain.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Balaam’s death fulfills a just end for one who led Israel into sin (Revelation 2:14 recalls his counsel). Joshua 13:21–22 records the same kings and Balaam’s death. Shared ground: privileged knowledge of God’s word does not protect someone who works against God’s people.

Numbers 31:9

9The children of Israel took the women of Midian captive with their little ones; and all their livestock, all their flocks, and all their goods, they took as plunder.

Arminian

The women and children are taken captive, and the livestock and goods are taken as plunder. Arminian readers note that the text reports this without comment, leaving Moses’ response for 31:14–18. Both traditions agree that ancient warfare commonly involved captives and spoil.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this verse sets up the conflict with Moses in the next section. The captives’ fate becomes the central moral difficulty of the chapter. Shared ground: the narrative reports actions in sequence, and readers should notice that the soldiers’ choice here is later questioned.

Numbers 31:10

10All their cities in the places in which they lived, and all their encampments, they burned with fire.

Arminian

The cities and encampments of Midian are burned. Arminian readers see the thoroughness of the judgment. Both traditions agree that burning the settlements prevented Israel from settling among the Midianites and so being drawn again into their worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the destruction of the towns removes the setting of idolatrous influence. The text does not give a reason, so readers should be careful not to overstate. Shared ground: the campaign is presented as judgment, not territorial expansion.

Numbers 31:11

11They took all the captives, and all the plunder, both of man and of animal.

Arminian

The army gathers all captives and plunder, both people and animals. Arminian readers note the stark language that groups people with animals, which modern readers rightly find jarring. Both traditions agree that the text describes ancient practice rather than endorsing it as a timeless norm.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the phrase “both of man and of animal” recurs in the chapter’s accounting. Shared ground: the text reflects the categories of its time, and both traditions read it within the larger biblical affirmation that every human bears God’s image (Genesis 1:27).

Numbers 31:12

12They brought the captives with the prey and the plunder, to Moses, and to Eleazar the priest, and to the congregation of the children of Israel, to the camp at the plains of Moab, which are by the Jordan at Jericho.

Arminian

The captives and plunder are brought to Moses, Eleazar, and the congregation at the plains of Moab by the Jordan near Jericho. Arminian readers note the setting at the edge of the promised land. Both traditions agree that the location places these events just before the conquest under Joshua.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the army brings everything to the leaders rather than keeping it, submitting the outcome to Moses and the priest. Shared ground: the plains of Moab are the setting for the last chapters of Numbers and for Deuteronomy, on the threshold of Canaan.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
