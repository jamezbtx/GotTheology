Passage

Numbers 25

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Israel is camped at Shittim in the plains of Moab, just after Balaam’s oracles of blessing (Numbers 22–24). The people the LORD would not let Balaam curse now bring judgment on themselves. Sexual sin with the women of Moab leads them into the sacrifices of Moab’s gods and into joining themselves to Baal Peor. The LORD’s anger burns, a plague breaks out, and Phinehas the priest acts to stop it. The text treats the sexual sin and the idolatry as one act of covenant unfaithfulness. Later Scripture remembers the episode in Ps 106:28–31, and Paul uses it as a warning to the church in 1 Cor 10:8. Numbers 31:16 later traces the scheme to the counsel of Balaam, and Rev 2:14 refers to it as well. Both traditions read the passage as a real warning to God’s people and as a display of both God’s holiness and his mercy in stopping the plague.

Numbers 25:1

1Israel stayed in Shittim; and the people began to play the prostitute with the daughters of Moab;

Arminian

Israel “began to play the prostitute with the daughters of Moab.” The phrase describes literal sexual sin, and the same language is used elsewhere in Scripture for unfaithfulness to the LORD. Here both meanings meet.

Arminian readers see a people who had just been blessed by God now turning away by their own choice. They read the account as a sober warning that the covenant people can fall into grave sin.

Reformed

Sexual immorality opens the way to idolatry. The setting at Shittim, at the edge of the promised land, makes the failure stark.

Reformed readers see the persistence of sin in the covenant community even after God has defended it against Balaam’s curse. Both traditions agree that God’s blessing did not make Israel immune to temptation, and that 1 Cor 10 applies the episode to the church as a warning.

Numbers 25:2

2for they called the people to the sacrifices of their gods. The people ate and bowed down to their gods.

Arminian

The women “called the people to the sacrifices of their gods.” The people ate and bowed down. Sexual sin led directly to shared worship of other gods.

Arminian readers note how one compromise led to another. They stress the need for watchfulness against temptations that come through relationships and appetite.

Reformed

Eating the sacrifices and bowing down were acts of worship. Ps 106:28 describes Israel as having “ate the sacrifices of the dead.”

Reformed readers stress that idolatry lies at the root of the sin. The heart turned from the LORD to other gods. Both traditions agree that the first commandment is at stake, not only sexual morality.

Numbers 25:3

3Israel joined himself to Baal Peor, and the LORD’s anger burned against Israel.

Arminian

“Israel joined himself to Baal Peor.” The verb pictures being bound or attached to the god of Peor, the local form of Baal worship. The LORD’s anger burned against Israel.

Arminian readers see a real breach of covenant relationship that provokes real divine anger. They read God’s wrath as his holy response to his people’s chosen unfaithfulness.

Reformed

The LORD’s anger is his holy and just response to his people’s attachment to another god.

Reformed readers stress the jealousy of the LORD for his covenant people, which the chapter will name directly in 25:11. Both traditions agree that the anger is not arbitrary. It answers a breach of the covenant Israel had sworn to keep.

Numbers 25:4

4The LORD said to Moses, “Take all the chiefs of the people, and hang them up to the LORD before the sun, that the fierce anger of the LORD may turn away from Israel.”

Arminian

The LORD commands that “all the chiefs of the people” be hung up “before the sun.” The exact form of execution in the Hebrew is debated; it may mean public exposure of the bodies. Leaders are held responsible for the people’s sin.

Arminian readers note that public judgment on the leaders was meant to turn away “the fierce anger of the LORD” from the whole nation.

Reformed

The leaders bear public judgment “to the LORD,” so that his anger may turn away from Israel.

Reformed readers stress the seriousness of leadership in God’s people and the public vindication of God’s holiness. Both traditions read the command as tied to the covenant order of ancient Israel, not as a pattern for the church, which deals with sin by discipline rather than the sword.

Numbers 25:5

5Moses said to the judges of Israel, “Everyone kill his men who have joined themselves to Baal Peor.”

Arminian

Moses tells the judges to kill those of their men who had joined themselves to Baal Peor. The order targets the guilty rather than the whole camp.

Arminian readers note that judgment falls on those who chose to join the idolatry.

Reformed

The judges are to act against those under their charge who have joined Baal Peor.

Reformed readers see God’s justice administered through the order he had set up in Israel (compare Exod 18:21–26). Both traditions note that the text does not narrate how the order was carried out before the events of 25:6, and both read the verse within Israel’s covenant law rather than as a model for today.

Numbers 25:6

6Behold, one of the children of Israel came and brought to his brothers a Midianite woman in the sight of Moses, and in the sight of all the congregation of the children of Israel, while they were weeping at the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

While the congregation is weeping at the door of the Tent of Meeting, an Israelite brings a Midianite woman “in the sight of Moses” and of all the congregation. The act is open and defiant.

Arminian readers see the contrast between those who mourn the sin and the man who flaunts it. They stress that repentance and rebellion stand side by side in the same camp.

Reformed

The weeping at the Tent shows grief and repentance. The man’s act shows contempt for God’s judgment while the plague is under way.

Reformed readers stress the boldness of sin even at the place of God’s presence. Both traditions agree that the act was a high-handed defiance of the LORD (compare Num 15:30–31).

Numbers 25:7

7When Phinehas, the son of Eleazar, the son of Aaron the priest, saw it, he rose up from the middle of the congregation, and took a spear in his hand.

Arminian

Phinehas, son of Eleazar and grandson of Aaron, sees the act and rises up from the congregation with a spear in his hand. He is a priest, charged with guarding the holiness of the sanctuary.

Arminian readers note Phinehas’s personal responsiveness to God’s honor when others stood by.

Reformed

Phinehas acts as one of the priestly family responsible for the sanctuary’s holiness.

Reformed readers note that God will later commend his act as zeal shared with God’s own (25:11). Both traditions agree that Phinehas acts in his priestly role, at a moment of open defiance during divine judgment.

Numbers 25:8

8He went after the man of Israel into the pavilion, and thrust both of them through, the man of Israel, and the woman through her body. So the plague was stopped among the children of Israel.

Arminian

Phinehas follows the man into the “pavilion,” a rare word that likely refers to an inner tent or chamber, and kills both. “So the plague was stopped among the children of Israel.”

Arminian readers see God responding to Phinehas’s act by halting the plague. Human action and divine mercy meet in the outcome.

Reformed

The plague stops when God’s judgment is executed on open rebellion.

Reformed readers stress that God himself stops the plague; Ps 106:30 says, “Then Phinehas stood up and executed judgment, so the plague was stopped.” Both traditions agree that the account is narrated as a unique act within Israel’s covenant judgment, approved by God, and not as a license for private violence.

Numbers 25:9

9Those who died by the plague were twenty-four thousand.

Arminian

“Those who died by the plague were twenty-four thousand.” Paul writes in 1 Cor 10:8 that “in one day twenty-three thousand fell.” Several explanations have been proposed. Paul may refer to those who died in one day, the numbers may be rounded, or the totals may count different groups (for example, whether the leaders of 25:4 are included). Readers in both traditions hold different views on how best to explain the difference.

Arminian readers stress that Paul uses the episode to warn believers that they too can fall (1 Cor 10:12).

Reformed

The large loss shows the seriousness of the sin and the cost of covenant unfaithfulness.

Reformed readers also read Paul’s warning as real. They often pair it with the promise in 1 Cor 10:13 that God is faithful and provides the way of escape. Both traditions agree that the warning is to be heeded and that God’s faithfulness is the believer’s help in temptation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
