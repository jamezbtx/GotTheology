Passage

Numbers 25

Verses 10–18 · WEB

The LORD commends Phinehas and gives him “my covenant of peace,” a covenant of an everlasting priesthood for him and his descendants, because he was jealous for his God and made atonement for Israel. The narrator then names the offenders, Zimri, a Simeonite leader, and Cozbi, daughter of a Midianite head, showing that the sin reached into the leadership of both peoples. Finally, the LORD commands Israel to harass and strike the Midianites, which is carried out in Numbers 31. Numbers 31:16 links the scheme at Peor to the counsel of Balaam, and Rev 2:14 refers to that teaching. Ps 106:31 says Phinehas’s act “was credited to him for righteousness, for all generations to come.” Both traditions read the passage as showing God’s holy jealousy for his people, his mercy in turning away wrath, and his faithfulness to the priestly line.

Numbers 25:10

10The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses to give his verdict on Phinehas’s act.

Arminian readers note that the act is interpreted by God’s own word, not left to human judgment.

Reformed

God himself speaks to evaluate what Phinehas did.

Reformed readers stress that Scripture’s approval rests on God’s verdict, not on the act taken in isolation. Both traditions agree that the following verses explain why this deed was approved.

Numbers 25:11

11“Phinehas, the son of Eleazar, the son of Aaron the priest, has turned my wrath away from the children of Israel, in that he was jealous with my jealousy among them, so that I didn’t consume the children of Israel in my jealousy.

Arminian

Phinehas “has turned my wrath away” because “he was jealous with my jealousy.” His zeal matched God’s own concern for his covenant people.

Arminian readers stress that Phinehas’s zeal was for God’s honor, not personal anger. God’s wrath was turned away, and he did not consume Israel.

Reformed

God’s jealousy is his exclusive covenant love that will not share his people with idols. Phinehas shared that jealousy.

Reformed readers stress that God restrained his wrath and spared Israel from being consumed. Both traditions agree that divine jealousy here is holy love for his people, not a petty emotion, and that the outcome was mercy.

Numbers 25:12

12Therefore say, ‘Behold, I give to him my covenant of peace.

Arminian

“Behold, I give to him my covenant of peace.” The gift is God’s own covenant, freely given.

Arminian readers note that God rewards faithful obedience with lasting blessing.

Reformed

The covenant of peace is God’s gift, given in response to Phinehas’s zeal.

Reformed readers stress that the covenant originates with God and rests on his promise. Both traditions agree that “peace” here speaks of a secure, restored relationship with God, and they note that Mal 2:4–5 links a covenant of life and peace with the priestly line of Levi.

Numbers 25:13

13It shall be to him, and to his offspring after him, the covenant of an everlasting priesthood, because he was jealous for his God, and made atonement for the children of Israel.’ ”

Arminian

The covenant is “the covenant of an everlasting priesthood” for Phinehas and his offspring, because he was jealous for his God and “made atonement for the children of Israel.” The atonement here was not a sacrifice. It was the turning away of wrath through judgment executed on open rebellion. Ps 106:31 says his act “was credited to him for righteousness.”

Arminian readers see the reward as God’s approval of a faithful deed. They generally read Ps 106:31 as God’s commendation of this act, not as a statement about how a sinner is justified before God.

Reformed

Phinehas’s act “made atonement” by turning away God’s wrath. Readers in both traditions often see this as one of many Old Testament pictures pointing to Christ’s work of turning away wrath once for all.

Reformed readers also generally read Ps 106:31 as God’s commendation of this deed and distinguish it from justification by faith as taught in Rom 4. Both traditions agree that the everlasting priesthood rests on God’s promise, and they discuss how that promise relates to the later history of the priesthood and to Christ’s priesthood in Hebrews.

Numbers 25:14

14Now the name of the man of Israel that was slain, who was slain with the Midianite woman, was Zimri, the son of Salu, a prince of a fathers’ house among the Simeonites.

Arminian

The slain Israelite is named as Zimri son of Salu, “a prince of a fathers’ house among the Simeonites.” The offender was a leader.

Arminian readers note that rank gave no protection from judgment and that leaders bear greater responsibility for their example.

Reformed

Naming Zimri as a leader shows how far the sin had reached into Israel’s leadership.

Reformed readers stress that God’s holiness applies equally to the powerful. Both traditions agree that leaders’ public sin does special harm to God’s people.

Numbers 25:15

15The name of the Midianite woman who was slain was Cozbi, the daughter of Zur. He was head of the people of a fathers’ house in Midian.

Arminian

The woman is named as Cozbi, daughter of Zur, “head of the people of a fathers’ house in Midian.” Zur is later named among the five kings of Midian (Num 31:8).

Arminian readers note that the incident involved leaders on both sides, which shows it was not a private lapse.

Reformed

Cozbi’s high rank suggests the seduction was part of a deliberate Midianite strategy.

Reformed readers connect this with 25:18 and Num 31:16. Both traditions agree that the text presents the incident as a scheme against Israel, not a chance meeting.

Numbers 25:16

16The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks again to Moses, this time about Midian.

Arminian readers note that God addresses the outside source of the temptation as well as Israel’s own sin.

Reformed

God turns from his judgment on Israel to the Midianites who led them astray.

Reformed readers see God’s justice dealing both with his people’s sin and with those who schemed against them. Both traditions read the verse as introducing the command that follows.

Numbers 25:17

17“Harass the Midianites, and strike them;

Arminian

“Harass the Midianites, and strike them.” The command is carried out in Numbers 31.

Arminian readers read the command within its specific historical setting, as God’s judgment on a people who sought to destroy Israel through deception.

Reformed

The command is God’s judicial response to Midian’s hostility.

Reformed readers read it as part of God’s particular judgments in Israel’s history. Both traditions agree that the command belongs to a unique covenant situation and is not a pattern for the church, whose warfare is spiritual (compare Eph 6:12).

Numbers 25:18

18for they harassed you with their wiles, wherein they have deceived you in the matter of Peor, and in the incident regarding Cozbi, the daughter of the prince of Midian, their sister, who was slain on the day of the plague in the matter of Peor.”

Arminian

The reason is that Midian “harassed you with their wiles” in the matter of Peor and of Cozbi. Numbers 31:16 later says this came “through the counsel of Balaam,” and Rev 2:14 refers to Balaam’s teaching. Moab is named in 25:1, but Midian had joined Moab against Israel from the start (22:4, 7).

Arminian readers note that what Balaam could not do by cursing was attempted through temptation. They stress that God’s people must guard against seduction as well as open attack.

Reformed

The deception at Peor is presented as an assault on Israel’s faithfulness.

Reformed readers stress that the enemy who could not curse Israel sought to draw it into sin, and that God defended his people. Both traditions agree that the greater danger to God’s people came from within, through temptation, and that God’s faithfulness preserved them.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
