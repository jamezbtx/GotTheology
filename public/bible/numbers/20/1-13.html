Passage

Numbers 20

Verses 1–13 · WEB

At Kadesh Miriam dies, the people again lack water, and they quarrel with Moses and Aaron. God commands Moses to take the rod and speak to the rock, but Moses addresses the people as rebels and strikes the rock twice. Water flows, yet God says Moses and Aaron did not believe him or sanctify him before Israel, so they will not bring the people into the land. Both traditions take this explanation plainly from the text, and both affirm that God remains faithful to his people while holding leaders accountable.

Numbers 20:1

1The children of Israel, even the whole congregation, came into the wilderness of Zin in the first month. The people stayed in Kadesh. Miriam died there, and was buried there.

Arminian

Israel arrives in the wilderness of Zin and stays at Kadesh, where Miriam dies and is buried. Arminian readers see the passing of a leader who had served alongside Moses and Aaron.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note Miriam’s death as the beginning of a chapter of loss. Both traditions agree that the brief notice marks a transition toward the end of the wilderness generation.

Numbers 20:2

2There was no water for the congregation; and they assembled themselves together against Moses and against Aaron.

Arminian

There is no water, and the people assemble against Moses and Aaron. Arminian readers see a familiar crisis testing whether the people will trust God this time.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see recurring need exposing the people’s hearts. Both traditions note the parallel with the earlier water crisis in Exodus 17. The same lesson returns in a new generation.

Numbers 20:3

3The people quarreled with Moses, and spoke, saying, “We wish that we had died when our brothers died before the LORD!

Arminian

The people quarrel with Moses and wish they had died with their brothers before the LORD. Arminian readers see despair turning into bitter complaint against God’s appointed leaders.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see unbelief expressed in quarreling. Both traditions agree that the complaint recalls earlier judgments and reveals how deeply the people have lost confidence in God.

Numbers 20:4

4Why have you brought the LORD’s assembly into this wilderness, that we should die there, we and our animals?

Arminian

They accuse Moses of bringing the LORD’s assembly into the wilderness to die. Arminian readers see the people blaming leaders for hardships that are part of God’s leading.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the complaint ultimately directed against God’s guidance. Both traditions agree the people still call themselves the LORD’s assembly while doubting his care.

Numbers 20:5

5Why have you made us to come up out of Egypt, to bring us in to this evil place? It is no place of seed, or of figs, or of vines, or of pomegranates; neither is there any water to drink.”

Arminian

They ask why they were brought out of Egypt to this evil place without crops or water. Arminian readers see longing for the past replacing trust in God’s promises.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the people’s ingratitude for deliverance. Both traditions agree the list of missing produce contrasts painfully with the fruitful land God had promised.

Numbers 20:6

6Moses and Aaron went from the presence of the assembly to the door of the Tent of Meeting, and fell on their faces. The LORD’s glory appeared to them.

Arminian

Moses and Aaron fall on their faces at the Tent of Meeting, and the LORD’s glory appears. Arminian readers see leaders turning to God in prayer rather than responding in anger at first.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God revealing his glory in response to the leaders’ humility. Both traditions agree that God’s presence, not the people’s complaint, determines what happens next.

Numbers 20:7

7The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses. Arminian readers see God answering the crisis with clear instructions rather than immediate judgment on the complaining people. God’s response begins with speech, not wrath.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God graciously addressing the need. Both traditions agree that the precise wording of the instruction that follows is crucial to understanding the episode.

Numbers 20:8

8“Take the rod, and assemble the congregation, you, and Aaron your brother, and speak to the rock before their eyes, that it pour out its water. You shall bring water to them out of the rock; so you shall give the congregation and their livestock drink.”

Arminian

God tells Moses to take the rod, gather the congregation, and speak to the rock before their eyes so it will give water. Arminian readers see a specific command meant to display God’s power publicly.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God appointing the exact means of provision. Both traditions agree that the command is to speak to the rock, which sets up the contrast with what Moses does.

Numbers 20:9

9Moses took the rod from before the LORD, as he commanded him.

Arminian

Moses takes the rod from before the LORD as commanded. Arminian readers see Moses beginning in obedience, following the first part of God’s instruction. The rod itself was not the problem.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Moses initially acting according to God’s word. Both traditions agree that the departure from the command comes in the actions that follow.

Numbers 20:10

10Moses and Aaron gathered the assembly together before the rock, and he said to them, “Hear now, you rebels! Shall we bring water out of this rock for you?”

Arminian

Moses gathers the people and says, “Hear now, you rebels! Shall we bring water out of this rock for you?” Arminian readers see anger and harsh words replacing the simple speech God commanded.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Moses’ words departing from the instruction. Both traditions note that Psalm 106:32–33 says Moses spoke rashly with his lips on this occasion.

Numbers 20:11

11Moses lifted up his hand, and struck the rock with his rod twice, and water came out abundantly. The congregation and their livestock drank.

Arminian

Moses strikes the rock twice, and water flows abundantly. Arminian readers see God graciously providing for the people even though Moses did not follow the command to speak.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s faithfulness to the people despite his servant’s disobedience. Both traditions agree that the provision of water does not mean God approved of how it came.

Numbers 20:12

12The LORD said to Moses and Aaron, “Because you didn’t believe in me, to sanctify me in the eyes of the children of Israel, therefore you shall not bring this assembly into the land which I have given them.”

Arminian

God says Moses and Aaron did not believe him or sanctify him before Israel, so they will not bring the people into the land. Arminian readers see unbelief and public dishonor of God as the stated sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the text’s own explanation: failing to believe and to treat God as holy. Many in both traditions see striking instead of speaking as part of that failure, though the exact nature of Moses’ sin is debated; both agree that even faithful leaders face real consequences.

Numbers 20:13

13These are the waters of Meribah; because the children of Israel strove with the LORD, and he was sanctified in them.

Arminian

These are the waters of Meribah, where Israel strove with the LORD and he was sanctified among them. Arminian readers see God upholding his holiness even through his servants’ failure.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God vindicating his own name at Meribah. Both note the WEB footnote that Meribah means quarreling, and both traditions connect the wilderness rock with Paul’s statement in 1 Corinthians 10:4 that the rock was Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
