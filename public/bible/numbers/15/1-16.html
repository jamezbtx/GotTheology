Passage

Numbers 15

Verses 1–16 · WEB

After the failure at Kadesh in Numbers 14, where the generation that left Egypt is condemned to die in the wilderness, the LORD gives instructions for life in the land: when you have come into the land. The laws set grain and drink offerings alongside animal sacrifices and insist on one law for native-born and foreigner. Placed here, they quietly reaffirm that God’s promise still stands for the next generation. Both traditions read the passage as shared ground.

Numbers 15:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses again, right after Israel’s rebellion and sentence in chapter 14. Arminian readers see God continuing to speak to his people after their failure. His word is not withdrawn because of their sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s faithfulness to his covenant purposes despite Israel’s unbelief. The next generation will still enter the land. Both traditions read this placement as a sign of grace.

Numbers 15:2

2“Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘When you have come into the land of your habitations, which I give to you,

Arminian

The instructions begin with a promise: when you come into the land I give you. Arminian readers see hope restored to the children of the condemned generation. God’s promise remains open to those who will trust him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s promise standing firm, even after judgment on many. The land is described as God’s gift. Both traditions read the verse as an assurance of future fulfillment.

Numbers 15:3

3and will make an offering by fire to the LORD—a burnt offering, or a sacrifice, to accomplish a vow, or as a free will offering, or in your set feasts, to make a pleasant aroma to the LORD, of the herd, or of the flock—

Arminian

Various offerings by fire are listed, from vows to freewill gifts and feasts. Arminian readers see a wide range of worship responses, including voluntary devotion. Worship in the land will include gratitude as well as duty.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God ordering worship for every occasion. A pleasing aroma describes offerings God accepts. Christians in both traditions read these sacrifices as fulfilled in Christ, as Ephesians 5:2 uses similar language.

Numbers 15:4

4then he who offers his offering shall offer to the LORD a meal offering of one tenth of an ephah of fine flour mixed with one fourth of a hin of oil.

Arminian

A grain offering with oil accompanies each offering. Arminian readers see the fruit of the land joined to animal sacrifice. Israel’s produce becomes part of worship. The WEB footnotes estimate the ephah and hin; ancient measures are approximate.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the whole of Israel’s life, including bread and oil, offered to God. Both traditions read the details as ceremonial instruction for worship in the land.

Numbers 15:5

5You shall prepare wine for the drink offering, one fourth of a hin, with the burnt offering or for the sacrifice, for each lamb.

Arminian

Wine for a drink offering accompanies each lamb. Arminian readers see joy and abundance represented in worship. The wine of the land is poured out before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the drink offering as part of complete worship. Paul uses the drink offering image for his own life in Philippians 2:17. Both traditions share this reading.

Numbers 15:6

6“ ‘For a ram, you shall prepare for a meal offering two tenths of an ephah of fine flour mixed with the third part of a hin of oil;

Arminian

A ram requires a larger grain offering. Arminian readers see offerings scaled to the size of the sacrifice. Worship is proportioned and ordered, not arbitrary.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see careful regulation of worship by God’s word. Both traditions read the detail descriptively, without forcing theological meaning onto the measurements. Worship follows God’s pattern.

Numbers 15:7

7and for the drink offering you shall offer the third part of a hin of wine, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian

A third of a hin of wine goes with the ram. Arminian readers see the pleasing aroma repeated as the goal of worship. God delights in offerings given as he directs.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s acceptance as the aim of sacrifice. Both traditions read the verse as part of shared ceremonial instruction. The aim of every offering is acceptance before God.

Numbers 15:8

8When you prepare a bull for a burnt offering or for a sacrifice, to accomplish a vow, or for peace offerings to the LORD,

Arminian

Instructions follow for a bull offered for a vow or peace offering. Arminian readers see different occasions of worship welcomed. Vows and fellowship offerings expressed gratitude and devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see worship ordered for many circumstances. Both traditions read the verse as introducing the largest offering in the series. The bull is the largest animal in the list, and its offerings are scaled accordingly.

Numbers 15:9

9then he shall offer with the bull a meal offering of three tenths of an ephah of fine flour mixed with half a hin of oil;

Arminian

The bull requires three tenths of an ephah of flour with oil. Arminian readers see offerings growing with the animal’s size. Larger sacrifices call for larger accompanying gifts.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see consistency in God’s instructions. Both traditions treat the measurements as ceremonial detail, with modern equivalents only approximate. The pattern of increase continues from lamb to ram to bull.

Numbers 15:10

10and you shall offer for the drink offering half a hin of wine, for an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian

Half a hin of wine accompanies the bull. Arminian readers see the series completed with the same aim of a pleasing aroma. Worship is offered wholeheartedly.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the completeness of the prescribed offerings. Both traditions share this reading. Grain, oil, wine, and animal together form a full offering made from the produce of the promised land.

Numbers 15:11

11Thus it shall be done for each bull, for each ram, for each of the male lambs, or of the young goats.

Arminian

These rules apply to each bull, ram, lamb, or goat. Arminian readers see consistency in worship across offerings. No sacrifice is left without its accompaniment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see order and uniformity in God’s instructions. Both traditions read the verse as summarizing the preceding regulations. No kind of animal is treated as exempt from the accompanying gifts.

Numbers 15:12

12According to the number that you shall prepare, so you shall do to everyone according to their number.

Arminian

The same procedure applies to each animal according to number. Arminian readers see fairness and consistency in worship. Each offering receives the same care. The rule holds for every sacrifice.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s ordered worship applied without exception. Both traditions read the verse as straightforward instruction. Regularity in worship guards against careless or improvised offerings and keeps the whole community on the same footing.

Numbers 15:13

13“ ‘All who are native-born shall do these things in this way, in offering an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian

Native-born Israelites shall offer this way. Arminian readers see clear expectations for the covenant community. Worship follows God’s pattern, not personal preference. Belonging to Israel brings both privilege and obligation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the regulation of worship by God’s command. Both traditions share this reading. Israelites by birth have no special exemption from the prescribed form; they too must offer as God directs.

Numbers 15:14

14If a stranger lives as a foreigner with you, or whoever may be among you throughout your generations, and will offer an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD, as you do, so he shall do.

Arminian

A foreigner who offers must do so the same way. Arminian readers see outsiders welcomed into worship on equal terms. God’s house is open to the stranger who joins Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the inclusion of foreigners anticipating the wider reach of God’s people. Both traditions share this reading. The foreigner who wishes to worship receives the same instructions and the same welcome as the native-born.

Numbers 15:15

15For the assembly, there shall be one statute for you and for the stranger who lives as a foreigner, a statute forever throughout your generations. As you are, so the foreigner shall be before the LORD.

Arminian

There is one statute for native and foreigner; as you are, so the foreigner is before the LORD. Arminian readers see equal standing before God. The stranger is not a second-class worshiper.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see equality before God within the covenant community. Christians in both traditions see this pointing toward the inclusion of Gentiles in Christ.

Numbers 15:16

16One law and one ordinance shall be for you and for the stranger who lives as a foreigner with you.’ ”

Arminian

One law and one ordinance apply to all. Arminian readers see the principle repeated for emphasis. God’s law treats insiders and outsiders alike. The repetition underlines the principle.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the consistency of God’s justice. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. The emphasis on one law for all recurs in 15:29 and shows God’s even-handed concern for every worshiper.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
