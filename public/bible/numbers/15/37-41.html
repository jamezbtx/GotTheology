Passage

Numbers 15

Verses 37–41 · WEB

Israel is told to wear fringes, or tassels, with a blue cord, so that seeing them they would remember and do the LORD’s commands rather than follow their own hearts and eyes. The chapter ends with God’s self-identification as the one who brought them out of Egypt. Both traditions see shared ground here: visible reminders serve memory, obedience, and holiness grounded in redemption.

Numbers 15:37

37The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses about a new instruction. Arminian readers see God giving a practical aid to obedience. The section turns from judgment to remembrance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God providing means for faithfulness. Both traditions read the verse as a transition. After the sober account of judgment, God provides a gentle aid to memory and faithfulness.

Numbers 15:38

38“Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them that they should make themselves fringes on the borders of their garments throughout their generations, and that they put on the fringe of each border a cord of blue.

Arminian

Israel is to make fringes with a blue cord on garment borders. Arminian readers see a visible reminder worn daily. The WEB footnotes note that fringe may also be rendered tassel.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God giving a visible sign of covenant identity. Jesus’ garment fringe appears in Matthew 9:20, showing the practice continued. Both traditions share this reading.

Numbers 15:39

39It shall be to you for a fringe, that you may see it, and remember all the LORD’s commandments, and do them; and that you don’t follow your own heart and your own eyes, after which you used to play the prostitute;

Arminian

The fringe helps them remember God’s commands and not follow their own hearts and eyes. Arminian readers see memory guarding against temptation. Wandering eyes and hearts lead to spiritual unfaithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fringe aiding obedience against the pull of desire. The image of playing the prostitute depicts idolatry. Both traditions share this reading.

Numbers 15:40

40so that you may remember and do all my commandments, and be holy to your God.

Arminian

Remember and do all God’s commands, and be holy. Arminian readers see memory leading to obedience and holiness. The goal is a life set apart for God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see holiness as the purpose of the law for the redeemed. Matthew 23:5 warns against using tassels for display. Both traditions share this reading.

Numbers 15:41

41I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, to be your God: I am the LORD your God.”

Arminian

I am the LORD your God who brought you out of Egypt. Arminian readers see redemption grounding obedience. God’s saving act gives the commands their meaning.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see grace preceding law, as in Exodus 20:2. Both traditions read the closing refrain as shared ground. The chapter ends with the identity of the God who redeems, giving every command its foundation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
