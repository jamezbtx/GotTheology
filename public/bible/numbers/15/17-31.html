Passage

Numbers 15

Verses 17–31 · WEB

The firstfruits of dough are dedicated to the LORD, and then the law distinguishes unintentional sin, for which atonement is provided for the congregation and for individuals, from sin done with a high hand, which despises the LORD’s word and brings being cut off. Both traditions affirm God’s provision for sins of weakness and the seriousness of defiant rebellion. They read the high-handed sinner somewhat differently when relating the passage to apostasy and perseverance, with diversity in each tradition.

Numbers 15:17

17The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses with a further instruction. Arminian readers see a new section introduced. God continues to shape Israel’s future life in the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see divine authority behind each command. Both traditions read the verse as a simple transition. The section that follows addresses firstfruits and then the distinction between unintentional and defiant sin.

Numbers 15:18

18“Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘When you come into the land where I bring you,

Arminian

The instruction applies when Israel enters the land. Arminian readers see hope again affirmed after chapter 14. God speaks of entering, not of loss. Entry into the land is assumed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s promise guiding Israel’s future. Both traditions share this reading. After the sentence of chapter 14, the repeated promise of entry reassures the next generation that God’s purpose stands.

Numbers 15:19

19then it shall be that when you eat of the bread of the land, you shall offer up a wave offering to the LORD.

Arminian

When they eat the land’s bread, they must offer a portion to the LORD. Arminian readers see gratitude expressed in giving back part of God’s gift. Daily bread becomes an occasion of worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God honored as the giver of provision. Both traditions share this reading. Giving the first portion acknowledges that every meal in the land comes from the LORD’s generosity.

Numbers 15:20

20Of the first of your dough you shall offer up a cake for a wave offering. As the wave offering of the threshing floor, so you shall heave it.

Arminian

A cake from the first dough is offered like the threshing-floor offering. Arminian readers see firstfruits dedicated from household baking. Even ordinary kitchen work is connected to worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the principle of firstfruits. Paul alludes to dough offered as firstfruits in Romans 11:16. Both traditions share this reading. Ordinary food is dedicated.

Numbers 15:21

21Of the first of your dough, you shall give to the LORD a wave offering throughout your generations.

Arminian

The dough offering continues throughout their generations. Arminian readers see a lasting habit of gratitude. Each generation acknowledges God’s provision. The practice is meant to endure as a family habit of thanksgiving.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see continuity in worship across generations. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. Teaching each generation to give the first portion keeps God’s provision in view across time.

Numbers 15:22

22“ ‘When you err, and don’t observe all these commandments which the LORD has spoken to Moses—

Arminian

If Israel errs and fails to keep the commandments. Arminian readers see God anticipating failure and providing for it. The law is realistic about human weakness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see grace provided for sins of ignorance. The word err points to unintentional failure. Both traditions share this reading. The law expects that a people who love God will still sometimes fail through error.

Numbers 15:23

23even all that the LORD has commanded you by Moses, from the day that the LORD gave commandment and onward throughout your generations—

Arminian

The scope covers all that the LORD commanded through Moses. Arminian readers see the seriousness of every command. Unintentional failure still needs atonement. Every command matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the comprehensive authority of God’s word. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. Even unknown failures against God’s commands are treated seriously enough to require atonement.

Numbers 15:24

24then it shall be, if it was done unwittingly, without the knowledge of the congregation, that all the congregation shall offer one young bull for a burnt offering, for a pleasant aroma to the LORD, with its meal offering and its drink offering, according to the ordinance, and one male goat for a sin offering.

Arminian

If the congregation sins unwittingly, it offers a bull and a male goat. Arminian readers see corporate responsibility for corporate failure. God provides a way back for the whole community.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see atonement provided for the covenant people. Both traditions share this reading of communal sin and remedy. A whole community can fail together, and God provides a corporate remedy through burnt offering and sin offering.

Numbers 15:25

25The priest shall make atonement for all the congregation of the children of Israel, and they shall be forgiven; for it was an error, and they have brought their offering, an offering made by fire to the LORD, and their sin offering before the LORD, for their error.

Arminian

The priest makes atonement, and the congregation is forgiven. Arminian readers see forgiveness truly granted through God’s appointed means. Grace meets real guilt. The verse ends with assurance of forgiveness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see substitutionary atonement prefigured. Hebrews 9 shows Christ as the final sacrifice. Both traditions share this reading. Forgiveness is granted because God has appointed the offering, not because the error was trivial.

Numbers 15:26

26All the congregation of the children of Israel shall be forgiven, as well as the stranger who lives as a foreigner among them; for with regard to all the people, it was done unwittingly.

Arminian

The whole congregation and the foreigner among them are forgiven. Arminian readers see forgiveness extending to all, including outsiders. No one is excluded from atonement.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the stranger included in the covenant provision. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. The verse explicitly includes the resident foreigner within the scope of corporate forgiveness.

Numbers 15:27

27“ ‘If a person sins unwittingly, then he shall offer a female goat a year old for a sin offering.

Arminian

An individual who sins unwittingly offers a female goat. Arminian readers see personal responsibility and personal forgiveness. God provides for individual failure too. Individuals are not lost in the crowd.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see atonement offered for each person. Both traditions share this reading. Each person who errs has a specific offering through which forgiveness is received, showing God’s care for individuals.

Numbers 15:28

28The priest shall make atonement for the soul who errs when he sins unwittingly before the LORD. He shall make atonement for him; and he shall be forgiven.

Arminian

The priest makes atonement, and the person is forgiven. Arminian readers see assurance of forgiveness for sins of weakness. God does not leave the penitent uncertain.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the priest’s mediation pointing to Christ. Hebrews 5:2 describes a high priest who can deal gently with the ignorant. Both traditions share this reading.

Numbers 15:29

29You shall have one law for him who does anything unwittingly, for him who is native-born among the children of Israel, and for the stranger who lives as a foreigner among them.

Arminian

One law applies to native and foreigner for unintentional sin. Arminian readers see equal access to forgiveness. Grace is not limited by birth. The same remedy is open to all.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see consistency in God’s provision. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. Equal provision for native-born and foreigner shows that grace in Israel was not restricted by ancestry.

Numbers 15:30

30“ ‘But the soul who does anything with a high hand, whether he is native-born or a foreigner, blasphemes the LORD. That soul shall be cut off from among his people.

Arminian

One who sins with a high hand blasphemes the LORD and is cut off. Arminian readers see defiant rebellion as distinct from weakness. Many link this to New Testament warnings such as Hebrews 10:26–29 and hold that a believer can turn to such defiance and be lost.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see deliberate, defiant rejection of God’s word. Many hold that such persistent defiance reveals a heart never truly renewed, while true believers, though they may sin grievously, are restored. Reformed writers differ on how closely to link this verse with apostasy.

Numbers 15:31

31Because he has despised the LORD’s word, and has broken his commandment, that soul shall be utterly cut off. His iniquity shall be on him.’ ”

Arminian

Because he despised the LORD’s word, he is utterly cut off. Arminian readers see contempt for God’s word as the root of high-handed sin. The guilt rests fully on the sinner.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see despising God’s word as the essence of the offense. Both traditions affirm the seriousness of this sin; the law provides no sacrifice for defiant rebellion.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
