Passage

Numbers 14

Verses 39–45 · WEB

When Moses reports God’s words, the people mourn and then decide to go up and take the land after all. Moses warns that the LORD is not with them and that they will be defeated. They go up anyway without the ark or Moses, and the Amalekites and Canaanites drive them back to Hormah.

Numbers 14:39

39Moses told these words to all the children of Israel, and the people mourned greatly.

Arminian

The people mourn greatly when they hear the sentence. Grief over consequences is not the same as trust in God; the next verses will show them trying to reverse the sentence by a battle God has now forbidden.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same distinction between sorrow over punishment and genuine repentance (compare 2 Corinthians 7:10). This is shared ground.

Numbers 14:40

40They rose up early in the morning and went up to the top of the mountain, saying, “Behold, we are here, and will go up to the place which the LORD has promised; for we have sinned.”

Arminian

They rise early to go up, admitting “we have sinned.” But they now attempt what God has just forbidden. Their confession does not lead to obedience to God’s present word.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a confession that falls short of submission. Both traditions see presumption replacing unbelief, which is still self-direction rather than faith.

Numbers 14:41

41Moses said, “Why now do you disobey the commandment of the LORD, since it shall not prosper?

Arminian

Moses asks why they now disobey, since it will not succeed. Obedience means following God’s word as it is given now, not as it was yesterday.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The same act, going up, is faith on one day and rebellion the next, depending on God’s command. This is shared ground.

Numbers 14:42

42Don’t go up, for the LORD isn’t among you; that way you won’t be struck down before your enemies.

Arminian

Moses warns that the LORD is not among them. Without his presence, they will be struck down. The presence Joshua and Caleb had relied on (verse 9) is now withdrawn from this venture.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same point: success depends on God’s presence, not human resolve. “Don’t go up, for the LORD isn’t among you” is a warning, not a dare; to go without him is to be struck down before their enemies.

This is shared ground.

Numbers 14:43

43For there the Amalekite and the Canaanite are before you, and you will fall by the sword because you turned back from following the LORD; therefore the LORD will not be with you.”

Arminian

Because they turned back from following the LORD, he will not be with them. The warning recalls their own words and choices.

Reformed

Reformed readers read this as the just consequence of rebellion. The Amalekite and the Canaanite are already ahead of them, and they will fall by the sword “because you turned back from following the LORD; therefore the LORD will not be with you.”

This is shared ground.

Numbers 14:44

44But they presumed to go up to the top of the mountain. Nevertheless, the ark of the LORD’s covenant and Moses didn’t depart out of the camp.

Arminian

They presume to go up anyway, but the ark of the covenant and Moses stay in the camp. Without the ark, the sign of God’s presence, the attempt is self-directed.

Reformed

Reformed readers also highlight the absence of the ark. Both traditions see presumption as trying to claim God’s promise on one’s own terms.

Numbers 14:45

45Then the Amalekites came down, and the Canaanites who lived in that mountain, and struck them and beat them down even to Hormah.

Arminian

The Amalekites and Canaanites strike them down as far as Hormah. The defeat confirms Moses’ warning and ends the episode in tragedy.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The chapter closes as a warning that both unbelief and presumption fail. The Amalekites and the Canaanites who lived in that mountain strike them and beat them down even to Hormah, just as Moses had said.

This is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
