Passage

Numbers 29

Verses 12–28 · WEB

From the fifteenth day of the seventh month Israel kept a seven-day feast to the LORD (29:12), the feast Leviticus 23:33–43 calls the feast of booths, when Israel lived in temporary shelters and rejoiced before the LORD after the harvest. Numbers gives only its offerings. These are the largest of the year: thirteen bulls on the first day, then one fewer bull each day, with two rams, fourteen lambs and one male goat daily. This section covers the first five days. The pattern is highly repetitive, and the small changes from day to day are the point of the list. Over the seven days the bulls total seventy (13 + 12 + 11 + 10 + 9 + 8 + 7). The text does not explain the decreasing count or the total, and any symbolic reading of them is interpretation.

Numbers 29:12

12“ ‘On the fifteenth day of the seventh month you shall have a holy convocation. You shall do no regular work. You shall keep a feast to the LORD seven days.

Arminian

On the fifteenth day a seven-day feast begins with a holy convocation. Leviticus calls it the feast of booths and tells Israel to “rejoice before the LORD your God seven days” (Lev 23:40).

Arminian readers note that after the humbling of the tenth day comes a week of joy. Repentance and gladness belong together in Israel’s calendar.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the feast as kept “to the LORD.” Its joy is centered on God, who brought Israel out of Egypt and gave the harvest (Lev 23:39–43).

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:13

13You shall offer a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD: thirteen young bulls, two rams, fourteen male lambs a year old, all without defect;

Arminian

The first day needs thirteen young bulls, two rams and fourteen lambs, the largest single-day offering in the calendar.

Arminian readers see the abundance as fitting thankfulness for an abundant harvest.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that God’s worship is lavish here, and that the scale is set by him, not by the worshipers’ mood.

Shared ground: the size of the offering matches the joy of the feast.

Numbers 29:14

14and their meal offering, fine flour mixed with oil: three tenths for every bull of the thirteen bulls, two tenths for each ram of the two rams,

Arminian

The meal offering is reckoned per animal: three tenths for each of the thirteen bulls and two tenths for each of the two rams. The flour rises with the number of animals.

Arminian readers note that larger offerings require larger gifts from the harvest, and the people respond in proportion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the ordinance applied consistently. The same rule that governs one bull governs thirteen.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:15

15and one tenth for every lamb of the fourteen lambs;

Arminian

Each of the fourteen lambs, twice the usual seven, receives its tenth of flour.

Arminian readers see the doubled number of lambs as part of the feast’s generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same exactness in the larger numbers as in the smaller. Fourteen lambs mean fourteen portions, each counted, none left to estimate.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:16

16and one male goat for a sin offering, in addition to the continual burnt offering, its meal offering, and its drink offering.

Arminian

Even at the most joyful feast, a male goat is offered for sin, and the continual offering goes on.

Arminian readers see that joy before God never forgets the need for forgiveness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that festive worship still rests on atonement and on the daily offering that never stops.

Shared ground: both traditions note that the sin offering appears every day of the feast.

Numbers 29:17

17“ ‘On the second day you shall offer twelve young bulls, two rams, and fourteen male lambs a year old without defect;

Arminian

On the second day there are twelve bulls, one fewer than on the first, while the rams and lambs stay the same.

Arminian readers notice that the feast keeps going day after day. Israel must keep coming back, not offer once and leave.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the decreasing number is fixed by God. The text does not explain it.

Shared ground: both traditions are cautious about building doctrine on the falling count.

Numbers 29:18

18and their meal offering and their drink offerings for the bulls, for the rams, and for the lambs, according to their number, after the ordinance;

Arminian

From here on, the meal and drink offerings are not counted out but given “according to their number, after the ordinance,” that is, by the rates already set (28:12–14; 29:14–15).

Arminian readers note that the people are trusted to apply what they have already been taught.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the phrase “after the ordinance” as summing up the chapter. Worship follows what God has prescribed.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:19

19and one male goat for a sin offering, in addition to the continual burnt offering, with its meal offering and their drink offerings.

Arminian

The second day again includes one male goat for a sin offering, alongside the continual offering.

Arminian readers see that God’s provision for sin is available each day, not just once at the start of the feast.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that the repeated sin offering points to the limits of these sacrifices, which had to be offered “year by year” (Heb 10:1).

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:20

20“ ‘On the third day: eleven bulls, two rams, fourteen male lambs a year old without defect;

Arminian

The third day has eleven bulls, with the same two rams and fourteen lambs.

Arminian readers see faithfulness in keeping the third day as carefully as the first, when the novelty of the feast has faded.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the steady pattern as part of God’s ordered worship. The count changes only as he directs.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:21

21and their meal offering and their drink offerings for the bulls, for the rams, and for the lambs, according to their number, after the ordinance;

Arminian

The third day’s meal and drink offerings follow the same rule as the second.

Arminian readers see that the people’s obedience is tested in repetition, not only in novelty.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the formula keeps the whole feast tied to the ordinance given once.

Shared ground: repetition does not make the offering routine in God’s sight.

Numbers 29:22

22and one male goat for a sin offering, in addition to the continual burnt offering, and its meal offering, and its drink offering.

Arminian

Again one male goat is offered for sin, with the continual offering.

Arminian readers see the people’s continuing need of mercy even while they rejoice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the continual offering as the steady background of every feast day. The sin offering is added to it, not put in its place.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:23

23“ ‘On the fourth day ten bulls, two rams, fourteen male lambs a year old without defect;

Arminian

The fourth day, the middle of the seven, has ten bulls.

Arminian readers note that halfway through the feast the people are still called to bring generous offerings.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the text makes no comment on the middle day. It simply continues the pattern God set.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:24

24their meal offering and their drink offerings for the bulls, for the rams, and for the lambs, according to their number, after the ordinance;

Arminian

The fourth day’s meal and drink offerings are reckoned by the same rule as the days before.

Arminian readers see the same faithful giving continue on the fourth day.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the meal and drink offerings measured by the same ordinance throughout the feast, so the fourth day is not left to improvisation.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:25

25and one male goat for a sin offering; in addition to the continual burnt offering, its meal offering, and its drink offering.

Arminian

The fourth day’s sin offering and the continual offering continue unchanged.

Arminian readers see grace offered again each day.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that no day of the feast passes without atonement. The fourth day’s sin offering stands beside the daily offering as before.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:26

26“ ‘On the fifth day: nine bulls, two rams, fourteen male lambs a year old without defect;

Arminian

The fifth day has nine bulls. The total so far is fifty-five bulls.

Arminian readers see the cost of the feast to the community, given willingly to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the scale of these offerings as showing how much the old covenant’s worship involved, all of it a shadow of the things to come (Col 2:16–17).

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:27

27and their meal offering and their drink offerings for the bulls, for the rams, and for the lambs, according to their number, after the ordinance,

Arminian

The fifth day’s meal and drink offerings follow the same rule.

Arminian readers note that the people do not tire of offering what God has asked.

Reformed

Reformed readers see each day’s portion matched to each day’s animals “according to their number,” so the fifth day follows the same rates as the days before.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:28

28and one male goat for a sin offering, in addition to the continual burnt offering, and its meal offering, and its drink offering.

Arminian

The fifth day ends with the same male goat for a sin offering and the continual offering.

Arminian readers see that the people go on needing, and receiving, God’s mercy day after day.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that the daily sacrifice and the feast sacrifice always stand side by side.

Shared ground: the section ends as each day has ended, with atonement and the continual offering.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
