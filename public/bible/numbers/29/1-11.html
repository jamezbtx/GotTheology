Passage

Numbers 29

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Numbers 28–29 sets out the public offerings for Israel’s calendar, day by day, week by week, month by month and feast by feast. Chapter 29 covers the seventh month, the most crowded month of the sacred year. These verses give the offerings for two of its days: the first day, “a day of blowing of trumpets” (29:1; compare Lev 23:23–25), and the tenth day, when Israel was to “afflict your souls” (29:7), the day of atonement described in Leviticus 16 and Leviticus 23:26–32. Both lists are offered “in addition to” the daily and monthly offerings already commanded (29:6, 11; 28:3–15). The chapter mostly specifies what to bring; it explains little of the meaning of each day, which is given in Leviticus.

Numbers 29:1

1“ ‘In the seventh month, on the first day of the month, you shall have a holy convocation; you shall do no regular work. It is a day of blowing of trumpets to you.

Arminian

The seventh month opens with a holy convocation, a rest from regular work, and “a day of blowing of trumpets.” Numbers does not explain the trumpets here. Elsewhere trumpets are blown over the offerings at feasts and new moons “for a memorial before your God” (10:10).

Arminian readers see the call as an invitation: Israel stops its own work to come before God in answer to his summons.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God himself sets the day, its rest and its sign. Israel does not invent its holy days but receives them.

Shared ground: both traditions read the trumpets as a public call to remember the LORD and gather before him.

Numbers 29:2

2You shall offer a burnt offering for a pleasant aroma to the LORD: one young bull, one ram, seven male lambs a year old without defect;

Arminian

The burnt offering is the same as on the new moon (28:11), except that one bull is offered instead of two. All the animals must be “without defect.”

Arminian readers see that God asks for Israel’s best, given wholly to him, as a fitting response to his goodness.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the offering is “to the LORD,” for his pleasure, a “pleasant aroma.” Worship is directed first to God, not to the worshiper.

Shared ground: both traditions read the unblemished animals as pointing to the spotless offering of Christ.

Numbers 29:3

3and their meal offering, fine flour mixed with oil: three tenths for the bull, two tenths for the ram,

Arminian

The meal offering follows the scale given earlier (28:12): three tenths of an ephah of fine flour for a bull, two tenths for a ram. The larger animal comes with the larger grain offering.

Arminian readers note that the produce of the field is brought alongside the flock, so the whole of life is offered to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note how exactly the measures are given. The worshiper does not have to guess what pleases God.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:4

4and one tenth for every lamb of the seven lambs;

Arminian

Each of the seven lambs has its own tenth of flour. No animal is offered without its grain offering.

Arminian readers see the care of the list as a reminder that faithfulness covers small duties as well as large ones.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same thoroughness. The ordinance is followed to the last lamb.

Shared ground: the detail shows that every part of Israel’s worship belongs to God.

Numbers 29:5

5and one male goat for a sin offering, to make atonement for you;

Arminian

Alongside the burnt offering, one male goat is offered as a sin offering “to make atonement for you.” Even a festive day includes provision for sin.

Arminian readers stress that this atonement is provided for the whole people, “for you,” as a gift of God’s grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that Israel cannot approach a holy God, even on a day of joy, apart from atonement.

Shared ground: both traditions see the repeated sin offerings as preparing for the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ (Heb 10:1–4).

Numbers 29:6

6in addition to the burnt offering of the new moon with its meal offering, and the continual burnt offering with its meal offering, and their drink offerings, according to their ordinance, for a pleasant aroma, an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

The first day of the seventh month is also a new moon. So its offerings are added to the new moon offering and the daily offering, not substituted for them. Nothing commanded earlier is dropped.

Arminian readers see a pattern of worship building up, layer on layer, over the year.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the phrase “according to their ordinance” as typical of the chapter. God’s own appointments fix the order of worship.

Shared ground: the daily offering continues underneath every special day.

Numbers 29:7

7“ ‘On the tenth day of this seventh month you shall have a holy convocation. You shall afflict your souls. You shall do no kind of work;

Arminian

The tenth day is a holy convocation on which Israel must “afflict your souls” and do no work at all. The same phrase is used for the day of atonement in Leviticus 16:29–31. Elsewhere afflicting the soul is linked with fasting (Ps 35:13; Isa 58:3).

Arminian readers see the self-denial as heartfelt repentance, a response the people are called to make.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this day of humbling is commanded, not left to personal feeling. The whole people humbles itself before God together.

Shared ground: both traditions see that atonement and repentance go together.

Numbers 29:8

8but you shall offer a burnt offering to the LORD for a pleasant aroma: one young bull, one ram, seven male lambs a year old, all without defect;

Arminian

The burnt offering matches that of the first day (29:2): one young bull, one ram and seven lambs. The day of humbling also includes a burnt offering given wholly to God.

Arminian readers see that repentance is not only sorrow but also renewed devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that even on the day of atonement, worship remains an offering “to the LORD for a pleasant aroma.”

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:9

9and their meal offering, fine flour mixed with oil: three tenths for the bull, two tenths for the one ram,

Arminian

The meal offering matches 29:3. The text speaks of “the one ram,” stressing that only one is offered.

Arminian readers again see the produce of daily work brought into worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same measured, prescribed portions. Worship on this day follows God’s pattern, not human invention.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:10

10one tenth for every lamb of the seven lambs;

Arminian

One tenth of flour goes with each of the seven lambs, as in 29:4.

Arminian readers note that the repetition from the first day to the tenth trains Israel in steady obedience through the month.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the repeated pattern keeps the people’s focus on what God has commanded. The tenth day’s meal offering matches the first day’s, lamb by lamb.

Shared ground.

Numbers 29:11

11one male goat for a sin offering, in addition to the sin offering of atonement, and the continual burnt offering, and its meal offering, and their drink offerings.

Arminian

One male goat is offered as a sin offering, “in addition to the sin offering of atonement,” the special offering of the day described in Leviticus 16. This list adds to that ritual rather than replacing it.

Arminian readers see two provisions for sin on one day as a sign of how fully God provides for his people’s cleansing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the day of atonement a clear picture of substitution, which Hebrews applies to Christ’s high-priestly work (Heb 9:7, 11–12).

Shared ground: both traditions read this day as foreshadowing Christ’s once-for-all atonement. They differ elsewhere on the scope of that atonement, but this verse does not raise the question.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
