Passage

Numbers 16

Verses 1–15 · WEB

Korah, a Levite, joins Reubenites Dathan, Abiram, and On, along with 250 leaders, to challenge Moses and Aaron. They claim the whole congregation is holy and accuse the leaders of exalting themselves. Moses proposes a test by incense before the LORD and exposes their desire for the priesthood, while Dathan and Abiram refuse to come. Both traditions read the passage as a warning against rebellion against God’s appointed order.

Numbers 16:1

1Now Korah, the son of Izhar, the son of Kohath, the son of Levi, with Dathan and Abiram, the sons of Eliab, and On, the son of Peleth, sons of Reuben, took some men.

Arminian

Korah, a Levite, joins Dathan, Abiram, and On of Reuben. Arminian readers see rebellion uniting people with different grievances. The coalition includes religious and tribal ambitions.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a mixed alliance against God’s order. Jude 11 refers to Korah’s rebellion as a warning. Both traditions share this reading. The coalition joins religious and tribal grievances.

Numbers 16:2

2They rose up before Moses, with some of the children of Israel, two hundred fifty princes of the congregation, called to the assembly, men of renown.

Arminian

They rise against Moses with 250 leaders of renown. Arminian readers see influential people misusing their standing. Prominence does not guarantee faithfulness. Many respected leaders join the challenge.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see rebellion among leaders. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. Even widely respected leaders can be drawn into opposing God’s appointed order when ambition and resentment take hold.

Numbers 16:3

3They assembled themselves together against Moses and against Aaron, and said to them, “You take too much on yourself, since all the congregation are holy, everyone of them, and the LORD is among them! Why do you lift yourselves up above the LORD’s assembly?”

Arminian

They claim all the congregation is holy and accuse Moses and Aaron of self-exaltation. Arminian readers see a half-truth twisted into rebellion. Israel was called holy, but that did not abolish God’s appointed roles.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a true doctrine misused to reject God’s order. Both traditions read the verse as a warning against using spiritual language to cover ambition.

Numbers 16:4

4When Moses heard it, he fell on his face.

Arminian

Moses falls on his face. Arminian readers see humility and perhaps intercession in his response. He does not answer with anger at first. His first move is toward God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Moses turning to God. Both traditions see his posture as fitting before a crisis. Falling on his face expresses humility and dependence rather than a defensive grasp for power.

Numbers 16:5

5He said to Korah and to all his company, “In the morning, the LORD will show who are his, and who is holy, and will cause him to come near to him. Even him whom he shall choose, he will cause to come near to him.

Arminian

Moses says the LORD will show who is his and who is holy. Arminian readers see God, not human claims, deciding who serves. 2 Timothy 2:19 echoes the phrase that the Lord knows those who are his.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s choice determining who may draw near. The verse is mostly about priestly service, not individual salvation. Both traditions share this reading.

Numbers 16:6

6Do this: have Korah and all his company take censers,

Arminian

Korah and his company are to take censers. Arminian readers see a test proposed before God. The outcome will be decided by the LORD. The decision is left with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s judgment awaited. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. The censer test places the dispute before God rather than settling it by force or argument.

Numbers 16:7

7put fire in them, and put incense on them before the LORD tomorrow. It shall be that the man whom the LORD chooses, he shall be holy. You have gone too far, you sons of Levi!”

Arminian

They will offer incense, and the one the LORD chooses is holy. Moses says they have gone too far. Arminian readers see God’s choice as decisive.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see divine election to office. Both traditions agree this concerns priestly service. The test concerns who may draw near as priest, and God alone will decide.

Numbers 16:8

8Moses said to Korah, “Hear now, you sons of Levi!

Arminian

Moses addresses Korah and the Levites. Arminian readers see Moses turning to the heart of the problem. Korah’s own tribe is involved. He speaks to Korah’s fellow Levites directly.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Moses confronting the instigators. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. Korah belonged to the Levites already set apart for tabernacle service, which makes his challenge especially pointed.

Numbers 16:9

9Is it a small thing to you that the God of Israel has separated you from the congregation of Israel, to bring you near to himself, to do the service of the LORD’s tabernacle, and to stand before the congregation to minister to them;

Arminian

Is it small that God separated you to serve the tabernacle? Arminian readers see ingratitude for privileges already given. Korah despises his own calling. Their privilege is already great.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see discontent with God’s appointed role. Both traditions see a warning against grasping for more. Moses reminds them that serving at the tabernacle is itself an honor from God that they now treat lightly.

Numbers 16:10

10and that he has brought you near, and all your brothers the sons of Levi with you? Do you seek the priesthood also?

Arminian

Do you seek the priesthood also? Arminian readers see ambition exposed. Korah wants what God has not given him. Envy seeks a role God has assigned to another.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see envy at the root of rebellion. Both traditions share this reading. The question exposes the real aim behind the claim that all the congregation is holy.

Numbers 16:11

11Therefore you and all your company have gathered together against the LORD! What is Aaron that you complain against him?”

Arminian

Their gathering is really against the LORD. Arminian readers see rebellion against God’s servants as rebellion against God. Aaron himself is not the issue. Complaint against Aaron is complaint against God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s authority behind his appointed leaders. Both traditions share this reading. Opposing those whom God has appointed is, in this setting, opposing the LORD who appointed them.

Numbers 16:12

12Moses sent to call Dathan and Abiram, the sons of Eliab; and they said, “We won’t come up!

Arminian

Dathan and Abiram refuse to come. Arminian readers see open defiance. They reject even the opportunity for dialogue. Their refusal is repeated in verse 14.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see hardened refusal. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. The Reubenites refuse even to meet Moses, showing a settled hostility rather than a request for justice.

Numbers 16:13

13Is it a small thing that you have brought us up out of a land flowing with milk and honey, to kill us in the wilderness, but you must also make yourself a prince over us?

Arminian

They call Egypt a land of milk and honey and accuse Moses of tyranny. Arminian readers see memory distorted by bitterness. The promise language is turned upside down.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see ingratitude rewriting history. Both traditions see a warning against nostalgia for bondage. Calling Egypt a land of milk and honey reverses God’s own promise language for Canaan.

Numbers 16:14

14Moreover you haven’t brought us into a land flowing with milk and honey, nor given us inheritance of fields and vineyards. Will you put out the eyes of these men? We won’t come up.”

Arminian

They complain that Moses has not brought them into the land. Arminian readers see blame shifted onto Moses for Israel’s own failure in chapter 14. They refuse responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see unbelief blaming God’s servant. The phrase about eyes likely suggests deception. Both traditions share this reading. They blame Moses for a delay that came from the people’s refusal to enter in chapter 14.

Numbers 16:15

15Moses was very angry, and said to the LORD, “Don’t respect their offering. I have not taken one donkey from them, neither have I hurt one of them.”

Arminian

Moses is angry and asks God not to accept their offering, insisting on his integrity. Arminian readers see righteous indignation. Moses has not abused his position.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a leader appealing to God’s judgment. Samuel makes a similar claim in 1 Samuel 12:3. Both traditions share this reading. Moses’ prayer is honest.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
