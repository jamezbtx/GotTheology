Passage

Numbers 16

Verses 36–50 · WEB

In the WEB and most English Bibles these verses are 16:36–50; in Hebrew Bibles they are 17:1–15. The rebels’ censers become a bronze covering for the altar as a warning. The next day the congregation blames Moses and Aaron for the deaths, a plague breaks out, and Aaron runs with incense to make atonement, standing between the dead and the living. Christians in both traditions share the reading of Aaron here as a picture of priestly intercession fulfilled in Christ.

Numbers 16:36

36The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses. In Hebrew Bibles this begins chapter 17. Arminian readers see the aftermath addressed by God. The story continues. The numbering does not affect the meaning.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the numbering difference without theological significance. Both traditions read the verse as a transition. Verse references in this unit follow the WEB numbering.

Numbers 16:37

37“Speak to Eleazar the son of Aaron the priest, that he take up the censers out of the burning, and scatter the fire away from the camp; for they are holy,

Arminian

Eleazar takes up the censers, for they are holy. Arminian readers see objects offered to God retaining holiness despite the sin. The fire is scattered.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see holiness attached to what was presented before God. Both traditions share this reading. The text gives Eleazar this task without explaining why, and both traditions leave it there.

Numbers 16:38

38even the censers of those who sinned against their own lives. Let them be beaten into plates for a covering of the altar, for they offered them before the LORD. Therefore they are holy. They shall be a sign to the children of Israel.”

Arminian

The censers are beaten into plates for the altar as a sign. Arminian readers see judgment turned into a lasting warning. The rebels sinned against their own lives.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a memorial teaching future generations. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. The censers become a visible warning at the altar for every generation.

Numbers 16:39

39Eleazar the priest took the bronze censers which those who were burned had offered; and they beat them out for a covering of the altar,

Arminian

Eleazar beats out the bronze censers for the altar covering. Arminian readers see obedience to God’s instruction. The warning becomes part of worship. Bronze censers become part of the altar.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the sign built into the place of sacrifice. Both traditions share this reading. The very instruments of rebellion now protect the people by reminding them of the cost.

Numbers 16:40

40to be a memorial to the children of Israel, to the end that no stranger who isn’t of the offspring of Aaron, would come near to burn incense before the LORD, that he not be as Korah and as his company; as the LORD spoke to him by Moses.

Arminian

The covering reminds Israel that only Aaron’s descendants may offer incense. Arminian readers see God’s appointed priesthood protected. Korah’s example is remembered. The memorial guards the priesthood.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the priesthood guarded. Christians in both traditions see Christ as the great high priest. This is shared ground. Hebrews 5:4 echoes the principle that no one takes this honor for himself.

Numbers 16:41

41But on the next day all the congregation of the children of Israel complained against Moses and against Aaron, saying, “You have killed the LORD’s people!”

Arminian

The next day the congregation accuses Moses and Aaron of killing the LORD’s people. Arminian readers see rebellion continuing despite judgment. The people misread what happened.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see hardened hearts blaming God’s servants. Both traditions share this reading. The people interpret God’s judgment as Moses’ crime. Blaming God’s servants hides their own guilt.

Numbers 16:42

42When the congregation was assembled against Moses and against Aaron, they looked toward the Tent of Meeting. Behold, the cloud covered it, and the LORD’s glory appeared.

Arminian

The cloud covers the tent, and the LORD’s glory appears. Arminian readers see God intervening again. His presence confronts the grumbling crowd. Once again the people gather in protest.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see divine glory revealed in a crisis. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. The cloud and glory signal that God sees the grumbling and will act.

Numbers 16:43

43Moses and Aaron came to the front of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

Moses and Aaron go to the front of the tent. Arminian readers see them standing before God on behalf of the people. Leadership faces the crisis.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see mediators approaching God’s presence. Both traditions share this reading. They come to the place of God’s presence, where mediation can take place.

Numbers 16:44

44The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses. Arminian readers see judgment about to be pronounced. The situation is urgent. God speaks again in the crisis. Danger is near.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s word preceding his action. Both traditions read the verse as a transition. The repeated pattern of divine word and human intercession structures the chapter.

Numbers 16:45

45“Get away from among this congregation, that I may consume them in a moment!” They fell on their faces.

Arminian

God threatens to consume the congregation, and Moses and Aaron fall on their faces. Arminian readers see a repeat of verse 21. Intercession is needed again.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s wrath against persistent rebellion. Both traditions read the threat in light of what follows. Moses and Aaron again fall on their faces before God on behalf of the people.

Numbers 16:46

46Moses said to Aaron, “Take your censer, put fire from the altar in it, lay incense on it, carry it quickly to the congregation, and make atonement for them; for wrath has gone out from the LORD! The plague has begun.”

Arminian

Moses tells Aaron to take incense quickly and make atonement, for the plague has begun. Arminian readers see urgent mercy. Atonement is made through incense here.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see priestly mediation averting wrath. Both traditions share this reading. Moses acts quickly, sending Aaron with incense to make atonement before the plague spreads.

Numbers 16:47

47Aaron did as Moses said, and ran into the middle of the assembly. The plague had already begun among the people. He put on the incense, and made atonement for the people.

Arminian

Aaron runs into the assembly and makes atonement. Arminian readers see the priest moving toward danger to save others. Mercy acts swiftly. He goes where the danger is greatest.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a picture of priestly intercession. Both traditions share this reading. Aaron enters the midst of the dying to bring atonement, a striking image of costly mediation.

Numbers 16:48

48He stood between the dead and the living; and the plague was stayed.

Arminian

He stands between the dead and the living, and the plague stops. Arminian readers see a powerful image of intercession. Christians in both traditions see Christ, who intercedes for his people, as in Hebrews 7:25.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Aaron as a type of Christ the mediator. This typology is shared ground. Both traditions see the high priest saving those who otherwise would perish.

Numbers 16:49

49Now those who died by the plague were fourteen thousand seven hundred, in addition to those who died about the matter of Korah.

Arminian

Fourteen thousand seven hundred die, besides those who died with Korah. Arminian readers see the cost of rebellion. The number is recorded soberly. The loss is great.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the seriousness of sin. Both traditions read the verse soberly without speculation. The death toll shows the cost of the people’s rebellion, recorded without embellishment.

Numbers 16:50

50Aaron returned to Moses to the door of the Tent of Meeting, and the plague was stopped.

Arminian

Aaron returns to Moses, and the plague stops. Arminian readers see mediation completed. The crisis ends through God’s appointed priest. Aaron returns to the tent of meeting.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see atonement accomplishing its purpose. Both traditions share this reading. The plague ends through the priest God appointed, the very priesthood the rebels had challenged.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
