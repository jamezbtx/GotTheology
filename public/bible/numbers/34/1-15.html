Passage

Numbers 34

Verses 1–15 · WEB

The LORD defines the borders of Canaan for the tribes that will settle west of the Jordan: the south from the wilderness of Zin and Kadesh Barnea to the brook of Egypt, the west at the great sea, the north toward the entrance of Hamath, and the east down to the sea of Chinnereth, the Jordan, and the Salt Sea. Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh have already received land east of the Jordan (Numbers 32). Several boundary points are uncertain, and the northern Mount Hor is a different place from the Mount Hor where Aaron died. Both traditions read the passage as God marking out the gift he promised to Abraham (Genesis 15:18–21), so that the inheritance has real, defined shape.

Numbers 34:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses about the borders of the land. The promise now receives specific shape.

Arminian readers note that God provides clear guidance for the people’s life in the land, inviting faithful stewardship of what he gives.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God defines the gift himself. The borders are not negotiated by Israel but set by his word.

Both traditions read this as God’s faithfulness taking concrete form, so that the promise to Abraham becomes a real land with real boundaries.

Numbers 34:2

2“Command the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘When you come into the land of Canaan (this is the land that shall fall to you for an inheritance, even the land of Canaan according to its borders),

Arminian

When Israel enters Canaan, this is the land that will fall to them as an inheritance according to its borders. The word inheritance frames the whole chapter.

Arminian readers note that inheritance is received within a relationship. Israel must remain faithful to enjoy what God gives.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that inheritance is a gift grounded in God’s promise to the patriarchs (Genesis 15:18–21). Israel receives what God swore.

Both traditions see the land as a foretaste of the greater inheritance promised to God’s people in Christ (compare 1 Peter 1:4).

Numbers 34:3

3then your south quarter shall be from the wilderness of Zin along by the side of Edom, and your south border shall be from the end of the Salt Sea eastward.

Arminian

The southern border runs from the wilderness of Zin along Edom, beginning at the end of the Salt Sea. The Salt Sea is the Dead Sea.

Arminian readers note the careful definition of the land, which allows each tribe to know what it is responsible for.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God sets limits as well as gifts. The border along Edom honors his earlier word concerning Esau’s descendants (Deuteronomy 2:4–5).

Both traditions see wisdom in boundaries that preserve peace and order among peoples.

Numbers 34:4

4Your border shall turn about southward of the ascent of Akrabbim, and pass along to Zin; and it shall pass southward of Kadesh Barnea; and it shall go from there to Hazar Addar, and pass along to Azmon.

Arminian

The border turns south of the ascent of Akrabbim, passes to Zin and south of Kadesh Barnea, then to Hazar Addar and Azmon. Several of these points are uncertain.

Arminian readers note that Kadesh Barnea, the place of failure, now lies within the border of the promised land.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s purposes include the very place where Israel once refused his gift. His grace overcomes their failure.

Both traditions treat the boundary points cautiously and draw from them a theme of restoration.

Numbers 34:5

5The border shall turn about from Azmon to the brook of Egypt, and it shall end at the sea.

Arminian

The border turns from Azmon to the brook of Egypt and ends at the sea. The brook of Egypt is often identified with a wadi in northern Sinai, though this is debated.

Arminian readers note that the southern limit marks a clear separation from Egypt, the land of bondage.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s gift sets his people apart from the land of their slavery. The border is a sign of their redemption.

Both traditions read the boundary with caution about its exact location.

Numbers 34:6

6“ ‘For the western border, you shall have the great sea and its border. This shall be your west border.

Arminian

The western border is the great sea, the Mediterranean. The coastline forms a natural limit.

Arminian readers note that the land includes access to the sea, a sign of God’s generous provision. The gift was broad enough for trade, travel, and blessing to the nations.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Israel did not fully possess the coast in many periods, which shows that the borders describe God’s promise rather than a record of what they achieved.

Both traditions see the gap between promise and possession as a reminder that God’s gifts call for faithful response.

Numbers 34:7

7“ ‘This shall be your north border: from the great sea you shall mark out for yourselves Mount Hor.

Arminian

The northern border runs from the great sea to Mount Hor. This northern Mount Hor is generally understood to be a different place from the Mount Hor where Aaron died.

Arminian readers note that the same name can belong to different places, and that careful reading avoids confusion.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the precise location of this northern mountain is uncertain.

Both traditions treat the identification cautiously and see the northern border as part of God’s full description of the promised land.

Numbers 34:8

8From Mount Hor you shall mark out to the entrance of Hamath; and the border shall pass by Zedad.

Arminian

From Mount Hor the border runs to the entrance of Hamath and passes by Zedad. The entrance of Hamath is uncertain, though many link it with a pass or town in the Lebanon region.

Arminian readers note that the northern reach of the land was rarely possessed in Israel’s history.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the borders were most fully approached under David and Solomon (compare 1 Kings 8:65). The promise was real but its fulfillment gradual.

Both traditions see the gradual fulfillment as part of God’s patient purpose.

Numbers 34:9

9Then the border shall go to Ziphron, and it shall end at Hazar Enan. This shall be your north border.

Arminian

The border goes to Ziphron and ends at Hazar Enan. These places are unidentified.

Arminian readers note that the people would need to trust God for land they had never seen. Faith in this sense means acting on God’s word before seeing its fulfillment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s promise covered territory beyond Israel’s knowledge. His gift exceeded what they could imagine.

Both traditions take encouragement that God’s promises are larger than our present understanding (compare Ephesians 3:20).

Numbers 34:10

10“ ‘You shall mark out your east border from Hazar Enan to Shepham.

Arminian

The eastern border runs from Hazar Enan to Shepham. The location of Shepham is unknown.

Arminian readers note that each border marker calls for careful obedience in respecting the limits God set. Respecting limits is part of honoring the Giver.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s word defines even the obscure points. Nothing is left to human guesswork in his promise.

Both traditions treat the unknown sites with humility and focus on the theological shape of the passage.

Numbers 34:11

11The border shall go down from Shepham to Riblah, on the east side of Ain. The border shall go down, and shall reach to the side of the sea of Chinnereth eastward.

Arminian

The border runs from Shepham to Riblah east of Ain and down to the side of the sea of Chinnereth, the Sea of Galilee. The Galilee region is included.

Arminian readers note that this region would later become the setting of much of Jesus’ ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s promise to Israel prepared the stage for the coming of Christ, who would minister by the same sea.

Both traditions see continuity between the land promise and the gospel, while treating such links as reflection rather than the verse’s stated meaning.

Numbers 34:12

12The border shall go down to the Jordan, and end at the Salt Sea. This shall be your land according to its borders around it.’ ”

Arminian

The border goes down the Jordan and ends at the Salt Sea. This completes the boundary of the land west of the Jordan.

Arminian readers note the clarity of the gift: the people know the shape of what God gives.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the circuit of borders shows the completeness of God’s promise. He defines the whole land.

Both traditions see the Jordan as a boundary that Israel would cross by faith, a theme that later Scripture and Christian hymnody often draw on.

Numbers 34:13

13Moses commanded the children of Israel, saying, “This is the land which you shall inherit by lot, which the LORD has commanded to give to the nine tribes, and to the half-tribe;

Arminian

Moses tells Israel that this is the land they will inherit by lot, commanded by the LORD for the nine and a half tribes. The lot ensures fairness.

Arminian readers note that the method of allotment protects each family’s share and guards against abuse of power.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the lot places the outcome in God’s hands (compare Proverbs 16:33). The inheritance is assigned by his providence.

Both traditions agree that God cares for the fair distribution of his gifts among his people.

Numbers 34:14

14for the tribe of the children of Reuben according to their fathers’ houses, the tribe of the children of Gad according to their fathers’ houses, and the half-tribe of Manasseh have received their inheritance.

Arminian

Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh have already received their inheritance. The tribes east of the Jordan are accounted for.

Arminian readers note that these tribes had made a request and accepted conditions (Numbers 32), showing real negotiation within God’s covenant.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God accommodated their request while still holding them to their obligations. His providence includes his people’s choices.

Both traditions see God working through human requests while keeping his covenant purposes intact.

Numbers 34:15

15The two tribes and the half-tribe have received their inheritance beyond the Jordan at Jericho eastward, toward the sunrise.”

Arminian

The two and a half tribes received their land east of the Jordan, toward the sunrise. The eastern inheritance is confirmed.

Arminian readers note that these tribes would need to remain faithful to their brothers, as Joshua 22 later shows.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God gave land on both sides of the Jordan, so that the whole people shared in the inheritance.

Both traditions read the verse as completing the account of the land’s distribution among all twelve tribes.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
