Passage

Numbers 23

Verses 1–12 · WEB

After the events of chapter 22, Balak has brought Balaam to curse Israel. They build seven altars and offer a bull and a ram on each. The LORD puts a word in Balaam’s mouth, and the first parable blesses Israel instead: Balaam cannot curse whom God has not cursed; Israel is a people that dwells alone, too many to count; Balaam even asks to die the death of the righteous. Balak protests that he hired a curse and received a blessing. Balaam answers that he must take heed to speak what the LORD puts in his mouth. Both traditions read the unit as God turning a hired curse into a blessing for the people he has already named blessed. A real difference, where the notes mark it, concerns how to speak of God’s control of Balaam’s utterance, not whether the word itself is the LORD’s.

Numbers 23:1

1Balaam said to Balak, “Build here seven altars for me, and prepare here seven bulls and seven rams for me.”

Arminian

Balaam tells Balak to build seven altars and to prepare seven bulls and seven rams.

Arminian readers see the hired prophet arranging a full, formal offering before he will go to seek a word. The number and the victims are Balaam’s request, not yet a word from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same elaborate preparation. Seven altars and the paired animals give the scene the look of serious worship, even as the aim is a curse.

Both traditions describe the rite as Balaam’s and Balak’s doing in this verse, without forcing a settled symbolism onto the number seven.

Numbers 23:2

2Balak did as Balaam had spoken; and Balak and Balaam offered on every altar a bull and a ram.

Arminian

Balak does as Balaam has spoken; together they offer a bull and a ram on every altar.

Arminian readers see Balak’s willing participation. The king supplies what the prophet asks, hoping the offerings will secure a curse.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the complete performance of the rite. The offerings are real, yet they will not purchase the word Balak wants.

Both traditions take the verse as narrative setup and do not treat the offerings as a point of contrast.

Numbers 23:3

3Balaam said to Balak, “Stand by your burnt offering, and I will go. Perhaps the LORD will come to meet me. Whatever he shows me I will tell you.” He went to a bare height.

Arminian

Balaam tells Balak to stand by his burnt offering while he goes: perhaps the LORD will come to meet him, and he will tell Balak whatever he is shown. He goes to a bare height.

Arminian readers hear his “Perhaps the LORD will come to meet me.” Balaam does not control the meeting. He goes apart, and any word will have to be given.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note that the LORD’s coming is not at Balaam’s command. The prophet withdraws to a bare height to be met, if at all, by God.

Both traditions read the verse as leaving the encounter in God’s hands, not in the success of the altars.

Numbers 23:4

4God met Balaam, and he said to him, “I have prepared the seven altars, and I have offered up a bull and a ram on every altar.”

Arminian

God meets Balaam. Balaam reports that he has prepared the seven altars and offered a bull and a ram on every altar.

Arminian readers see God actually meeting him, as Balaam had hoped, while Balaam’s first words point to the offerings he has arranged, as if those rites were his claim to be heard.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the meeting is God’s act. Balaam’s report of the altars does not obligate the LORD to say what Balak paid to hear.

Both traditions note the meeting and the report without claiming a single agreed reading of Balaam’s motive in mentioning the offerings.

Numbers 23:5

5The LORD put a word in Balaam’s mouth, and said, “Return to Balak, and thus you shall speak.”

Arminian

The LORD puts a word in Balaam’s mouth and tells him to return to Balak and speak thus.

Arminian readers take this as a special act: God gives the very words Balaam must now speak. Balaam remains a responsible agent, but this utterance is constrained so that it is the LORD’s word, not Balak’s curse. They do not treat the episode as a proof that God determines every human speech.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a word placed in Balaam’s mouth, and they typically read it as God’s determining control of this speech, while still holding Balaam accountable for his own course.

Both traditions agree that the word about to be spoken is the LORD’s, and that Balaam is not innocent merely because he is used to bless.

Key contrast Both affirm that Balaam must speak the word the LORD gives, and that he remains morally accountable. Arminian readers typically see God overriding or constraining this prophetic utterance as a special act, while Balaam’s will remains his own; they do not take the scene as implying meticulous determination of every human word. Reformed readers typically see God’s determining control of Balaam’s words in this event, while Balaam remains culpable.

Numbers 23:6

6He returned to him, and behold, he was standing by his burnt offering, he, and all the princes of Moab.

Arminian

Balaam returns and finds Balak standing by his burnt offering, with all the princes of Moab.

Arminian readers see the court assembled for the word they hope will be a curse. The burnt offering is still there, and the princes are witnesses.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same public setting. What is about to be spoken will be heard by the king and his princes, not whispered in private.

Both traditions read the verse as staging for the first parable.

Numbers 23:7

7He took up his parable, and said, “From Aram has Balak brought me, the king of Moab from the mountains of the East. Come, curse Jacob for me. Come, defy Israel.

Arminian

Balaam takes up his parable: Balak, king of Moab, has brought him from Aram, from the mountains of the East, with the charge, “Come, curse Jacob for me. Come, defy Israel.”

Arminian readers hear the commission stated in Balak’s own terms. The hired task is a curse and a defiance of Israel, named also as Jacob.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise let the opening of the parable name the human errand, so that the refusal that follows is unmistakably a refusal of that errand.

Both traditions take the quoted charge as Balak’s purpose, which God will not grant.

Numbers 23:8

8How shall I curse whom God has not cursed? How shall I defy whom the LORD has not defied?

Arminian

The refusal is put as two questions: “How shall I curse whom God has not cursed? How shall I defy whom the LORD has not defied?”

Arminian readers hear Balaam stopped at the outset. He cannot originate a curse God has not pronounced. The prophet’s mouth is not for sale against that fact.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the issue is God’s already settled stance: God has not cursed, and the LORD has not defied, this people.

Both traditions see the questions as the theological center of the first parable, in line with God’s earlier word that the people are blessed (Numbers 22:12).

Numbers 23:9

9For from the top of the rocks I see him. From the hills I see him. Behold, it is a people that dwells alone, and shall not be listed among the nations.

Arminian

From the rocks and hills Balaam sees Israel: “Behold, it is a people that dwells alone, and shall not be listed among the nations.”

Arminian readers see a people set apart, distinct from the nations Balak represents. Their isolation is not a weakness here, but a mark of the life God has given them.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear a people who will not be counted as just another nation. Their identity is God-given, visible even to a seer hired to undo them.

Both traditions read the line as Israel’s distinctness under God, without pressing a single later political meaning as settled.

Numbers 23:10

10Who can count the dust of Jacob, or count the fourth part of Israel? Let me die the death of the righteous! Let my last end be like his!”

Arminian

Balaam asks who can count the dust of Jacob, or the fourth part of Israel, and then says, “Let me die the death of the righteous! Let my last end be like his!”

Arminian readers hear both the promise of uncountable number and a sudden longing: Balaam wants the end of the righteous, even as he stands among those who hired him to curse them.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the dust image as a sign of promised multitude, and the wish for a righteous death as a true word in his mouth, whatever his later path will be.

Both traditions take the wish seriously as part of this given oracle, without treating it as a settled verdict on Balaam’s final state, which later texts will address in their own place.

Numbers 23:11

11Balak said to Balaam, “What have you done to me? I took you to curse my enemies, and behold, you have blessed them altogether.”

Arminian

Balak protests: “What have you done to me? I took you to curse my enemies, and behold, you have blessed them altogether.”

Arminian readers hear the king’s shock. The bargain has failed: the man brought to curse has blessed Israel completely.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress Balak’s own summary. Even the one who paid for a curse must admit that the word was a thorough blessing.

Both traditions let Balak’s complaint confirm what the parable has done.

Numbers 23:12

12He answered and said, “Must I not take heed to speak that which the LORD puts in my mouth?”

Arminian

Balaam answers, “Must I not take heed to speak that which the LORD puts in my mouth?”

Arminian readers hear Balaam acknowledging the constraint already described in verse 5. He must attend to the given word; he may not simply deliver what Balak bought. His will is still his own as he answers, but the utterance is not his to invent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear a confession that the LORD’s word governs the speech. Balaam must take heed; the word in his mouth is not from the king.

Both traditions read the answer as consistent with verse 5, without repeating that verse’s contrast as a new dispute.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
