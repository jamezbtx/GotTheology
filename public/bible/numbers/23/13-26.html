Passage

Numbers 23

Verses 13–26 · WEB

Balak tries a second site, the field of Zophim on the top of Pisgah, hoping that a partial view of Israel will make a curse possible. The same seven altars and offerings are repeated. The LORD again puts a word in Balaam’s mouth. The second parable tells Balak that God is not a man, that he should lie or repent: he has blessed, and Balaam cannot reverse it. The LORD sees no iniquity in Jacob as a ground of curse; God brought them out of Egypt; there is no enchantment or divination against them; the people rises like a lion. Balak, now desperate, tells Balaam neither to curse nor to bless; Balaam answers that he must do all that the LORD speaks. Both traditions take 23:19 as shared ground on God’s truthfulness and immutability, including the classical Arminian affirmation of immutability and exhaustive foreknowledge, and they do not portray God as revising his purpose as a human being would.

Numbers 23:13

13Balak said to him, “Please come with me to another place, where you may see them. You shall see just part of them, and shall not see them all. Curse them from there for me.”

Arminian

Balak asks Balaam to come to another place, where he will see only part of the people, and to curse them from there.

Arminian readers see a change of tactic: if the whole camp cannot be cursed, perhaps a partial view will serve. The king still wants the curse he paid for.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same stratagem. A new vantage is meant to change the word, as if location could alter what God has said.

Both traditions read the move as human persistence against a word already given, not as a promising new approach.

Numbers 23:14

14He took him into the field of Zophim, to the top of Pisgah, and built seven altars, and offered up a bull and a ram on every altar.

Arminian

Balak takes him to the field of Zophim, to the top of Pisgah, builds seven altars, and offers a bull and a ram on every altar.

Arminian readers see the first rite copied in a new place. The repetition itself shows that the earlier offerings did not buy a curse.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the same seven altars and the same offerings on Pisgah. A new height does not create a new God.

Both traditions take the verse as a narrative parallel to verses 1–2, without a fresh doctrinal dispute.

Numbers 23:15

15He said to Balak, “Stand here by your burnt offering, while I meet God over there.”

Arminian

Balaam tells Balak to stand by his burnt offering while he meets God over there.

Arminian readers hear the same pattern as verse 3: the king remains by the offering, and Balaam goes apart to be met.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Balaam now speaks of meeting God, not only of a possible coming of the LORD. The encounter is still sought apart from the royal party.

Both traditions read the verse as preparation for the second word, on shared descriptive ground.

Numbers 23:16

16The LORD met Balaam, and put a word in his mouth, and said, “Return to Balak, and say this.”

Arminian

The LORD meets Balaam, puts a word in his mouth, and tells him to return to Balak and say this.

Arminian readers see the same special constraint as in verse 5. God again gives the word that must be spoken; Balaam does not compose a second, more useful curse.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the repeated act: the LORD meets him and puts a word in his mouth. The second parable, like the first, will be God’s speech.

Both traditions take the repetition as confirming verse 5, and they do not open a new contrast here.

Numbers 23:17

17He came to him, and behold, he was standing by his burnt offering, and the princes of Moab with him. Balak said to him, “What has the LORD spoken?”

Arminian

Balaam comes back to Balak, still standing by his burnt offering with the princes of Moab. Balak asks, “What has the LORD spoken?”

Arminian readers notice that Balak now asks for the LORD’s word, even as he still hopes it will be a curse. The question itself admits whose word matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear the king’s question. After the first blessing, Balak knows that the issue is what the LORD has spoken, not what the altars have earned.

Both traditions take the question as the right one, whatever Balak still wants the answer to be.

Numbers 23:18

18He took up his parable, and said, “Rise up, Balak, and hear! Listen to me, you son of Zippor.

Arminian

Balaam takes up his parable: “Rise up, Balak, and hear! Listen to me, you son of Zippor.”

Arminian readers hear a summons to the king himself. The word will not be adjusted to soothe him; he must rise and listen.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the formal address. The son of Zippor is called to hear a word he cannot edit.

Both traditions read the opening as a claim on Balak’s attention before the theology of verse 19 is stated.

Numbers 23:19

19God is not a man, that he should lie, nor a son of man, that he should repent. Has he said, and he won’t do it? Or has he spoken, and he won’t make it good?

Arminian

The parable states, “God is not a man, that he should lie, nor a son of man, that he should repent. Has he said, and he won’t do it? Or has he spoken, and he won’t make it good?”

Arminian readers, in the classical sense, take this as a statement of God’s truthfulness and immutability. God does not lie or change as human beings do, and he exhaustively knows and will perform what he has spoken. The immediate point is that the blessing of Israel will not be taken back.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear that God is not fickle or false. What he has said he will do; what he has spoken he will make good.

Both traditions treat the verse as shared ground. Classical Arminian teaching, like Reformed teaching, affirms God’s immutability and exhaustive foreknowledge. The verse teaches that God will not revise this blessing as a man might repent of a plan; it is not a claim that God is learning as events unfold.

Numbers 23:20

20Behold, I have received a command to bless. He has blessed, and I can’t reverse it.

Arminian

Balaam says he has received a command to bless: “He has blessed, and I can’t reverse it.”

Arminian readers hear both a received command and an inability to undo what God has already done. The blessing stands, and Balaam cannot turn it back for Balak.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the completed act: God has blessed. Balaam’s inability to reverse it follows from God’s deed, not from Balaam’s preference.

Both traditions read the verse as the practical outworking of verse 19. The spoken blessing will be made good.

Numbers 23:21

21He has not seen iniquity in Jacob. Neither has he seen perverseness in Israel. The LORD his God is with him. The shout of a king is among them.

Arminian

The oracle says that God has not seen iniquity in Jacob or perverseness in Israel; the LORD his God is with him, and “The shout of a king is among them.”

Arminian readers hear why a curse cannot land: God is with this people, and a king’s shout is among them. “He has not seen iniquity in Jacob” is a real word of favor; interpreters differ on whether it speaks of sin overlooked in covenant mercy, or of the absence of a ground for Balak’s curse, and that question should not be treated as settled by the verse alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise start from God’s presence and royal shout among Israel. They too refrain from claiming a single agreed explanation of unseen iniquity, while affirming that God here regards Israel as the people he has chosen to bless.

Both traditions keep the line in its context: this is why Balaam cannot curse them, not a claim that Israel has never sinned.

Numbers 23:22

22God brings them out of Egypt. He has as it were the strength of the wild ox.

Arminian

“God brings them out of Egypt. He has as it were the strength of the wild ox.”

Arminian readers hear the exodus still being described as God’s present act of strength. The people Balak fears are the people God brought out, with a power likened to the wild ox.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the deliverance from Egypt is God’s doing, and that the wild-ox image is a picture of formidable strength given by God, not a zoological riddle to be over-read.

Both traditions take the verse as a reminder of the exodus power that still marks this people.

Numbers 23:23

23Surely there is no enchantment with Jacob; neither is there any divination with Israel. Now it shall be said of Jacob and of Israel, ‘What has God done!’

Arminian

There is no enchantment with Jacob and no divination with Israel. It shall be said of Jacob and of Israel, “What has God done!”

Arminian readers hear that the arts Balak hired will not work against this people. The proper question is not what a diviner can do, but what God has done.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see enchantment and divination ruled out. Israel’s story is to be told as God’s work, not as the success or failure of omens.

Both traditions read the verse as a rebuke of the very means by which Balak hoped to manage Israel’s future.

Numbers 23:24

24Behold, a people rises up as a lioness. As a lion he lifts himself up. He shall not lie down until he eats of the prey, and drinks the blood of the slain.”

Arminian

The people rises as a lioness, lifts himself as a lion, and will not lie down until he has eaten the prey and drunk the blood of the slain.

Arminian readers hear a fierce image of a people that will not be prey, but will itself prevail. The blessing now includes future victory, not only safety.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the lion and lioness as pictures of strength already hinted at in the wild ox of verse 22. The people will finish what it rises to do.

Both traditions take the hunting language as oracular promise of victory, without turning each clause into a separate later campaign.

Numbers 23:25

25Balak said to Balaam, “Neither curse them at all, nor bless them at all.”

Arminian

Balak now says, “Neither curse them at all, nor bless them at all.”

Arminian readers hear a king who would rather have silence than a second blessing. If he cannot buy a curse, he will try to stop the blessing.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same retreat. Balak can no longer ask for the original errand; he can only plead for the word to cease.

Both traditions take the line as the failure of the second attempt, which the next verse will answer.

Numbers 23:26

26But Balaam answered Balak, “Didn’t I tell you, saying, ‘All that the LORD speaks, that I must do’?”

Arminian

Balaam answers, “Didn’t I tell you, saying, ‘All that the LORD speaks, that I must do’?”

Arminian readers hear Balaam repeating the constraint he has already named. He must do what the LORD speaks, including bless when Balak now wants neither blessing nor curse.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the prophet bound to the LORD’s speech. The king’s new request for silence cannot cancel the word God gives.

Both traditions read the answer as consistent with verses 5 and 12, still without a new contrast.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
