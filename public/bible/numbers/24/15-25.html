Passage

Numbers 24

Verses 15–25 · WEB

Balaam takes up a further parable as the man whose eyes are open, who hears God, knows the knowledge of the Most High, and sees the vision of the Almighty. He sees someone, but not now and not near: a star will come out of Jacob, a scepter will rise out of Israel, and will strike Moab and crush the sons of Sheth; Edom and Seir will be a possession; one out of Jacob will have dominion. Shorter parables then follow on Amalek, the Kenite, and ships from Kittim that afflict Asshur and Eber. Balaam returns to his place, and Balak goes his way. Both traditions commonly read the star and scepter in 24:17 christologically, and also in connection with a nearer fulfillment in David, without treating that double reading as the only possible one or as a formal consensus. Identifications of some of the later names are discussed rather than settled, and no single agreed map of every people mentioned is invented.

Numbers 24:15

15He took up his parable, and said, “Balaam the son of Beor says, the man whose eyes are open says;

Arminian

The new parable opens with the same self-introduction as verse 3: Balaam the son of Beor, the man whose eyes are open.

Arminian readers hear the seer again presenting himself as one made to see. The latter-day word will come from the same opened eyes.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the formal repetition, which verse 16 will deepen. The fourth parable claims the same granted sight as the third.

Both traditions take the opening as a heading for the vision that follows, on shared ground.

Numbers 24:16

16he says, who hears the words of God, knows the knowledge of the Most High, and who sees the vision of the Almighty, falling down, and having his eyes open:

Arminian

He hears the words of God, knows the knowledge of the Most High, and sees the vision of the Almighty, falling down, and having his eyes open.

Arminian readers notice the added line: he “knows the knowledge of the Most High,” a gift of knowledge as well as vision, still received, not invented.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear words, knowledge, and vision all ascribed to God and to the Almighty. The seer’s falling down and open eyes remain the posture of a man overtaken, not of a man in command.

Both traditions read the verse as an intensified form of verse 4, still without a theory of the experience beyond the text.

Numbers 24:17

17I see him, but not now. I see him, but not near. A star will come out of Jacob. A scepter will rise out of Israel, and shall strike through the corners of Moab, and crush all the sons of Sheth.

Arminian

Balaam says, “I see him, but not now. I see him, but not near. A star will come out of Jacob. A scepter will rise out of Israel, and shall strike through the corners of Moab, and crush all the sons of Sheth.”

Arminian readers hear a figure seen at a distance: a star from Jacob and a scepter from Israel, with victory over Moab. The “not now” and “not near” keep the vision from being collapsed into the present camp alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the delayed horizon and the royal scepter. Both traditions commonly read the star and scepter christologically, and also in connection with a nearer fulfillment in David’s victories over Moab (2 Samuel 8:2).

That double reading is widely held, but it should not be treated as the only reading or as a formal consensus. The identity of “the sons of Sheth” is itself discussed rather than settled, and no more is claimed than the verse states.

Numbers 24:18

18Edom shall be a possession. Seir, his enemy, also shall be a possession, while Israel does valiantly.

Arminian

Edom shall be a possession, and Seir, his enemy, also, while Israel does valiantly.

Arminian readers hear the vision widening from Moab to Edom and Seir. Israel’s success is described as valiant action under the rising scepter of verse 17.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same widening. David’s later garrisons in Edom (2 Samuel 8:14) are often read as a near fulfillment alongside the longer messianic horizon already mentioned, again without treating that reading as the only one required by the wording itself.

Both traditions take Edom and Seir as Israel’s foe made a possession in this oracle, and they keep the same caution already stated at verse 17.

Numbers 24:19

19Out of Jacob shall one have dominion, and shall destroy the remnant from the city.”

Arminian

“Out of Jacob shall one have dominion, and shall destroy the remnant from the city.”

Arminian readers hear a ruler coming out of Jacob, finishing the victory so that a remnant does not remain in the city as a threat.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see dominion arising from Jacob, in continuity with the scepter of verse 17. The “one” is not named beyond that origin.

Both traditions keep this verse attached to the star-and-scepter vision, with the same care not to claim a single exclusive identification as consensus.

Numbers 24:20

20He looked at Amalek, and took up his parable, and said, “Amalek was the first of the nations, but his latter end shall come to destruction.”

Arminian

Looking at Amalek, Balaam says, “Amalek was the first of the nations, but his latter end shall come to destruction.”

Arminian readers hear a people once first among the nations brought to a last end of destruction. The verse states the reversal without explaining the title “first.”

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the contrast of first and latter end as the point. Interpretations of “first of the nations” vary, and the text itself does not settle them.

Both traditions read a sure destruction of Amalek as part of this latter-day word, without inventing a consensus on the epithet.

Numbers 24:21

21He looked at the Kenite, and took up his parable, and said, “Your dwelling place is strong. Your nest is set in the rock.

Arminian

Looking at the Kenite, he says, “Your dwelling place is strong. Your nest is set in the rock.”

Arminian readers hear a present strength: a secure dwelling, a nest in the rock. The Kenites appear safe in their height.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same image of a rock nest, a picture of security that verse 22 will immediately qualify.

Both traditions take the verse as the first half of the Kenite word, not as a final promise of lasting safety.

Numbers 24:22

22Nevertheless Kain shall be wasted, until Asshur carries you away captive.”

Arminian

Nevertheless Kain shall be wasted, until Asshur carries the Kenite away captive.

Arminian readers hear the nest in the rock fail at last. A strong dwelling does not prevent wasting, or captivity under Asshur.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the opening word nevertheless. Asshur, a great eastern power, is named as the one who carries them away; the verse does not date that captivity or explain every later use of the name.

Both traditions read a limit to Kenite security, and they do not treat the historical mapping of Asshur here as a point of Arminian or Reformed contrast.

Numbers 24:23

23He took up his parable, and said, “Alas, who shall live when God does this?

Arminian

Another parable begins: “Alas, who shall live when God does this?”

Arminian readers hear a cry of awe before the next act of God. The question assumes that what follows is God’s doing, and that it will be hard to endure.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress “when God does this?” The last vision is not a mere forecast of navies; it is framed as God’s act.

Both traditions take the lament as the heading of verses 23–24, on shared ground.

Numbers 24:24

24But ships shall come from the coast of Kittim. They shall afflict Asshur, and shall afflict Eber. He also shall come to destruction.”

Arminian

Ships will come from the coast of Kittim; they will afflict Asshur and afflict Eber; he also shall come to destruction.

Arminian readers hear a further wave of power from the coast, striking even the Asshur just named, and Eber as well, until destruction comes. The vision looks beyond Israel’s near neighbors.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same distant ships and the same end in destruction. Kittim and Eber have been identified in more than one way, and no single identification is presented as agreed.

Both traditions let the verse stand as a word about coming affliction and final ruin under God’s “this” of verse 23, without a doctrinal fork over the names.

Numbers 24:25

25Balaam rose up, and went and returned to his place; and Balak also went his way.

Arminian

Balaam rises, returns to his place, and Balak also goes his way.

Arminian readers see the scene close without a curse and without the honor Balak had offered. The two men part, and the hired project is over.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same plain ending. Later texts will say more: Balaam is later killed, and he is remembered for counsel that led Israel into trespass in the matter of Peor (Numbers 31:8, 16; cf. Revelation 2:14). Those reports belong to their own places and should not be read back into this verse as if they were narrated here.

Both traditions take 24:25 as the close of the encounter, while they allow the later notices to fill out Balaam’s story without rewriting this parting.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
