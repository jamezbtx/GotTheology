Passage

Numbers 24

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Balaam now sees that it pleased the LORD to bless Israel, and he does not go, as at the other times, to use divination. He looks toward the wilderness, sees Israel camped by tribes, and the Spirit of God comes on him. The third parable praises Israel’s tents, promises overflow and a king higher than Agag, repeats the exodus and wild-ox images, and closes with a lion saying, and with blessing on those who bless this people and a curse on those who curse them. Balak, angry, sends Balaam away without the honor he had expected, saying the LORD has kept him back from it. Balaam answers that he cannot go beyond the LORD’s word, and then offers to tell what this people will do to Moab in the latter days. Both traditions read the third oracle as the LORD’s blessing made still plainer, and they treat Balak’s rage as the failure of the hired curse, not as a sign that Balaam has now become Israel’s faithful friend in every respect.

Numbers 24:1

1When Balaam saw that it pleased the LORD to bless Israel, he didn’t go, as at the other times, to use divination, but he set his face toward the wilderness.

Arminian

When Balaam sees that it pleased the LORD to bless Israel, he does not go, as at the other times, to use divination, but sets his face toward the wilderness.

Arminian readers hear a change of practice: Balaam has now seen what pleases the LORD, and he does not resort again to divination. The blessing is recognized as God’s pleasure, not as a technique still to be tried.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same turning. The text itself contrasts this moment with “the other times” when divination was used. The prophet faces the wilderness to see the people, rather than going aside to seek an omen.

Both traditions let 23:23 and this verse stand together: enchantment and divination are not the way Israel’s future is decided.

Numbers 24:2

2Balaam lifted up his eyes, and he saw Israel dwelling according to their tribes; and the Spirit of God came on him.

Arminian

Balaam lifts up his eyes and sees Israel dwelling according to their tribes, and the Spirit of God comes on him.

Arminian readers see the people as God has arranged them, tribe by tribe, and hear that the Spirit of God comes on Balaam for this utterance. The third parable is given by the Spirit, not by Balak’s hire or by divination.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the Spirit’s coming. The seer is overtaken by God’s Spirit as he looks at the ordered camp.

Both traditions take “the Spirit of God came on him” as the source of the words that follow, in keeping with the word put in his mouth in chapter 23, without opening a new dispute here about ordinary providence.

Numbers 24:3

3He took up his parable, and said, “Balaam the son of Beor says, the man whose eyes are open says;

Arminian

He takes up his parable: “Balaam the son of Beor says, the man whose eyes are open says;”

Arminian readers hear Balaam name himself as one whose eyes are open. In this moment he is made to see, whatever else is true of his path.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the formal self-introduction, which verse 4 will fill out. WEB’s “eyes are open” is the wording of the oracle, and no attempt is made to replace it with another translation.

Both traditions take the opening as the seer’s claim to have been given sight for this word.

Numbers 24:4

4he says, who hears the words of God, who sees the vision of the Almighty, falling down, and having his eyes open:

Arminian

He is the one who hears the words of God, who sees the vision of the Almighty, falling down, and having his eyes open.

Arminian readers hear a description of a man laid low and yet made to see and hear. The word and the vision are God’s; Balaam is a recipient, not the source.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress hearing the words of God and seeing the vision of the Almighty. The falling down belongs to the encounter; the open eyes belong to the given sight.

Both traditions describe the experience as the text does, without claiming a settled theory of prophetic trance beyond what is written.

Numbers 24:5

5How goodly are your tents, Jacob, and your dwellings, Israel!

Arminian

The vision turns to praise: “How goodly are your tents, Jacob, and your dwellings, Israel!”

Arminian readers hear delight in the camp itself. The people who were to be cursed are seen as well housed and fair in their dwellings.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the address to Jacob and Israel. The goodliness is declared over the whole people in their tents, the object of Balak’s fear.

Both traditions take the line as blessing in the form of praise, the opposite of the requested curse.

Numbers 24:6

6As valleys they are spread out, as gardens by the riverside, as aloes which the LORD has planted, as cedar trees beside the waters.

Arminian

The camp is likened to spread valleys, gardens by the river, aloes which the LORD has planted, and cedars beside the waters.

Arminian readers see images of fruitfulness and settled beauty, all under the LORD’s planting. Israel is not a passing raiding party in this vision, but a planted people.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the aloes are those “which the LORD has planted,” and that the life of the camp is God’s gardening, not Moab’s to uproot.

Both traditions read the similes as pictures of abundance and planting, without forcing a separate meaning onto each tree and stream.

Numbers 24:7

7Water shall flow from his buckets. His seed shall be in many waters. His king shall be higher than Agag. His kingdom shall be exalted.

Arminian

Water will flow from his buckets, his seed will be in many waters, his king will be higher than Agag, and his kingdom will be exalted.

Arminian readers hear overflow, numerous offspring, and a coming royal height. A king and a kingdom are now named, not only a camp of tents.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the royal turn. Agag is a name later borne by an Amalekite king, and the verse promises a king of Israel higher than that rival, with an exalted kingdom; they do not treat later identifications as if the verse itself named them all.

Both traditions read a promise of fruitfulness and kingship, and they wait for verses 17–19 for a still sharper royal image, without claiming a single agreed list of fulfillments here.

Numbers 24:8

8God brings him out of Egypt. He has as it were the strength of the wild ox. He shall consume the nations his adversaries, shall break their bones in pieces, and pierce them with his arrows.

Arminian

The exodus and wild-ox lines of 23:22 return, and are extended: he will consume adversary nations, break their bones, and pierce them with his arrows.

Arminian readers hear the people, brought out by God and strong as the wild ox, now described as victors over hostile nations. The blessing includes defense and conquest, not only beauty of tents.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the repeated exodus formula and the added language of bones and arrows. The God who brought them out also gives them victory over adversaries.

Both traditions take the verse as an intensification of 23:22 and 23:24, still as oracular promise rather than a battle chronicle.

Numbers 24:9

9He couched, he lay down as a lion, as a lioness; who shall rouse him up? Everyone who blesses you is blessed. Everyone who curses you is cursed.”

Arminian

Israel is again a lion and a lioness: who shall rouse him up? Then the parable says, “Everyone who blesses you is blessed. Everyone who curses you is cursed.”

Arminian readers hear both the danger of rousing this people and a covenant-style blessing and curse over those who bless or curse them. Balak’s whole project falls under that closing line.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the echo of God’s word to Abram, that he would bless those who bless him and curse the one who treats him with contempt (Genesis 12:3), now spoken over Israel in Balaam’s mouth.

Both traditions read the close as a warning to Balak and as a confirmation of the older promise, without forcing the wording of Genesis into this verse’s own sentences.

Numbers 24:10

10Balak’s anger burned against Balaam, and he struck his hands together. Balak said to Balaam, “I called you to curse my enemies, and, behold, you have altogether blessed them these three times.

Arminian

Balak’s anger burns. He strikes his hands together and says he called Balaam to curse his enemies, and that Balaam has altogether blessed them these three times.

Arminian readers hear the king’s rage after a third blessing. The counted “three times” marks the complete failure of the hiring.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress Balak’s own tally. The enemy of Israel must admit a threefold, thorough blessing.

Both traditions take the anger and the hand-clapping as the human end of the three formal attempts.

Numbers 24:11

11Therefore, flee to your place, now! I thought to promote you to great honor; but, behold, the LORD has kept you back from honor.”

Arminian

Balak tells Balaam to flee to his place. He had thought to promote him to great honor, but, he says, “the LORD has kept you back from honor.”

Arminian readers notice that even Balak now names the LORD as the one who has withheld the reward. The lost honor is traced to God, not merely to Balaam’s incompetence.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear a confession, however bitter, that the LORD has blocked the honor Balak intended. The wages of the curse have been withheld by God.

Both traditions take the line as an unwilling witness to the LORD’s government of the whole episode.

Numbers 24:12

12Balaam said to Balak, “Didn’t I also tell your messengers whom you sent to me, saying,

Arminian

Balaam begins his answer by recalling what he had already told Balak’s messengers.

Arminian readers see him pointing back to a word given before the oracles began. He is not claiming a new policy only after the money has failed.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the appeal to the earlier exchange with the messengers (Numbers 22:18). The constraint has been named from the start, even if Balak would not accept it.

Both traditions read the verse as introduction to the fuller statement in verse 13.

Numbers 24:13

13‘If Balak would give me his house full of silver and gold, I can’t go beyond the LORD’s word, to do either good or bad from my own mind. I will say what the LORD says’?

Arminian

Balaam repeats that even if Balak gave him his house full of silver and gold, “I can’t go beyond the LORD’s word, to do either good or bad from my own mind. I will say what the LORD says”

Arminian readers hear the limit clearly: Balaam may not go beyond the LORD’s word or act from his own mind in this matter. The special constraint of chapter 23 still holds; his own mind is not the source of the blessing.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same inability and the same pledge: he will say what the LORD says. The house full of silver and gold cannot buy a word beyond that.

Both traditions take the confession as the truth of these chapters, consistent with 23:5, 12, and 26, without restating that contrast as a new keyContrast.

Numbers 24:14

14Now, behold, I go to my people. Come, I will inform you what this people shall do to your people in the latter days.”

Arminian

Balaam says he is going to his people, but first, “Come, I will inform you what this people shall do to your people in the latter days.”

Arminian readers hear a further word offered, not another attempt at a curse, but a disclosure of what Israel will do to Moab in the latter days. The horizon now stretches beyond the present camp.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the phrase “in the latter days” as a turn toward a more distant future, which the fourth parable will unfold.

Both traditions take the verse as the hinge into 24:15–25, not as a separate oracle of its own.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
