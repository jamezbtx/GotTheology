Passage

Numbers 1

Verses 20–46 · WEB

The census results are given tribe by tribe in a fixed formula: a heading verse naming the tribe and the terms of the count, then a verse giving the total. Gad is listed third here, after Reuben and Simeon, in the place it takes in the camp of Reuben in chapter 2. Judah is the largest tribe and Manasseh the smallest. The tribal figures add up to the grand total of 603,550 in 1:46, the same total given for the half-shekel count in Exod 38:26. Interpreters discuss how such large totals should be understood, and readers in both traditions hold different views on that question. Both traditions agree that the figures display God’s fulfilled promise to multiply Abraham’s descendants. They also agree that this counted generation, apart from Caleb and Joshua, will die in the wilderness (Num 14:29–30), and a second census of the next generation follows in Numbers 26.

Numbers 1:20

20The children of Reuben, Israel’s firstborn, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, one by one, every male from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Reuben is counted first as “Israel’s firstborn.” The heading repeats the terms of the census: families, fathers’ houses, names, and men able to go to war.

Arminian readers note that Reuben keeps his place in the list even though Jacob’s blessing removed his preeminence (Gen 49:3–4). Past failure does not erase belonging.

Reformed

The formula is repeated for each tribe, stressing that the whole count followed one command.

Reformed readers see Reuben listed first by birth while leadership passes elsewhere, in keeping with God’s purposes in Genesis. Both traditions agree that the repetition underlines careful obedience to the LORD’s instruction.

Numbers 1:21

21those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Reuben, were forty-six thousand five hundred.

Arminian

Reuben numbers 46,500.

Arminian readers treat the figure as part of the record of a people God has multiplied and now prepares for the journey.

Reformed

The total for Reuben is the first of twelve that make up the grand total.

Reformed readers stress that God knows and orders his people down to their numbers. Both traditions read the count as evidence of God’s blessing on Jacob’s family.

Numbers 1:22

22Of the children of Simeon, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, those who were counted of it, according to the number of the names, one by one, every male from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Simeon’s heading follows the same pattern, adding the phrase “those who were counted of it.”

Arminian readers note that each tribe is counted on identical terms, without special treatment.

Reformed

The repeated formula shows that all the tribes stand under the same command.

Reformed readers see the uniformity as part of God’s ordering of one people. Both traditions agree that the tribes share equal standing in the census.

Numbers 1:23

23those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Simeon, were fifty-nine thousand three hundred.

Arminian

Simeon numbers 59,300. In the second census of Numbers 26 the tribe has fallen to 22,200 (Num 26:14), the largest decline of any tribe.

Arminian readers often see in the later drop a sober reminder that the wilderness generation’s choices had real consequences.

Reformed

The later decline belongs to the story of judgment in the wilderness, especially after Numbers 25.

Reformed readers note that God both multiplies and disciplines his people. Both traditions read the two censuses together as showing God’s faithfulness and his seriousness about sin. The link between the decline and specific events is a matter of interpretation.

Numbers 1:24

24Of the children of Gad, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Gad is counted third here, after Reuben and Simeon. This matches its place in the camp of Reuben in chapter 2, and it fills the third place that Levi holds in the birth order of Jacob’s sons.

Arminian readers see the order as practical arrangement for the camp rather than a ranking of worth.

Reformed

The tribal order shifts from the list of leaders in 1:5–15 to match the camp arrangement.

Reformed readers note that the order serves God’s design for the camp around his presence. Both traditions agree that the order reflects function, not merit.

Numbers 1:25

25those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Gad, were forty-five thousand six hundred fifty.

Arminian

Gad numbers 45,650, the only tribal figure here given to the nearest fifty.

Arminian readers note the precision as part of the record’s concern with each counted person.

Reformed

The exact figure fits the careful tone of the census.

Reformed readers see God’s detailed knowledge of his people reflected in the detail of the record. Both traditions read the precision as respect for the record, not as a hidden code.

Numbers 1:26

26Of the children of Judah, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Judah’s heading follows. Judah heads the camp on the east side and sets out first (2:3, 9).

Arminian readers note that Judah’s leadership, promised in Jacob’s blessing (Gen 49:8–10), begins to take visible shape here.

Reformed

Judah’s prominence is anticipated in Genesis and unfolds through Israel’s history to David and the Messiah.

Reformed readers stress God’s sovereign purpose in raising up Judah. Both traditions agree that Judah’s place points forward to the royal line.

Numbers 1:27

27those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Judah, were seventy-four thousand six hundred.

Arminian

Judah numbers 74,600, the largest tribe in the census.

Arminian readers see God’s blessing on Judah’s growth in keeping with his promise.

Reformed

Judah’s size fits its leading role in the camp.

Reformed readers note God’s providence in preparing Judah for the leadership he intends. Both traditions read the number as part of Judah’s rise rather than as a mark of the tribe’s own merit.

Numbers 1:28

28Of the children of Issachar, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Issachar’s heading follows the same pattern. Issachar camps beside Judah on the east (2:5).

Arminian readers note that each tribe is counted for its part in the shared journey.

Reformed

Issachar is counted in its place among the tribes that will camp with Judah.

Reformed readers see the census preparing the camp God will order in chapter 2. Both traditions agree that the census and the camp belong together.

Numbers 1:29

29those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Issachar, were fifty-four thousand four hundred.

Arminian

Issachar numbers 54,400.

Arminian readers see the figure as part of the growth God gave to Jacob’s descendants in Egypt (Exod 1:7).

Reformed

The figure adds to Judah’s eastern camp in 2:5–6.

Reformed readers note that each total is a building block in God’s ordering of the whole people. Both traditions read the figure as part of the fulfilled promise of increase.

Numbers 1:30

30Of the children of Zebulun, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Zebulun’s heading follows. Zebulun also camps with Judah on the east (2:7).

Arminian readers note the steady repetition, which presents every tribe as equally answerable to God’s command.

Reformed

Zebulun is counted within the same framework as the others.

Reformed readers stress that God’s ordering embraces each tribe. Both traditions see shared standing under one covenant.

Numbers 1:31

31those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Zebulun, were fifty-seven thousand four hundred.

Arminian

Zebulun numbers 57,400. In the camp arrangement that follows, Zebulun will stand with Judah and Issachar on the east, the lead side of the march.

Arminian readers read the figure as part of the record of a people ready to march, each tribe counted and placed.

Reformed

Zebulun’s total completes the tribes that will join Judah’s camp.

Reformed readers see providence in the arrangement of the people. Both traditions agree that the numbers serve the organization of the camp.

Numbers 1:32

32Of the children of Joseph: of the children of Ephraim, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Joseph’s descendants are counted through Ephraim first. Ephraim will lead the western camp (2:18).

Arminian readers note that Jacob placed the younger Ephraim ahead of Manasseh (Gen 48:19–20), and the order here follows that blessing.

Reformed

Ephraim’s precedence over Manasseh follows Jacob’s blessing in Genesis 48.

Reformed readers often see in this a recurring pattern in which God’s choice does not follow birth order. Both traditions agree that the order reflects Jacob’s blessing.

Numbers 1:33

33those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Ephraim, were forty thousand five hundred.

Arminian

Ephraim numbers 40,500.

Arminian readers note that Ephraim outnumbers Manasseh here, though in the second census Manasseh is larger (Num 26:34, 37).

Reformed

The figures change between the two censuses, with Ephraim at 32,500 and Manasseh at 52,700 in Numbers 26.

Reformed readers see the shifting totals as part of God’s governance over the rise and fall of tribes. Both traditions read the change as history under God rather than as a problem in the text.

Numbers 1:34

34Of the children of Manasseh, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Manasseh’s heading follows, completing Joseph’s descendants.

Arminian readers note that the two sons of Joseph are counted as separate tribes with equal standing.

Reformed

Manasseh is counted beside Ephraim, giving Joseph a double portion among the tribes.

Reformed readers see God keeping the word spoken through Jacob. Both traditions agree that the arrangement honors Joseph as Genesis anticipated.

Numbers 1:35

35those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Manasseh, were thirty-two thousand two hundred.

Arminian

Manasseh numbers 32,200, the smallest tribe in this census.

Arminian readers note that a tribe’s size does not determine its standing before God.

Reformed

Manasseh’s smaller total does not diminish its place among the tribes.

Reformed readers stress that God’s ordering, not size, sets each tribe’s place. Both traditions agree that small and large tribes belong equally to the covenant people.

Numbers 1:36

36Of the children of Benjamin, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Benjamin’s heading follows. Benjamin camps with Ephraim and Manasseh on the west, so Rachel’s descendants camp together (2:18–22).

Arminian readers note the family ties preserved in the camp.

Reformed

Benjamin is counted in its place among Rachel’s descendants.

Reformed readers see God ordering the camp along the lines of the covenant family. Both traditions read the grouping as rooted in Genesis.

Numbers 1:37

37those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Benjamin, were thirty-five thousand four hundred.

Arminian

Benjamin numbers 35,400.

Arminian readers read the figure as a record of the tribe that would later give Israel its first king.

Reformed

Benjamin’s total adds to the western camp.

Reformed readers note the tribe’s later importance in Israel’s history, including Saul and Paul. Both traditions agree that the census records real tribes with real futures.

Numbers 1:38

38Of the children of Dan, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Dan’s heading follows. Dan leads the northern camp and serves as the rear guard on the march (2:25, 31; Num 10:25).

Arminian readers note the tribe’s important role in protecting the people as they travel.

Reformed

Dan is given a significant position in God’s arrangement of the camp.

Reformed readers see God assigning each tribe its duty. Both traditions read the arrangement as ordered service rather than rank.

Numbers 1:39

39those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Dan, were sixty-two thousand seven hundred.

Arminian

Dan numbers 62,700, the second largest tribe, though Genesis names only one son of Dan (Gen 46:23).

Arminian readers see in Dan’s growth God’s blessing on a family that began small.

Reformed

Dan’s growth from a single named son shows the increase God gave in Egypt.

Reformed readers stress God’s power to multiply his people beyond human expectation. Both traditions read the figure as evidence of the promise of increase.

Numbers 1:40

40Of the children of Asher, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Asher’s heading follows. Asher camps with Dan on the north (2:27).

Arminian readers note again that every tribe is counted on the same terms.

Reformed

Asher is counted in its place within the northern camp.

Reformed readers see the census serving the camp’s design. Both traditions agree that the order prepares for the arrangement in chapter 2.

Numbers 1:41

41those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Asher, were forty-one thousand five hundred.

Arminian

Asher numbers 41,500. In the camp that follows, Asher will stand with Dan and Naphtali on the north.

Arminian readers read the figure as part of the people’s readiness for the journey, not as a bare total detached from the life of the camp.

Reformed

Asher’s total adds to Dan’s camp.

Reformed readers note that God orders every part of his people. Both traditions read the figure as part of one coherent record.

Numbers 1:42

42Of the children of Naphtali, their generations, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war:

Arminian

Naphtali’s heading follows, the last tribe counted.

Arminian readers note that the formula repeated twelve times shows faithful completion of the task.

Reformed

The final heading completes the pattern for all twelve tribes.

Reformed readers stress that nothing in God’s command was left undone. Both traditions see thorough obedience in the full repetition.

Numbers 1:43

43those who were counted of them, of the tribe of Naphtali, were fifty-three thousand four hundred.

Arminian

Naphtali numbers 53,400.

Arminian readers read the figure as completing the record of the fighting strength of the tribes.

Reformed

Naphtali’s total completes the twelve totals.

Reformed readers see God’s ordering of his people brought to completion. Both traditions agree that the record is now ready to be summed.

Numbers 1:44

44These are those who were counted, whom Moses and Aaron counted, and the twelve men who were princes of Israel, each one for his fathers’ house.

Arminian

The summary names Moses, Aaron, and the twelve princes as those who did the counting, each for his fathers’ house.

Arminian readers note that the census was a shared work done in obedience.

Reformed

The count is attributed to the leaders God appointed in 1:4–16.

Reformed readers see God’s appointed order carried through to the end. Both traditions agree that the work was done by the means God set.

Numbers 1:45

45So all those who were counted of the children of Israel by their fathers’ houses, from twenty years old and upward, all who were able to go out to war in Israel—

Arminian

The summary introduces the grand total. The terms are repeated one last time: by fathers’ houses, twenty years and upward, able to go to war.

Arminian readers note that the whole people now stand ready for the campaign God intends.

Reformed

The full congregation of fighting men is now counted.

Reformed readers see Israel organized as the LORD’s army. Both traditions read the verse as the conclusion of the muster.

Numbers 1:46

46all those who were counted were six hundred three thousand five hundred fifty.

Arminian

The total of 603,550 matches the sum of the tribal figures and the count in Exod 38:26. Interpreters discuss how such large totals should be understood. Numbers 14:29 later says that those counted from twenty years old and upward would fall in the wilderness, all except Caleb and Joshua.

Arminian readers often take this as a real warning that being counted among God’s people does not by itself guarantee entering his promise. They connect it with the warning in Hebrews 3–4.

Reformed

The total shows God’s fulfilled promise of a great people.

Reformed readers also read the later judgment and the warning in Hebrews 3–4 as real. They often stress that God uses such warnings as means to keep his people, and they distinguish outward membership from saving faith. Both traditions agree that the warning is to be heeded and that God remained faithful to his promise through the next generation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
