Passage

Numbers 1

Verses 1–19 · WEB

Numbers (Hebrew Bemidbar, “in the wilderness”) opens one month after the tabernacle was raised (Exod 40:17) and before Israel leaves Sinai. The LORD orders a census of men twenty years old and upward who are able to go to war, counted by families and fathers’ houses. Moses and Aaron do the counting with one leader from each tribe, and verses 5–15 name those twelve leaders. The list keeps twelve tribes by counting Joseph as Ephraim and Manasseh, since Levi is set apart (1:47–54). Both traditions read the census first as Israel’s mustering for the march toward Canaan. Both see in it God’s faithfulness to the promise of a numerous people and his care to order the community gathered around his presence.

Numbers 1:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses in the wilderness of Sinai, in the Tent of Meeting, on the first day of the second month, in the second year after they had come out of the land of Egypt, saying,

Arminian

The Hebrew title of the book comes from this verse: Israel is “in the wilderness of Sinai.” The census is dated one month after the tabernacle was raised. The LORD who now dwells among his people speaks from the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian readers note that God has redeemed Israel from Egypt and now prepares them for the journey ahead. His grace precedes and equips the people for faithful response.

Reformed

The setting ties the census to God’s presence in the newly raised Tent and to the exodus behind it.

Reformed readers stress that God initiates every stage of Israel’s life, from deliverance to ordering the camp. Both traditions agree that the wilderness journey begins with God speaking and the people being organized by his word.

Numbers 1:2

2“Take a census of all the congregation of the children of Israel, by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, every male, one by one,

Arminian

The count is “by their families, by their fathers’ houses,” and “one by one.” Israel is a nation, but it is made up of households and named persons.

Arminian readers see here God’s regard for each individual within the covenant people. Each counted man is personally called to take his place in the journey.

Reformed

The census treats Israel as one congregation structured by households and tribes.

Reformed readers often draw out the covenantal and corporate shape of God’s people, in which families and generations matter. Both traditions hold together the people as a whole and the individuals named within it.

Numbers 1:3

3from twenty years old and upward, all who are able to go out to war in Israel. You and Aaron shall count them by their divisions.

Arminian

The count is limited to men twenty years and older who can go to war. This is a military muster for the coming entry into the land. The same age line governs the half-shekel offering of Exod 30:14.

Arminian readers note that the people are being equipped for an actual campaign in which their obedience and courage will matter.

Reformed

The census organizes Israel as the LORD’s host under his command, counted by divisions.

Reformed readers stress that the battles ahead are the LORD’s, and he arranges his people for them. Both traditions agree that the muster prepares a real people for a real journey under God’s rule.

Numbers 1:4

4With you there shall be a man of every tribe, each one head of his fathers’ house.

Arminian

A head of each fathers’ house stands with Moses and Aaron. The census is carried out with the leaders of the people, not by Moses alone.

Arminian readers see shared responsibility in the covenant community, with recognized leaders serving alongside God’s appointed mediators.

Reformed

God provides ordered leadership for his people, with tribal heads assisting Moses and Aaron.

Reformed readers often connect this with the biblical pattern of appointed, accountable leadership in the community. Both traditions value orderly leadership under God’s word.

Numbers 1:5

5These are the names of the men who shall stand with you: Of Reuben: Elizur the son of Shedeur.

Arminian

The list of tribal leaders begins with Reuben, Jacob’s firstborn. Elizur son of Shedeur heads the tribe.

Arminian readers note that these leaders are named individuals with real responsibility. The same men reappear in Numbers 2, 7, and 10.

Reformed

The roll begins in birth order with Reuben. Elizur represents his tribe before the LORD.

Reformed readers note that God’s ordering of his people includes the naming of their leaders. Both traditions read the list as a record of real people in Israel’s history.

Numbers 1:6

6Of Simeon: Shelumiel the son of Zurishaddai.

Arminian

Shelumiel son of Zurishaddai leads Simeon, the second of Leah’s sons.

Arminian readers see each tribe represented in the work, so that no part of the people is left out of the mustering.

Reformed

Simeon follows Reuben in the order of Jacob’s sons by Leah.

Reformed readers note that the list keeps the whole covenant family in view. Both traditions agree that each tribe has its place in God’s people.

Numbers 1:7

7Of Judah: Nahshon the son of Amminadab.

Arminian

Nahshon son of Amminadab leads Judah. He was the brother-in-law of Aaron (Exod 6:23), and he appears in the line of David (Ruth 4:20) and in Matthew’s genealogy of Jesus (Matt 1:4).

Arminian readers see God working through ordinary family lines toward his saving purpose.

Reformed

Nahshon’s place in the royal line links this list to the promise that leadership would come from Judah (Gen 49:10).

Reformed readers often trace here God’s providential direction of history toward the Messiah. Both traditions agree that the genealogy matters for the story of redemption.

Numbers 1:8

8Of Issachar: Nethanel the son of Zuar.

Arminian

Nethanel son of Zuar leads Issachar.

Arminian readers note that even tribes about whom little is said elsewhere are given a named leader and a full place in the community.

Reformed

Issachar continues the list of Leah’s sons.

Reformed readers stress that God orders all his people, not only those who stand out in the story. Both traditions see the whole people honored by the naming.

Numbers 1:9

9Of Zebulun: Eliab the son of Helon.

Arminian

Eliab son of Helon leads Zebulun, the last of Leah’s sons.

Arminian readers see continuity between Jacob’s family and the nation now gathered at Sinai.

Reformed

Zebulun completes Leah’s six sons, apart from Levi, who is set apart (1:47–49).

Reformed readers note that the twelve-tribe structure is preserved by God’s arrangement. Both traditions read the list as covenant continuity from the patriarchs to the nation.

Numbers 1:10

10Of the children of Joseph: of Ephraim: Elishama the son of Ammihud; of Manasseh: Gamaliel the son of Pedahzur.

Arminian

Joseph’s line is counted as two tribes, Ephraim and Manasseh, following Jacob’s adoption of Joseph’s sons (Gen 48:5). This keeps the number of tribes at twelve while Levi is set apart.

Arminian readers see God honoring the blessing Jacob spoke over Joseph’s sons.

Reformed

The double portion for Joseph reflects Jacob’s blessing and the place of Ephraim and Manasseh among the tribes (Gen 48).

Reformed readers note God’s faithfulness to what he has promised through the patriarchs. Both traditions agree that the arrangement is rooted in Genesis.

Numbers 1:11

11Of Benjamin: Abidan the son of Gideoni.

Arminian

Abidan son of Gideoni leads Benjamin, Rachel’s younger son.

Arminian readers note that Rachel’s sons are listed together after Leah’s, keeping the family record in view.

Reformed

Benjamin follows Joseph’s sons, completing the sons of Rachel.

Reformed readers see the careful order as part of God’s ordering of the covenant family. Both traditions read the order as genealogical rather than as ranking worth.

Numbers 1:12

12Of Dan: Ahiezer the son of Ammishaddai.

Arminian

Ahiezer son of Ammishaddai leads Dan, the first of the sons born to the handmaids.

Arminian readers note that the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah have full standing among the tribes.

Reformed

Dan begins the four tribes descended from Jacob’s sons by the handmaids.

Reformed readers stress that all twelve tribes belong to the one covenant people. Both traditions agree that no tribe is left outside God’s purpose.

Numbers 1:13

13Of Asher: Pagiel the son of Ochran.

Arminian

Pagiel son of Ochran leads Asher.

Arminian readers see each tribe represented by a leader who will stand with Moses in the work.

Reformed

Asher continues the handmaids’ sons.

Reformed readers note that leadership is distributed across the whole people. Both traditions read the list as the inclusion of every tribe in the census.

Numbers 1:14

14Of Gad: Eliasaph the son of Deuel.

Arminian

Eliasaph son of Deuel leads Gad. In 2:14 the WEB names his father Reuel, and ancient manuscripts and versions differ over the name.

Arminian readers treat the variation as a textual matter that does not affect the passage’s teaching.

Reformed

Gad continues the list. The spelling difference between this verse and 2:14 is generally treated as a textual question about similar Hebrew letters.

Reformed readers likewise see no doctrinal weight in the variation. Both traditions handle such details as matters for careful textual study.

Numbers 1:15

15Of Naphtali: Ahira the son of Enan.”

Arminian

Ahira son of Enan leads Naphtali, the last named, and the divine speech ends.

Arminian readers note that the full list of twelve leaders is given by the LORD himself. Every tribe has a part in the work God commands.

Reformed

The list closes with Naphtali, completing the twelve.

Reformed readers see God himself naming those who will serve. Both traditions agree that the leaders’ authority rests on God’s appointment.

Numbers 1:16

16These are those who were called of the congregation, the princes of the tribes of their fathers; they were the heads of the thousands of Israel.

Arminian

The named men are “the princes of the tribes of their fathers” and “heads of the thousands of Israel.” They are recognized leaders called from the congregation.

Arminian readers note that they are drawn from among the people to serve the people.

Reformed

These leaders are called for a task and hold recognized office in the tribes.

Reformed readers stress that God calls particular people to serve, and the community receives them. Both traditions affirm that leaders are servants called within the congregation.

Numbers 1:17

17Moses and Aaron took these men who are mentioned by name.

Arminian

Moses and Aaron take the named men to carry out the census. God’s chosen mediators work with the tribal heads.

Arminian readers see willing cooperation between leaders in obedience to God’s command.

Reformed

Moses and Aaron act exactly on the names God gave.

Reformed readers note that God’s word determines who serves and how. Both traditions see faithful obedience in following the LORD’s instruction precisely.

Numbers 1:18

18They assembled all the congregation together on the first day of the second month; and they declared their ancestry by their families, by their fathers’ houses, according to the number of the names, from twenty years old and upward, one by one.

Arminian

The whole congregation gathers on the first day of the second month, the same day the command was given in 1:1. Each man declares his ancestry by family and fathers’ house, and the count is made “one by one.”

Arminian readers note how promptly the people obey, and that each person takes part in being counted.

Reformed

The census is carried out immediately and in full detail.

Reformed readers stress the importance of belonging to the covenant people through its families and lines. Both traditions agree that each person’s place among God’s people is recognized and recorded.

Numbers 1:19

19As the LORD commanded Moses, so he counted them in the wilderness of Sinai.

Arminian

The section closes, “As the LORD commanded Moses, so he counted them.” This census is carried out under God’s command, unlike the one that later brought judgment on David (2 Sam 24).

Arminian readers note that obedience to God’s word is the pattern Numbers sets out at its start, before the people’s later failures.

Reformed

The census is God’s initiative and is done by his command.

Reformed readers stress that God governs his people through his revealed word. Both traditions agree that faithful obedience marks the opening chapters of Numbers.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
