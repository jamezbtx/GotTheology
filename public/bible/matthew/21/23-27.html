Passage

Matthew 21

Verses 23–27 · ESV · Voices

Back in the temple, chief priests and elders challenge Jesus’ authority while He teaches. He answers with a question about John’s baptism; trapped between heaven’s claim and fear of the crowd, they plead ignorance—and He withholds further disclosure of His authority.

Matthew 21:23

23And when he entered the temple, the chief priests and the elders of the people came up to him as he was teaching, and said, "By what authority are you doing these things, and who gave you this authority?"

Arminian

Jesus teaches in the temple when chief priests and elders interrupt: by what authority, and who gave it? Contested authority meets the King mid-word; their demand sounds pious but resists the light already given.

Classical Arminians hear a real opportunity. Grace has already confronted them in John’s ministry and in Jesus’ deeds; they could inquire in faith. Instead they press a challenge that dodges responsible response to known light.

Reformed

Leaders confront Jesus in the temple over authority—right after cleansing, healing, and praise. The question is not neutral curiosity but opposition under judgment.

Reformed readers see providence: contested authority belongs to Passion Week’s appointed conflict. The King teaches; hardened shepherds demand credentials they will not honor—history advancing toward the cross under God’s purpose.

Key contrast Both treat the challenge as contested authority, not honest seeking. Arminians typically stress a real chance to respond to prior light that leaders squander by resistant interrogation; Reformed readers typically stress hardness under judgment—leaders opposing the King as God’s purpose unfolds.

Matthew 21:24

24Jesus answered them, "I also will ask you one question, and if you tell me the answer, then I also will tell you by what authority I do these things.

Arminian

Jesus answers with a counter-question: answer Him, and He will tell them by what authority He acts. Disclosure is conditioned on honest dealing with the light already before them.

Arminian pastoral reading hears sincerity without naivety. Enabling grace has given them John’s witness; if they respond truthfully, further clarity follows. The door is not slammed before they refuse—they are summoned to face what they already know.

Reformed

Jesus ties His disclosure to their answer. He is not bargaining from weakness; He exposes whether they will handle truth about John.

Reformed emphasis: the King remains Master of revelation. He sets the terms; further light is not owed to those who suppress prior witness. Sovereignty over what He discloses stands even as He questions them.

Matthew 21:25

25The baptism of John, from where did it come? From heaven or from man?" And they discussed it among themselves, saying, "If we say, 'From heaven,' he will say to us, 'Why then did you not believe him?'

Arminian

The question is sharp: was John’s baptism from heaven or from man? They huddle and admit the trap—if from heaven, why did they not believe him? Prior light from John still presses; belief was the fitting response they withheld.

Classical Arminians linger here: responsible refusal of known light. John had given a real, gracious summons; they squandered opportunity. Their private logic proves they know what heaven’s claim would require—and they will not yield.

Reformed

Jesus forces the issue back onto John’s baptism—heaven or man. Their deliberation reveals hearts that calculate consequences, not truth: heaven would indict their unbelief.

Reformed sobriety: hardness shows itself in refusing a prophet God sent. Fear of self-condemnation binds them; the light of John stands as witness against them under God’s judicial purpose.

Key contrast Both hear John’s baptism as decisive prior light. Arminians typically emphasize a real opportunity from heaven that leaders responsibly refused; Reformed readers typically emphasize hardness that will not confess heaven’s claim—unbelief exposed and judged.

Matthew 21:26

26But if we say, 'From man,' we are afraid of the crowd, for they all hold that John was a prophet."

Arminian

The other horn is fear of the crowd: call John merely from man, and the people who hold him a prophet will turn on them. Reputation and safety outweigh truth.

Arminian warning: fear of man is a real moral failure. Grace had given public witness through John; instead of courage before God, they manage optics. Opportunity to confess is lost to people-pleasing.

Reformed

They will not say “from man” because they fear the crowd. Bondage to human opinion governs the answer they will give.

Reformed diagnosis: fear of man is slavery of the heart, not mere social awkwardness. Providence has placed them where truth costs; hardness chooses self-preservation over confession of John’s prophetic status.

Matthew 21:27

27So they answered Jesus, "We do not know." And he said to them, "Neither will I tell you by what authority I do these things."

Arminian

They answer, “We do not know”—a calculated agnosticism. Jesus replies that He will not tell them by what authority He acts. Further light is withheld after they refuse the light already given.

Classical Arminians hear judicial seriousness without fatalism: they squandered a real chance to speak truth about John. Responsible refusal closes the door they were invited to walk through; the King will not indulge evasion with more disclosure.

Reformed

“We do not know” is strategic ignorance. Jesus withholds His authority’s explanation—neither will He tell them.

Reformed confession: Christ sovereignly withholds further light. Revelation is His gift, not their right; after hardness and fear of man, He judicially refuses disclosure. The King remains free—authority intact, silence itself a sentence.

Key contrast Both read the silence as judicial withholding after refused light. Arminians typically stress a real opportunity squandered by responsible evasion; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s sovereign right to withhold disclosure from hardened, fear-bound hearts.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on back in the temple, chief priests and elders challenge Jesus’ authority while He teaches—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 21:23–27.

Arminian Notable voices

“When he was come into the temple, the chief priests came - Who thought he violated their right: and the elders of the people - Probably, members of the sanhedrim, to whom that title most properly belonged: which is the more probable, as they were the persons under whose cognizance the late action of Christ, in purging the temple, would naturally fall. These, with the chief priests, seem purposely to have appeared in a considerable company, to give the more weight to what they said, and if need were, to bear a united testimony against him.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 21:23–27)

Reformed Notable voices

“By what authority doest thou these things. As the other schemes and open attempts to attack Christ had not succeeded, the priests and scribes now attempt, by indirect methods, if they may possibly cause him to desist from the practice of teaching.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 21:23–27)
