Passage

Matthew 21

Verses 12–17 · ESV

After entering Jerusalem as Zechariah’s King, Jesus goes straight to the temple. He drives out commerce, heals the blind and lame, and receives the children’s Hosanna—while priests and scribes grow indignant—so Passion Week begins with cleansing, mercy, and contested praise.

Matthew 21:12

12And Jesus entered the temple and drove out all who sold and bought in the temple, and he overturned the tables of the money-changers and the seats of those who sold pigeons.

Arminian

Jesus enters the temple and acts with holy zeal: sellers and buyers are driven out; tables and seats overturned. The house meant for God is being cleared of traffic that crowds out prayer.

Classical Arminians hear a real moral summons. Grace exposes what distorts worship and calls people to turn. The King’s anger is not capricious; it presses a responsible response—leave exploitation and make room for God.

Reformed

The temple scene is deliberate royal judgment. Jesus’ driving out and overturning display His authority over Israel’s worship center at the start of Passion Week.

Reformed readers see providence and lordship together: the Son cleanses what belongs to His Father. Commerce that hindered true worship meets the King’s appointed confrontation—history moving on schedule toward the cross.

Matthew 21:13

13He said to them, "It is written, 'My house shall be called a house of prayer,' but you make it a den of robbers."

Arminian

Jesus grounds His act in Scripture: “My house shall be called a house of prayer,” yet they have made it “a den of robbers.” Isaiah’s hope and Jeremiah’s indictment meet in one sentence.

Arminian pastoral reading stresses sincere invitation beneath the rebuke. God still names His house a place of prayer; grace calls hearers to repent of robbery—spiritual and economic—and return to communion with Him.

Reformed

“It is written” frames the cleansing as fulfillment of God’s Word, not private opinion. The temple’s true purpose and Israel’s present distortion stand under biblical judgment.

Reformed emphasis: Christ speaks as Lord of the house. Hardness that turns prayer into plunder is exposed by Scripture itself; the King’s word both condemns the den and vindicates God’s holy claim.

Key contrast Both hear Scripture naming the temple a house of prayer against a den of robbers. Arminians typically stress a real call to repent and restore prayerful worship; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s authoritative judgment fulfilling God’s Word against hardness that has corrupted the house.

Matthew 21:14

14And the blind and the lame came to him in the temple, and he healed them.

Arminian

Immediately the blind and lame come to Jesus in the temple, and He heals them. Cleansing makes space for mercy; the King who drives out robbers welcomes the needy.

Classical Arminians linger on the approach: they came, and He healed. Assisting grace draws the weak toward Christ; healing answers a real seeking. The temple becomes again a place where the broken find the Savior.

Reformed

Mercy follows judgment in the same sacred space. Those shut out by custom or weakness are healed by the Messiah in the temple itself.

Reformed comfort: the Lord’s sovereign kindness is not delayed by human schemes. He who overturns tables also opens eyes and strengthens limbs—signs that the true King restores what God intends His house to be.

Matthew 21:15

15But when the chief priests and the scribes saw the wonderful things that he did and the children crying out in the temple, "Hosanna to the Son of David!" they were indignant,

Arminian

Priests and scribes see wonderful works and hear children cry, “Hosanna to the Son of David!”—yet they are indignant. Light and praise provoke resentment instead of faith.

Arminian warning with compassion: grace can be resisted. The same temple that hosts healing and Hosanna becomes a place of stubborn refusal. Responsible unbelief hardens when invitation is met with envy.

Reformed

Wonderful deeds and children’s Messianic praise should compel worship; leaders answer with indignation. Matthew exposes hearts closed under judgment.

Reformed sobriety: God may grant clear signs and true confession from little ones while the religious elite stiffen. Hardness here is not surprise outside providence; it advances the conflict that will lead to the cross.

Key contrast Both treat indignation at healing and Hosanna as tragic unbelief. Arminians typically emphasize a real grace resisted by responsible refusal; Reformed readers typically emphasize hardness under judgment—God’s clear witness advancing His purpose even through leaders’ opposition.

Matthew 21:16

16and they said to him, "Do you hear what these are saying?" And Jesus said to them, "Yes; have you never read, 'Out of the mouth of infants and nursing babies you have prepared praise'?"

Arminian

Leaders challenge Jesus: “Do you hear what these are saying?” He answers Yes—and cites Psalm 8: God has prepared praise from infants and nursing babies. The children’s Hosanna is not noise to silence but worship God intended.

Classical Arminians rejoice that God delights in responsive praise, even from the small. Grace opens mouths; Scripture legitimates their confession. Adults who refuse are summoned to learn from the little ones.

Reformed

Jesus affirms the children’s cry and roots it in Psalm 8: God Himself has prepared this praise. The question “have you never read?” indicts learned unbelief.

Reformed confession: praise is God’s preparation before it is human performance. Infants become instruments of ordained worship; the King receives what the Father has appointed, whether priests approve or not.

Key contrast Both affirm that the children’s Hosanna is God-approved praise from Psalm 8. Arminians typically stress grace enabling real, welcomed confession that summons adults to join; Reformed readers typically stress that God has prepared the praise—sovereign appointment using infants to shame resistant leaders.

Matthew 21:17

17And leaving them, he went out of the city to Bethany and lodged there.

Arminian

Jesus leaves them and lodges in Bethany outside the city. Withdrawal is not defeat; it is purposeful distance after confrontation.

Arminian pastoral note: grace does not always remain where it is scorned. The open invitation of the day stands; those left behind must reckon with a King who will return—and with their response to cleansing, healing, and praise.

Reformed

Jesus departs to Bethany for the night. The day’s work—cleansing, healing, contested praise—ends under His timing, not theirs.

Reformed assurance: the King is never trapped by indignation. Providence orders both presence and departure; Passion Week advances as He chooses, lodging where the Father’s plan places Him until the appointed hour.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
