Passage

Matthew 21

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

Jesus draws near Jerusalem from Bethphage and the Mount of Olives at the start of Passion Week. He enters the city as Zechariah’s humble King—hailed by crowds, soon to confront the temple—so that readers see royal claim and cross-bound purpose together.

Matthew 21:1

1Now when they drew near to Jerusalem and came to Bethphage, to the Mount of Olives, then Jesus sent two disciples,

Arminian

The journey turns toward Jerusalem. From Bethphage and the Mount of Olives, Jesus sends two disciples—active command that draws them into His purpose.

Classical Arminians hear grace that invites cooperation: the King does not need helpers, yet He summons them. Discipleship is response to a real call, not spectatorship at a distance.

Reformed

Matthew marks the approach to the city where the Son of David will suffer. The sending of two disciples is deliberate, not improvisation.

Reformed readers see providence tightening: every step toward Jerusalem is ordered by the Father’s will. The disciples move because the Lord sends; the Passion path is already under His sovereign timing.

Matthew 21:2

2saying to them, "Go into the village in front of you, and immediately you will find a donkey tied, and a colt with her. Untie them and bring them to me.

Arminian

Jesus’ knowledge is precise: a donkey and colt, tied, ready. He directs ordinary obedience—“untie… bring them to me”—toward a royal sign.

Arminian pastoral reading stresses trust that acts. Grace enables the disciples to obey what they cannot yet fully see; the King’s word is sufficient reason to go.

Reformed

Foreknowledge and command meet: Jesus knows what they will find and tells them what to do. The animals are already in place for the appointed entry.

Reformed comfort: nothing in this hour is left to chance. The Lord who rules history also rules the village lane; the colt waits because God has prepared the sign.

Matthew 21:3

3If anyone says anything to you, you shall say, 'The Lord needs them,' and he will send them at once."

Arminian

The password is confession: “The Lord needs them.” Ownership yields to the Master’s claim. Jesus anticipates a human question and supplies a gracious answer that should free the animals.

Classical Arminians note both the Lord’s request and the owner’s expected response. Grace can open a hand; the word of Jesus invites willing release rather than forced seizure.

Reformed

“The Lord needs them” asserts Christ’s rightful claim. The promised “he will send them at once” shows that even a stranger’s compliance sits inside Jesus’ certain word.

Reformed emphasis: the King’s need is not lack but lordship. God moves hearts to yield what His Son requires for the prophetic sign—assurance that the entry will not stall on a tied colt.

Key contrast Both confess Jesus’ rightful claim over the animals. Arminians typically stress a real, grace-enabled yielding invited by the Lord’s word; Reformed readers typically stress the certainty of Christ’s word and God’s effectual ordering of the owner’s response.

Matthew 21:4

4This took place to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet, saying,

Arminian

Matthew pauses to teach: the scene fulfills prophecy. Scripture’s promise is not decorative; it guides how we read the King’s arrival.

Arminian preaching rejoices that God keeps His word and still calls people to recognize the fulfillment. Prophecy invites faith—eyes opened to see Jesus as the promised King.

Reformed

Fulfillment language is Matthew’s theological heartbeat. What happens on the road is God keeping what He spoke.

Reformed readers hear decree and promise together: the prophet spoke because God determined; the entry occurs because God performs. Scripture interprets the event before the crowd fully understands it.

Matthew 21:5

5"Say to the daughter of Zion, 'Behold, your king is coming to you, humble, and mounted on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a beast of burden.'"

Arminian

Zechariah’s word arrives as invitation: “Behold, your king is coming to you.” The King is humble—mounted on a donkey, not a warhorse. Zion is addressed so she may welcome Him.

Classical Arminians linger on “coming to you”: sincere royal approach. Grace brings the King near; humility dismantles excuses. The proper response is glad reception, not armed revolt or cold indifference.

Reformed

The quotation crowns Christ as Zion’s King and defines His manner: humble, donkey-mounted. Majesty and meekness meet by prophetic design.

Reformed confession: this is the King God promised and now presents. Humility is not weakness of purpose but the appointed shape of redemption—sovereignty riding toward the cross in lowliness.

Key contrast Both adore the humble King of Zechariah 9:9. Arminians typically emphasize the sincere “coming to you” that summons Zion’s willing welcome; Reformed readers typically emphasize fulfillment under God’s decree—the King’s humility as the ordained path of saving purpose.

Matthew 21:6

6The disciples went and did as Jesus had directed them.

Arminian

Simple obedience: they went and did as directed. Faith here looks like feet and hands, not only confession.

Arminian pastoral note: assisting grace strengthens ordinary compliance. The disciples’ “yes” matters; God works through willing servants who take Jesus at His word.

Reformed

The disciples’ obedience is the appointed means. They do exactly what Jesus directed—no improvisation needed.

Reformed gratitude: God grants the obedience His plan requires. Their going is fruit of the Lord’s command and Spirit-enabled faithfulness within His larger design for the entry.

Matthew 21:7

7They brought the donkey and the colt and put on them their cloaks, and he sat on them.

Arminian

Cloaks become a makeshift saddle; Jesus sits as King. Disciples honor Him with what they have.

Classical Arminians see love answering grace: personal belongings offered for Christ’s use. Worship begins before the city gates—costly enough to give a cloak, humble enough to serve a donkey-mounted Lord.

Reformed

The enacted sign is complete: animals brought, cloaks laid, Jesus seated. Matthew’s detail seals the prophetic picture.

Reformed eyes see liturgy in motion. The disciples’ cloaks serve the King’s ordained entrance; every gesture advances the fulfillment God wrote beforehand.

Matthew 21:8

8Most of the crowd spread their cloaks on the road, and others cut branches from the trees and spread them on the road.

Arminian

The crowd joins: cloaks and branches carpet the road—a royal welcome. Public honor rises around Jesus.

Arminian caution with hope: praise can be real yet shallow. Grace still uses this moment to confront Jerusalem with her King; the same voices will need a deeper faith than festival excitement.

Reformed

Crowd enthusiasm carpets the way. God permits and uses the acclaim, even knowing how quickly it can turn.

Reformed sobriety: providence includes Hosanna week and Friday’s cry. The branches do not surprise the Father; the King’s path to the cross runs through this praise.

Matthew 21:9

9And the crowds that went before him and that followed him were shouting, "Hosanna to the Son of David! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord! Hosanna in the highest!"

Arminian

“Hosanna” and “Son of David” confess Messianic hope drawn from Psalm 118. The crowds bless the One who comes in the Lord’s name.

Classical Arminians urge that such light is genuine invitation. God presents Jesus as David’s Son; hearers are summoned to trust Him truly—not only shout in a parade, but receive the King on His terms.

Reformed

The acclamation is theologically true: Jesus is Son of David, the Blessed One who comes in Yahweh’s name. Heaven’s “Hosanna in the highest” frames earthly shouts.

Reformed preaching holds both celebration and sovereignty: God draws true confession from the crowd’s lips even when many hearts remain mixed. The title stands; the King’s identity does not depend on the crowd’s later loyalty.

Key contrast Both affirm the truth of the Hosanna confession. Arminians typically stress a real Messianic witness that calls for persevering faith beyond the parade; Reformed readers typically stress God’s purpose in the true confession itself—Christ’s identity secured even amid mixed human motives.

Matthew 21:10

10And when he entered Jerusalem, the whole city was stirred up, saying, "Who is this?"

Arminian

Jerusalem is “stirred up” into a question: “Who is this?” Recognition is not automatic; the city must face the claim.

Arminian pastoral edge: grace presses the question on a whole city. Stirring can become seeking. The honest inquiry is an open door—if they will follow the light toward the true answer.

Reformed

Entry shakes the city into interrogation. The question “Who is this?” is the hinge between parade and Passion.

Reformed reading: God unsettles Jerusalem on schedule. The stirred city is not chaos outside His plan; the question He raises will be answered by cross and resurrection, whether hearers bow or harden.

Key contrast Both treat the city’s question as a pivotal moment of revelation. Arminians typically emphasize a genuine opportunity to seek and receive the true answer; Reformed readers typically emphasize divine unsettling within providence—the question itself advancing God’s redemptive timetable.

Matthew 21:11

11And the crowds said, "This is the prophet Jesus, from Nazareth of Galilee."

Arminian

The crowd’s answer is partial: “the prophet Jesus, from Nazareth.” True as far as it goes, yet less than “Son of David” and Lord. Light received can still be incomplete.

Classical Arminians urge growth from partial confession toward fuller trust. Grace keeps teaching; Nazareth’s prophet is also Zion’s King—and the city will be invited to see more.

Reformed

Matthew records a true but thin identification. “Prophet… from Nazareth” names Jesus without exhausting who He is.

Reformed comfort and warning: God uses even incomplete confessions in the story, yet saving knowledge is His gift. The fuller revelation of this King will come through suffering, Scripture, and Spirit-opened eyes.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus draws near Jerusalem from Bethphage and the Mount of Olives at the start of Passion Week—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 21:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“The daughter of Sion - That is, the inhabitants of Jerusalem: the first words of the passage are cited from Isaiah lxii, 11; the rest from Zech. ix, 9. The ancient Jewish doctors were wont to apply these prophecies to the Messiah. On an ass - The Prince of Peace did not take a horse, a warlike animal.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 21:1–11)

Reformed Notable voices

“Then Jesus sent two disciples Jesus sends his disciples to bring an ass to him, not because he was wearied with the journey, but for a different reason; for, in consequence of the time of his death being at hand, he intended to show, by a solemn performance, what was the nature of his kingdom. He had begun, indeed, to do this at his baptism, but it remained that this demonstration should be given by him towards the end of his calling: for why did he hitherto refrain from the title of King, and now at length openly declare himself to be a King, but because he is not far from the end of his course?”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 21:1–11)
