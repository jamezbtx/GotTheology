Passage

Matthew 21

Verses 18–22 · ESV · Voices

The morning after the temple confrontation, Jesus returns toward Jerusalem hungry, curses a leafy but fruitless fig tree, and teaches the marveling disciples about faith that does not doubt and prayer that receives—linking judgment on barren religion with a call to living trust.

Matthew 21:18

18In the morning, as he was returning to the city, he became hungry.

Arminian

Morning finds Jesus returning to the city and genuinely hungry. The King’s true humanity frames what follows: need that seeks fruit, not a staged puzzle.

Classical Arminians hear grace still moving toward Jerusalem. The One who cleansed the temple comes again; hunger becomes the occasion for a living parable about fruit and faith that disciples must take to heart.

Reformed

Matthew notes the hour and Jesus’ hunger as He returns to the city. Ordinary need sits inside the Passion Week timetable.

Reformed readers see providence in the detail: the Son’s hunger is not accident but the appointed doorway to a sign. What follows will interpret fruitless religion under the Lord’s sovereign purpose.

Matthew 21:19

19And seeing a fig tree by the wayside, he went to it and found nothing on it but only leaves. And he said to it, "May no fruit ever come from you again!" And the fig tree withered at once.

Arminian

Leaves without fruit meet a word of judgment: “May no fruit ever come from you again!” The tree withers at once—an acted warning against show without substance, echoing Israel’s temple leadership and fruitless religion.

Arminian pastoral reading hears a real summons. Grace exposes barren profession and calls for living faith that bears fruit. Enabling grace is given so hearers may repent of leaf-only religion and become fruitful rather than wither under deserved warning.

Reformed

Jesus finds leaves only and pronounces a curse; the tree withers immediately. The sign judges appearance without fruit—aimed at fruitless Israel and temple religion under the King’s authority.

Reformed emphasis: this is a judicial act within providence, not impulsive frustration. The Lord who rules creation and covenant history displays what barrenness receives; the withering is certain because His word and purpose stand.

Key contrast Both read the withered fig as judgment on fruitless religion tied to Israel and the temple motif. Arminians typically stress a real warning that presses repentance, with enabling grace toward fruitful faith; Reformed readers typically stress a judicial sign under providence—Christ’s authoritative sentence on barrenness advancing God’s purpose.

Matthew 21:20

20When the disciples saw it, they marveled, saying, "How did the fig tree wither at once?"

Arminian

The disciples marvel and ask how the tree withered at once. Wonder is appropriate; the question opens them to Jesus’ teaching on faith.

Classical Arminians welcome honest amazement. Grace uses their surprise to draw them further—from spectacle to trust. The right response to the sign is not mere curiosity but readiness to learn what living faith and prayer require.

Reformed

Marvel and question mark the disciples’ response to the sudden withering. Jesus will interpret the sign for them.

Reformed comfort: God grants both the miracle and the teaching that follows. Their amazement is the appointed hinge; the Lord who withered the tree will also instruct His own about faith under His sovereign hand.

Matthew 21:21

21And Jesus answered them, "Truly, I say to you, if you have faith and do not doubt, you will not only do what has been done to the fig tree, but even if you say to this mountain, 'Be taken up and thrown into the sea,' it will happen.

Arminian

Jesus answers with a solemn promise: faith that does not doubt can share in what was done to the fig tree—and even command a mountain into the sea. The call is to undivided trust, not half-hearted profession.

Classical Arminians hear a real invitation empowered by assisting grace. God enables believers to trust without wavering; mountain language presses confident reliance on Him. Faith is living response God strengthens, not empty bravado—fruitful because grace makes steadfast trust possible.

Reformed

“Truly, I say to you” grounds mountain-moving faith in Jesus’ certain word. Faith without doubt is the instrument of God’s power, not human self-assertion.

Reformed confession: such faith is God’s gift, wrought by the Spirit. The promise does not hand sovereignty to the disciple; it assures that God works through true faith according to His purpose—removing what He wills, as surely as He withered the tree.

Key contrast Both take Jesus’ call to undoubting faith seriously. Arminians typically emphasize a real, grace-enabled trust believers are summoned to exercise; Reformed readers typically emphasize faith as God’s gift and instrument—mountain-moving power according to His sovereign purpose, not independent human will.

Matthew 21:22

22And whatever you ask in prayer, you will receive, if you have faith."

Arminian

The lesson widens to prayer: whatever you ask, you will receive, if you have faith. Asking is genuine; receiving is tied to trusting dependence on God.

Arminian pastoral hope: enabling grace sustains believing prayer. God sincerely invites His people to ask and receive; faith that leans on Him is met with the Father’s generosity. The warning of the fig tree and the promise of prayer together call for fruitful, living communion—not leafy unbelief.

Reformed

Prayer that receives is prayer of faith. “Whatever you ask” is held inside the larger biblical witness: God answers according to His will and glory.

Reformed assurance: faith does not bend God to our whims; it aligns asking with the Father’s purpose. The Spirit grants both the faith and the prayers God intends to answer—so the promise comforts without turning prayer into a blank check outside providence.

Key contrast Both affirm that faith-filled prayer receives from God. Arminians typically stress a sincere invitation and enabling grace for asking that truly receives; Reformed readers typically stress prayer answered according to God’s will and purpose—faith itself the gift that asks within His sovereign design.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on The morning after the temple confrontation, Jesus returns toward Jerusalem hungry—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 21:18–22.

Arminian Notable voices

“The disciples seeing it - As they went by, the next day. Jesus answering, said, If ye have faith - Whence we may learn, that one great end of our Lord in this miracle was to confirm and increase their faith: another was, to warn them against unfruitfulness.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 21:18–22)

Reformed Notable voices

“Matthew says that the city was moved, in order to inform us that the transaction did not take place secretly, or by stealth, but in the presence of all the people, and that the priests and scribes were not ignorant of it. Under this despicable aspect of the flesh the majesty of the Spirit was apparent; for how would they have endured that Christ should be conducted into the city, attended by the splendor of royalty, with so great danger to themselves, if they had not been seized with astonishment?”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 21:18–22)
