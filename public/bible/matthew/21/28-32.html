Sample passage

Matthew 21

Verses 28–32 · ESV

Still answering the temple leaders, Jesus tells the parable of the two sons: one refuses then goes; the other agrees then stays away. Saying yes is not the same as doing the Father’s will—and surprising repentant sinners enter the kingdom ahead of those who refused John’s light.

Matthew 21:28

28"What do you think? A man had two sons. And he went to the first and said, 'Son, go and work in the vineyard today.'

Arminian

Jesus opens with a question: What do you think? A father sends the first son to work in the vineyard today. The summons is clear, personal, and urgent—obedience is asked now, not in theory.

Classical Arminians hear enabling grace in the call itself. The Father’s word reaches both sons with real moral weight; they are responsible to answer. The parable will turn on whether that summons produces actual work, not mere words.

Reformed

Jesus presses the leaders with a parable about a father’s command: go work in the vineyard today. Authority is paternal and immediate; the vineyard is God’s field of obedience.

Reformed readers hear the King’s authority still in view after the John-question. Providence places this story before hardened shepherds so that saying and doing will expose them—God’s purpose advancing through a simple, searching illustration.

Matthew 21:29

29And he answered, 'I will not,' but afterward he changed his mind and went.

Arminian

The first son says, “I will not,” then afterward changes his mind and goes. Initial refusal is real; so is the later turn. Repentance here is a genuine change of mind that produces obedience.

Classical Arminians linger on that “afterward.” Under grace, a real change of mind remains possible—even after a blunt no. Responsible agency is not erased by prior refusal; the son who once refused can still enter the vineyard and do the Father’s will.

Reformed

The first son refuses, then afterward changes his mind and goes. The turn is not mere regret; it issues in actual work. Effectual turning shows itself in obedience, not in a softer tone alone.

Reformed confession: God can grant a genuine change of mind where there was open refusal. The parable’s point is fruit—going into the vineyard—by God’s purpose through a real, effectual repentance that does what the Father asked.

Key contrast Both treat the first son’s turn as true repentance that issues in obedience. Arminians typically stress a real change of mind still possible under grace after responsible refusal; Reformed readers typically stress effectual turning—God’s purpose bringing the son from no to actual work in the vineyard.

Matthew 21:30

30And he went to the other son and said the same. And he answered, 'I go, sir,' but did not go.

Arminian

The second son receives the same command and answers politely: “I go, sir”—yet he does not go. Respectful speech without obedience is empty. Saying yes can be a way of refusing while looking faithful.

Arminian pastoral warning: religious agreement is not the same as doing the Father’s will. Grace has given clear light; responsible unbelief can hide behind pious words. The tragedy is not rudeness but a yes that never becomes work.

Reformed

The other son says, “I go, sir,” but does not go. Verbal assent without obedience is exposed as disobedience. Politeness is not righteousness.

Reformed sobriety: hardness often wears religious manners. Leaders who confess with the lips while refusing the Father’s call illustrate judicial blindness—profession without fruit under God’s searching judgment.

Matthew 21:31

31Which of the two did the will of his father?" They said, "The first." Jesus said to them, "Truly, I say to you, the tax collectors and the prostitutes go into the kingdom of God before you.

Arminian

They correctly name the first son as the one who did the father’s will. Jesus then applies it sharply: tax collectors and prostitutes go into the kingdom of God before you. Surprising faith and repentance outrun religious refusal.

Classical Arminians hear both hope and indictment. Grace made a real change of mind possible for notorious sinners who responded; the leaders remain responsible for unbelief. Kingdom entry is by faith that obeys—not by pedigree or polite profession.

Reformed

They answer rightly—“the first”—and Jesus turns the blade: tax collectors and prostitutes enter the kingdom before you. God’s purpose brings unlikely believers in ahead of self-assured religion.

Reformed emphasis: kingdom entry is by God’s purpose through faith, not by religious status. The surprising advance of repentant sinners and the leaders’ lag both serve His sovereign design—grace effectual for some, judicial hardness for those who only say “sir.”

Key contrast Both hear surprising repentant sinners entering ahead of religious leaders. Arminians typically stress real response under grace versus responsible unbelief that keeps saying yes without going; Reformed readers typically stress God’s purpose—kingdom entry through faith for unlikely believers, and judicial hardness for polite non-doers.

Matthew 21:32

32For John came to you in the way of righteousness, and you did not believe him, but the tax collectors and the prostitutes believed him. And even when you saw it, you did not afterward change your minds and believe him."

Arminian

John came in the way of righteousness; the leaders did not believe him, but tax collectors and prostitutes did. Even after seeing that fruit, they did not afterward change their minds and believe. Prior light from John was clear; refusal remained responsible.

Classical Arminians press the “even when you saw it.” A real opportunity to change their minds persisted after public evidence of John’s fruit. Grace invited a later turn like the first son’s; they would not. Religious refusal against known light stands accountable.

Reformed

John came in righteousness; leaders refused belief while tax collectors and prostitutes believed. Seeing that response, they still did not afterward change their minds and believe. Prior prophetic light became a witness against hardened hearts.

Reformed diagnosis: judicial hardness of the leaders. God had sent John; some believed by His purpose through faith, and the leaders’ persistent unbelief—despite visible fruit—displays hardness under judgment, not mere lack of information.

Key contrast Both root the parable in belief or unbelief toward John’s prior light. Arminians typically emphasize a real chance to change their minds after seeing repentant sinners believe—opportunity responsibly refused; Reformed readers typically emphasize effectual faith in the unlikely and judicial hardness in leaders who would not afterward believe.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
