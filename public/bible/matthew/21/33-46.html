Passage

Matthew 21

Verses 33–46 · ESV · Voices

Jesus tells the parable of the wicked tenants: a master plants a vineyard, leases it, and sends servants—then his son—for fruit. The tenants beat, kill, and finally murder the heir. Citing Psalm 118, Jesus warns that the rejected stone becomes the cornerstone, and the kingdom will be taken from fruitless leaders and given to a people who produce its fruits.

Matthew 21:33

33"Hear another parable. There was a master of a house who planted a vineyard and put a fence around it and dug a winepress in it and built a tower and leased it to tenants, and went into another country.

Arminian

Jesus calls them to hear another parable. A master plants a vineyard, fences it, digs a winepress, builds a tower, leases it to tenants, and goes away. The setup echoes Isaiah 5: careful provision, entrusted stewardship, expected fruit.

Classical Arminians hear gracious investment. God has given Israel real privileges and a real charge to produce fruit. The lease is not a charade; tenants are responsible stewards of what the Master has sincerely entrusted.

Reformed

Another parable: a master plants, protects, and leases a vineyard, then goes abroad. Isaiah’s vineyard imagery stands behind the story—God’s care for His people and His claim on fruit.

Reformed readers hear the Owner’s purpose from the start. Providence plants and leases; history moves under His design. The vineyard is His, the tenants accountable, the coming conflict already framed by sovereign purpose.

Matthew 21:34

34When the season for fruit drew near, he sent his servants to the tenants to get his fruit.

Arminian

When fruit-season draws near, the master sends servants to get his fruit. The claim is rightful; the summons is real. Prophets arrive as genuine messengers of the Owner’s will.

Arminian pastoral reading: enabling grace has spoken through the prophets. The call to yield fruit is sincere and responsible. What follows will show whether tenants answer the Owner’s word or refuse it.

Reformed

Fruit-season comes; servants are sent for the Owner’s fruit. Prophetic mission belongs to God’s claim on His people.

Reformed emphasis: the Owner’s purpose advances through appointed messengers. Sending is not hope against His decree but the means by which His claim is pressed and history moves toward judgment and mercy.

Matthew 21:35

35And the tenants took his servants and beat one, killed another, and stoned another.

Arminian

The tenants seize the servants: beat one, kill another, stone another. Prophetic witness meets violent refusal. The tragedy is not confusion about the message but responsible rejection of known messengers.

Classical Arminians linger on the cost of refused light. Grace sent real servants; violence answers. Hardness here is moral agency against the Owner’s sincere claim—not mere misfortune.

Reformed

Servants are beaten, killed, stoned. Israel’s long pattern of resisting the prophets is compressed into the parable’s first wave.

Reformed sobriety: hardness under judgment shows itself in blood. God sent His servants; rejection of them serves His larger purpose even as it remains culpable rebellion against the Owner.

Matthew 21:36

36Again he sent other servants, more than the first. And they did the same to them.

Arminian

Again he sends other servants, more than the first—and the tenants treat them the same. Persistence in sending underscores the Owner’s patience; persistence in violence underscores responsible refusal.

Arminian accent: repeated, enlarging missions are sincere invitations to yield fruit. Grace does not quit at the first rejection; tenants remain accountable each time they refuse.

Reformed

More servants are sent; the same violence answers. Prophetic history lengthens under the Owner’s hand.

Reformed reading: God’s purpose includes repeated witness and repeated rejection. Patience is real, yet not aimless—every refused mission advances the story toward the Son and toward judicial reckoning.

Matthew 21:37

37Finally he sent his son to them, saying, 'They will respect my son.'

Arminian

Finally he sends his son, saying, “They will respect my son.” The climax is personal: the heir himself comes. The Owner’s expectation is expressed in good faith—the sending is sincere, not a setup for theater.

Classical Arminians hear here a real mission of the Son. Grace reaches its peak in Christ; the call to honor Him is genuine. That tenants will refuse does not empty the sincerity of the sending or the moral weight of the summons to respect the Son.

Reformed

Finally the son is sent: “They will respect my son.” The parable speaks with the Owner’s voice of rightful expectation even as the larger narrative knows the cross lies ahead.

Reformed confession: the Son’s mission unfolds under the Father’s decree. He is truly sent; rejection is truly wicked; yet the sending serves God’s purpose toward redemption and judgment. Providence frames the climactic mission without making the tenants’ guilt less real.

Key contrast Both treat the sending of the Son as the parable’s climax. Arminians typically stress a sincere sending and real call to respect the Son—responsible rejection of genuine grace; Reformed readers typically stress the Son’s mission under the Father’s decree—truly sent, truly rejected, yet advancing God’s purpose toward the cross.

Matthew 21:38

38But when the tenants saw the son, they said to themselves, 'This is the heir. Come, let us kill him and have his inheritance.'

Arminian

Seeing the son, the tenants plot: this is the heir—kill him and seize the inheritance. Recognition without reverence. They know who He is and choose murder for gain.

Classical Arminians press responsible rejection. Light enough to identify the heir becomes the occasion of calculated crime. Grace has made the Son known; unbelief answers with willful violence.

Reformed

They recognize the heir and conspire to kill him for the inheritance. Knowledge of identity fuels hatred, not worship.

Reformed diagnosis: judicial hardness can see and still destroy. God’s purpose will turn their plot toward the cross, yet their counsel remains wicked—recognition without faith under judgment.

Key contrast Both hear recognition of the heir twisted into murderous intent. Arminians typically emphasize responsible rejection of clear light about the Son; Reformed readers typically emphasize hardness that knows and still kills—evil counsel God will overrule for His purpose without excusing the crime.

Matthew 21:39

39And they took him and threw him out of the vineyard and killed him.

Arminian

They seize him, throw him outside the vineyard, and kill him. Rejection becomes exile and death—echoes of the cross outside the city. The Owner’s climactic gift is answered with murder.

Arminian pastoral grief: the sincere sending of the Son meets responsible, violent refusal. The tragedy is not that grace was unclear but that known light was hated to the end.

Reformed

The son is cast out and killed. The parable foreshadows Jesus’ rejection and death outside the gate.

Reformed confession: the cross is both human wickedness and divine purpose. Tenants act freely in evil; God is not surprised—His decree includes the rejected Son who will become the cornerstone.

Matthew 21:40

40When therefore the owner of the vineyard comes, what will he do to those tenants?"

Arminian

Jesus asks what the owner will do when he comes. The question invites their own verdict. Judgment is not arbitrary; it answers responsible bloodshed.

Classical Arminians hear a real summons to moral clarity. They are pressed to name justice for refused grace. The warning itself still invites repentance before the Owner’s coming falls.

Reformed

What will the owner do? Jesus draws the verdict from their own lips. Judgment belongs to the Owner’s coming.

Reformed emphasis: God’s judicial purpose is already in view. The question exposes hearts and sets up their self-condemning answer under providence.

Matthew 21:41

41They said to him, "He will put those wretches to a miserable death and let out the vineyard to other tenants who will give him the fruits in their seasons."

Arminian

They answer: miserable death for the wretches, and the vineyard leased to others who will give fruit in season. They pronounce the right sentence without yet seeing themselves in it.

Arminian reading: fruit remains the Owner’s just demand. A real transfer to fruit-bearing tenants is warned; the call to produce fruit still stands as genuine moral obligation under grace.

Reformed

They name death for the tenants and a new lease to fruit-bearing others. Their theology of justice is sound; their self-application is blind.

Reformed sobriety: God will judge and transfer stewardship according to His purpose. Fruit in season is the mark of those to whom He gives the vineyard—His design, not human renegotiation.

Matthew 21:42

42Jesus said to them, "Have you never read in the Scriptures: 'The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone; this was the Lord's doing, and it is marvelous in our eyes'?"

Arminian

Jesus cites Psalm 118: the stone builders rejected has become the cornerstone—the Lord’s doing, marvelous in our eyes. Rejection of the Son does not defeat God’s plan; it becomes the foundation of salvation for those who believe.

Classical Arminians hold both: builders responsibly reject, and the Lord exalts the rejected Stone. Marvel does not cancel human guilt; it magnifies grace that turns refused Christ into the church’s cornerstone for all who trust Him.

Reformed

Psalm 118 interprets the parable: rejected stone becomes cornerstone—this was the Lord’s doing. The builders’ refusal is real; God’s exaltation of Christ is decisive.

Reformed confession: the cross and exaltation are the Lord’s doing. Human rejection serves divine purpose; the marvelous turn from rejected Stone to cornerstone displays sovereignty that judges builders and saves a people.

Key contrast Both hear Psalm 118 as the key to the rejected Son. Arminians typically stress responsible rejection by the builders alongside the Lord’s marvelous exaltation of Christ for believers; Reformed readers typically stress that the cornerstone’s triumph is the Lord’s doing—sovereign purpose through and over the builders’ refusal.

Matthew 21:43

43Therefore I tell you, the kingdom of God will be taken away from you and given to a people producing its fruits.

Arminian

Therefore: the kingdom will be taken from you and given to a people producing its fruits. Transfer follows fruitlessness. The warning is sharp and still summons repentance—produce the fruit the kingdom requires.

Classical Arminians hear a real threat and a real call. Privilege without fruit can be lost; grace invites a people who will actually yield what the Owner seeks. Responsibility for fruit remains central.

Reformed

The kingdom will be taken from them and given to a fruit-bearing people. Judicial transfer follows hardened refusal of the Son.

Reformed emphasis: God gives the kingdom according to His purpose. Fruit marks the people He grants it to; removal from fruitless leaders is judgment under His decree, not a renegotiation of grace.

Key contrast Both hear kingdom transfer tied to fruit. Arminians typically stress a real warning that invites repentance and responsible fruit-bearing; Reformed readers typically stress judicial transfer by God’s purpose—kingdom given to a people He makes fruitful.

Matthew 21:44

44And the one who falls on this stone will be broken to pieces; and when it falls on anyone, it will crush him."

Arminian

Falling on the stone breaks; the stone falling crushes. Encounter with Christ is never neutral. Stubbling in unbelief wounds; final judgment destroys.

Arminian pastoral urgency: the warning still invites turning. Grace has set the Stone before them; responsible rejection leads toward crushing. The severity of the image presses repentance while there is time.

Reformed

The Stone either breaks the one who falls or crushes the one on whom it falls. Christ as cornerstone is also Christ as Judge.

Reformed confession: judicial outcome is certain for those who reject the Stone. God’s purpose exalts Him; opposition ends in ruin. Fear of the Stone’s fall is wiser than fear of crowds.

Key contrast Both treat the Stone as either Savior or Judge. Arminians typically emphasize a severe warning that still calls for repentance before crushing comes; Reformed readers typically emphasize certain judicial ruin under God’s purpose for all who finally oppose the cornerstone.

Matthew 21:45

45When the chief priests and the Pharisees heard his parables, they perceived that he was speaking about them.

Arminian

Chief priests and Pharisees perceive He is speaking about them. The parable lands. Recognition of indictment is not yet repentance, but light has named its hearers.

Classical Arminians note the moment of clarity: they know they are the tenants. Grace has exposed them; responsible response would be to turn, not to plot arrest.

Reformed

They perceive the parable is about them. Conviction of identity dawns without soft hearts.

Reformed diagnosis: light can illuminate and still harden. Providence uses the parable to unmask leaders; perception without faith advances the conflict toward the cross.

Matthew 21:46

46And although they were seeking to arrest him, they feared the crowds, because they held him to be a prophet.

Arminian

They seek to arrest Him but fear the crowds who hold Him a prophet. Ambition to silence Jesus is checked by reputation management, not by reverence for God.

Arminian warning: fear of man blocks both open violence and open repentance. The crowds’ regard for Jesus as prophet is light they could heed; instead they wait for a safer moment to refuse the Son.

Reformed

Arrest is desired; fear of the crowds restrains them. They dread popular opinion more than the Owner of the vineyard.

Reformed contrast: fear of man versus fear of God. Hardness calculates politics while providence holds the timetable. The King they would seize remains under the Father’s purpose until the appointed hour.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus tells the parable of the wicked tenants: a master plants a vineyard, leases it—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 21:33–46.

Arminian Notable voices

“A certain householder planted a vineyard - God planted the Church in Canaan; and hedged it round about - First with the law, then with his peculiar providence: and digged a wine press - Perhaps it may mean Jerusalem: and built a tower - The temple: and went into a far country - That is, left the keepers of his vineyard, in some measure, to behave as they should see good. He sent his servants - His extraordinary messengers, the prophets: to the husbandmen - The ordinary preachers or ministers of the Jews.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 21:33–46)

Reformed Notable voices

“The words of Luke are somewhat different; for he says that Christ spoke to the people, while here the discourse is addressed to the priests and scribes. But the solution is easy; for, though Christ spoke against them, he exposed their baseness in the presence of all the people.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 21:33–46)
