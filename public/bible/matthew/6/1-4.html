Passage

Matthew 6

Verses 1–4 · ESV · Voices

In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus turns from kingdom righteousness to the danger of performing piety for human applause—beginning with almsgiving that seeks the Father’s eye, not the crowd’s praise.

Matthew 6:1

1“Beware of practicing your righteousness before other people in order to be seen by them, for then you will have no reward from your Father who is in heaven.

Arminian

Jesus opens with a pastoral warning, not a ban on public good works. The issue is motive: righteousness practiced “in order to be seen” forfeits the Father’s reward. Shared gospel ground is clear—performance religion empties devotion of its true audience.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere call to heart-obedience enabled by grace. Believers truly can, under assisting grace, choose secrecy over show; the Father’s promised reward is gracious encouragement for those who respond in living faith, not a wage that purchases favor.

Reformed

The warning exposes the heart. God sees whether righteousness is theater or worship. Hypocrisy is not a small flaw; it empties the deed of heavenly reward because the deed was aimed at people, not at the Father.

Reformed readers stress that true piety is fruit of a new heart. The Father who is in heaven is not manipulated by spectacle; He rewards what His grace has wrought in secret integrity. Omniscience makes every audience of one decisive.

Key contrast Both condemn showy religion and affirm the Father’s reward. Arminians often stress grace-enabled moral agency against hypocrisy; Reformed readers often stress new-heart piety as the fruit of regenerating grace under God’s all-seeing care.

Matthew 6:2

2“Thus, when you give to the needy, sound no trumpet before you, as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and in the streets, that they may be praised by others. Truly, I say to you, they have received their reward.

Arminian

Almsgiving is assumed—“when you give”—yet trumpet-blowing for praise is rejected. Hypocrites get exactly what they sought: human applause. That is their full payment; heaven owes them nothing further.

Arminian pastoral emphasis: generosity must be a real act of love toward the needy, not a stage for the self. Grace enables sincere compassion; believers are responsible not to trade mercy for reputation.

Reformed

Jesus names the transaction: praise from others is the hypocrite’s reward—already received in full. Public religion for applause is spiritually bankrupt even when the gift helps someone.

Reformed comfort and warning meet here. God unmasks motives; the praise of streets and synagogues cannot substitute for the Father’s approval. True giving flows from hearts remade to love God and neighbor.

Matthew 6:3

3But when you give to the needy, do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing,

Arminian

The vivid image presses for self-forgetful generosity. Even the giver’s own self-congratulation is to be refused. Secret mercy protects the neighbor’s dignity and the giver’s humility.

Classical Arminians often linger on genuine agency under grace: you can learn, by the Spirit’s help, to give without rehearsing your virtue. Holiness here is practical and relational—love that does not keep score for the ego.

Reformed

Left-hand ignorance is hyperbolic wisdom against self-advertising piety. The disciple’s giving is to be so free of self-display that even private boasting is cut off.

Reformed accent: such humility is not native to fallen hearts. The Spirit forms a people who give because they have first received mercy—fruit of grace, not a ladder of self-made righteousness.

Matthew 6:4

4so that your giving may be in secret. And your Father who sees in secret will reward you.

Arminian

Secrecy is purposeful: the Father is the true audience. He “sees in secret” and “will reward you”—language of gracious fatherly encouragement, not cold transaction. Shared confession: God alone is worthy to be the eye for which we give.

Arminian reading treasures both the call and the promise. Grace enables secret obedience; the Father’s reward invites trust and persevering generosity without the narcotic of public praise.

Reformed

The Father’s omniscience is pastoral: nothing done for Him is wasted, and nothing done for show escapes His sight. Secret giving is covenant piety before the God who cares for His own.

Reformed assurance: the reward is certain because the Father is faithful. He sees; He will reward. Hypocrisy is exposed, and quiet faithfulness is held in His keeping.

Key contrast Both affirm the Father who sees in secret and rewards. Arminians commonly present the reward as gracious encouragement that motivates sincere, grace-enabled obedience; Reformed readers commonly present it as covenant care from the Father toward those whose secret piety His grace has produced.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on in the Sermon on the Mount—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 6:1–4.

Arminian Notable voices

“In the foregoing chapter our Lord particularly described the nature of inward holiness. In this he describes that purity of intention without which none of our outward actions are holy. This chapter contains four parts, The right intention and manner of giving alms, ver. 1-4.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 6:1–4)

Reformed Notable voices

“Beware In this passage, Christ exhorts his people to devote themselves sincerely to good works; that is, to endeavor, with simplicity, to do what is right before God, and not to make a parade before men. A very necessary admonition; for in all virtues the entrance of ambition is to be dreaded, and there is no work so laudable, as not to be in many instances corrupted and polluted by it.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 6:1–4)
