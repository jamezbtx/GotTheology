Passage

Matthew 6

Verses 5–15 · ESV

Jesus next reforms prayer: reject hypocritical display and empty pagan wordiness; pray to the Father in secret; and learn the pattern of the Lord’s Prayer, with a sober word on forgiving others as we seek forgiveness.

Matthew 6:5

5“And when you pray, you must not be like the hypocrites. For they love to stand and pray in the synagogues and at the street corners, that they may be seen by others. Truly, I say to you, they have received their reward.

Arminian

Prayer, like almsgiving, is assumed—but love of being seen ruins it. The hypocrite’s reward is again human attention, paid in full. Shared ground: prayer is for God, not for an audience.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere summons to honest communion. Grace enables real turning from performance; believers must refuse the addiction of spiritual reputation and practice prayer as trust.

Reformed

Jesus diagnoses a love: they love to be seen. Prayer becomes theater. The verdict is the same as with alms—they have their reward already.

Reformed preaching warns that even sacred words can serve the flesh. True prayer is Spirit-wrought dependence on the Father, not a public credential of piety.

Matthew 6:6

6But when you pray, go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret. And your Father who sees in secret will reward you.

Arminian

The closed door trains the heart. Private prayer does not forbid corporate worship; it forbids using prayer as display. The Father “who is in secret” meets the child who comes without an audience.

Arminian pastoral note: this is grace-enabled real communion. The universal call stands—come to the Father who sees and rewards. Secrecy protects sincerity so that prayer becomes relationship, not performance.

Reformed

Room and shut door dramatize undivided attention to the Father. He is present in secret; He sees; He rewards. Prayer’s dignity rests on who God is, not on who watches us.

Reformed comfort: the Father’s certain care for His own means secret prayer is never wasted. Dependence is learned where applause cannot reach.

Key contrast Both urge secret prayer to the Father who sees and rewards. Arminians often stress sincere, grace-enabled communion open to all who come; Reformed readers often stress Spirit-wrought dependence and the Father’s sure care for His people.

Matthew 6:7

7“And when you pray, do not heap up empty phrases as the Gentiles do, for they think that they will be heard for their many words.

Arminian

Jesus rejects pagan technique: heaping empty phrases to coerce the divine. Length and volume are not leverage. God is not impressed by word-count.

Arminians and Reformed alike warn against mechanical religion. The Arminian accent adds that prayer remains a real conversation—grace frees us to speak simply and sincerely rather than to manipulate.

Reformed

Gentile wordiness assumes that deities must be worn down. Jesus dismantles that theology. Many words do not purchase a hearing.

Reformed note: prayer is not a technology of the will. It is the cry of those who know they are heard by grace, not by verbal accumulation.

Matthew 6:8

8Do not be like them, for your Father knows what you need before you ask him.

Arminian

The Father’s prior knowledge is good news. We pray not to inform an ignorant God but to trust a knowing Father. Asking still matters—as communion, dependence, and alignment of desire.

Classical Arminian piety stresses the Father’s sincere fatherliness toward those who seek Him: His knowledge invites confidence, not fatalistic silence. Prayer is how grace-taught children live with a Father who already cares.

Reformed

Omniscience undercuts anxiety and superstition. The Father knows before we ask—so prayer is not briefing God but depending on Him.

Reformed assurance leans here: God’s fatherly knowledge of His people’s needs is certain. We ask because He commands and delights to provide, not because He lacks information.

Matthew 6:9

9Pray then like this: “Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name.

Arminian

Jesus gives a pattern, not a mere formula. “Our Father” gathers the family; “in heaven” keeps reverence; “hallowed be your name” puts God’s honor first. Shared worship: prayer begins with God, not with self.

Arminian reading gladly hears the wide welcome of “Father”—a sincere invitation to approach by grace-enabled faith. Holiness of the Name shapes every request that follows.

Reformed

Adoption language (“Father”) and transcendence (“in heaven”) meet. The first petition seeks God’s Name to be treated as holy—God’s glory before our shopping list.

Reformed confession: we call God Father because of the Son and the Spirit’s work. The pattern forms a people whose first concern is the honor of the God who has claimed them.

Matthew 6:10

10Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.

Arminian

We ask for the King’s reign and for obedient alignment with His will—on earth matching heaven’s glad submission. Prayer resists both despair and self-rule.

Classical Arminians emphasize that this petition is a real commitment: grace enables us to seek and yield to God’s will rather than merely recite it. The kingdom call invites active, willing discipleship.

Reformed

Kingdom and will petitions confess that history and hearts belong to God. We pray for what God has promised—His reign advancing—and for our wills to be bent to His.

Reformed accent: God’s kingdom comes by His power; our praying is Spirit-taught agreement with that purpose, not human engineering of the age to come.

Matthew 6:11

11Give us this day our daily bread,

Arminian

Daily bread teaches dependent simplicity. We ask for today’s provision—physical and, by extension, all needful sustenance—without anxious hoarding of trust.

Arminian pastoral tone: the Father invites every disciple to ask. Grace makes petition natural; we exercise real trust by bringing ordinary needs to God rather than pretending self-sufficiency.

Reformed

“This day” and “daily” train contentment and continual reliance. Bread is gift, not entitlement seized from chance.

Reformed comfort: the Father who knows needs (v. 8) is the Father who gives bread. Providence is personal; prayer is how dependents live under that care.

Matthew 6:12

12and forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors.

Arminian

Forgiveness is requested in the same breath as bread—daily mercy for moral debt. The “as we also have forgiven” clause binds our vertical plea to horizontal practice. Shared ground: no disciple may seek pardon while clutching vengeance.

Classical Arminians often hear a living relational dynamic: forgiven people must practice forgiveness; grace enables this hard obedience as a real condition of ongoing fellowship with the Father.

Reformed

Debts name guilt before God. The petition assumes ongoing need of pardon in the Christian life, and it refuses a spirituality that wants mercy without giving mercy.

Reformed readers typically see forgiving others as the mark and fruit of those who have tasted the Father’s forgiveness—not a meritorious ladder by which sinners climb into grace, but evidence of grace already at work.

Key contrast Both insist forgiven people forgive. Arminians commonly stress a genuine relational condition believers must practice under assisting grace; Reformed theology commonly stresses that forgiving is a necessary mark of the regenerate life God works, not the basis on which forgiveness is earned.

Matthew 6:13

13And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil.

Arminian

We confess weakness: we ask not to be led into trial that would undo us, and we plead for rescue from evil (or the evil one). Prayer is honest about moral frailty.

Arminian emphasis: this is grace-enabled dependence. Believers truly seek the Father’s protection and cooperate with that help by fleeing temptation; deliverance is requested because we cannot secure ourselves alone.

Reformed

The petition rejects self-confidence. God is asked to spare us from entrapment and to deliver us from evil’s power. Holiness is not autonomous grit.

Reformed pastoral use: the same God who keeps His people teaches them to pray for keeping. Preservation and prayer belong together—dependence, not presumption.

Matthew 6:14

14For if you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you,

Arminian

Jesus restates the prayer’s logic in prose. Forgiving others is bound to receiving the Father’s forgiveness. The warning is pastoral and serious, not ornamental.

Classical Arminians treat this as a real condition in the believer’s walk: an unforgiving spirit resists the relational flow of mercy. Grace enables and requires the hard practice of release; fellowship with the Father is not compatible with cherished bitterness.

Reformed

The “for” explains the petition. A life that refuses to forgive cannot coherently claim the Father’s forgiveness. Jesus protects the gospel from cheap grace that never becomes gracious.

Reformed reading: this is diagnostic of true faith. Those whom God has forgiven become forgiving; the Father’s forgiveness is not purchased by our mercy, yet it is never present where vengeance is cherished as lord.

Key contrast A classic pastoral tension. Arminians often stress condition and ongoing relationship—believers must forgive. Reformed readers often stress evidence and fruit—an unforgiving spirit reveals a heart not living in the forgiveness it claims. Both reject both license and works-righteousness.

Matthew 6:15

15but if you do not forgive others their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses.

Arminian

The negative mirror is stark. Withheld forgiveness blocks the path we ask to walk. Jesus loves His disciples enough to threaten false assurance that keeps score against neighbors while seeking erasure of its own debts.

Arminian pastoral care: repent of unforgiveness; seek enabling grace to release others. The verse fuels urgency, not despair—the Father still calls, and grace still assists those who turn.

Reformed

The severity matches the Sermon’s honesty. An unforgiving posture and the Father’s forgiveness cannot finally coexist as a settled way of life. This is not a denial of justification by faith; it is a refusal of a faith that never forgives.

Reformed comfort in the warning: God works a forgiving heart in His people. Where bitterness reigns unchecked, the call is to examine whether we have known the Father’s mercy at all—and to flee to Christ, who alone makes forgiven forgivers.

Key contrast Fairness on vv. 14–15: neither tradition treats forgiveness of others as optional. Arminians typically describe a relational condition practiced by grace-enabled agency; Reformed theology typically describes an inseparable fruit and mark of those truly forgiven—warning the hypocrite, assuring the humbled believer who struggles yet seeks to forgive.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
