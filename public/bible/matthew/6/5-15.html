Passage

Matthew 6

Verses 5–15 · WEB · Voices

Jesus next reforms prayer: reject hypocritical display and empty pagan wordiness; pray to the Father in secret; and learn the pattern of the Lord’s Prayer, with a sober word on forgiving others as we seek forgiveness.

Matthew 6:5

5“When you pray, you shall not be as the hypocrites, for they love to stand and pray in the synagogues and in the corners of the streets, that they may be seen by men. Most certainly, I tell you, they have received their reward.

Arminian

Prayer, like almsgiving, is assumed—but love of being seen ruins it. The hypocrite’s reward is again human attention, paid in full. Both confess: prayer is for God, not for an audience.

Wesley’s heirs hear a sincere summons to honest communion. Grace enables turning from performance; believers must refuse the addiction of spiritual reputation and practice prayer as trust.

Reformed

Jesus diagnoses a love: they love to be seen. Prayer becomes theater. The verdict is the same as with alms—they have their reward already.

Reformed preaching warns that even sacred words can serve the flesh. True prayer is Spirit-wrought dependence on the Father, not a public credential of piety.

Matthew 6:6

6But you, when you pray, enter into your inner room, and having shut your door, pray to your Father who is in secret; and your Father who sees in secret will reward you openly.

Arminian

The closed door trains the heart. Private prayer does not forbid corporate worship; it forbids using prayer as display. The Father “who is in secret” meets the child who comes without an audience.

This is grace-enabled communion. The universal call stands—come to the Father who sees and rewards. Secrecy protects sincerity so that prayer becomes relationship, not performance.

Reformed

Room and shut door dramatize undivided attention to the Father. He is present in secret; He sees; He rewards. Prayer’s dignity rests on who God is, not on who watches us.

The Father’s certain care for His own means secret prayer is never wasted. Dependence is learned where applause cannot reach.

Key contrast Both urge secret prayer to the Father who sees and rewards. Arminians often stress sincere, grace-enabled communion open to all who come; Reformed readers often stress Spirit-wrought dependence and the Father’s sure care for His people.

Matthew 6:7

7In praying, don’t use vain repetitions as the Gentiles do; for they think that they will be heard for their much speaking.

Arminian

Jesus rejects pagan technique: heaping empty phrases to coerce the divine. Length and volume are not leverage. God is not impressed by word-count.

Arminians and Reformed alike warn against mechanical religion. The Arminian accent adds that prayer remains a conversation—grace frees us to speak simply and sincerely rather than to manipulate.

Reformed

Gentile wordiness assumes that deities must be worn down. Jesus dismantles that theology. Many words do not purchase a hearing.

Prayer is not a technology of the will. It is the cry of those who know they are heard by grace, not by verbal accumulation.

Matthew 6:8

8Therefore don’t be like them, for your Father knows what things you need before you ask him.

Arminian

The Father’s prior knowledge is good news. We pray not to inform an ignorant God but to trust a knowing Father. Asking still matters—as communion, dependence, and alignment of desire.

Classical Arminian piety stresses the Father’s sincere fatherliness toward those who seek Him: His knowledge invites confidence, not fatalistic silence. Prayer is how grace-taught children live with a Father who already cares.

Reformed

Omniscience undercuts anxiety and superstition. The Father knows before we ask—so prayer is not briefing God but depending on Him.

God’s fatherly knowledge of His people’s needs is certain. We ask because He commands and delights to provide, not because He lacks information.

Matthew 6:9

9Pray like this: “‘Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy.

Arminian

Jesus gives a pattern, not a mere formula. “Our Father” gathers the family; “in heaven” keeps reverence; “may your name” puts God’s honor first. Shared worship: prayer begins with God, not with self.

Arminian reading gladly hears the wide welcome of “Father”—a sincere invitation to approach by grace-enabled faith. Holiness of the Name shapes every request that follows.

Reformed

Adoption language (“Father”) and transcendence (“in heaven”) meet. The first petition seeks God’s Name to be treated as holy—God’s glory before our shopping list.

We call God Father because of the Son and the Spirit’s work. The pattern forms a people whose first concern is the honor of the God who has claimed them.

Matthew 6:10

10Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven.

Arminian

We ask for the King’s reign and for obedient alignment with His will—on earth matching heaven’s glad submission. Prayer resists both despair and self-rule.

Classical Arminians emphasize that this petition is a commitment: grace enables us to seek and yield to God’s will rather than merely recite it. The kingdom call invites active, willing discipleship.

Reformed

Kingdom and will petitions confess that history and hearts belong to God. We pray for what God has promised—His reign advancing—and for our wills to be bent to His.

God’s kingdom comes by His power; our praying is Spirit-taught agreement with that purpose, not human engineering of the age to come.

Matthew 6:11

11Give us today our daily bread.

Arminian

Daily bread teaches dependent simplicity. We ask for today’s provision—physical and, by extension, all needful sustenance—without anxious hoarding of trust.

The Father invites every disciple to ask. Grace makes petition natural; we exercise genuine trust by bringing ordinary needs to God rather than pretending self-sufficiency.

Reformed

“Today” and “daily” train contentment and continual reliance. Bread is gift, not entitlement seized from chance.

The Father who knows needs (v. 8) is the Father who gives bread. Providence is personal; prayer is how dependents live under that care.

Matthew 6:12

12Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors.

Arminian

Forgiveness is requested in the same breath as bread—daily mercy for moral debt. The “as we also forgive” clause binds our vertical plea to horizontal practice. Either reading: no disciple may seek pardon while clutching vengeance.

Arminian interpreters often hear a living relational dynamic: forgiven people must practice forgiveness; grace enables this hard obedience as a condition of ongoing fellowship with the Father.

Reformed

Debts name guilt before God. The petition assumes ongoing need of pardon in the Christian life, and it refuses a spirituality that wants mercy without giving mercy.

Reformed commentators often see forgiving others as the mark and fruit of those who have tasted the Father’s forgiveness—not a meritorious ladder by which sinners climb into grace, but evidence of grace already at work.

Key contrast Both insist forgiven people forgive. Arminians commonly stress a relational condition believers must practice under assisting grace; Reformed theology commonly stresses that forgiving is a necessary mark of the regenerate life God works, not the basis on which forgiveness is earned.

Matthew 6:13

13Bring us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one. For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen.’

Arminian

We confess weakness: we ask not to be led into trial that would undo us, and we plead for rescue from evil (or the evil one). Prayer is honest about moral frailty.

This is grace-enabled dependence. Believers truly seek the Father’s protection and cooperate with that help by fleeing temptation; deliverance is requested because we cannot secure ourselves alone.

Reformed

The petition rejects self-confidence. God is asked to spare us from entrapment and to deliver us from evil’s power. Holiness is not autonomous grit.

The God who keeps His people teaches them to pray for keeping. Preservation and prayer belong together—dependence, not presumption.

Matthew 6:14

14“For if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you.

Arminian

Jesus restates the prayer’s logic in prose. Forgiving others is bound to receiving the Father’s forgiveness. The warning is pastoral and serious, not ornamental.

Wesleyan Arminians treat this as a condition in the believer’s walk: an unforgiving spirit resists the relational flow of mercy. Grace enables and requires the hard practice of release; fellowship with the Father is not compatible with cherished bitterness.

Reformed

The “for” explains the petition. A life that refuses to forgive cannot coherently claim the Father’s forgiveness. Jesus protects the gospel from cheap grace that never becomes gracious.

This is diagnostic of true faith. Those whom God has forgiven become forgiving; the Father’s forgiveness is not purchased by our mercy, yet it is never present where vengeance is cherished as lord.

Key contrast The verse raises a classic pastoral tension. Arminians often stress condition and ongoing relationship—believers must forgive. Reformed readers often stress evidence and fruit—an unforgiving spirit reveals a heart not living in the forgiveness it claims. Both reject both license and works-righteousness.

Matthew 6:15

15But if you don’t forgive men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses.

Arminian

The negative mirror is stark. Withheld forgiveness blocks the path we ask to walk. Jesus loves His disciples enough to threaten false assurance that keeps score against neighbors while seeking erasure of its own debts.

Repent of unforgiveness; seek enabling grace to release others. The verse fuels urgency, not despair—the Father still calls, and grace still assists those who turn.

Reformed

The severity matches the Sermon’s honesty. An unforgiving posture and the Father’s forgiveness cannot finally coexist as a settled way of life. This is not a denial of justification by faith; it is a refusal of a faith that never forgives.

Reformed comfort in the warning: God works a forgiving heart in His people. Where bitterness reigns unchecked, the call is to examine whether we have known the Father’s mercy at all—and to flee to Christ, who alone makes forgiven forgivers.

Key contrast Fairness on vv. 14–15: neither tradition treats forgiveness of others as optional. Arminians typically describe a relational condition practiced by grace-enabled agency; Reformed theology typically describes an inseparable fruit and mark of those truly forgiven—warning the hypocrite, assuring the humbled believer who struggles yet seeks to forgive.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 6:5–15)

“It is uncertain whether this form was once only or twice delivered by Christ to his disciples. Some think that the latter is more probable; because Luke says that he was requested to do it, while Matthew represents him as teaching it of his own accord.”
