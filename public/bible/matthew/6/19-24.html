Sample passage

Matthew 6

Verses 19–24 · ESV

Jesus turns from secret piety to the heart’s treasure: refuse perishable earthly stockpiles; store up in heaven; keep a healthy eye; and confess that no one can serve both God and money.

Matthew 6:19

19“Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy and where thieves break in and steal,

Arminian

Earthly treasure is vulnerable—moth, rust, thieves. Jesus does not forbid wise provision; He forbids hoarding that anchors the self in what decays. Shared warning: greed and security-in-stuff are spiritual dangers.

Classical Arminians press real responsibility: under grace, disciples can and must refuse to build identity on perishable wealth. The call is sincere—turn from mammon’s false shelter.

Reformed

Jesus exposes the fragility of earthly stockpiles. What moth and rust ruin cannot bear the weight of a human heart. The command redirects desire, not merely accounting.

Reformed readers hear providence and judgment together: God orders a world where idols fail. Hearts clinging to what thieves can take need the Lord who cannot be stolen.

Matthew 6:20

20but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust destroys and where thieves do not break in and steal.

Arminian

The alternative is positive: invest in heaven—generosity, obedience, love that endures. Heavenly treasure is secure because God keeps it. Shared hope: the Father’s realm is the true vault.

Arminian pastoral note: grace enables this reordering. Believers are invited and responsible to pursue lasting good—faith working through love—rather than to drift into accumulation.

Reformed

Heavenly treasure is secure by God’s keeping. The disciple’s true riches are with the Father, beyond decay and theft.

Reformed comfort: this laying up is not self-salvation by philanthropy; it is the fruit of hearts taught to value what God values—kingdom priorities wrought by grace.

Matthew 6:21

21For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.

Arminian

Treasure locates the heart. Desire follows investment; the map of spending and clinging reveals worship. Shared diagnosis: the heart is not neutral about money.

Classical Arminians linger on agency under grace: examine where you store hope, and by the Spirit’s help relocate treasure toward God. Responsibility and invitation meet—your heart can be re-aimed.

Reformed

Jesus states a law of the soul: treasure pulls the heart after it. The issue is not only budget but belonging—what owns you.

Reformed accent: a new heart learns new treasure. Regenerating grace reorders loves so that heavenward investment is joy, not mere duty.

Key contrast Both teach that treasure reveals and directs the heart. Arminians often stress grace-enabled responsibility to relocate desire from greed to God; Reformed readers often stress new affections from regenerating grace that make heavenly treasure the heart’s true home.

Matthew 6:22

22“The eye is the lamp of the body. So, if your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light,

Arminian

A healthy (single, generous) eye fills the person with light. Vision shapes life: what you fix on illumines or darkens conduct. Shared wisdom: spiritual sight matters for discipleship.

Arminian reading hears a call to clear-eyed loyalty—grace enabling undivided focus on God rather than envious or greedy looking.

Reformed

The eye-as-lamp image teaches that perception governs the self. A sound eye means the whole person walks in light.

Reformed note: clarity of spiritual vision is gift before it is achievement—the Spirit opens eyes to treasure God rightly.

Matthew 6:23

23but if your eye is bad, your whole body will be full of darkness. If then the light in you is darkness, how great is the darkness!

Arminian

A bad (stingy, evil, double) eye darkens the whole person. Worst of all is darkness mistaken for light—self-deceived greed wearing the mask of wisdom. Shared urgency: examine what you call “light.”

Arminians press the warning as real moral responsibility: refuse envious sight; seek grace to see truly before darkness becomes total.

Reformed

Bad eye equals darkness throughout. The climax is terrifying: when what you take for light is darkness, the ruin is great.

Reformed preaching unmasks the heart’s capacity for self-deception about wealth and desire—and points to the Lord who alone gives light.

Matthew 6:24

24“No one can serve two masters, for either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve God and money.

Arminian

Exclusive allegiance is required. Dual mastery is impossible; love and devotion will sort themselves. “You cannot serve God and money” is a sincere, non-negotiable summons. Shared confession: mammon is a rival lord.

Classical Arminian accent: grace enables a real choice of masters. Believers are responsible to refuse money’s claim and to serve God with undivided loyalty—not as self-salvation, but as living faith.

Reformed

Jesus states impossibility, not mere difficulty. Two masters yield hate/love and devotion/despising. Money (mammon) competes for the throne of the heart.

Reformed accent: only a Spirit-wrought reorientation of lordship frees from mammon. Serving God is the fruit of a claimed heart, not a negotiated split allegiance.

Key contrast Both insist you cannot serve God and money. Arminians commonly stress grace-enabled, responsible exclusive loyalty against greed; Reformed readers commonly stress Spirit-wrought transfer of lordship—new affections that dethrone mammon.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
