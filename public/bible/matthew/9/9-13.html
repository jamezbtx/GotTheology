Passage

Matthew 9

Verses 9–13 · ESV · Voices

From the healed paralytic, Jesus passes to a tax booth and calls Matthew. Table fellowship with tax collectors and sinners draws Pharisaic objection; Jesus answers with the physician saying and Hosea’s word: He desires mercy, and He came to call sinners, not the self-assured “righteous.”

Matthew 9:9

9As Jesus passed on from there, he saw a man called Matthew sitting at the tax booth, and he said to him, “Follow me.” And he rose and followed him.

Arminian

Jesus sees Matthew at the booth—compromised work, public shame—and speaks two words of summons: “Follow me.” Matthew rises and follows. Calling meets a real response.

Classical Arminians treasure responsible discipleship under preceding grace. The call is sincere and sufficient to enable following; Matthew’s rising is not coerced theater but faith’s obedience. Shared ground: Jesus initiates; sinners do not climb into apostleship alone.

Reformed

The King sees and speaks, and the tax collector rises. Effectual calling is pictured in narrative form: the word creates followers.

Reformed accent: Matthew’s rising displays regenerating grace. “Follow me” is not a bare offer left to autonomous choice; it is the Shepherd’s voice that makes sheep follow. Same Jesus who forgave the paralytic now claims a collaborator with Rome.

Key contrast Both affirm Jesus’ initiative and Matthew’s real following. Arminians typically stress a grace-enabled, resistible-in-principle call that Matthew freely obeys; Reformed readers typically stress an effectual call that infallibly brings the elect to follow.

Matthew 9:10

10And as Jesus reclined at table in the house, behold, many tax collectors and sinners came and were reclining with Jesus and his disciples.

Arminian

Mission becomes a meal. Many like Matthew gather; Jesus and His disciples recline with them. Holiness here is not quarantine but welcoming presence that transforms.

Arminian pastoral reading: the gospel makes room at the table for the morally messy who will come. Grace invites company; disciples learn mercy by sitting where Jesus sits.

Reformed

“Behold” again: scandalous fellowship under Messiah’s lordship. The house becomes a sign of the kingdom’s guest list.

Reformed comfort: Christ is not defiled by sinners’ company; He consecrates the gathering. Discipleship includes learning who belongs because He called them.

Matthew 9:11

11And when the Pharisees saw this, they said to his disciples, “Why does your teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?”

Arminian

Pharisees question the disciples, not Jesus directly—pressure sideways. Their “why” assumes purity means separation from the unclean and the compromised.

Arminian warning: religious boundary-keeping can miss the Physician. The question still faces every church: will we eat with those Jesus seeks, or only with those who already look whole?

Reformed

Sight without insight. They see table fellowship and miss covenant mercy. Objection to Jesus’ company is objection to His mission.

Reformed reading: unbelief polices holiness while refusing Hosea. The critique reveals hearts that need the same call Matthew received.

Matthew 9:12

12But when he heard it, he said, “Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick.”

Arminian

Jesus answers with a clinic, not a club. The sick need a doctor; admitting need is the doorway. Self-assessed “wellness” keeps people from the only Healer.

Arminian invitation: come as sick. Grace does not require you to pretend health. The Physician is willing; the call is to recognize your need and trust Him.

Reformed

Diagnosis first: humanity’s problem is sickness unto death, not a minor scratch. Christ is Physician by office, not hobby.

Reformed accent: the metaphor assumes inability to self-cure. Those who feel “well” in their own righteousness expose the deeper illness of pride. Mercy finds the sick Christ came to heal.

Matthew 9:13

13Go and learn what this means: ‘I desire mercy, and not sacrifice.’ For I came not to call the righteous, but sinners.”

Arminian

Hosea 6:6 rebukes sacrifice without mercy. Jesus sends Pharisees to school: learn God’s heart. His mission statement is clear—He came to call sinners.

Classical Arminians hear a universal gospel tone in the coming and calling: sinners are summoned in truth; the “righteous” who refuse need are self-excluded. Mercy is what God desires; responding sinners are welcomed. Pastoral shared ground: no one is saved by ritual pride.

Reformed

“Go and learn” is prophetic rebuke. Mercy over sacrifice is Yahweh’s own preference; Jesus embodies it. “I came” states incarnation’s purpose: to call sinners.

Reformed reading: “the righteous” are the self-righteous in irony; none are righteous before God (Rom 3). The call that saves is Christ’s particular, powerful summons of sinners—Matthew at the booth as exhibit A.

Key contrast Both reject empty religion and celebrate Jesus’ mission to sinners. Arminians typically stress a sincere call extended to sinners who may respond; Reformed readers typically stress that the effective saving call reaches those the Father gives the Son, while exposing counterfeit righteousness.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus passes to a tax booth and calls Matthew—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 9:9–13.

Arminian Notable voices

“He saw a man named Matthew - Modestly so called by himself. The other evangelists call him by his more honourable name, Levi. Sitting - In the very height of his business, at the receipt of custom - The custom house, or place where the customs were received.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 9:9–13)

Reformed Notable voices

“Jesus saw a man sitting at the customhouse. The custom-house has usually been a place noted for plundering and for unjust exactions, and was at that time particularly infamous. In the choice of Matthew out of that place, not only to be admitted into the family of Christ, but even to be called to the office of Apostle, we have a striking instance of the grace of God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 9:9–13)
