Passage

Matthew 9

Verses 27–31 · ESV

Two blind men follow Jesus, crying for mercy as Son of David. Indoors He probes their faith, touches their eyes, and opens them—then warns silence they cannot keep as fame spreads.

Matthew 9:27

27And as Jesus passed on from there, two blind men followed him, crying aloud, “Have mercy on us, Son of David!”

Arminian

Blind yet following, shouting for mercy, confessing Jesus as Son of David—Messiah. Need becomes loud prayer.

Arminian pastoral reading: cry out. Mercy is asked because it is available. Messianic title on needy lips is already faith speaking.

Reformed

Davidic confession from the sightless. Providence draws them after the Healer.

Reformed accent: “Have mercy” is the right liturgy of the helpless. The title they use is Spirit-taught truth, not mere flattery.

Matthew 9:28

28When he entered the house, the blind men came to him, and Jesus said to them, “Do you believe that I am able to do this?” They said to him, “Yes, Lord.”

Arminian

Jesus questions ability-faith before He acts: Do you believe I am able? Their “Yes, Lord” is personal confession.

Classical Arminians treasure this dialogue. Christ invites responsible trust; they answer freely under grace. Ability confessed, lordship named—faith’s proper object.

Reformed

The question draws out faith He will honor. “Yes, Lord” is the confession regeneration produces.

Reformed reading: Jesus is not uncertain of His power; He is forming and revealing trust. Shared ground: without faith in His ability, the request is hollow.

Key contrast Both prize the men’s “Yes, Lord.” Arminians typically stress a sincere, grace-enabled confession they must make; Reformed readers typically stress that this believing answer is itself God’s gift.

Matthew 9:29

29Then he touched their eyes, saying, “According to your faith be it done to you.”

Arminian

Touch and word unite. “According to your faith” measures reception by trust in Him, not by technique.

Arminian application: expect according to confidence in Christ’s ability—not according to self-worth. Faith is the hand; He is the giver.

Reformed

Faith is the appointed measure of reception, never the fountain of power. Christ’s word effects what faith receives.

Reformed nuance: “according to your faith” chastens unbelief and comforts weak trust without making faith a meritorious cause.

Matthew 9:30

30And their eyes were opened. And Jesus sternly warned them, “See that no one knows about it.”

Arminian

Sight given; silence commanded. Jesus manages messianic fame for mission wisdom.

Arminian note: obedience after gift matters. Gratitude listens to the Giver’s timing, even when joy wants to speak.

Reformed

Opening eyes is creative ease. The stern warning protects the path to the cross from distorted kingship expectations.

Reformed reading: Lordship includes the right to silence witnesses for a season.

Matthew 9:31

31But they went away and spread his fame through all that district.

Arminian

They disobey the silence—common human failure under joy. Fame spreads anyway.

Arminian pastoral honesty: even healed people can stumble in timing. Christ’s mission is not finally thwarted by imperfect messengers; still, learn to heed His warnings.

Reformed

Human failure sits beside divine mercy. Their disobedience does not erase the miracle or derail the Father’s plan.

Reformed comfort: God can overrun flawed witness; that never excuses ignoring Jesus’ word.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
