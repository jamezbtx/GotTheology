Passage

Matthew 9

Verses 18–26 · ESV · Voices

While Jesus teaches, a ruler kneels for his dead daughter; on the way, a woman with a twelve-year hemorrhage touches His fringe and is healed. At the house, mockery yields to Jesus’ hand, and the girl arises—twin mercies of interrupted journey and restored life.

Matthew 9:18

18While he was saying these things to them, behold, a ruler came in and knelt before him, saying, “My daughter has just died, but come and lay your hand on her, and she will live.”

Arminian

A ruler interrupts teaching with worshipful kneeling and daring faith: touch her, and she will live—even after death. Desperation becomes trust.

Arminian pastoral reading: come as you are, with the need you cannot fix. Grace meets faith that asks Jesus to act. The plea is personal and hopeful, not presumptuous magic.

Reformed

“Behold” again: authority figures kneel. Faith names Jesus’ hand as life’s instrument after death has spoken.

Reformed accent: such trust is gift. The ruler’s words rest on Christ’s power under the Father’s will. Death is not final where the Lord of life is present.

Matthew 9:19

19And Jesus rose and followed him, with his disciples.

Arminian

Jesus rises and goes. The Teacher becomes the companion of grief. Disciples learn mercy by walking toward a death-house.

Arminian note: the willing Savior. He does not delay behind excuses; He follows need. Shared ground: compassion in motion.

Reformed

The King accompanies the request. Disciples are drawn into His path of mercy.

Reformed reading: providence includes this detour that will double as miracle for a bleeding woman and a dead child.

Matthew 9:20

20And behold, a woman who had suffered from a discharge of blood for twelve years came up behind him and touched the fringe of his garment,

Arminian

Twelve years of uncleanness and isolation; she approaches from behind—ashamed yet believing. The fringe of the garment is enough if Jesus is who He is.

Arminian pastoral care: chronic suffering can still reach for Christ. Faith may be trembling and quiet; it is still faith that comes.

Reformed

Hidden misery meets holy fringe. Levitical uncleanness sought the Holy One without announcement.

Reformed comfort: no years of bleeding outrun Christ’s reach. The approach itself is ordered under mercy.

Matthew 9:21

21for she said to herself, “If I only touch his garment, I will be made well.”

Arminian

Inner speech of faith: contact with Jesus means wellness. She reasons from who He is to what He can do.

Classical Arminians honor this responsible trust. She acts on conviction grace has awakened. Minimal contact, maximal confidence in Him—not in technique.

Reformed

Faith speaks before lips do. “I will be made well” rests on Christ’s sufficiency.

Reformed accent: saving and healing trust are God-wrought. Her resolve does not create power; it receives the One who has it.

Matthew 9:22

22Jesus turned, and seeing her he said, “Take heart, daughter; your faith has made you well.” And instantly the woman was made well.

Arminian

He turns, sees, and names her “daughter”—public honor for private faith. “Your faith has made you well” (literally saved/healed) ties her trust to His gift; healing is instant.

Arminian reading: faith is the appointed means of receiving mercy. Jesus affirms her trust without flattering her strength. Shared ground: He is the healer; faith is the empty hand.

Reformed

Jesus will not leave her anonymous. Daughter-language adopts; faith-language explains the instrument, not the ultimate cause.

Reformed nuance: “your faith has made you well” means faith as God-given channel, not autonomous force. Instant healing seals the word of the Son.

Key contrast Both honor the woman’s faith and Christ’s power. Arminians typically stress faith as grace-enabled means that truly receives; Reformed readers typically stress that even this faith is gift, while Christ alone effects the healing.

Matthew 9:23

23And when Jesus came to the ruler’s house and saw the flute players and the crowd making a commotion,

Arminian

Professional mourning is already underway—death treated as settled. Noise fills the house where Jesus intends life.

Arminian warning: culture’s verdict is not final. Do not let commotion drown the Lord’s arrival.

Reformed

Funeral liturgy meets the Lord of the living. The scene is set for unbelief’s laugh.

Reformed reading: human certainty about death will be overturned by the One who commands.

Matthew 9:24

24he said, “Go away, for the girl is not dead but sleeping.” And they laughed at him.

Arminian

Jesus reframes death as sleep under His authority. The crowd laughs—faithlessness as mockery.

Arminian pastoral note: ridicule often greets hope. Keep company with Jesus’ word, not the laugh track of despair.

Reformed

Sleep is death’s temporary name when Christ is present. Their laughter judges them.

Reformed accent: He speaks reality they cannot see. Resurrection power is already implied in His speech.

Matthew 9:25

25But when the crowd had been put outside, he went in and took her by the hand, and the girl arose.

Arminian

Mockers expelled; Jesus takes her hand; she arises. Touch and life again—gentle power.

Arminian comfort: the Savior enters rooms of grief and restores. Arise is both gift and call into renewed life.

Reformed

Authority clears the room, then raises the dead by hand. Narrative economy matches divine ease.

Reformed comfort: the girl’s arising previews greater resurrection hope secured in Christ.

Matthew 9:26

26And the report of this went through all that district.

Arminian

News spreads. Mercy refuses to stay secret for long. Testimony invites others to seek the same Lord.

Arminian invitation: let the report reach you as summons—come to Jesus with death-sized needs.

Reformed

Providence multiplies witness through the district. Fame serves the kingdom’s advance under the Father’s plan.

Reformed note: report is not chaos; it prepares ears for the King.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on while Jesus teaches, a ruler kneels for his dead daughter; on the way—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 9:18–26.

Arminian Notable voices

“Just dead - He had left her at the point of death, Mark v, 23. Probably a messenger had now informed him she was dead. Coming behind - Out of bashfulness and humility. Take courage - Probably she was struck with fear, when he turned and looked upon her, Mark v, 33; Luke viii, 47; lest she should have offended him, by touching his garment privately; and the more so, because she was unclean according to the law, Lev. xv, 25.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 9:18–26)

Reformed Notable voices

“While he was speaking these things to them. Those who imagine that the narrative, which is here given by Mark and Luke, is different from that of Matthew, are so clearly refuted by the passage itself, that there is no necessity for a lengthened debate.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 9:18–26)
