Passage

Matthew 9

Verses 1–8 · ESV · Voices

Back in His own city, Jesus meets a paralytic brought by friends. He first forgives sins, then heals the body—proving the Son of Man’s authority on earth against scribal charges of blasphemy. Mercy that reaches the deeper need also vindicates itself in visible power.

Matthew 9:1

1And getting into a boat he crossed over and came to his own city.

Arminian

Matthew picks up after the Gadarenes: Jesus returns by boat to “his own city” (Capernaum). Ministry resumes where ordinary life and synagogue controversy already know Him.

Classical Arminians notice movement toward people. The Lord who was rejected on one shore willingly returns to another. Grace keeps seeking; geography does not exhaust His compassion.

Reformed

Crossing and arrival are not random. The King returns under the Father’s purpose to the place Scripture will soon fill with teaching, calling, and conflict.

Reformed readers hear providence in the route. What looks like travel is mission advancing—same Messiah, same authority, now aimed at forgiveness publicly claimed.

Matthew 9:2

2And behold, some people brought to him a paralytic, lying on a bed. And when Jesus saw their faith, he said to the paralytic, “Take heart, my son; your sins are forgiven.”

Arminian

Friends carry what the man cannot. Jesus “saw their faith” and speaks courage plus pardon before any request for legs. The deepest need is named first.

Arminian pastoral reading treasures faith that acts—bringing, trusting, interceding. Grace enables such trust; the word “Take heart, my son” invites personal reception of forgiveness freely offered in Christ.

Reformed

Faith is seen, and forgiveness is declared. Jesus addresses the root: sin before symptoms. Sonship language comforts while authority claims what only God may grant.

Reformed accent: saving mercy is not negotiated by the bedridden. Christ initiates the decisive word. Shared ground with Arminians: forgiveness is Jesus’ gift, not human self-repair.

Key contrast Both rejoice that Jesus forgives and that friends’ faith matters. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled, responsible trust that brings and receives; Reformed readers typically stress that faith itself is God’s gift and that Christ’s sovereign word creates the pardon it announces.

Matthew 9:3

3And behold, some of the scribes said to themselves, “This man is blaspheming.”

Arminian

Scribes reason silently: forgiveness belongs to God alone, so Jesus’ claim must be blasphemy—unless He is who He is. Unbelief diagnoses wrongly what mercy has done.

Arminian warning: religious knowledge can harden into resistance. The offer of pardon confronts hearts that prefer control to grace. Listeners are responsible for how they answer the claim.

Reformed

The charge assumes monotheism correctly and identity wrongly. If Jesus is not God the Son, the scribes are right; Matthew’s narrative will show they are not.

Reformed reading: hostility to forgiveness exposes the heart. Providence places this conflict so the Son’s authority will be displayed, not debated into neutrality.

Matthew 9:4

4But Jesus, knowing their thoughts, said, “Why do you think evil in your hearts?”

Arminian

Jesus knows unspoken thoughts. Evil here is not honest question but hostile verdict against mercy. He invites self-examination: why resist the forgiveness God sends?

Classical Arminians hear a real moral address. Hearts can think evil or turn. Christ’s knowledge leaves no private refuge; His question still calls for repentance.

Reformed

Omniscience meets unbelief. The Lord of the heart names evil thinking before it is spoken.

Reformed comfort and warning: nothing is hidden from the Judge who also saves. The same knowledge that exposes the scribes will soon vindicate the Son of Man’s claim.

Matthew 9:5

5For which is easier, to say, ‘Your sins are forgiven,’ or to say, ‘Rise and walk’?

Arminian

Words of pardon are “easier” to say because they cannot be checked by eyes; words of healing invite immediate proof. Jesus sets up a visible seal for an invisible gift.

Arminian application: do not settle for cheap talk about grace. True authority shows itself. Faith may trust forgiveness and also expect the Lord who speaks to act.

Reformed

The riddle exposes empty religion. Claiming God’s prerogative without power would be fraud; Jesus will join the harder-to-fake sign to the greater gift.

Reformed accent: both forgiveness and healing rest on the same divine authority. The question prepares the proof that the Son of Man truly forgives.

Matthew 9:6

6But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins”—he then said to the paralytic—“Rise, pick up your bed and go home.”

Arminian

Purpose clause: “that you may know.” Healing serves revelation. “Son of Man” claims Daniel’s figure with earth-side authority to forgive—now.

Arminians stress knowable, offered assurance. Jesus wants people to know His authority so they may trust Him. The command “Rise… go home” is grace that enables response; the man must rise, and he can.

Reformed

Authority is declared, then exercised. Forgiveness is not deferred to a distant court alone; the Son of Man holds it “on earth.”

Reformed reading: the miracle is pedagogical and effectual. Christ’s word creates what it commands. Knowledge of His authority is given so faith may rest on the One the Father sent.

Key contrast Both confess the Son of Man’s real authority to forgive. Arminians often emphasize that Jesus makes this knowable so hearers may respond in faith; Reformed readers often emphasize that the same sovereign word both proves and effects what God has purposed.

Matthew 9:7

7And he rose and went home.

Arminian

Obedience is immediate: rise, carry, go. Forgiven and healed, he walks into ordinary life as living evidence.

Arminian pastoral note: grace restores agency. The man is not a prop; he acts. Home becomes the first mission field of a pardoned life.

Reformed

The narrative is spare because the power is total. He rose—exactly as commanded.

Reformed comfort: when Christ speaks life, paralysis yields. The walk home is fruit of divine authority, not self-generated strength.

Matthew 9:8

8When the crowds saw it, they were afraid, and they glorified God, who had given such authority to men.

Arminian

Fear and glory mingle. Crowds rightly ascribe the gift to God, yet Matthew has shown the authority centered in Jesus. Wonder should become worshipful trust.

Arminian invitation: do not stop at amazement. The God who gives such authority calls you to receive the forgiveness the Son of Man still offers.

Reformed

Awe and doxology are proper first responses. “To men” glimpses the incarnate mystery—authority given in the Son who is truly man, yet more than a mere man.

Reformed reading: God is glorified when Christ’s authority is seen. The crowd’s praise is true as far as it goes; fuller confession will name Jesus as Lord.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on back in His own city, Jesus meets a paralytic brought by friends—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 9:1–8.

Arminian Notable voices

“Seeing their faith - Both that of the paralytic, and of them that brought him. Son - A title of tenderness and condescension. This man blasphemeth - Attributing to himself a power (that of forgiving sins) which belongs to God only. Which is easier - Do not both of them argue a Divine power?”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 9:1–8)

Reformed Notable voices

“This passage shows, that Capernaum was generally believed to be the birth-place of Christ, because his visits to it were frequent: for there is no room to doubt, that it is the same history which is related by the three Evangelists, though some circumstances may be more exactly related by one of them than by another. Luke says that scribes had come from various parts of Judea, who were spectators when Christ healed the paralytic; and at the same time states indirectly, that there were others who also received healing through the grace of Christ.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 9:1–8)
