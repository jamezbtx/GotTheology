Sample passage

Matthew 9

Verses 14–17 · ESV

John’s disciples ask why they and the Pharisees fast while Jesus’ disciples do not. Jesus answers with wedding joy, a coming removal of the bridegroom, and twin images of new cloth and new wine—kingdom life that cannot be patched onto unreformed forms.

Matthew 9:14

14Then the disciples of John came to him, saying, “Why do we and the Pharisees fast, but your disciples do not fast?”

Arminian

A sincere puzzle from John’s circle: disciplined fasting marks their piety and the Pharisees’, yet Jesus’ disciples feast. Comparison can breed confusion even among the serious.

Arminian pastoral note: honest questions are welcome. Grace does not mock seekers. Still, the question assumes that spiritual vitality is measured by shared austerity rather than by the Bridegroom’s presence.

Reformed

Disciples of the forerunner press the Messiah with a piety comparison. Fasting is not condemned; timing and meaning are.

Reformed reading: revelation progresses. What fit John’s waiting season is not the full measure of life with the Bridegroom present. Scripture interprets piety under Christ’s lordship, not peer pressure.

Matthew 9:15

15And Jesus said to them, “Can the wedding guests mourn as long as the bridegroom is with them? The days will come when the bridegroom is taken away from them, and then they will fast.”

Arminian

Wedding imagery: Jesus is Bridegroom; disciples are guests. Mourning-fasts clash with His presence. Yet He foretells removal—cross-shaped absence—and then fasting will fit.

Classical Arminians hear both joy and warning. Stay near the Bridegroom while He offers Himself; do not invent grief He has not appointed. When He is “taken away,” disciplined longing has its place. Shared ground: Jesus, not ritual calendars, sets the meaning of fasting.

Reformed

Messianic self-claim: Bridegroom of God’s people. Presence defines the feast; “taken away” quietly points to the passion under the Father’s plan.

Reformed accent: redemptive history governs practice. The church will fast in the overlap of ages, but never as if the Bridegroom failed. Joy and sorrow both submit to His timeline.

Key contrast Both center fasting on Jesus’ presence and coming absence. Arminians often emphasize responsive discipleship that rejoices or fasts as He appoints; Reformed readers often emphasize the sovereign schedule of redemption that defines those seasons.

Matthew 9:16

16No one puts a piece of unshrunk cloth on an old garment, for the patch tears away from the garment, and a worse tear is made.

Arminian

New cloth on old cloth worsens the tear. Kingdom newness cannot be a patch on unreformed religion.

Arminian application: do not reduce Jesus to an upgrade for prideful systems. He calls for new life—repentance and faith—not cosmetic religion. Hearers remain responsible to receive the new rather than force it onto the old.

Reformed

The parable exposes incompatibility. Messiah’s work is not a repair kit for self-made righteousness.

Reformed comfort: God brings a new covenant reality in Christ. Trying to stitch gospel wine onto works-garments only deepens the rip.

Matthew 9:17

17Neither is new wine put into old wineskins. If it is, the skins burst and the wine is spilled and the skins are destroyed. But new wine is put into fresh wineskins, and so both are preserved.”

Arminian

New wine needs fresh skins—or both are lost. Jesus preserves what is truly new by fitting forms to new life.

Arminian pastoral reading: receive the gospel’s newness; let structures serve life in Christ. Preservation is the goal—both wine and skins—when hearts yield to the Bridegroom’s way.

Reformed

New wine is the kingdom inaugurated in Christ; fresh skins are the people and patterns God appoints for it.

Reformed accent: God preserves His work. The point is not novelty for its own sake but fidelity to what the Father gives in the Son—life that old wineskins of unbelief cannot contain.

Key contrast Both reject forcing Jesus into unreformed religion. Arminians typically stress willing reception of new life; Reformed readers typically stress God’s provision of new covenant reality that unbelief’s forms cannot hold.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
