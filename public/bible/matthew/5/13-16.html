Passage

Matthew 5

Verses 13–16 · ESV

After the Beatitudes name the blessed character of the kingdom, Jesus turns to identity: disciples are salt and light in the world. The Sermon moves from who they are under the King’s blessing to how that life preserves, flavors, and illuminates for the Father’s glory.

Matthew 5:13

13“You are the salt of the earth, but if salt has lost its taste, how shall its saltiness be restored? It is no longer good for anything except to be thrown out and trampled under people’s feet.”

Arminian

“You are” is identity before strategy. Salt preserves and seasons; disciples, shaped by the Beatitudes, are meant to keep the world from decay and give gospel flavor where life has gone bland.

Classical Arminians hear a serious call: grace has made them salt, and they must not lose savor through compromise. Responsible discipleship matters—witness can grow dull, and the warning is real. Shared ground: saltiness is gift and vocation, not self-made superiority.

Reformed

Jesus declares what His people are by His word. Salt of the earth is not a ladder they climb but a status the King confers on those He has blessed in the Beatitudes.

Reformed accent: useless “salt” warns against empty profession, yet true discipleship is preserved by the Lord who named them. Identity precedes performance; fruitlessness exposes counterfeit, while genuine saltiness is maintained under Christ’s keeping grace.

Key contrast Both treat salt as God-given vocation with a real warning. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled responsibility not to lose savor; Reformed readers typically stress identity conferred by the King and the Lord’s preservation of true discipleship.

Matthew 5:14

14“You are the light of the world. A city set on a hill cannot be hidden.”

Arminian

Light of the world echoes Israel’s calling and Christ’s own claim—now shared with disciples. Visibility is part of the gift: a hill-city is meant to be seen, offering hope to those still in darkness.

Arminian pastoral tone: light is offered to the world. Grace places the church where others can find the way; hiding is neither humility nor faithfulness. The invitation goes out—come toward the light the Father has set among the nations.

Reformed

Again the indicative: “You are.” The world’s light is not invented by activist zeal but given by the One who is Light. The hill-city image stresses corporate witness under God’s placement.

Reformed comfort: God sets His people where they cannot finally be concealed. Mission is providence as much as program—the Father positions the church so that Christ’s light, not human spotlight, draws attention to Himself.

Matthew 5:15

15“Nor do people light a lamp and put it under a basket, but on a stand, and it gives light to all in the house.”

Arminian

Lamps belong on stands. Concealment contradicts the purpose of lighting. Disciples who have received the gospel are meant to illuminate the household and, by extension, the wider world.

Classical Arminians press the practical: do not smother conscience, testimony, or mercy under fear or convenience. Grace-enabled witness is for “all in the house”—near neighbors first, then beyond. Light unused is light misused.

Reformed

The proverb assumes a lit lamp—God’s initiative in kindling—and then refuses the absurdity of covering it. Placement on the stand serves others’ good under the Master’s design.

Reformed reading: usefulness is fruit of what God has already lit. The stand is vocation and circumstance ordered by providence; the lamp does not boast of self-ignition, yet neither may it hide what the Lord has kindled for the house.

Matthew 5:16

16“In the same way, let your light shine before others, so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven.”

Arminian

Shining is commanded so that others see good works and glorify the Father—not the disciple. Visible holiness and mercy are means of witness: the world is meant to notice grace at work in ordinary obedience.

Arminian emphasis: responsible discipleship offers light; good works invite glory to God and can draw people toward faith. Shared confession: the goal is the Father’s honor, never self-display or works-righteousness.

Reformed

“Let your light shine” flows from identity already given. Good works are seen so that glory rises to the Father in heaven—fruit that points upward, not a ladder for earning standing.

Reformed accent: works are evidence and instrument of witness, never the ground of acceptance. The Father who named His children in the Beatitudes is the One glorified when their lit lives commend His grace.

Key contrast Both reject showy self-glory and affirm that good works serve the Father’s praise. Arminians often stress grace-enabled shining as responsible witness offered to the watching world; Reformed readers often stress works as Spirit-wrought fruit that display an identity already given, never a basis for righteousness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
