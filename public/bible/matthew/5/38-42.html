Passage

Matthew 5

Verses 38–42 · ESV

Jesus next addresses retaliation. The “eye for an eye” principle limited vengeance in Israel’s courts; the King now calls disciples to absorb insult and excess demand without returning blow for blow—kingdom generosity that breaks the cycle of payback.

Matthew 5:38

38“You have heard that it was said, ‘An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.’

Arminian

Lex talionis restrained revenge by matching penalty to harm. Jesus quotes what “was said,” preparing to press beyond courtroom equivalence into personal discipleship.

Classical Arminians hear continuity with God’s justice and a call beyond minimal fairness. Grace invites hearers to refuse the scorekeeping heart—not because justice is wrong, but because the kingdom forms a people who can absorb cost for love’s sake.

Reformed

The Mosaic measure protected the weak from endless blood feuds. Christ, Lord of the Law, now addresses His disciples’ private retaliations.

Reformed reading: the King interprets His own law. What limited public justice is not a blank check for personal vengeance; Jesus shepherds His people into a deeper righteousness He Himself will embody.

Matthew 5:39

39But I say to you, Do not resist the one who is evil. But if anyone slaps you on the right cheek, turn to him the other also.

Arminian

“But I say” is royal authority. Turning the other cheek renounces payback for insult; it is costly love, not cowardice or approval of evil.

Arminian pastoral note: disciples, enabled by grace, can choose non-retaliation. The command is sincere and demanding—kingdom people may break the cycle. Shared ground: Jesus is not licensing oppression of the helpless, but forbidding personal revenge.

Reformed

The slap on the right cheek pictures humiliation more than mortal combat. Christ’s word forms a people who refuse to answer insult with insult.

Reformed accent: such meekness is Spirit-wrought likeness to the crucified Lord. Non-resistance here is discipleship under the King, not a denial that magistrates bear the sword (Rom 13).

Key contrast Both reject personal vengeance. Arminians typically stress a real, grace-enabled choice to absorb insult; Reformed readers typically stress Spirit-formed conformity to Christ while distinguishing personal non-retaliation from the state’s just use of force.

Matthew 5:40

40And if anyone would sue you and take your tunic, let him have your cloak as well.

Arminian

Lawsuit and tunic/cloak escalate the call: surrender more than the plaintiff demands. Kingdom honor is not clinging to garments when love can disarm greed.

Arminian emphasis: responsible generosity under grace—disciples may willingly lose rights rather than win bitter contests. The aim is peace and witness, not naïveté about every circumstance.

Reformed

Jesus trains hearts free from possessiveness. Extra giving under pressure displays trust in the Father’s care.

Reformed comfort: the King who calls for open-handedness also provides for His own. Fruit of new hearts includes readiness to prefer reconciliation over clinging to cloak and status.

Matthew 5:41

41And if anyone forces you to go one mile, go with him two miles.

Arminian

Forced escort (familiar under occupation) becomes an opportunity for surprising kindness—the second mile. Grace turns compulsion into voluntary excess.

Classical Arminians urge intentional love that exceeds the demand. Disciples choose the extra mile as witness; resentment is refused even when rights are pressed.

Reformed

The second mile is kingdom freedom inside constraint. Christ’s people are not defined by the oppressor’s power but by the Father’s character.

Reformed note: such willingness is not self-salvation by heroics; it is fruit of belonging to the Servant King who went farther than any mile for His people.

Matthew 5:42

42Give to the one who begs from you, and do not refuse the one who would borrow from you.”

Arminian

Open-handedness toward beggar and borrower crowns the paragraph. Love shares; stinginess contradicts the Father’s generosity already shown in the Beatitudes’ world.

Arminian pastoral wisdom: give freely while exercising stewardship and discernment—Jesus forms a liberal heart, not reckless enabling. Grace received becomes grace extended.

Reformed

Generosity is the family likeness of those who know a giving Father. Refusal that springs from fear or greed is rebuked.

Reformed accent: open hands flow from hearts made new. Giving is obedience and witness, never a ladder into the kingdom already given in Christ.

Key contrast Both commend liberal giving. Arminians often stress grace-enabled, responsible open-handedness; Reformed readers often stress generosity as Spirit fruit from those already secured in Christ, not a work that purchases standing.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
