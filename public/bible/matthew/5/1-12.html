Passage

Matthew 5

Verses 1–12 · ESV · Voices

Jesus opens the Sermon on the Mount by sitting as Israel’s Teacher-King. Crowds gather; disciples draw near; and the Beatitudes announce who is truly blessed under the kingdom of heaven—not the self-secure, but the needy who receive God’s grace and walk in its fruit.

Matthew 5:1

1Seeing the crowds, he went up on the mountain, and when he sat down, his disciples came to him.

Arminian

Jesus sees the crowds, ascends, and sits—the posture of a rabbi whose word carries weight. Disciples come to Him: teaching begins with nearness, not distance.

Classical Arminians hear an open-air invitation already in the scene. Crowds may listen; disciples lean in. Grace draws people toward the Teacher, and coming to Him is a real, grace-enabled response that the sermon will deepen.

Reformed

Matthew’s mountain echoes Sinai and signals a new covenant word from the greater Moses. The King sits; disciples assemble under His authority.

Reformed reading stresses providence in the gathering: the same Lord who will speak blessing has arranged hearers. Discipleship here is not self-appointment but coming to the seated Christ under the Father’s purpose.

Matthew 5:2

2And he opened his mouth and taught them, saying:

Arminian

“Opened his mouth” marks deliberate, weighty teaching. What follows is not casual advice but kingdom announcement meant to be heard and obeyed.

Arminian pastoral note: Jesus teaches so that people may truly receive and live the word. Instruction assumes responsible hearers under preceding grace—ears that can attend, hearts that may believe.

Reformed

The formula introduces authoritative revelation. Christ does not suggest; He teaches as Lord of the kingdom He describes.

Reformed comfort: the Beatitudes are Christ’s own catechism of blessedness. The church receives them as gift and norm—word that creates and shapes a people, not mere moral slogans.

Matthew 5:3

3“Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”

Arminian

Blessedness begins with poverty of spirit—honest emptiness before God, not spiritual self-sufficiency. The kingdom is promised to those who know they cannot buy it.

Classical Arminians stress that such humility is grace-awakened and sincerely invited. Prevenient grace convicts pride; the gospel offers the kingdom to the needy who trust Christ. Shared ground: no one enters by boasting.

Reformed

“Poor in spirit” names those stripped of claim before God. Present tense—“theirs is”—announces real kingdom belonging now, not only someday.

Reformed accent: spiritual poverty is the Spirit’s work in the elect, the opposite of autonomous virtue. The kingdom is gift to beggars God has made willing to receive Christ.

Key contrast Both reject self-righteous entry into the kingdom. Arminians typically emphasize grace-enabled recognition of need and a sincere offer to the poor in spirit; Reformed readers typically emphasize that such poverty and kingdom possession are wrought by God in those He brings to Christ.

Matthew 5:4

4“Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted.”

Arminian

Kingdom mourning is grief over sin, brokenness, and a world askew—not mere temperament. Comfort is promised: God meets contrite sorrow with consolation.

Arminian reading treasures repentance that is real and invitational. Grace softens the heart to mourn; the Comforter (and the Father’s mercy in Christ) answers those who turn. Tears are not wasted when they lead to Him.

Reformed

Holy mourning is covenant grief under God’s light. The future “shall be comforted” is certain because God Himself pledges consolation.

Reformed note: repentance and comfort are both gifts. The Spirit wounds to heal; assurance rests not on the quality of our tears but on the God who binds up the brokenhearted in Christ.

Matthew 5:5

5“Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth.”

Arminian

Meekness is strength under God’s hand—gentleness that yields rather than grasping. Inheritance of the earth overturns the world’s scramble for control.

Classical Arminians see meekness as the fruit of a will surrendered under grace. Disciples choose the low road Jesus walks; the promise invites trust that God’s future belongs to the humble, not the domineering.

Reformed

Psalm 37 stands behind the promise: the meek inherit because the Lord upholds them. Meekness is not weakness; it is settled trust in God’s vindication.

Reformed accent: inheritance is covenantal gift. Those the Father gives the Son are shaped into Christ’s meekness and will share His reign over a renewed earth.

Matthew 5:6

6“Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied.”

Arminian

Hunger and thirst picture intense longing for right relationship with God and right living among people. Satisfaction is promised—God fills what He awakens.

Arminian pastoral emphasis: grace stirs holy desire; believers keep seeking righteousness in faith and obedience. The promise is sincere: those who pursue what God loves will not be left empty.

Reformed

Appetite for righteousness is itself evidence of new life. Filling is eschatological and present—justification’s gift and sanctification’s growth under God’s faithfulness.

Reformed comfort: God both creates the hunger and supplies the bread. Satisfaction is secured by Christ’s righteousness imputed and His Spirit’s work within.

Key contrast Both affirm deep longing for righteousness and God’s sure filling. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled, ongoing pursuit and a trustworthy promise to seekers; Reformed readers typically stress that holy hunger and final satisfaction flow from God’s regenerating and justifying grace in Christ.

Matthew 5:7

7“Blessed are the merciful, for they shall receive mercy.”

Arminian

Mercy received becomes mercy given. Those who forgive and relieve the needy mirror the Father’s heart and are promised mercy in return.

Classical Arminians hear a real moral correspondence under grace: forgiven people forgive. The beatitude warns hard hearts and encourages compassionate discipleship without turning mercy into a wage God owes.

Reformed

Merciful character marks those who know the gospel. The promise of receiving mercy rests on God’s covenant faithfulness, not a strict bargain.

Reformed reading: we are merciful because we have been shown mercy. The beatitude describes the family likeness of the redeemed and assures them of the mercy that will meet them at the judgment.

Matthew 5:8

8“Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.”

Arminian

Purity of heart reaches deeper than external religion—undivided devotion, sincerity before God. The vision of God is the supreme reward.

Arminian hope: grace cleanses and calls for continued integrity. Believers pursue a single heart by the Spirit’s help; the promise of seeing God fuels holiness without despairing perfectionism.

Reformed

“Pure in heart” echoes Psalm 24: who may ascend God’s hill? Only those made clean. Seeing God is the beatific hope secured for the saints.

Reformed accent: heart-purity is gift before it is achievement—Christ’s blood and the Spirit’s sanctifying work. The vision of God is promised to those God has purified for Himself.

Matthew 5:9

9“Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God.”

Arminian

Peacemakers do more than avoid conflict; they actively seek reconciliation under God’s peace in Christ. Being “called sons of God” names family resemblance to the Father of peace.

Classical Arminians urge responsible peacemaking as gospel fruit. Grace reconciles us to God and sends us as agents of peace; the title “sons” honors those who live the Father’s reconciling way.

Reformed

Sonship language is covenantal: peacemakers bear the family name because they share the Father’s work through the Prince of Peace.

Reformed note: true peacemaking flows from peace already made at the cross. Adoption’s status grounds the calling; the beatitude describes heirs who extend the reconciliation God accomplished.

Matthew 5:10

10“Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”

Arminian

Persecution for righteousness—not for folly or pride—marks genuine discipleship. The same kingdom promised to the poor in spirit is promised here, framing the Beatitudes with present belonging.

Arminian pastoral word: remaining faithful under pressure is a real, grace-sustained choice. The kingdom is theirs; suffering does not cancel the offer or the identity of those who cling to Christ.

Reformed

The inclusio with verse 3 (“theirs is the kingdom”) seals assurance: persecution does not unseat kingdom citizens. Righteousness’ sake means union with Christ’s cause.

Reformed comfort: belonging is secure under God’s hand even when the world hates. The persecuted remnant is kept; their kingdom is present gift, not fragile reward for toughness.

Key contrast Both honor costly fidelity and present kingdom belonging. Arminians typically emphasize persevering response under assisting grace; Reformed readers typically emphasize that persecution confirms, rather than creates, a kingdom identity God has already given.

Matthew 5:11

11“Blessed are you when others revile you and persecute you and utter all kinds of evil against you falsely on my account.”

Arminian

The address shifts to “you”: personal, direct. Reviling, persecution, and false slander “on my account” tie suffering to loyalty to Jesus Himself.

Arminian reading: disciples knowingly identify with Christ and may refuse to deny Him. Grace enables steadfast confession; blessing attaches to union with Jesus, not to enjoying abuse.

Reformed

Christ centers the beatitude: “on my account.” Insult and lie become fellowship with the Master’s path.

Reformed accent: such endurance is held by the Shepherd who keeps His own. Falsely accused disciples are not abandoned; their blessedness is anchored in belonging to Him who was Himself reviled.

Matthew 5:12

12“Rejoice and be glad, for your reward is great in heaven, for so they persecuted the prophets who were before you.”

Arminian

Joy in persecution sounds impossible until heaven’s reward and the prophets’ company are named. Suffering with Christ places disciples in a long line of faithful witnesses.

Classical Arminians hear both promise and summons: rejoice, because God’s reward is real and generous. Grace sustains gladness; responsible faith looks beyond present shame to the Father’s approval.

Reformed

Commanded joy rests on two pillars: great heavenly reward and prophetic precedent. God writes His people’s story into the history of the prophets.

Reformed assurance: reward is gracious gift consistent with God’s sovereign purpose—not wages that obligate Him, but promised honor for those He has kept faithful. The prophets’ path was ordained; so is the disciples’ share in it.

Key contrast Both urge joy grounded in heavenly reward and prophetic solidarity. Arminians typically stress a sincere promise that motivates persevering gladness; Reformed readers typically stress that reward and endurance alike are secured by God’s faithful purpose for His people.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus opens the Sermon on the Mount by sitting as Israel’s Teacher-King—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 5:1–12.

Arminian Notable voices

“And seeing the multitudes - At some distance, as they were coming to him from every quarter. He went up into the mountain - Which was near: where there was room for them all. His disciples - not only his twelve disciples, but all who desired to learn of him.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 5:1–12)

Reformed Notable voices

“Those who think that Christ's sermon, which is here related, is different from the sermon contained in the sixth chapter of Luke's Gospel, rest their opinion on a very light and frivolous argument. Matthew states, that Christ spoke to his disciples on a mountain, while Luke seems to say, that the discourse was delivered on a plain.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 5:1–12)
