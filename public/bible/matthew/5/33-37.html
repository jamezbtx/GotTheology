Passage

Matthew 5

Verses 33–37 · ESV · Voices

In the Sermon’s antithesis on oaths, Jesus takes the old word against false swearing and presses speech toward simple truthfulness. The King who fulfills the Law will not let disciples prop up shaky words with heaven, earth, Jerusalem, or their own heads—and He calls for a Yes and No that need no sacred scaffolding, because anything more comes from evil.

Matthew 5:33

33“Again you have heard that it was said to those of old, ‘You shall not swear falsely, but shall perform to the Lord what you have sworn.’”

Arminian

Jesus begins again with what the people know: do not swear falsely; perform to the Lord what you have sworn. The old word guards truth and vows before God.

Classical Arminians hear continuity with Torah and a call to listen deeper. The commandment against false oaths still stands; the question is whether disciples will stop at avoiding perjury or receive the Teacher’s fuller claim on everyday speech.

Reformed

“You have heard” recalls Israel’s received tradition against false swearing and for vowed performance to the Lord. Jesus does not scrap the concern for truth; He intensifies it.

Reformed accent: the King stands over the Word He gave. What follows will not license lying but expose how oath-systems can mask a heart unwilling to simply speak true.

Matthew 5:34

34“But I say to you, Do not take an oath at all, either by heaven, for it is the throne of God,”

Arminian

“But I say to you” widens the ban: do not take an oath at all—beginning with heaven, God’s throne. Sacred geography cannot launder unreliable speech.

Arminian pastoral tone: the warning is earnest. Responsible discipleship refuses the habit of bolstering weak words with holy names. Shared ground: Jesus will not let us treat God’s throne as verbal furniture for our convenience.

Reformed

Christ’s “I say” claims authority over the disciple’s tongue. Heaven is God’s throne—so swearing by it is never a neutral flourish; it drags the Holy into human spin.

Reformed reading: the Law-giver exposes oath-casuistry. Grace does not need heaven as a prop; the King who sits there already owns the truth His people must speak.

Matthew 5:35

35“or by the earth, for it is his footstool, or by Jerusalem, for it is the city of the great King.”

Arminian

Earth is His footstool; Jerusalem is the city of the great King. Lesser “holy” substitutes still belong to God—so they cannot serve as loopholes for half-true speech.

Classical Arminians hear a practical summons: stop ranking oaths by sacredness to decide when truth is optional. Responsible love of neighbor means words that stand without borrowed majesty.

Reformed

Footstool and holy city alike fall under the great King’s claim. Graded oaths that treat some spheres as less binding are still evasion under Christ’s eye.

Reformed comfort and cut: creation and Zion are His. Disciples cannot invent a zone where speech is cheap. The King’s ownership of earth and city leaves no safe harbor for manipulative swearing.

Key contrast Both reject oath hierarchies that make some speech less binding. Arminians often stress responsible, grace-enabled plain truthfulness toward neighbors; Reformed readers often stress God’s total claim on creation and city, which leaves no room for casuistry.

Matthew 5:36

36“And do not take an oath by your head, for you cannot make one hair white or black.”

Arminian

Even the self is off-limits as oath-collateral. You cannot change one hair’s color—so swearing by your head pretends a sovereignty you do not have.

Arminian emphasis: grace enables humble speech. The warning is real if we inflate ourselves with vows we cannot underwrite; the invitation is real if we simply tell the truth. Shared confession: we are not lords of our own heads.

Reformed

The head-oath collapses under creaturely limit. Hair color lies outside our decree—image of how little we control even ourselves.

Reformed accent: pride wants a guarantee it can swear by; providence answers that we cannot whiten a single hair. Truthful speech rests not on self-sovereignty but on the God who made the head.

Matthew 5:37

37“Let what you say be simply ‘Yes’ or ‘No’; anything more than this comes from evil.”

Arminian

Simple Yes and No—anything more comes from evil. Discipleship of the tongue means reliability without sacred props or verbal padding.

Arminian pastoral close: take the warning to heart—speak true, keep your word, drop the oath theater. Responsible response to Christ’s word means not gambling that embellished speech will somehow become honest. Shared hope: the same Lord who warns also forms a people whose ordinary Yes is enough.

Reformed

“Simply ‘Yes’ or ‘No’” is the King’s positive command; excess that props up speech “comes from evil.” The issue is not mere etiquette but the source of manipulative talk.

Reformed accent: evil loves complexity that hides the heart. Grace frees disciples from needing oaths as crutches—Christ’s people speak under His eye, where a plain Yes already stands before the throne.

Key contrast Both treat simple Yes/No as the disciple’s norm and “anything more” as morally serious. Arminians typically stress responsible, grace-enabled integrity in speech; Reformed readers typically stress that oath-excess springs from evil and that grace frees the tongue under the King’s claim.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on in the Sermon’s antithesis on oaths—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 5:33–37.

Arminian Notable voices

“Our Lord here refers to the promise made to the pure in heart of seeing God in all things, and points out a false doctrine of the scribes, which arose from their not thus seeing God. What he forbids is, the swearing at all, 1, by any creature, 2, in our ordinary conversation: both of which the scribes and Pharisees taught to be perfectly innocent.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 5:33–37)

Reformed Notable voices

“Thou shalt not perjure thyself This also is not a correction of the law, but a true interpretation of it. For God condemned in the law not only acts of perjury, but lightness in swearing, which lessens the reverence for his name. The man who perjures himself is not the only person who takes the name of God in vain, (Exodus 20:7.) He does so, who idly and contemptuously pronounces the name of God on trivial occasions, or in ordinary conversation.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 5:33–37)
