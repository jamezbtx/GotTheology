Passage

Matthew 5

Verses 17–20 · ESV · Voices

Still on the mountain, Jesus turns from salt and light to a charge many feared He meant to erase Moses: the Law and the Prophets. He refuses abolish and claims fulfill—then presses a kingdom righteousness that must go deeper than scribal and Pharisaic religion.

Matthew 5:17

17“Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them.”

Arminian

Jesus answers a rumor before it hardens: He has not come to scrap Moses and the Prophets. “Abolish” is denied twice; “fulfill” names His mission—bringing the Scriptures to their intended goal in His person, teaching, and work.

Classical Arminians hear continuity of God’s moral will under grace. Ceremonial shadows find their end in Christ, yet the holy demand of love is not repealed. Fulfillment means clearer light and Spirit-enabled life, not license to ignore what God has spoken.

Reformed

The King of the new covenant refuses antinomian caricature. Law and Prophets stand; Christ claims to complete them as covenant Lord—filling what they pointed toward and embodying their righteousness.

Reformed accent: fulfillment includes His perfect obedience and the unveiling of Scripture’s true aim. He does not invent a rival religion; He brings Israel’s story to its Messiah and makes the written word stand firm under His authority.

Key contrast Both reject “Christ vs. Moses” as abolish. Arminians typically stress grace that establishes the moral law’s spiritual fullness for obedient disciples; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s covenant fulfillment—His obedience and lordship—as the decisive “fill up” of Law and Prophets.

Matthew 5:18

18“For truly, I say to you, until heaven and earth pass away, not an iota, not a dot, will pass from the Law until all is accomplished.”

Arminian

“Truly” seals certainty: heaven and earth are the horizon; the smallest stroke of the Law is safer than the cosmos. “Until all is accomplished” looks to God’s purpose completed—not a loophole that empties commandments early.

Arminian pastoral reading: God’s will does not wobble with fashion. Disciples may trust that every jot matters, and that grace aims at the righteousness the Law always sought, not at a cheaper path around holiness.

Reformed

Iota and dot are proverb for total permanence. Scripture’s authority is not provisional until a more lenient teacher arrives; Christ Himself stakes creation’s endurance on the Law’s standing until God’s design is finished.

Reformed comfort: the same Lord who will accomplish all things upholds His word. Believers rest under a Law that has found its Yes in Christ, not under a God who shrugs at what He once wrote.

Matthew 5:19

19“Therefore whoever relaxes one of the least of these commandments and teaches others to do the same will be called least in the kingdom of heaven, but whoever does them and teaches them will be called great in the kingdom of heaven.”

Arminian

“Therefore” ties status in the kingdom to handling God’s commands. Relaxing even a “least” precept—and teaching others to do so—earns the title least; doing and teaching earn greatness. Example and doctrine travel together.

Classical Arminians press a real warning: grace does not authorize soft peddling sin. Teachers and hearers are responsible under assisting grace to keep and commend the Lord’s word. Shared ground: greatness is measured by fidelity, not by clever exemptions.

Reformed

Kingdom ranking tracks fidelity to the King’s law. Dissolving obligation—especially while instructing others—is not gospel freedom but rebellion dressed as insight.

Reformed note: those Christ brings into the kingdom are still under the law to Christ as a rule of life. Doing and teaching flow from hearts the Spirit renews; antinomian “least” is exposed as empty profession, while greatness reflects Spirit-wrought obedience under the Mediator.

Matthew 5:20

20“For I tell you, unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven.”

Arminian

Entry language is blunt: without a righteousness greater than the scribes’ and Pharisees’, no one enters. Their reputation for exactness is not enough; Jesus will soon show how heart-deep the Law always was.

Arminian accent: the call is sincere and searching. Prevenient and assisting grace enable a surpassing righteousness—inward holiness and love that outstrip external religion. The warning is real; the gospel offers power to walk in what God commands, not a shrug that Pharisaic form was already enough.

Reformed

“Never enter” bars self-made religion. Pharisaic righteousness, however meticulous outwardly, cannot open the kingdom. What exceeds it is first of all alien righteousness—Christ’s obedience counted to sinners—and then Spirit-wrought heart-obedience that the Law always required.

Reformed accent: imputed righteousness grounds entry; sanctifying grace produces a life that the Pharisees’ glosses never reached. The Sermon that follows does not raise the bar beyond Moses; it restores Moses against hypocrisy and drives hearers to the only righteousness that avails before God.

Key contrast Both agree Pharisaic righteousness cannot save and that kingdom righteousness must go deeper. Arminians typically emphasize a sincere, grace-enabled call to surpassing inward obedience with a real warning for empty religion; Reformed readers typically emphasize that exceeding righteousness is first Christ’s imputed obedience, then Spirit-wrought holiness under the law’s abiding authority—never self-achieved superiority to the Pharisees.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

On Christ and the Law, Arminian and Reformed teachers both refuse antinomian shortcuts—yet they weigh fulfillment, imputed righteousness, and Spirit-enabled obedience with different accents. Hear them in their own words on this hinge of the Sermon.

Arminian Notable voices

“But the moral law, contained in the Ten Commandments, and enforced by the prophets, He did not take away. It was not the design of His coming to revoke any part of this.… “I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil.” … Without question, his meaning in this place is … I am come to establish it in its fullness, in spite of all the glosses of men: I am come to declare the true and full import of every part of it; to show the length and breadth, the entire extent of every commandment contained therein, and the height and depth, the inconceivable purity and spirituality of it in all its branches.”
John Wesley · Sermon 25, “Upon Our Lord’s Sermon on the Mount,” Discourse Five
“I believe that sinners are accounted righteous solely by the obedience of Christ; and that the righteousness of Christ is the only meritorious cause on account of which God pardons the sins of believers and reckons them as righteous as if they had perfectly fulfilled the law. But since God imputes the righteousness of Christ to none except believers, I conclude that, in this sense, it may be well and properly said … faith is imputed for righteousness through grace.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“That man does not possess saving grace of himself, nor of the energy of his free will … but that it is necessary that he be born again of God in Christ, through his Holy Spirit, and renewed in understanding, inclination, and will, and all his faculties, in order that he may rightly understand, think, will, and effect what is truly good, according to the Word of Christ, “Without me you can do nothing.””
The Remonstrance · Article III (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“It is very easy, however, to confute this error, which proceeds on the supposition that Christ added to the Law, whereas he only restored it to its integrity by maintaining and purifying it when obscured by the falsehood, and defiled by the leaven of the Pharisees.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 2.8.7
“The moral law doth forever bind all, as well justified persons as others, to the obedience thereof; and that, not only in regard of the matter contained in it, but also in respect of the authority of God the Creator, who gave it: neither doth Christ, in the Gospel, any way dissolve, but much strengthen this obligation.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · 19.5
“The Old Testament stands in all its parts, both as to the law and the prophets.… He establishes in its deepest sense all that is written in Holy Scripture, and puts a new fullness into it.… He is himself the fulfillment and substance of the types, and prophecies, and commands of the law.… Believers are not to be worse in conduct, but far better than the most precise legalists.… The kingdom is not for rebels, but for the exactly obedient.… The gospel … produces outward sanctity through working in our inmost soul a glorious freedom in the law of the Lord.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · Commentary / Exposition on Matthew 5:17, 20

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
