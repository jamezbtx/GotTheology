Passage

Matthew 5

Verses 31–32 · ESV

In another Sermon antithesis, Jesus takes the familiar permission of a divorce certificate and presses marriage toward the heart of the covenant. The King who fulfills the Law will not let disciples treat a spouse as disposable paper—and He names the adultery that casual divorce and remarriage can create, while marking sexual immorality as the stated exception.

Matthew 5:31

31“It was also said, ‘Whoever divorces his wife, let him give her a certificate of divorce.’”

Arminian

Jesus recalls a received saying tied to Deuteronomy’s certificate practice. The old word regulated a hard reality; it did not bless hardness of heart as ideal. Disciples already “know” the paperwork path.

Classical Arminians hear continuity with Torah’s civil provision and a summons to listen again. The certificate is not dismissed as fiction; the question is whether Jesus’ hearers will stop at legal exit or receive His fuller claim on covenant faithfulness.

Reformed

“It was also said” sets the tradition Jesus will deepen. Moses’ certificate restrained chaos in a fallen world; it never redefined marriage as a contract one may discard at will.

Reformed accent: the Law-giver interprets His own Word. What follows will not erase Moses but expose how far the King’s standard reaches when hardness of heart is no longer the assumed frame.

Matthew 5:32

32“But I say to you that everyone who divorces his wife, except on the ground of sexual immorality, makes her commit adultery, and whoever marries a divorced woman commits adultery.”

Arminian

“But I say to you” raises the bar from certificate to consequence. Unjust divorce can place a wife in an adulterous position; marrying a divorced woman (in the sense Jesus targets) is likewise named adultery—except on the ground of sexual immorality (porneia).

Arminian pastoral tone: the warning is earnest and the exception is real. Responsible discipleship honors marriage, refuses casual dismissal, and still receives Christ’s word where covenant has already been shattered by sexual immorality. Shared ground: Jesus protects the vulnerable spouse and will not baptize hardness of heart. Grace calls for repentance where we have treated marriage lightly—and for wise, Scripture-bound care in broken homes.

Reformed

Christ’s “I say” claims authority over marriage itself. Divorce without the porneia exception “makes her commit adultery”; remarriage in that frame is adultery. The exception clause is Christ’s own merciful precision, not a loophole for convenience.

Reformed reading: the King exposes how seriously God joins husband and wife. External legality cannot cleanse a heart that discards a spouse. The warning drives disciples to the grace that both upholds the one-flesh bond and shepherds the wreckage sin leaves—never as permission to despise the covenant Christ defends.

Key contrast Both affirm Jesus’ high view of marriage, the seriousness of adultery language, and the stated exception for sexual immorality. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled responsibility to honor the bond and to heed the exception where porneia has broken covenant; Reformed readers typically stress the Law-giver’s exposure of hard hearts and the need for grace that upholds marriage beyond mere certificate compliance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
