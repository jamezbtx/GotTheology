Passage

Matthew 5

Verses 21–26 · ESV

In the Sermon’s first antithesis, Jesus takes the commandment against murder and presses it inward. The King who fulfills the Law exposes anger, contempt, and broken fellowship as matters of judgment—and calls disciples to reconcile before they worship and settle quickly before the court closes.

Matthew 5:21

21“You have heard that it was said to those of old, ‘You shall not murder; and whoever murders will be liable to judgment.’”

Arminian

Jesus begins with what the people already know: the sixth commandment and the liability attached to murder. The old word is not dismissed; it is the starting place for a deeper hearing.

Classical Arminians hear continuity with Torah and a call to listen again. The commandment still stands; the question is whether disciples will stop at the external act or receive the Teacher’s fuller claim on the heart.

Reformed

“You have heard” recalls Israel’s received tradition; Jesus does not cancel Moses but speaks as the Law-giver who interprets His own Word. Murder’s liability to judgment remains true.

Reformed accent: the King stands over the commandment He gave. What follows will not lower the bar but expose how far the Law already reaches when Christ Himself applies it.

Matthew 5:22

22“But I say to you that everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment; whoever insults his brother will be liable to the council; and whoever says, ‘You fool!’ will be liable to the hell of fire.”

Arminian

“But I say to you” deepens the commandment to the heart. Anger, insult, and contempt stand under judgment—not only the bloody act. Murder of the heart is real sin before God.

Arminian pastoral tone: the warning is earnest. Responsible repentance means naming rage and scorn as guilt, not as mere temperament. Shared ground: Jesus refuses a righteousness that never spills blood yet nurses contempt. Grace calls disciples to turn from anger before it hardens.

Reformed

Christ’s “I say” claims divine authority to judge the inner life. Liability climbs from judgment to council to Gehenna—showing that heart-sin is not a minor footnote to the Law.

Reformed reading: the Law-giver exposes what external compliance cannot cure. Anger and contempt reveal a heart that needs grace deeper than “I never killed anyone.” The hell of fire is not hyperbole to ignore but a merciful unveiling of need for a righteousness Christ alone supplies.

Key contrast Both affirm that Jesus deepens Torah to the heart and that anger and contempt are truly liable. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled responsibility to repent of rage and scorn; Reformed readers typically stress the Law-giver’s exposure of the heart and the need for grace beyond external compliance.

Matthew 5:23

23“So if you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you,”

Arminian

Worship and broken fellowship collide. At the altar the disciple remembers a brother’s grievance—not only his own hurt. Relationship repair is not optional piety; it belongs beside the gift.

Classical Arminians hear a practical summons: do not pretend all is well with God while leaving a brother offended. Responsible love interrupts the ritual to seek reconciliation.

Reformed

The “so” ties altar and anger together. God is not impressed by gifts offered while contempt or unresolved offense remains. Memory at the altar is providence interrupting self-justifying worship.

Reformed comfort and cut: Christ will not let His people hide behind liturgy. The heart He has exposed must be dealt with before the gift is completed—grace that refuses hypocrisy.

Matthew 5:24

24“leave your gift there before the altar and go. First be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift.”

Arminian

Leave—go—first be reconciled—then come. Order matters: reconciliation before the resumed offering. Worship waits on mended fellowship when a brother has something against you.

Arminian emphasis: grace enables the costly step toward the brother. The warning is real if we refuse; the invitation is real if we go. Shared confession: God desires reconciled people more than unfinished gifts left on the stone.

Reformed

Priority is unmistakable: first the brother, then the gift. The King who searches the heart also orders the path of obedience.

Reformed accent: reconciliation is fruit of a heart already claimed by Christ, not a work that purchases acceptance. Yet the command stands—those whom grace has made His leave the altar and go, then return to worship in truth.

Key contrast Both insist reconciliation precedes resumed worship. Arminians often stress responsible, grace-enabled initiative toward the offended brother; Reformed readers often stress that Christ-ordered obedience flows from a heart already under grace, never as the ground of standing before God.

Matthew 5:25

25“Come to terms quickly with your accuser while you are going with him to court, lest your accuser hand you over to the judge, and the judge to the guard, and you be put in prison.”

Arminian

Urgency replaces delay. Settle with the opponent on the way—before the chain of judge, guard, and prison locks in. Unresolved conflict compounds; time is not neutral.

Classical Arminians hear a pastoral alarm: do not postpone peacemaking. Responsible discipleship acts quickly while opportunity remains. The warning is concrete—procrastination can become captivity.

Reformed

The courtroom image presses eschatological seriousness under everyday dispute. “Quickly” is mercy: settle while the road still allows terms.

Reformed reading: the Judge’s court is not a game. Heart-exposed disciples flee delay; providence still offers a window for peace. Urgency is gift as much as threat—grace that will not let pride linger until the door shuts.

Matthew 5:26

26“Truly, I say to you, you will never get out until you have paid the last penny.”

Arminian

Amen-word and full payment: unfinished debt remains until the last penny. Soft anger left to fester can become a debt we cannot casually dismiss.

Arminian pastoral close: take the warning to heart—repent, reconcile, settle. Responsible response to Christ’s word means not gambling that contempt will somehow clear itself. Shared hope: the same Lord who warns also opens the way of reconciliation now.

Reformed

“Truly, I say” seals the severity. The last penny images a debt beyond casual settlement—echoing how thoroughly the Law claims us when Christ applies it to the heart.

Reformed accent: none of us pays our way out by moral nickels. The warning drives disciples to the grace of the King who exposes murder of the heart and then reconciles sinners to God—and sends them to seek peace with one another.

Key contrast Both treat the warning as earnest and the debt image as serious. Arminians typically stress responsible, timely repentance and reconciliation; Reformed readers typically stress that the Law’s full claim reveals inability and drives us to Christ’s grace, which then fuels peacemaking.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
