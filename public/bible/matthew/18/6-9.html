Passage

Matthew 18

Verses 6–9 · ESV

Having set a child before the disciples and tied greatness to humble welcome, Jesus turns to those who harm “little ones who believe.” He pronounces severe judgment on causing them to stumble and calls His hearers to radical, costly dealing with whatever in themselves leads to sin.

Matthew 18:6

6but whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him to have a great millstone fastened around his neck and to be drowned in the depth of the sea.

Arminian

Jesus protects vulnerable believers. “Little ones who believe” are not disposable; leading them into sin is worse than a drowning death.

Arminian pastoral weight: grace that receives the weak also binds the strong. God sincerely warns against stumbling others—responsibility is real, and enabling grace presses us to repent of careless influence rather than shrug it off.

Reformed

Christ’s millstone saying is not hyperbole for shock alone; it measures how precious believing little ones are to Him.

Reformed sobriety: the warning lands with holy fear. Left to ourselves we exploit the weak; only a Spirit-wrought love for Christ’s flock takes this threat with the seriousness Jesus intends.

Matthew 18:7

7"Woe to the world for temptations to sin! For it is necessary that temptations come, but woe to the one by whom the temptation comes!

Arminian

Temptations are lamented, not celebrated. That they “must” come describes the tragic certainty of a fallen world of free agents—not God’s delight in evil.

Classical Arminians hear a double truth: scandals will arise, yet the one who causes them remains fully blameworthy. Prevenient grace awakens conscience; the woe is a sincere call to refuse becoming the channel of another’s fall.

Reformed

Jesus holds together necessity and guilt. Offenses come within God’s sovereign governance of a sinful world, yet “woe” falls on the human instrument without excuse.

Reformed accent: providence never excuses the tempter. The warning itself is a means God uses; holy fear of being that instrument is fruit of grace, not a tip the flesh can casually adopt.

Key contrast Both insist the tempter is truly guilty even though scandals are certain in this age. Arminians typically stress that God permits but does not author the evil, so the warning sincerely addresses resistible responsibility; Reformed readers typically stress that necessity under God’s decree never softens culpability, and that heeding the woe requires effectual grace.

Matthew 18:8

8And if your hand or your foot causes you to sin, cut it off and throw it away. It is better for you to enter life crippled or lame than with two hands or two feet to be thrown into the eternal fire.

Arminian

Jesus demands ruthless dealing with personal sources of sin—not literal self-harm, but decisive separation from whatever repeatedly trips you.

Arminian preaching: grace enables real repentance. Eternal fire is not an empty threat; hearers remain responsible to act on the warning, cutting off cherished paths that lead to ruin.

Reformed

The imagery is surgical and eschatological: better maimed life than intact damnation. Christ prizes entrance into life over intact habits.

Reformed gravity: natural self-preservation will not amputate beloved sins. The Spirit must grant both hatred of sin and power to put it to death; the eternal fire shows how serious unrepentant stumbling is before a holy God.

Key contrast Both take the call to radical self-denial as serious, not ornamental. Arminians typically stress enabling grace and a real choice to cut off sin’s instruments; Reformed readers typically stress that such cutting off is the fruit of God’s effectual renewing work, not native moral resolve.

Matthew 18:9

9And if your eye causes you to sin, tear it out and throw it away. It is better for you to enter life with one eye than with two eyes to be thrown into the hell of fire.

Arminian

The eye intensifies the point: even what we look at and desire must be surrendered if it leads to sin. Entering life one-eyed beats Gehenna with full sight.

Arminian hope and warning together: Christ still invites costly repentance, and grace that enlightens also enables turning from lust of the eyes. The hell of fire makes refusal a real, accountable tragedy.

Reformed

Parallel to the hand and foot, the eye shows no faculty is exempt. Preferring intact desire over life ends in the hell of fire.

Reformed comfort and fear: Christ speaks to preserve His people. Mortification of sight and desire is Spirit-wrought; the same Lord who warns of hell grants the new heart that values life with Him above any cherished look.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
