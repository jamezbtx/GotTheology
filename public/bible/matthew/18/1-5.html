Passage

Matthew 18

Verses 1–5 · ESV

After teaching on the temple tax, the disciples ask who ranks highest in the kingdom. Jesus places a child in their midst and redefines greatness as humility and welcome—entrance itself depends on turning to become like children.

Matthew 18:1

1At that time the disciples came to Jesus, saying, “Who is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven?”

Arminian

The question assumes a ladder of status inside the kingdom. Coming right after talk of the cross and of equal sons (17:24–27), it shows how quickly even close followers chase rank.

Arminian pastoral reading: grace has brought them near Jesus, yet ambition still needs repentance. The kingdom’s doors are open by mercy, not by jockeying for place; hearers remain responsible to turn from self-exaltation when the Spirit convicts.

Reformed

Disciples who have heard much still ask the wrong question. Greatness talk exposes hearts still shaped by the world’s honor code.

Reformed note: Christ will answer by restructuring desire, not by flattering the askers. The kingdom’s order is His to define; left to ourselves we climb, and only grace remakes what we prize.

Matthew 18:2

2And calling to him a child, he put him in the midst of them

Arminian

Jesus answers with a living parable. A child—low status, dependent, without résumé—stands where rivalry wanted a podium.

Arminian warmth: the Lord still places the lowly before us. Prevenient grace softens pride so we can see what He shows; we must not shrug off the object lesson.

Reformed

Christ initiates the teaching aid. He calls; He places. The disciples do not invent the model of greatness.

Reformed accent: revelation comes by Christ’s action. He rearranges the circle so that the overlooked stand in the middle, and the ambitious must look down to learn.

Matthew 18:3

3and said, “Truly, I say to you, unless you turn and become like children, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven.

Arminian

“Turn” (conversion of direction) and “become like children” are conditions of entrance, not tips for climbing higher. Without humility that trusts and depends, there is no entry.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere warning and a real possibility: grace enables turning, yet the “unless” is not empty. God holds us accountable to repent of proud self-reliance and receive the kingdom as little ones.

Reformed

Jesus speaks with Amen-authority: no turn, no entrance. Childlikeness here is not naïveté but humble dependence.

Reformed sobriety: natural ambition cannot self-reform into kingdom fitness. The new birth and Spirit-wrought humility are gifts; without them the gate stays shut—and the warning proves how serious pride is.

Key contrast Both treat childlike humility as necessary for entrance, not optional polish. Arminians typically stress grace that enables a real turning we must not resist; Reformed readers typically stress that such turning is the fruit of God’s effectual renewing work.

Matthew 18:4

4Whoever humbles himself like this child is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven.

Arminian

Greatness is redefined as self-lowering. The child does not grasp for thrones; the disciple who humbles himself mirrors that posture.

Arminian preaching presses the will: grace invites and enables this humility. Status in Christ’s kingdom grows by descending—serving, trusting, refusing to inflate the self.

Reformed

Paradox of the kingdom: the greatest is the one who takes the low place. Humbling oneself is real obedience, yet it flows from a heart Christ has claimed.

Reformed comfort: ranking contests die where this rule rules. Christ’s measure of greatness protects the church from celebrity piety and crowns lowliness.

Matthew 18:5

5“Whoever receives one such child in my name receives me.

Arminian

Welcome to the lowly “in my name” is welcome to Jesus Himself. Hospitality becomes Christology.

Arminian mission tone: grace that receives us also sends us to receive others—especially the overlooked. Rejecting “little ones” is refusing the Lord who identifies with them.

Reformed

Christ so unites Himself with His little ones that receiving them is receiving Him. Union and representation stand behind the welcome.

Reformed pastoral weight: how the church treats the weak reveals whether it knows its King. Ministry to the least is not side work; it is meeting Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
