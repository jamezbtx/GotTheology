Passage

Matthew 18

Verses 10–14 · WEB · Voices

After warning against causing little ones who believe to stumble, Jesus forbids despising them and roots their dignity in the Father’s heavenly care. The parable of the lost sheep follows at once, showing His searching joy over recovering even one who has gone astray.

Matthew 18:10

10See that you don’t despise one of these little ones, for I tell you that in heaven their angels always see the face of my Father who is in heaven.

Arminian

Jesus guards the dignity of “little ones.” To despise them is to contradict the Father’s regard, pictured here by angels who always behold His face.

Grace that welcomes the weak also binds the church. God sincerely forbids contempt; hearers remain responsible not to dismiss the overlooked, and prevenient light presses us to honor those heaven itself attends.

Reformed

Christ ties the little ones’ worth to the Father’s court: their angels always see His face. Despising them assaults what heaven holds dear.

Particular care stands behind the command. The Father’s regard for His little ones is not vague goodwill; it is covenant attentiveness, and the warning protects those He values.

Matthew 18:12

12“What do you think? If a man has one hundred sheep, and one of them goes astray, doesn’t he leave the ninety-nine, go to the mountains, and seek that which has gone astray?

Arminian

The shepherd’s question assumes a searching love. One gone astray is worth the chase; leaving the ninety-nine dramatizes how seriously God pursues the wandering.

Seeking grace is authentic and personal. The Father goes after the stray, yet the parable still summons response—do not despise the wanderer, and do not resist the Shepherd who comes looking.

Reformed

Jesus invites agreement: of course the shepherd seeks the one. The initiative is the shepherd’s; the sheep does not first find itself.

This is particular, costly seeking. Christ portrays a love that leaves ease to recover His own—providence and electing care expressed as pursuit, not indifference to the lost among His little ones.

Key contrast Both rejoice that God seeks the astray rather than writing them off. Arminians stress searching grace that can be resisted and that summons church and wanderer to respond; Reformed interpreters typically press a seeking love that effectually recovers those the Father intends to keep.

Matthew 18:13

13If he finds it, most certainly I tell you, he rejoices over it more than over the ninety-nine which have not gone astray.

Arminian

Finding brings disproportionate joy. Heaven’s gladness over one recovered sheep outruns quiet satisfaction in the ninety-nine who stayed.

The Father’s heart is not cold bookkeeping. Grace that seeks also celebrates return; the church should mirror that joy when little ones are restored, never despising those who needed to be found.

Reformed

Amen-certainty: recovery is met with greater rejoicing. The shepherd’s joy reveals the Father’s delight in bringing the stray home.

Salvation’s celebration is God’s before it is ours. The found sheep is cause for heaven’s gladness—fruit of seeking love that does not fail to bring joy when His own are restored.

Matthew 18:14

14Even so it is not the will of your Father who is in heaven that one of these little ones should perish.

Arminian

Jesus grounds the parable in the Father’s will: He does not desire that one of these little ones perish. The statement expresses sincere, searching love—not a hollow wish.

Arminians hear universal fatherly desire toward these little ones, paired with responsibility. Grace pursues so that none need perish; resisting that grace, or despising the weak, remains a tragic, accountable contradiction of the Father’s revealed will.

Reformed

“So” ties the Father’s will to the shepherd’s successful joy. His will is that His little ones not perish—and the parable shows seeking that finds.

This is not frustrated intention but securing purpose. The Father’s will effectually guards those Christ names as little ones; particular care and electing love mean none of His own are finally lost, without denying the church’s duty to seek and welcome them.

Key contrast Both refuse a God who is indifferent when little ones wander. Arminians take “not the will… that one… should perish” as sincere desire and resistible searching love; Calvinists read it as the Father’s effectual will that His little ones be kept from final ruin.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What earlier interpreters on each side wrote about Matthew 18:10–14.

Arminian Notable voices

“See that ye despise not one of these little ones - As if they were beneath your notice. Be careful to receive and not to offend, the very weakest believer in Christ: for as inconsiderable as some of these may appear to thee, the very angels of God have a peculiar charge over them: even those of the highest order, who continually appear at the throne of the Most High.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 18:10–14)

Reformed Notable voices

“Christ now employs his own example in persuading his disciples to honor even weak and despised brethren; for he came down from heaven to save not them only, but even the dead who were lost. It is in the highest degree unreasonable that we should disdainfully reject those whom the Son of God has so highly esteemed.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 18:10–14)
