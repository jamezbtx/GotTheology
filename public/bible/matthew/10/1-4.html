Passage

Matthew 10

Verses 1–4 · WEB · Voices

After mercy and conflict in Galilee, Jesus calls the Twelve, gives them authority over spirits and sickness, and names them as apostles. The mission discourse begins with a named circle—ordinary men under the King’s delegated power, including the one who will betray Him.

Matthew 10:1

1He called to himself his twelve disciples, and gave them authority over unclean spirits, to cast them out, and to heal every disease and every sickness.

Arminian

Jesus summons twelve and gives authority—not for status, but for casting out and healing. Discipleship becomes mission: what they received in nearness they now exercise for others.

This is a serious call and a gift. The Lord invites and equips; the Twelve are responsible to use what grace has placed in their hands. Common reading: authority is Christ’s gift, never self-made.

Reformed

Jesus joins the call of the Twelve to their commission. The King who has shown authority now delegates it for a defined work against unclean spirits and every affliction.

This is not democracy of power but sovereign appointment. The Twelve act under Christ’s word; success hangs on the One who called them, not on native strength.

Key contrast Both confess that authority is given by Jesus. Arminians emphasize grace-enabled responsibility to answer and serve; Reformed Christians typically underline Christ’s sovereign choice and effectual equipping of those He names.

Matthew 10:2

2Now the names of the twelve apostles are these. The first, Simon, who is called Peter; Andrew, his brother; James the son of Zebedee; John, his brother;

Arminian

“Apostles” names the sent ones. Peter is listed first; brothers come in pairs. The kingdom advances through known persons, not anonymous force.

The apostles’ names matter. God works through people who can be known, trusted, and followed—and who remain accountable for how they walk.

Reformed

Matthew pauses to list. History is particular: these men, this order, this mission under the Father’s plan.

The church’s foundation is not a vague movement but apostles Christ Himself appointed (Eph 2:20). Providence writes names into the story.

Matthew 10:3

3Philip; Bartholomew; Thomas; Matthew the tax collector; James the son of Alphaeus; Lebbaeus, who was also called Thaddaeus;

Arminian

Tax collector sits beside Thomas; diverse pasts enter one list. Grace does not erase history; it conscripts it for witness.

Arminians treasure this breadth. The call is sincere toward unlikely people. Matthew’s own name in the roster is living proof that sinners may be sent.

Reformed

The Twelve show great variety under one Lord. Matthew the tax collector is not an exception that invents the rule; he is evidence of electing mercy that gathers whom it will.

The list is Christ’s choosing. Different temperaments, one commission—unity grounded in the Sender, not in sameness of background.

Matthew 10:4

4Simon the Zealot; and Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him.

Arminian

Zealot and betrayer close the list. Nearness to Jesus does not automatically equal perseverance. Judas’s place warns every hearer: privilege without faith ends in ruin.

Arminian warning with pastoral care: the gospel truly offers life; resisting and betraying remains a tragic possibility. Stay near—and believe.

Reformed

Even the betrayer is named inside the Twelve. Scripture does not hide the wound. Judas’s role unfolds under divine knowledge without making God the author of sin.

Christ’s mission is not derailed by treachery He foreknew. The cross is not Plan B; the list already tells the truth about human failure and sovereign purpose.

Key contrast Both treat Judas’s inclusion as sober warning, not cynicism. Arminians highlight responsible apostasy after privilege; Reformed interpreters stress God’s foreknowledge and purpose that include even betrayal without excusing it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic Arminian and Reformed comment on Matthew 10:1–4, in the writers’ own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“His twelve disciples - Hence it appears that he had already chosen out of his disciples, those whom he afterward termed apostles. The number seems to have relation to the twelve patriarchs, and the twelve tribes of Israel. The first, Simon - The first who was called to a constant attendance on Christ; although Andrew had seen him before Simon.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 10:1–4)

Reformed Notable voices

“And having called the twelve disciples The number, twelve, was intended to point out the future restoration of the Church. As the nation was descended from twelve patriarchs, so its scattered remains are now reminded by Christ of their origin, that they may entertain a fixed hope of being restored. Although the kingdom of God was not in so flourishing a state in Judea, as to preserve the nation entire, but, on the contrary, that people, which already had miserably fallen, deserved doubly to die on account of ingratitude in despising the grace which had been offered to them, yet this did not prevent a new nation from afterwards springing up.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 10:1–4)
