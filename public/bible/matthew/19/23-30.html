Passage

Matthew 19

Verses 23–30 · WEB · Voices

After the rich young man leaves, Jesus warns how hard it is for the rich to enter the kingdom, heightens the image to camel and needle, and answers the disciples’ despair: with man impossible, with God possible. Peter asks about their reward; Jesus promises thrones, hundredfold return, eternal life—and the reversal of first and last.

Matthew 19:23

23Jesus said to his disciples, “Most certainly I say to you, a rich man will enter into the Kingdom of Heaven with difficulty.

Arminian

Amen-authority names the danger: riches make kingdom entrance hard. Wealth easily becomes trust, identity, and rival lord—as the young man just showed.

Grace warns the prosperous without despising them. Difficulty is not in doubt; so is the call to loosen the grip and seek the kingdom first.

Reformed

Jesus turns from the departing man to His disciples with a solemn “Most certainly.” Affluence is a spiritual hazard, not a covenant badge of favor.

Providence may grant riches; fallen hearts misuse them. The kingdom’s door is not sized for baggage of self-sufficiency.

Matthew 19:24

24Again I tell you, it is easier for a camel to go through a needle’s eye than for a rich man to enter into God’s Kingdom.”

Arminian

The image intensifies “difficulty” into proverbial impossibility—camel through a needle’s eye. Jesus is not joking away the threat; He is shocking complacency.

Feel the impossibility humanly speaking, Wesley would say. Grace alone opens what wealth cannot buy; the rich must tremble and trust, not negotiate.

Reformed

Hyperbole with teeth: easier for the huge to pass the tiny than for the rich to enter. Softening the proverb into a Jerusalem gate myth misses Jesus’ point.

Christ means to crush confidence in means. Kingdom entry is not a wealth-management problem; it is a miracle problem.

Matthew 19:25

25When the disciples heard it, they were exceedingly astonished, saying, “Who then can be saved?”

Arminian

Astonishment is pastoral gold: if the advantaged cannot enter, who can? Their question universalizes the crisis beyond one rich man.

Grace uses despair of self to prepare for God’s power. The right question is not “How hard must I try?” but “Who then can be saved?”—answered in the next verse.

Reformed

Disciples equate riches with blessing and conclude the situation is hopeless for everyone. Their shock shows how deeply prosperity-theology ran.

The Spirit often begins salvation’s comfort by killing false hopes. “Who then can be saved?” is the doorway to sola gratia.

Key contrast Both treat the disciples’ cry as the right crisis before grace. Arminian readers find a universal impossibility that God’s assisting, resistible grace can overcome in those who do not finally refuse; Reformed readers hear total human inability that only God’s effectual power overcomes in the elect.

Matthew 19:26

26Looking at them, Jesus said, “With men this is impossible, but with God all things are possible.”

Arminian

Jesus looks at them—compassion with clarity. Man-side: impossible. God-side: all things possible. Salvation is God’s work, not self-salvation by deeds or dollars.

Arminian interpreters gladly confess impossibility apart from grace—and insist that the God who makes all things possible sincerely enables and invites; hearers must not resist the grace that alone can save.

Reformed

The hinge of the paragraph: impossibility with man, possibility with God. Calvin hears Christ bowing us down so we rely on grace alone.

God saves the otherwise unsavable—including the rich He chooses to free from their treasure. Omnipotence, not human ascent, opens the kingdom.

Key contrast Common confession: self-salvation is impossible; God alone can save. Arminians typically describe God’s possibility as powerful, enabling grace that can be resisted; Reformed commentators often describe it as sovereign, effectual grace that actually brings the dead to life and the rich to follow.

Matthew 19:27

27Then Peter answered, “Behold, we have left everything and followed you. What then will we have?”

Arminian

Peter contrasts their leaving with the young man’s clinging. The question mixes faith and calculation: we followed—what is our portion?

Grace does not scold honest cost-counting. Disciples may ask what following yields; Jesus will answer with promise, not with shame for having left all.

Reformed

Peter voices the Twelve’s investment: everything left, Jesus followed. Reward language is not forbidden; it is about to be reframed by Christ.

Following is already gift; yet the Lord freely attaches future reward. Peter’s “we” rests on grace that first called them, not on bargaining leverage.

Matthew 19:28

28Jesus said to them, “Most certainly I tell you that you who have followed me, in the regeneration when the Son of Man will sit on the throne of his glory, you also will sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel.

Arminian

Amen again: regeneration of the world (palingenesia), glorious throne, shared rule. The Twelve who followed receive unique honor in Israel’s restoration under the Son of Man.

Grace that called them to leave also secures a future. Following Jesus now is not loss without recompense; the kingdom’s renewal includes participation for His apostles.

Reformed

Christ locates reward in the new world under His throne. Apostolic thrones are His to assign; judgment of Israel’s tribes belongs to the followers He names.

Eschatology is Christ-centered. The same sovereign Lord who makes salvation possible grants kingdom roles—not as wages earned, but as royal gift to those He kept following.

Matthew 19:29

29Everyone who has left houses, or brothers, or sisters, or father, or mother, or wife, or children, or lands, for my name’s sake, will receive one hundred times, and will inherit eternal life.

Arminian

The promise widens beyond the Twelve: everyone who leaves for Jesus’ name. Hundredfold and eternal life answer both loss and the young man’s original quest.

Costly discipleship is not elite only. Grace enables leaving; the Lord repays beyond calculation—family and lands redefined in the church now, and life forever.

Reformed

“For my name’s sake” is the motive that counts. Inheritance language (“eternal life”) shows gift, not paycheck; hundredfold is lavish generosity from the King.

No sacrifice for Christ is forgotten. Providence may strip houses and kin; the covenant keeps better goods—and ends in life that cannot die.

Matthew 19:30

30But many will be last who are first, and first who are last.

Arminian

Reversal closes the unit: status now does not equal status then. The rich “first” may finish last; the leaving “last” may be first.

Grace overturns rankings. Do not trust present advantage; do not despair in low place—follow Jesus, and let Him order the line.

Reformed

The proverb prepares the vineyard parable (20:1–16). God’s kingdom scrambles human scoreboards.

Election and reward both humble the proud. Those who look first by wealth or résumé may be last; the last whom grace lifts will stand first when the Son of Man reigns.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on grace, human impossibility, and God’s power to save, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle… than for a rich man to go through the strait gate: that is, humanly speaking, it is an absolute impossibility.… With men this is impossible; but with God all things are possible.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 19:24, 26)
“In his lapsed and sinful state, man is not capable, of and by himself, either to think, to will, or to do that which is really good.… I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)

Reformed Notable voices

“Christ… so regulates his style that, after men have been bowed down within themselves, he teaches them to rely on the grace of God alone.… Christ plainly declares, that it is not possible for men to keep the way of salvation, except so far as the grace of God assists them.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 19:26)
“We cannot, by our best works, merit pardon of sin, or eternal life at the hand of God… but when we have done all we can, we have done but our duty, and are unprofitable servants.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · Chapter 16.5 (Of Good Works)
