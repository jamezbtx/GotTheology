Passage

Matthew 19

Verses 16–22 · WEB · Voices

A wealthy young man asks Jesus what good deed wins eternal life. Jesus points him to the one who is good, rehearses neighbor-love commandments, exposes the lack beneath his claim of full obedience, and calls him to sell, give, and follow—after which he leaves sorrowful.

Matthew 19:16

16Behold, one came to him and said, “Good teacher, what good thing shall I do, that I may have eternal life?”

Arminian

The question is earnest and transactional: name the deed; I will do it. Eternal life is framed as achievement by a good work.

Many still approach Jesus as a moral coach for self-secured life. Grace meets the seeker honestly—welcoming the hunger while refusing the premise that one more deed can purchase heaven.

Reformed

“Behold” flags a pivotal encounter. The asker wants a doable good that yields life—law-performance as ladder.

The question already misses the gospel’s center. Eternal life is not wages for a completed checklist; Christ will dismantle the premise without mocking the man’s seriousness.

Matthew 19:17

17He said to him, “Why do you call me good? No one is good but one, that is, God. But if you want to enter into life, keep the commandments.”

Arminian

Jesus redirects “good” to God alone, then answers on the man’s terms: enter life by keeping the commandments. The Law is not dismissed; it is God’s holy standard.

Grace upholds the Law’s goodness and the hearer’s responsibility. If life is sought by works, the commandments stand in full force—driving the conscience toward either honest obedience or honest lack.

Reformed

Only God is good; the Teacher will not be flattered as a rival source of goodness. “Keep the commandments” is true pedagogy for a works-seeker: the Law must be faced, not bypassed.

Christ uses the Law as a mirror. Those who would enter by doing must meet the standard that exposes every heart—and points beyond self to the One who is good.

Matthew 19:18

18He said to him, “Which ones?” Jesus said, “‘You shall not murder.’ ‘You shall not commit adultery.’ ‘You shall not steal.’ ‘You shall not offer false testimony.’

Arminian

“Which ones?” seeks a manageable subset. Jesus lists second-table commands—neighbor ethics that can be claimed outwardly while the heart still clings to idols.

Selective commandment-keeping is common. Grace presses the whole neighbor-love demand; we remain accountable for the life we claim to have kept.

Reformed

Jesus cites concrete prohibitions from the Decalogue’s second table. The list is not random; it tests love of neighbor in public and private.

The Law’s surface can be recited while its depth is missed. Christ chooses commands that sound keepable—preparing to reveal what still rules the man.

Matthew 19:19

19‘Honor your father and your mother.’ And, ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself.’”

Arminian

Honor of parents and Leviticus 19:18 crown the list: love of neighbor as self. The summary makes half-obedience impossible to hide for long.

Love of neighbor is the living test of claimed Law-keeping. Grace enables such love; refusing it while quoting commandments is self-deception we must repent of.

Reformed

The summary command gathers the second table into one word: love. External restraint without neighbor-love is not the righteousness the Law requires.

Christ interprets Moses by love’s fullness. The young man’s résumé will now be measured against this total claim, not against a short checklist.

Matthew 19:20

20The young man said to him, “All these things I have observed from my youth. What do I still lack?”

Arminian

He claims total keeping—and still feels a lack. Conscience or Spirit-stirring has not gone quiet despite his moral confidence.

Grace often begins here—the uneasy sense that something is missing. The question “What do I still lack?” is a mercy if it leads to Christ’s call rather than another self-fix.

Reformed

Self-assessment says “all these”; the leftover question betrays incompleteness. Moral seriousness without new birth still ends in lack.

The Law has not yet finished its work. Claiming full obedience while asking what remains shows both pride and an unquiet heart Christ will now address.

Matthew 19:21

21Jesus said to him, “If you want to be perfect, go, sell what you have, and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me.”

Arminian

“Perfect” here means complete, whole—not a second-tier elite track. Sell, give, follow: the lack is not another rule but allegiance. Treasure in heaven replaces treasure clutched on earth; discipleship is personal—“come, follow me.”

Grace enables the surrender Jesus asks. The call is serious and costly; hearers may receive or refuse. Wealth is not damned as such, but when it rivals Jesus it must be relinquished for Him.

Reformed

Christ locates the man’s idol and issues a particular command that exposes it. Following Jesus, not philanthropy alone, is the goal; selling and giving clear the rival lord.

The Law’s demand becomes gospel summons—leave the treasure, take the Treasure. Perfection is not self-made wholeness but undivided attachment to Christ, which only grace can work.

Key contrast Both treat v. 21 as the hinge: the issue is not a missing ritual but whether Jesus displaces wealth. Arminians stress an enabled, resistible call the young man truly could have answered; Reformed readers underline that wholehearted following is the fruit of sovereign grace—and that his sorrowful exit reveals a heart not yet made willing.

Matthew 19:22

22But when the young man heard this, he went away sad, for he was one who had great possessions.

Arminian

He leaves sad, not sneering—conviction without surrender. Great possessions outweigh the call to follow.

Grace can be refused. Sorrow is not yet repentance; the invitation remains, but walking away from Jesus with full hands and an empty heart is a tragic choice.

Reformed

Hearing produces departure. The narrative links sorrow to wealth: the rival treasure wins for now.

Moral earnestness collapses where the heart’s treasure is threatened. Apart from God’s regenerating power, even serious seekers cling to what cannot save—and exit grieving rather than following.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on a wealthy young man asks Jesus what good deed wins eternal life, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“And behold one came - Many of the poor had followed him from the beginning. Why callest thou me good - Whom thou supposest to be only a man. There is none good - Supremely, originally, essentially, but God. If thou wilt enter into life, keep the commandments - From a principle of loving faith.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 19:16–22)

Reformed Notable voices

“Luke says that he was a ruler, (archon,) that is, a man of very high authority, not one of the common people. And though riches procure respect, yet he appears to be here represented to have been held in high estimation as a good man. For my own part, after weighing all the circumstances, I have no doubt that, though he is called a young man, he belonged to the class of those who upheld the integrity of the Elders, by a sober and regular life.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 19:16–22)
