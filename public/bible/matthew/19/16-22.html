Passage

Matthew 19

Verses 16–22 · ESV

A wealthy young man asks Jesus what good deed wins eternal life. Jesus points him to the one who is good, rehearses neighbor-love commandments, exposes the lack beneath his claim of full obedience, and calls him to sell, give, and follow—after which he leaves sorrowful.

Matthew 19:16

16And behold, a man came up to him, saying, “Teacher, what good deed must I do to have eternal life?”

Arminian

The question is earnest and transactional: name the deed; I will do it. Eternal life is framed as achievement by a good work.

Arminian pastoral reading: many still approach Jesus as a moral coach for self-secured life. Grace meets the seeker honestly—welcoming the hunger while refusing the premise that one more deed can purchase heaven.

Reformed

“Behold” flags a pivotal encounter. The asker wants a doable good that yields life—law-performance as ladder.

Reformed note: the question already misses the gospel’s center. Eternal life is not wages for a completed checklist; Christ will dismantle the premise without mocking the man’s seriousness.

Matthew 19:17

17And he said to him, “Why do you ask me about what is good? There is only one who is good. If you would enter life, keep the commandments.”

Arminian

Jesus redirects “good” to God alone, then answers on the man’s terms: enter life by keeping the commandments. The Law is not dismissed; it is God’s holy standard.

Arminian accent: grace upholds the Law’s goodness and the hearer’s responsibility. If life is sought by works, the commandments stand in full force—driving the conscience toward either honest obedience or honest lack.

Reformed

Only God is good; the Teacher will not be flattered as a rival source of goodness. “Keep the commandments” is true pedagogy for a works-seeker: the Law must be faced, not bypassed.

Reformed sobriety: Christ uses the Law as a mirror. Those who would enter by doing must meet the standard that exposes every heart—and points beyond self to the One who is good.

Matthew 19:18

18He said to him, “Which ones?” And Jesus said, “You shall not murder, You shall not commit adultery, You shall not steal, You shall not bear false witness,

Arminian

“Which ones?” seeks a manageable subset. Jesus lists second-table commands—neighbor ethics that can be claimed outwardly while the heart still clings to idols.

Arminian warning: selective commandment-keeping is common. Grace presses the whole neighbor-love demand; we remain accountable for the life we claim to have kept.

Reformed

Jesus cites concrete prohibitions from the Decalogue’s second table. The list is not random; it tests love of neighbor in public and private.

Reformed clarity: the Law’s surface can be recited while its depth is missed. Christ chooses commands that sound keepable—preparing to reveal what still rules the man.

Matthew 19:19

19Honor your father and mother, and, You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”

Arminian

Honor of parents and Leviticus 19:18 crown the list: love of neighbor as self. The summary makes half-obedience impossible to hide for long.

Arminian pastoral weight: love of neighbor is the living test of claimed Law-keeping. Grace enables such love; refusing it while quoting commandments is self-deception we must repent of.

Reformed

The summary command gathers the second table into one word: love. External restraint without neighbor-love is not the righteousness the Law requires.

Reformed accent: Christ interprets Moses by love’s fullness. The young man’s résumé will now be measured against this total claim, not against a short checklist.

Matthew 19:20

20The young man said to him, “All these I have kept. What do I still lack?”

Arminian

He claims total keeping—and still feels a lack. Conscience or Spirit-stirring has not gone quiet despite his moral confidence.

Arminian honesty: grace often begins here—the uneasy sense that something is missing. The question “What do I still lack?” is a mercy if it leads to Christ’s call rather than another self-fix.

Reformed

Self-assessment says “all these”; the leftover question betrays incompleteness. Moral seriousness without new birth still ends in lack.

Reformed diagnosis: the Law has not yet finished its work. Claiming full obedience while asking what remains shows both pride and an unquiet heart Christ will now address.

Matthew 19:21

21Jesus said to him, “If you would be perfect, go, sell what you possess and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me.”

Arminian

“Perfect” here means complete, whole—not a second-tier elite track. Sell, give, follow: the lack is not another rule but allegiance. Treasure in heaven replaces treasure clutched on earth; discipleship is personal—“come, follow me.”

Arminian preaching: grace enables the surrender Jesus asks. The call is real and costly; hearers may receive or refuse. Wealth is not damned as such, but when it rivals Jesus it must be relinquished for Him.

Reformed

Christ locates the man’s idol and issues a particular command that exposes it. Following Jesus, not philanthropy alone, is the goal; selling and giving clear the rival lord.

Reformed pastoral weight: the Law’s demand becomes gospel summons—leave the treasure, take the Treasure. Perfection is not self-made wholeness but undivided attachment to Christ, which only grace can work.

Key contrast Both treat v. 21 as the hinge: the issue is not a missing ritual but whether Jesus displaces wealth. Arminians typically stress an enabled, resistible call the young man truly could have answered; Reformed readers typically stress that wholehearted following is the fruit of sovereign grace—and that his sorrowful exit reveals a heart not yet made willing.

Matthew 19:22

22When the young man heard this he went away sorrowful, for he had great possessions.

Arminian

He leaves sad, not sneering—conviction without surrender. Great possessions outweigh the call to follow.

Arminian lament: grace can be refused. Sorrow is not yet repentance; the invitation remains, but walking away from Jesus with full hands and an empty heart is a real and tragic choice.

Reformed

Hearing produces departure. The narrative links sorrow to wealth: the rival treasure wins for now.

Reformed gravity: moral earnestness collapses where the heart’s treasure is threatened. Apart from God’s regenerating power, even serious seekers cling to what cannot save—and exit grieving rather than following.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
