Passage

Matthew 19

Verses 13–15 · ESV · Voices

Right after the hard word on marriage and kingdom celibacy, parents bring children for Jesus’ hands and prayer. The disciples push them back; Jesus welcomes the little ones and names them as kingdom people.

Matthew 19:13

13Then children were brought to him that he might lay his hands on them and pray. The disciples rebuked the people,

Arminian

Parents seek Jesus’ touch and prayer for their children—a simple act of trust. The disciples, still sorting status and cost (vv. 10–12), rebuke the bringers as if little ones waste the Teacher’s time.

Arminian pastoral note: grace still draws the overlooked to Jesus. Gatekeepers in the church can hinder what the Lord means to bless; hearers remain responsible not to scold away those who come in need of His hands.

Reformed

Matthew pairs blessing sought with disciples’ rebuke. Even close followers misread who belongs near the King.

Reformed framing: Christ will correct His own. The church’s instincts about “important” ministry often exclude the weak until the Lord reorders the circle.

Matthew 19:14

14but Jesus said, “Let the little children come to me and do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of heaven.”

Arminian

Jesus’ double command—let them come; do not hinder—opens the way and forbids obstruction. “To such belongs the kingdom” echoes 18:3–4: childlike dependence, not achievement, marks kingdom people.

Arminian warmth: the invitation is sincere. Grace brings children (and the childlike) to Christ; adults must not block that approach. The kingdom is gift received, not prize earned by the impressive.

Reformed

Authority speaks: come, and stop hindering. Kingdom membership is assigned “to such”—the lowly and dependent—not to the self-important.

Reformed accent: Christ defines belonging. The same Lord who builds His church welcomes little ones as rightful heirs of heaven’s reign, exposing pride that treats them as interruption.

Key contrast Both hear a real welcome and a real warning against hindering. Arminians typically stress the open summons grace enables parents and children to answer; Reformed readers typically stress that kingdom belonging is Christ’s to grant—displayed here in the little ones He claims—rather than a status disciples may police.

Matthew 19:15

15And he laid his hands on them and went away.

Arminian

Jesus does what was asked: He lays hands on them. The scene ends not with a lecture to the children but with His blessing enacted, then departure.

Arminian mission tone: grace that welcomes also touches. Ministry to the young is not sentimental add-on; it is following the Lord who stops for them before He moves on.

Reformed

Word and gesture agree. Hands confirm the verbal welcome; then He proceeds on His appointed way.

Reformed pastoral weight: Christ’s blessing is concrete. The church that knows its King will neither romanticize childhood nor despise it—receiving little ones because He first received them.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on right after the hard word on marriage and kingdom celibacy, parents bring children for Jesus’ hands and prayer—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 19:13–15.

Arminian Notable voices

“That he should lay his hands on them - This was a rite which was very early used, in praying for a blessing on young persons. The disciples rebuked them - That is, them that brought them: probably thinking such an employ beneath the dignity of their Master.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 19:13–15)

Reformed Notable voices

“If a crown had been put on his head, they would have admitted it willingly, and with approbation; for they did not yet comprehend his actual office. But they reckon it unworthy of his character to receive children; and their error wanted not plausibility; for what has the highest Prophet and the Son of God to do with infants?”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 19:13–15)
