Passage

Matthew 8

Verses 5–13 · ESV · Voices

In Capernaum a Roman centurion appeals for a paralyzed servant. His insight into authority draws Jesus’ marvel and opens a window onto Gentile inclusion and the danger of covenant presumption—faith that trusts the word, not proximity or pedigree.

Matthew 8:5

5When he had entered Capernaum, a centurion came forward to him, appealing to him,

Arminian

Capernaum receives a Gentile officer who comes forward and appeals. Empire’s rank does not bar approach; need and humility do the talking.

Classical Arminians see grace widening the circle. The centurion is not chased away; he is heard. Responsibility remains: he must actually come and ask.

Reformed

Matthew places a centurion inside Jesus’ Capernaum ministry. Gentile faith will soon be praised above Israel’s average response—already under the story God is writing.

Reformed readers note providence: the Lord of Israel draws a Roman soldier near at the appointed hour, preparing the theme of east-and-west guests at the kingdom table.

Matthew 8:6

6“Lord, my servant is lying paralyzed at home, suffering terribly.”

Arminian

The plea is concrete and compassionate: a servant paralyzed and suffering. Authority here bends toward the weak.

Arminian pastoral note: faith often begins with love for another’s pain. Grace enables intercession; the centurion uses his voice for someone who cannot walk to Jesus.

Reformed

“Lord” on a Gentile tongue already signals more than courtesy. The description is stark—paralyzed, suffering terribly.

Reformed comfort: Christ receives petitions for the helpless. The servant’s misery is not beneath the King’s notice; this need will showcase faith and authority together.

Matthew 8:7

7And he said to him, “I will come and heal him.”

Arminian

Jesus answers with readiness: He will come and heal. Willingness meets the appeal without delay or disdain.

Classical Arminians hear a Savior eager to help. The offer to come is sincere; grace does not play hard to get when true need is presented.

Reformed

The King’s “I will come” shows initiative. Healing is not bargained; it is granted from sovereign kindness.

Reformed accent: Christ’s resolve to heal stands before the centurion’s famous reply. Mercy originates in Jesus, not in human negotiation.

Matthew 8:8

8But the centurion replied, “Lord, I am not worthy to have you come under my roof, but only say the word, and my servant will be healed.

Arminian

Unworthiness and confidence sit together: not worthy of a visit, yet sure that a word is enough. Faith grasps distance-bridging authority.

Arminian reading: this is living trust—humble about self, bold about Christ. Grace enables such insight; the man freely confesses both need and Jesus’ power.

Reformed

The centurion refuses presumption. Roof-worthiness is denied; word-sufficiency is affirmed. That is the anatomy of true faith.

Reformed praise: such humility and confidence are gifts. He sees that Christ’s spoken will is as effective as His bodily presence—authority that creates what it commands.

Key contrast Both celebrate humble, word-trusting faith. Arminians typically stress the centurion’s responsible insight and plea under assisting grace; Reformed readers typically stress faith as Spirit-wrought gift that rests wholly on Christ’s efficacious word.

Matthew 8:9

9For I too am a man under authority, with soldiers under me. And I say to one, ‘Go,’ and he goes, and to another, ‘Come,’ and he comes, and to my servant, ‘Do this,’ and he does it.”

Arminian

Military chain-of-command becomes a parable of Jesus’ power. Orders work at a distance; so, he reasons, must the Lord’s word.

Classical Arminians admire clear-eyed reasoning in faith. Light given is used well. The centurion thinks truly about authority and then trusts accordingly.

Reformed

Analogy from barracks to kingdom: delegated command implies greater command in the Commander. The centurion’s logic is theological.

Reformed hearing: God has granted unusual clarity. This Gentile perceives what many near the covenant signs miss—Jesus’ word carries irresistible authority.

Matthew 8:10

10When Jesus heard this, he marveled and said to those who followed him, “Truly, I tell you, with no one in Israel have I found such faith.

Arminian

Jesus marvels—and Israel is put on notice. Covenant nearness without trust is empty; a Gentile’s faith can outshine privileged hearers.

Arminian pastoral warning and hope: privilege does not save; faith does. Grace was offered widely in Israel; many resisted. This centurion shows what receptive faith looks like.

Reformed

Divine marvel is real in the narrative: Jesus highlights faith rare even in Israel. Election’s surprise is already visible—outsiders believing, insiders missing.

Reformed sobriety: finding such faith “with no one in Israel” judges presumption. God raises faith where He wills; pedigree is no substitute for trusting Christ’s word.

Key contrast Both take Jesus’ marvel as rebuke to covenant presumption and praise of Gentile faith. Arminians typically emphasize Israel’s real opportunity refused and the centurion’s receptive response; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s freedom to grant faith beyond ethnic Israel and expose empty privilege.

Matthew 8:11

11I tell you, many will come from east and west and recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven,

Arminian

East and west guests at the patriarchs’ table: the kingdom’s feast is wider than ethnic Israel imagined. Faith, not bloodline, seats the diner.

Classical Arminians rejoice in global gospel invitation. Many will come—real inclusion for those who believe. Abraham’s family grows by faith’s welcome.

Reformed

Promise to Abraham expands in Jesus’ mouth: nations stream to the kingdom banquet. The patriarchs’ table is secured by God’s covenant purpose.

Reformed hope: Gentile inclusion is not Plan B. The same Lord who called Abraham now gathers east and west according to electing mercy fulfilled in Christ.

Matthew 8:12

12while the sons of the kingdom will be thrown into the outer darkness. In that place there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.”

Arminian

Sons of the kingdom face outer darkness if they refuse the King’s faith-call. Nearness without trust ends in weeping.

Arminian gravity: privilege heightens responsibility. Grace was near; rejection is culpable. The warning aims to awaken, not to entertain despair.

Reformed

Judgment language is stark: outer darkness, weeping, gnashing. Covenant labels do not cancel unbelief’s end.

Reformed warning: God’s justice is not sentimental. Those who presume on lineage while rejecting the Messiah meet the judgment Scripture names—under the same sovereign hand that seats Gentiles at the feast.

Key contrast Both preach real judgment on unbelieving privilege. Arminians typically stress culpable refusal of near grace; Reformed readers typically stress judicial outcome under God’s righteous decree against unbelief, even among “sons of the kingdom.”

Matthew 8:13

13And to the centurion Jesus said, “Go; let it be done for you as you have believed.” And the servant was healed at that very moment.

Arminian

“As you have believed” ties the gift to living trust; healing arrives that very moment. Faith receives what it entrusted to Jesus’ word.

Arminian pastoral close: believe and go. The centurion’s trust was not empty; grace answered. Believers today are summoned to the same word-dependent confidence.

Reformed

Authority speaks: “let it be done.” Belief is the appointed instrument; Christ is the efficient cause. Instant healing seals the lesson.

Reformed assurance: faith does not create power; it rests on power already present in Jesus. The servant’s cure proves the word sufficient and the King faithful.

Key contrast Both join faith and healing without making faith a human merit. Arminians typically present believing as the grace-enabled condition that receives; Reformed readers typically present faith as the Spirit-given instrument through which Christ’s sovereign word heals.
