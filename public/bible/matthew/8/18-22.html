Passage

Matthew 8

Verses 18–22 · WEB · Voices

Seeing crowds, Jesus orders a crossing—and two would-be followers meet the cost of discipleship: the Son of Man’s homelessness and the demand that following Him outrank even sacred family duty.

Matthew 8:18

18Now when Jesus saw great multitudes around him, he gave the order to depart to the other side.

Arminian

Crowds press; Jesus orders a crossing. Popularity does not steer the mission—He directs the next step.

Arminian interpreters note responsive leadership under the Father’s will. Disciples must be ready to move when He commands, not when the crowd is comfortable.

Reformed

Jesus “saw” and “gave the order.” The King sets the itinerary. Crossing is not escape from need but obedience to the next appointed work.

Providence guides geography. The other side awaits—storm and demoniacs included—under His purposeful command.

Matthew 8:19

19A scribe came and said to him, “Teacher, I will follow you wherever you go.”

Arminian

A scribe volunteers bold loyalty: wherever You go. Zeal sounds right; Jesus will test whether it has counted the cost.

Sincere words still need truth. Grace invites following; responsibility includes hearing what following actually requires.

Reformed

“Teacher” and “wherever” sound impressive. Matthew lets the claim stand so Jesus can expose shallow enthusiasm.

Not every volunteer is yet a disciple. The Lord who calls also discerns; this exchange will strip romance from discipleship.

Matthew 8:20

20Jesus said to him, “The foxes have holes and the birds of the sky have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.”

Arminian

Foxes and birds have homes; the Son of Man does not. Following Jesus may mean insecurity the world calls failure.

Arminians detect a free, honest invitation: count the cost, then come. Grace does not bait-and-switch; it tells the truth so faith can choose Christ over comfort.

Reformed

The Son of Man’s homelessness is messianic pathos and warning. Kingdom loyalty may forfeit earthly nest.

The homeless Son is the Lord of glory. Disciples share His path by grace; security is finally in Him, not in holes and nests.

Key contrast Both refuse romantic discipleship. Arminians typically underline clear cost presented for a grace-enabled decision; Reformed commentators typically stress union with the Son’s appointed path—homelessness as the way God has set for His Messiah and His people.

Matthew 8:21

21Another of his disciples said to him, “Lord, allow me first to go and bury my father.”

Arminian

“Lord” plus “allow me first” mixes confession with delay. Even sacred family duty can become a rival first.

Good duties can displace the best call. Grace enables right ordering; the disciple remains responsible not to postpone Jesus with a pious “first.”

Reformed

A disciple speaks—yet “first” reveals divided allegiance. Burying a father was honored duty; Jesus will relativize even that.

The heart’s priorities are exposed. The King’s claim is absolute; secondary loves, however proper in themselves, must not outrank Him.

Matthew 8:22

22But Jesus said to him, “Follow me, and leave the dead to bury their own dead.”

Arminian

“Follow me” is the command; leaving the dead to bury their dead shocks social piety. Kingdom urgency outranks even burial customs when they compete with Jesus.

The note struck is a summons that demands decisive response. Grace makes following possible; refusing for “first” reasons is still refusal.

Reformed

The paradox is sharp: spiritually dead handle the dead; the living follow the Life. Jesus claims priority over the strongest kinship obligations.

Discipleship is not negotiated around family calendars. The Lord who calls also defines the terms—follow, and let competing loyalties fall where they must.

Key contrast Both affirm Jesus’ absolute claim over disciples. Arminians highlight a decisive, grace-enabled choice against delay; Reformed interpreters stress the King’s sovereign right to reorder every human loyalty under His call.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on seeing crowds, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“He commanded to go to the other side - That both himself and the people might have a little rest. The Son of man - The expression is borrowed from Dan. vii, 13, and is the appellation which Christ generally gives himself: which he seems to do out of humility, as having some relation to his mean appearance in this world.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 8:18–22)

Reformed Notable voices

“Two men are here presented to us by Matthew, and three by Luke, all of whom were prepared to become disciples of Christ, but who, having been prevented by a diversity of vices from following the right course, receive a corresponding variety of replies. It might at first sight appear strange, that Christ sends back, and does not admit into his family, one who offers to follow him immediately and without delay: while he detains another along with him who, by asking leave for a time, showed himself to be slower and less willing.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 8:18–22)
