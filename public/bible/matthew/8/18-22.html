Passage

Matthew 8

Verses 18–22 · ESV · Voices

Seeing crowds, Jesus orders a crossing—and two would-be followers meet the cost of discipleship: the Son of Man’s homelessness and the demand that following Him outrank even sacred family duty.

Matthew 8:18

18Now when Jesus saw a crowd around him, he gave orders to go over to the other side.

Arminian

Crowds press; Jesus orders a crossing. Popularity does not steer the mission—He directs the next step.

Classical Arminians note responsive leadership under the Father’s will. Disciples must be ready to move when He commands, not when the crowd is comfortable.

Reformed

Jesus “saw” and “gave orders.” The King sets the itinerary. Crossing is not escape from need but obedience to the next appointed work.

Reformed accent: providence guides geography. The other side awaits—storm and demoniacs included—under His purposeful command.

Matthew 8:19

19And a scribe came up and said to him, “Teacher, I will follow you wherever you go.”

Arminian

A scribe volunteers bold loyalty: wherever You go. Zeal sounds right; Jesus will test whether it has counted the cost.

Arminian pastoral caution: sincere words still need truth. Grace invites following; responsibility includes hearing what following actually requires.

Reformed

“Teacher” and “wherever” sound impressive. Matthew lets the claim stand so Jesus can expose shallow enthusiasm.

Reformed reading: not every volunteer is yet a disciple. The Lord who calls also discerns; this exchange will strip romance from discipleship.

Matthew 8:20

20And Jesus said to him, “Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head.”

Arminian

Foxes and birds have homes; the Son of Man does not. Following Jesus may mean insecurity the world calls failure.

Classical Arminians hear a free, honest invitation: count the cost, then come. Grace does not bait-and-switch; it tells the truth so faith can choose Christ over comfort.

Reformed

The Son of Man’s homelessness is messianic pathos and warning. Kingdom loyalty may forfeit earthly nest.

Reformed comfort and cross: the homeless Son is the Lord of glory. Disciples share His path by grace; security is finally in Him, not in holes and nests.

Key contrast Both refuse romantic discipleship. Arminians typically stress clear cost presented for a real, grace-enabled decision; Reformed readers typically stress union with the Son’s appointed path—homelessness as the way God has set for His Messiah and His people.

Matthew 8:21

21Another of the disciples said to him, “Lord, let me first go and bury my father.”

Arminian

“Lord” plus “let me first” mixes confession with delay. Even sacred family duty can become a rival first.

Arminian pastoral edge: good duties can displace the best call. Grace enables right ordering; the disciple remains responsible not to postpone Jesus with a pious “first.”

Reformed

A disciple speaks—yet “first” reveals divided allegiance. Burying a father was honored duty; Jesus will relativize even that.

Reformed diagnosis: the heart’s priorities are exposed. The King’s claim is absolute; secondary loves, however proper in themselves, must not outrank Him.

Matthew 8:22

22And Jesus said to him, “Follow me, and leave the dead to bury their own dead.”

Arminian

“Follow me” is the command; leaving the dead to bury their dead shocks social piety. Kingdom urgency outranks even burial customs when they compete with Jesus.

Classical Arminians hear a summons that demands decisive response. Grace makes following possible; refusing for “first” reasons is still refusal.

Reformed

The paradox is sharp: spiritually dead handle the dead; the living follow the Life. Jesus claims priority over the strongest kinship obligations.

Reformed gravity: discipleship is not negotiated around family calendars. The Lord who calls also defines the terms—follow, and let competing loyalties fall where they must.

Key contrast Both affirm Jesus’ absolute claim over disciples. Arminians typically emphasize a decisive, grace-enabled choice against delay; Reformed readers typically emphasize the King’s sovereign right to reorder every human loyalty under His call.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on seeing crowds—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 8:18–22.

Arminian Notable voices

“He commanded to go to the other side - That both himself and the people might have a little rest. The Son of man - The expression is borrowed from Dan. vii, 13, and is the appellation which Christ generally gives himself: which he seems to do out of humility, as having some relation to his mean appearance in this world.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 8:18–22)

Reformed Notable voices

“Two men are here presented to us by Matthew, and three by Luke, all of whom were prepared to become disciples of Christ, but who, having been prevented by a diversity of vices from following the right course, receive a corresponding variety of replies. It might at first sight appear strange, that Christ sends back, and does not admit into his family, one who offers to follow him immediately and without delay: while he detains another along with him who, by asking leave for a time, showed himself to be slower and less willing.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 8:18–22)
