Passage

Matthew 8

Verses 14–17 · ESV · Voices

In Peter’s house Jesus heals a fevered mother-in-law who rises to serve; that evening He casts out demons with a word and heals all who are sick. Matthew reads the mercy as Isaiah 53:4 fulfilled—the Servant who takes illnesses and bears diseases.

Matthew 8:14

14And when Jesus entered Peter’s house, he saw his mother-in-law lying sick with a fever.

Arminian

Jesus enters a disciple’s home and sees need lying in plain sight: Peter’s mother-in-law sick with fever. Kingdom power does not stay in the street; it crosses the threshold into ordinary family life.

Classical Arminians hear grace that notices and draws near. Households matter; the Lord who answered the centurion now meets fever under Peter’s roof. Shared ground: Christ is not distant from domestic suffering.

Reformed

Matthew places the Messiah inside Peter’s house. The King who taught and healed in public now stands over a fevered woman among His own.

Reformed readers see providence in the scene: the same Lord who ordered Capernaum’s ministry times this visit. Nothing here is accidental; mercy arrives under the Father’s purpose for the Servant’s path.

Matthew 8:15

15He touched her hand, and the fever left her, and she rose and began to serve him.

Arminian

A touch, fever gone, and immediate service: healing restores her to love’s work. Grace that lifts also frees the healed to serve the Healer.

Arminian pastoral note: recovery is for worshipful usefulness, not self-indulgence. Under assisting grace she rises and serves; discipleship answers mercy with glad obedience.

Reformed

Touch and departure of fever display authority as gentle as it is effective. She rises and serves Him—fruit of restoration, not a bargain for the cure.

Reformed comfort: Christ’s touch creates what disease had stolen. Service follows because life has returned at His will—a small picture of hearts remade to love the Lord who first loved us.

Matthew 8:16

16That evening they brought to him many who were oppressed by demons, and he cast out the spirits with a word and healed all who were sick.

Arminian

Evening brings the many: demon-oppressed and sick alike. Jesus casts out spirits with a word and heals all who were sick—authority that does not ration compassion.

Classical Arminians stress both the crowd’s responsible bringing and Christ’s free mercy. Need is carried to Jesus; He answers without partiality. Shared confession: His word is enough against darkness and disease.

Reformed

Word-exorcism and total healing mark the King’s dominion. Spirits obey speech; sickness yields. The evening harvest of mercy is not chaotic pity but sovereign power at work.

Reformed accent: “with a word” and “all who were sick” reveal efficacy that human technique cannot supply. The Lord who speaks creation’s order also dismisses demons and disease under the Father’s plan.

Key contrast Both confess Christ’s word-authority over demons and sickness. Arminians typically emphasize grace-enabled approach and Christ’s sincere willingness to heal all brought; Reformed readers typically emphasize the efficacious word that accomplishes what the Father appointed in the Servant’s mission.

Matthew 8:17

17This was to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet Isaiah: “He took our illnesses and bore our diseases.”

Arminian

Matthew cites Isaiah 53:4: Jesus takes illnesses and bears diseases. Healing is not a side hobby; it is Servant-fulfillment—mercy that points toward the cross where deeper burdens are carried.

Arminian reading holds the fulfillment close to pastoral hope: the same Servant who bore sickness also invites trust for sin’s deeper cure. Grace that fulfilled Isaiah still summons faith; believers may bring need without pretending Scripture flatters human strength.

Reformed

Fulfillment language anchors the healings in prophecy. The Servant’s bearing is already underway in Capernaum’s evening—a preview of the substitution that climaxes at the cross.

Reformed assurance: God’s word through Isaiah is not guesswork. Christ’s healings unfold the decree of Scripture; the One who bears diseases is the same Lord who will bear our sins according to the Father’s will.

Key contrast Both read Matthew’s Isaiah citation as true Servant fulfillment, not mere illustration. Arminians often stress the open invitation to trust the bearing Savior; Reformed readers often stress God’s sovereign fulfillment of prophecy in Christ’s appointed mission of bearing.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on in Peter’s house Jesus heals a fevered mother-in-law who rises to serve; that evening He casts out demons with a word—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 8:14–17.

Arminian Notable voices

“Peter's wife's mother - St. Peter was then a young man, as were all the apostles. Whereby was fulfilled what was spoken by the Prophet Isaiah - He spoke it in a more exalted sense. The evangelist here only alludes to those words, as being capable of this lower meaning also.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 8:14–17)

Reformed Notable voices

“That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Isaiah the prophet. This prediction has the appearance of being inappropriate, and even of being tortured into a meaning which it does not bear: for Isaiah does not there speak of miracles, but of the death of Christ, — and not of temporal benefits, but of spiritual and eternal grace.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 8:14–17)
