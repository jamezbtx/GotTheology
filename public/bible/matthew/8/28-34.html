Passage

Matthew 8

Verses 28–34 · WEB · Voices

On the other side, two demon-possessed men confront Jesus. The demons recognize the Son of God, beg, and enter a herd of pigs that perish—while the town asks Jesus to leave. Authority over hell meets human preference for pigs and peace over the Liberator.

Matthew 8:28

28When he came to the other side, into the country of the Gergesenes, two people possessed by demons met him there, coming out of the tombs, exceedingly fierce, so that nobody could pass that way.

Arminian

Tombs, fierceness, blocked road: demonic oppression isolates and terrifies. Jesus arrives where others cannot pass.

Arminians see grace invading darkness. The Liberator goes to the unboundable; people may yet bring such bondage to Him in prayer and hope.

Reformed

The crossing’s destination is revealed: Gadara’s tombs and two fierce demoniacs. No one could pass—until the King does.

Evil’s territory is still God’s. Demonic fierceness is authentic and limited; Christ enters by appointment, not by accident.

Matthew 8:29

29Behold, they cried out, saying, “What do we have to do with you, Jesus, Son of God? Have you come here to torment us before the time?”

Arminian

Demons know His name—Son of God—and dread “the time.” Orthodoxy without love still trembles. They expect torment, not mercy for themselves.

Right confession can exist without saving trust. Even hell believes and shudders; humans are summoned to obedient faith.

Reformed

Behold again: hell’s theologians recognize the Son and the clock of judgment. “Before the time” assumes God’s set day.

Eschatology is known in the abyss. Christ’s authority is already eschatological; demons cannot renegotiate the decree.

Key contrast Both note demonic recognition without saving faith. Arminians typically warn that knowledge alone does not save; Reformed readers press God’s fixed judgment timetable that even demons confess.

Matthew 8:30

30Now there was a herd of many pigs feeding far away from them.

Arminian

Pigs at a distance set the stage for the strange request to come. Gentile territory markers fill the scene.

Narrative pause: Matthew prepares the cost the region will feel when liberation collides with livelihood.

Reformed

Matthew notes the herd before the bargain. Geography and economy sit inside the exorcism account.

Nothing in the scene is incidental. The pigs will become the demons’ permitted exit and the town’s preferred loss-assessment.

Matthew 8:31

31The demons begged him, saying, “If you cast us out, permit us to go away into the herd of pigs.”

Arminian

They beg—already conceding His power to cast them out. Negotiating destination is not dictating whether they leave.

Jesus is not peer to demons. Grace for the possessed rests on a Lord demons must petition.

Reformed

Begging proves hierarchy. “If you cast us out” assumes His decision; the herd is a plea, not a right.

Even unclean spirits move only by permission. Sovereignty frames the exorcism—hell asks; the Son grants or refuses.

Matthew 8:32

32He said to them, “Go!” They came out and went into the herd of pigs; and behold, the whole herd of pigs rushed down the cliff into the sea and died in the water.

Arminian

One word—“Go”—and exit becomes stampede and drowning. Liberation for men; ruin for the herd. Demonic intent remains destructive even in departure.

Arminian interpreters celebrate the freed men first. Costly miracles still call towns to value people over pigs.

Reformed

“Go” is enough. Permission becomes judgment-shaped exit; the herd’s death displays the destroyer’s nature.

Christ’s word governs spirits and swine. The scene previews final expulsion—authority now, consummation later.

Key contrast Both see absolute authority in Jesus’ “Go.” Arminians typically press the moral priority of freed persons over economic loss; Reformed readers commonly press sovereign permission and judgment previewed in the demons’ destructive end.

Matthew 8:33

33Those who fed them fled and went away into the city and told everything, including what happened to those who were possessed with demons.

Arminian

Herdsmen flee and report everything—especially the men. News spreads; response remains open.

Testimony creates opportunity. The city can welcome the Liberator or push Him away.

Reformed

Flight and report carry the story to town. Emphasis falls on the demoniacs—proof of power.

Witness goes out under providence. How the city answers will reveal hearts more than herds.

Matthew 8:34

34Behold, all the city came out to meet Jesus. When they saw him, they begged that he would depart from their borders.

Arminian

The whole city meets Jesus—and begs Him to leave. Presence that frees men threatens a preferred order. Rejection can be communal and polite.

Arminian readers mourn refusal. Light came; they asked it to depart. Grace had drawn near; responsibility remains for that tragic ask.

Reformed

Behold the irony: welcome that is dismissal. They beg like the demons begged—wanting distance from the Son.

Hardness can prefer pigs and quiet over Christ. Judicial blindness may ask the only Savior to leave—and He may grant that dreadful request.

Key contrast Both treat the city’s plea as tragic unbelief. Arminians stress culpable rejection of near grace; Reformed interpreters typically press judicial hardness that would rather lose Jesus than lose control of its region.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What earlier interpreters on each side wrote about Matthew 8:28–34.

Arminian Notable voices

“The country of the Gergesenes - Or of the Gadarenes - Gergesa and Gadara were towns near each other. Hence the country between them took its name, sometimes from the one, sometimes from the other. There met him two demoniacs - St. Mark and St. Luke mention only one, who was probably the fiercer of the two, and the person who spoke to our Lord first.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 8:28–34)

Reformed Notable voices

“Willingly would they, by this word, drive him far from them. But when they see that they are held under restraint, and that it is in vain for them to decline his authority, they complain that they are tormented before the time, and likewise mingle entreaty.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 8:28–34)
