Passage

Matthew 8

Verses 1–4 · ESV · Voices

Jesus comes down from the Sermon on the Mount into concrete mercy: a leper kneels, Jesus touches the unclean, and Mosaic law is honored as the man is sent to the priest. Kingdom authority shown in word now appears in healing compassion.

Matthew 8:1

1When he came down from the mountain, great crowds followed him.

Arminian

The mountain sermon does not stay on the hill. Jesus descends, and crowds follow—teaching that has authority now walks among need.

Classical Arminians hear grace that keeps drawing near. The same Lord who called for heart-obedience now invites approach. Following is available; crowds can press close, and individuals within them will yet be summoned to personal faith.

Reformed

Matthew links the sermon to the healings that follow. Descent from the mountain is not anticlimax; it is the King moving among His people under the Father’s purpose.

Reformed readers see crowds as providence, not chaos. The Lord who taught with authority now displays that authority in mercy—still the same Messiah advancing toward the cross.

Matthew 8:2

2And behold, a leper came to him and knelt before him, saying, “Lord, if you will, you can make me clean.”

Arminian

A leper kneels and confesses both need and confidence: “if you will, you can.” Faith does not dictate the timeline; it trusts Jesus’ power and yields to His will.

Arminian pastoral reading treasures responsible trust. The man comes, kneels, and asks. Grace has made approach possible; the plea is sincere, not presumptuous. Shared ground: only Jesus can cleanse what uncleanness cannot fix.

Reformed

“Behold” marks wonder: the unclean draws near the Holy One. His prayer holds power (“you can”) under submission (“if you will”).

Reformed accent: such faith is itself gift. The leper’s words echo true discipleship—sovereign ability confessed, personal will trusted. Nothing here flatters human strength; everything hangs on the Lord’s mercy.

Key contrast Both honor the leper’s humble faith. Arminians typically stress a real, grace-enabled approach and plea; Reformed readers typically stress that even this trust is wrought by God, resting wholly on Christ’s willing power.

Matthew 8:3

3And Jesus stretched out his hand and touched him, saying, “I will; be clean.” And immediately his leprosy was cleansed.

Arminian

Jesus touches what others avoided and speaks what only God can effect: “I will; be clean.” Immediate cleansing shows willing compassion joined to effective power.

Classical Arminians linger on the personal “I will.” The Savior freely chooses to heal; grace is not reluctant. Believers may come expecting a merciful Lord who delights to make unclean people clean.

Reformed

Word and touch unite. The Holy One is not defiled by the leper; uncleanness yields to holiness. Immediacy seals divine authority.

Reformed comfort: regeneration’s pattern is glimpsed—Christ’s will and word create cleanness the sinner cannot produce. The touch that should have polluted instead purifies, because this is the Lord of life.

Matthew 8:4

4And Jesus said to him, “See that you say nothing to anyone, but go, show yourself to the priest and offer the gift that Moses commanded, for a proof to them.”

Arminian

Silence, priest, and offering: Jesus honors Moses while proving the miracle. The cleansed man is sent into Israel’s appointed path, not around it.

Arminian application: gratitude obeys. Grace that heals also directs. Testimony to the priests is both obedience and witness—light offered to those who must examine what God has done.

Reformed

The command protects mission timing and fulfills Torah. Moses is not discarded; the gift becomes “proof to them.”

Reformed reading: Christ is Lord of the law He gave. Healing serves a larger purpose—witness inside Israel’s structures under the Father’s wise schedule for revelation.

Key contrast Both affirm Jesus’ respect for Moses and the priestly witness. Arminians often emphasize obedient response after grace received; Reformed readers often emphasize Christ’s lordship over the law and providential timing of public proof.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus comes down from the Sermon on the Mount into concrete mercy: a leper kneels, Jesus touches the unclean—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 8:1–4.

Arminian Notable voices

“A leper came - Leprosies in those countries were seldom curable by natural means, any more than palsies or lunacy. Probably this leper, though he might not mix with the people, had heard our Lord at a distance. See thou tell no man - Perhaps our Lord only meant here, Not till thou hast showed thyself to the priest-who was appointed to inquire into the case of leprosy.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 8:1–4)

Reformed Notable voices

“And when he had come down from the mountain Matthew now returns to the course of the history. He had formerly said, that Christ went up into a mountain, (verse 1) then he threw, as it were, into one heap, many leading points of the doctrine of Christ; and now he adds that, about the time when he preached on the mountain, he healed a certain leper The same event is related by Mark and Luke, though they do not mention the time.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 8:1–4)
