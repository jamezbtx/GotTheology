Passage

Matthew 8

Verses 23–27 · ESV

Jesus embarks; a sudden storm terrifies the disciples while He sleeps. Their cry, His rebuke of wind and sea, and their wonder disclose both little faith and lordship over creation.

Matthew 8:23

23And when he got into the boat, his disciples followed him.

Arminian

Disciples follow into the boat—obedience that will soon be tested by water and wind. Following is real before the crisis arrives.

Arminian note: grace has brought them this far; they still must keep trusting when the lake turns violent.

Reformed

Embarking fulfills the earlier order. Disciples follow the King into danger He already knows.

Reformed comfort: the storm ahead is not outside His plan. Their following places them under the same providence that rules wind and wave.

Matthew 8:24

24And behold, there arose a great storm on the sea, so that the boat was being swamped by the waves; but he was asleep.

Arminian

Great storm, swamped boat, sleeping Lord—the contrast is pastoral and piercing. Crisis can feel like divine absence even when Jesus is present.

Classical Arminians allow real fear in believers. Faith is summoned to cry out rather than despair; His sleep is not indifference.

Reformed

“Behold” marks the shock. Nature rages; the Creator naps. The scene is designed to reveal hearts and glory.

Reformed reading: sleep is not neglect but sovereign calm. The Lord who appoints the storm also rests in the boat—fear meets providence.

Matthew 8:25

25And they went and woke him, saying, “Save us, Lord; we are perishing.”

Arminian

They wake Him with a true prayer: Save us, Lord. Panic and faith tangle—“we are perishing” beside “Lord.”

Arminian pastoral help: imperfect faith may still cry rightly. Grace invites the plea; responsibility is to bring fear to Jesus rather than only to the oars.

Reformed

The cry is half right: Lord, save. Perishing language shows little faith’s math—present waves outweigh present Christ.

Reformed mercy: even weak prayer reaches Him. He will answer, then diagnose. Salvation in the storm comes from the One they woke.

Matthew 8:26

26And he said to them, “Why are you afraid, O you of little faith?” Then he rose and rebuked the winds and the sea, and there was a great calm.

Arminian

Little faith is named, then winds and sea are rebuked into great calm. Jesus addresses fear before He addresses weather—discipleship of the heart.

Classical Arminians hear a call to grow. Assisting grace strengthens trust; believers are responsible not to let fear contradict the Lord in the boat.

Reformed

Question first, miracle second. Little faith is real in the elect yet rebuked. Then creation obeys its Lord—great calm.

Reformed wonder: the same voice that justifies and sanctifies stills storms. Fear shrinks when sovereignty is seen; calm is His gift and His credential.

Key contrast Both affirm little faith rebuked and Christ as Lord of nature. Arminians typically stress growth in trust under assisting grace; Reformed readers typically stress God’s sovereign calm—fear yields as the disciples glimpse who sleeps in their boat.

Matthew 8:27

27And the men marveled, saying, “What sort of man is this, that even winds and sea obey him?”

Arminian

Marvel asks the right question: What sort of man? Creation’s obedience points beyond a mere teacher to divine authority.

Arminian invitation: keep following the question to its answer. Grace keeps revealing Jesus; faith responds by deeper worship.

Reformed

Wonder is incomplete confession moving toward truth. Winds and sea obey—Psalm-like dominion in a man.

Reformed confession: this is the Lord of Hosts in flesh. The storm narrative catechizes Christology; providence has preached His deity to trembling disciples.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
