Passage

Matthew 11

Verses 20–24 · ESV · Voices

After exposing this generation’s refusal of both John’s austerity and the Son of Man’s table fellowship (vv. 16–19), Jesus denounces the Galilean cities where most of His mighty works had been done—Chorazin, Bethsaida, and Capernaum—comparing their unrepentance with Tyre, Sidon, and Sodom.

Matthew 11:20

20Then he began to denounce the cities where most of his mighty works had been done, because they did not repent.

Arminian

Jesus’ denunciation is grounded in a clear reason: these cities received extraordinary light—most of His mighty works—and still would not repent. Privilege heightens responsibility; miracles were not entertainment but summons to turn.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere opportunity wasted. Grace went first in works and word; the cities remain accountable for rejecting revealed light. Judgment is not arbitrary—it answers real refusal of what God had genuinely set before them.

Reformed

Matthew frames Jesus’ woes as judicial speech after prolonged revelation. The cities where most of His mighty works were done failed to repent; greater light exposes greater guilt.

Reformed readers hear both justice and sovereignty: God had placed decisive witness in these towns, and hardness under that light is not outside His purpose. Denunciation is righteous accountability within the story God is advancing toward the cross and the day of judgment.

Key contrast Both affirm that greater revelation intensifies guilt for unrepentance. Arminians typically stress a real, grace-enabled opportunity sincerely wasted by free refusal; Reformed readers typically stress judicial hardness under rejected light, with accountability unfolding inside God’s sovereign purpose.

Matthew 11:21

21“Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida! For if the mighty works done in you had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes.

Arminian

The double woe names towns that saw Jesus’ power yet stayed unmoved. The comparison is devastating: pagan Tyre and Sidon, given the same works, “would have repented long ago” in sackcloth and ashes.

Classical Arminians take the counterfactual seriously as moral indictment. Chorazin and Bethsaida had light sufficient for a real response; their neighbors’ imagined repentance underlines how freely—and culpably—these cities refused what grace had shown.

Reformed

Jesus’ woe is prophetic judgment, not mere scolding. He measures Chorazin and Bethsaida against Tyre and Sidon to show how deeply privileged hardness runs.

Reformed preaching hears Christ’s omniscient verdict: relative to the light given, these Galilean towns stand worse than notorious Gentile cities. The comparison serves justice and warning; distribution of mighty works remains God’s sovereign prerogative, and refusal under that light confirms judicial blindness.

Key contrast Both use the Tyre/Sidon comparison to shame unrepentant privilege. Arminians typically hear proof that the same works could have elicited genuine repentance elsewhere, underscoring wasted free response; Reformed readers typically hear Christ’s judicial assessment of comparative hardness under sovereignly distributed light, not a lesson in libertarian counterfactuals.

Matthew 11:22

22But I tell you, it will be more bearable on the day of judgment for Tyre and Sidon than for you.

Arminian

Jesus escalates from woe to the day of judgment: Tyre and Sidon will find that day “more bearable” than Chorazin and Bethsaida. Degrees of judgment match degrees of light refused.

Arminian moral theology gladly affirms proportionate justice. God does not flatten all guilt; those who reject clearer revelation bear heavier accountability. The warning is pastoral as well as judicial—repent while the light still shines.

Reformed

Christ speaks with authority about the last day. Judgment will be graded: more light rejected means a less bearable verdict.

Reformed comfort and fear meet here. God’s justice is exact, not crude; His purpose includes a day when every privilege and refusal is weighed. The same Lord who worked mighty deeds in Galilee will judge those cities with perfect equity.

Matthew 11:23

23And you, Capernaum, will you be exalted to heaven? You will be brought down to Hades. For if the mighty works done in you had been done in Sodom, it would have remained until this day.

Arminian

Capernaum—Jesus’ ministry base—is addressed with irony: will it be exalted to heaven? No; it will be brought down to Hades. Even Sodom, given Capernaum’s works, “would have remained until this day.”

Classical Arminians hear pride rebuked and opportunity measured. Capernaum’s nearness to Christ was sincere privilege, not automatic safety. Refusing such light is self-exaltation that ends in ruin; judgment fits the height from which they fell.

Reformed

Capernaum’s imagined exaltation is shattered by Jesus’ verdict: down to Hades. The Sodom comparison is the sharpest possible—history’s emblem of judgment would have endured had it received Capernaum’s signs.

Reformed readers stress Christ’s sovereign knowledge and holy justice. Proximity to miracles does not equal election’s comfort; under rejected light, a favored town faces deeper condemnation. God weighs privilege inside His purpose, and Capernaum’s fall displays that righteousness.

Key contrast Both see Capernaum’s fall as judgment proportionate to unmatched privilege. Arminians typically emphasize a wasted, grace-attended opportunity that Capernaum truly could have answered with repentance; Reformed readers typically emphasize judicial abasement after rejected light, with the Sodom saying exposing hardness under God’s sovereign allocation of revelation.

Matthew 11:24

24But I tell you that it will be more tolerable on the day of judgment for the land of Sodom than for you.”

Arminian

The pericope closes by repeating the graded-judgment theme: Sodom’s land will find the day “more tolerable” than Capernaum. Nearness to Jesus’ works without repentance is worse than ancient wickedness without that light.

Arminian application is urgent and fair: responsibility tracks revelation. The gospel’s privileges are sincere gifts; to refuse them is to choose a heavier judgment. Soft hearts still may turn while the summons stands.

Reformed

Jesus seals Capernaum’s indictment with eschatological certainty. Even Sodom’s land will fare more tolerably than a town that saw the Messiah’s power and would not repent.

Reformed doxology and warning: the Judge of all the earth does right. Greater light means greater accountability within God’s purpose; the day will vindicate both His generosity in revelation and His justice toward those who hardened under it.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on after exposing this generation’s refusal of both John’s austerity and the Son of Man’s table fellowship—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 11:20–24.

Arminian Notable voices

“Then began he to upbraid the cities - It is observable he had never upbraided them before. Indeed at first they received him with all gladness, Capernaum in particular. Woe to thee, Chorazin - That is, miserable art thou. For these are not curses or imprecations, as has been commonly supposed; but a solemn, compassionate declaration of the misery they were bringing on themselves.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 11:20–24)

Reformed Notable voices

“Luke states the time when, and the reason why, Christ uttered such invectives against those cities. It was while he was sending the disciples away into various parts of Judea, to proclaim, as they passed along, that the kingdom of God was at hand.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 11:20–24)
