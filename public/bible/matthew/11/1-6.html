Passage

Matthew 11

Verses 1–6 · ESV

After commissioning the Twelve (ch. 10), Jesus resumes His own ministry while John the Baptist—now in prison—sends disciples to ask whether Jesus is the Coming One. Jesus answers with Isaiah-shaped messianic works and a beatitude for those not offended by Him.

Matthew 11:1

1When Jesus had finished instructing his twelve disciples, he went on from there to teach and preach in their cities.

Arminian

Matthew marks a transition: the mission charge ends, yet Jesus Himself keeps teaching and preaching. The kingdom does not pause while the Twelve go out; the Master remains the primary herald of good news.

Classical Arminians notice the pattern of grace that goes first and invites response. Jesus’ ongoing word in “their cities” keeps the door of hearing open—revelation offered so that hearers may trust and follow.

Reformed

The verse bridges commission and controversy. Jesus finishes instructing the Twelve and immediately continues His own public ministry—teaching and preaching under the Father’s purpose.

Reformed readers hear providence in the movement: the same Lord who sends laborers also advances the gospel Himself. The story that follows (John’s question) unfolds while Christ’s word is already at work in the towns.

Matthew 11:2

2Now when John heard in prison about the deeds of the Christ, he sent word by his disciples

Arminian

John is in prison—suffering, constrained, and yet still seeking clarity about “the deeds of the Christ.” Matthew does not shame the Baptist for struggle; he shows a faithful prophet who wants to understand what he hears.

Arminian pastoral reading honors real human doubt under pressure. Grace does not pretend prisons are easy. John’s inquiry is a living response to news about Jesus, seeking confirmation that can sustain trust.

Reformed

John’s imprisonment is not outside God’s story. Even from a cell he hears of “the deeds of the Christ”—messianic works already bearing witness—and sends disciples with a question.

Reformed comfort: providence rules the darkness of prison as surely as the brightness of miracles. God is advancing His purpose for John, for Jesus’ identity, and for those who will overhear this exchange.

Matthew 11:3

3and said to him, “Are you the one who is to come, or shall we look for another?”

Arminian

The question is direct and costly: Is Jesus the Coming One, or should hope keep searching? John had pointed others to Jesus; now, under suffering, he needs reassurance for himself and his followers.

Classical Arminians see here a sincere seeking that grace meets with evidence. The Lord does not crush the questioner; He will give grounds for faith. Prevenient and assisting grace enable a struggling heart to receive revelation and not turn away.

Reformed

John’s question names the heart of Christology: Is this Jesus the promised Coming One? The Baptist’s earlier certainty meets the hard providence of prison, and the question is brought to Jesus Himself.

Reformed preaching holds both John’s real struggle and God’s sure purpose: the Messiah will answer in a way that confirms His identity. Faith’s assurance rests finally on Christ’s self-attestation, not on unbroken emotional certainty.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat John’s struggle as unbelief to be mocked. Arminians typically stress grace that meets genuine doubt with inviting evidence and enables a free response of trust; Reformed readers typically stress God’s purpose advancing through the prison crisis and Christ’s effective attestation that steadies those He keeps.

Matthew 11:4

4And Jesus answered them, “Go and tell John what you hear and see:

Arminian

Jesus does not answer with a bare title alone; He sends John’s disciples back with reportable evidence—“what you hear and see.” Faith is invited to rest on the works and words God has already displayed.

Arminian accent: revelation is given so that it may be received. The command “go and tell” treats John’s messengers as responsible witnesses carrying light that can strengthen trust.

Reformed

Christ’s reply is revelatory and pastoral. He points to public deeds and heard preaching as the Father’s attestation of the Son.

Reformed emphasis: Jesus authoritatively interprets His own ministry. The signs are not ambiguous curiosities; they are divine witness that John—and we—are meant to receive as confirmation of the Coming One.

Matthew 11:5

5the blind receive their sight and the lame walk, lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them.

Arminian

Jesus stacks Isaiah-shaped mercies: sight, walking, cleansing, hearing, resurrection life, and gospel for the poor. The Messiah’s identity is shown in restorative power and preached good news—grace that heals and invites.

Classical Arminians gladly hear an open-handed display: these works summon trust. The same Lord who opens blind eyes offers light to consciences; assisting grace enables sinners to welcome what God has shown rather than stumble over unmet expectations.

Reformed

The catalogue echoes Isaiah’s messianic hope and presents Christ’s works as God’s own attestation. Blind, lame, leprous, deaf, dead, and poor—each category displays the kingdom breaking in under Jesus’ authority.

Reformed doxology: these are not merely persuasive samples; they are the appointed signs by which the Father marks His Son. Providence has timed them for John’s hour and for the church’s confession that Jesus is the Christ.

Key contrast Both see Isaiah fulfilled in Jesus’ mercies. Arminians often emphasize the signs as gracious evidence that sincerely invites and enables trust; Reformed readers often emphasize the signs as God’s effective attestation of the Messiah within a purpose that will not fail.

Matthew 11:6

6And blessed is the one who is not offended by me.”

Arminian

Jesus ends with a beatitude: blessing rests on the one who does not stumble over Him—His person, pace, or unexpected way of bringing the kingdom. The word is a sincere summons not to take offense when Jesus does not match our timetable.

Classical Arminians hear a real call addressed to hearers who may receive or refuse. Prevenient grace enables the heart not to take offense; the blessing is offered earnestly to all who will hold fast to Christ.

Reformed

The beatitude guards discipleship under mystery. Offense at Jesus—His cross-shaped path, delayed judgment, or mercy to the unlikely—is a real danger; blessing belongs to those kept from that stumble.

Reformed comfort: the “blessed” are not self-made sturdy souls. God grants the grace that keeps sinners from being scandalized by the Messiah He has sent, even when providence is dark as a prison.

Key contrast Both treat the beatitude as pastoral warning and promise. Arminians typically hear a genuine invitation not to stumble, enabled by assisting grace; Reformed readers typically hear blessing for those whom God effectually keeps from offense by Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
