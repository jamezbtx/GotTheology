Passage

Matthew 11

Verses 28–30 · ESV

From thanksgiving over the Father’s revelatory will (vv. 25–27), Jesus turns outward to the weary. He invites all who labor and are heavy laden to come to Him for rest, to take His yoke, learn from His gentleness and lowliness, and find that His yoke is easy and His burden light.

Matthew 11:28

28Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.

Arminian

The invitation is wide and personal: “Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden.” Jesus addresses the exhausted—burdened by sin, law-heavy religion, sorrow, or the weight of a world that will not rest—and promises rest as His gift.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere gospel call. “All” names a real offer; prevenient and assisting grace enable the weary to come without coercion. The rest is not earned by cleverness (vv. 25–27) but received by coming to Christ. Pastoral tone: the door is open; the Savior means what He says.

Reformed

Christ Himself is the resting place. The call is gospel-shaped and authoritative: come to Me—not to a technique, a party, or another yoke—and I will give you rest. The weary are summoned to the Son who alone knows the Father (v. 27).

Reformed preaching holds the free offer together with effectual grace. The outward call is genuinely addressed to the laboring; those who come do so because the Father draws and the Son’s rest is actually given. Comfort: rest is Christ’s gift, not the fruit of our self-rescue.

Key contrast Both treasure the wide, pastoral invitation and Christ’s promised rest. Arminians typically emphasize a sincere universal call enabled by resistible grace so that all the weary truly may come; Reformed readers typically emphasize that the same Lord who invites also effectually brings His own—outward call sincere, inward call saving—so that those who rest in Him do so by grace that prevails.

Matthew 11:29

29Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls.

Arminian

Coming is not passivity without discipleship. Jesus invites a yoke—His teaching and lordship—and a school: “learn from me.” The Teacher’s heart is gentle and lowly; the rest He gives reaches the soul, not merely the schedule.

Arminian accent: the command to take and learn treats hearers as responsible disciples. Grace enables a free embrace of Christ’s easy authority in place of crushing burdens. Gentleness in the Master encourages rather than terrifies the weak who come.

Reformed

Rest and yoke belong together. Christ does not offer a vacation from obedience but a new Master whose character is gentle and lowly. Soul-rest is found under His teaching, not beside it.

Reformed pastoral note: the same sovereign Son who chooses to reveal the Father (v. 27) is meek toward those He calls. Effectual grace does not cancel discipleship; it creates learners who find that Christ’s yoke fits the soul He has claimed.

Key contrast Both refuse a grace that excuses discipleship or a yoke that denies rest. Arminians often stress the responsible, grace-enabled choice to take Christ’s yoke; Reformed readers often stress that those effectually drawn find themselves gladly learning under the gentle Lord who has given them rest.

Matthew 11:30

30For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light.”

Arminian

Jesus closes with contrast: His yoke is easy (kind, well-fitting), His burden light. Compared with Pharisaic heaviness or the unbearable weight of self-salvation, Christ’s way gives life.

Classical Arminians preach this as true encouragement to the hesitant. The invitation remains honest: those who come will not find a new tyranny but a Savior whose demands are matched by His mildness and help. Grace that enables coming also sustains walking under His light burden.

Reformed

The rationale for coming is Christ’s own character and gift: easy yoke, light burden. This is not sentimental license; it is the goodness of the Lord whose commandments are not crushing when received in union with Him.

Reformed comfort for the heavy-laden: if the yoke still feels only crushing, look again to the Giver. The One who hands over rest also defines the burden—and His people discover, by grace, that what He lays on them He also carries with them.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
