Passage

Matthew 11

Verses 7–15 · ESV

After John’s disciples leave with Jesus’ answer (vv. 2–6), Jesus turns to the crowds and interprets John’s ministry: the Baptist is no reed or soft courtier but more than a prophet—Malachi’s messenger, Elijah who is to come—yet the least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he.

Matthew 11:7

7As they went away, Jesus began to speak to the crowds concerning John: “What did you go out into the wilderness to see? A reed shaken by the wind?

Arminian

Jesus does not wait for rumor to define John. While the messengers depart, He asks the crowds what they sought in the wilderness—beginning with a picture of instability: a reed shaken by the wind.

Classical Arminians hear a pastoral defense that also summons honest memory. Grace invites hearers to reconsider what they already witnessed in John, so that prejudice and fickleness do not cancel a prophet’s witness.

Reformed

Christ interprets John for the crowds before misunderstanding hardens. The first question rules out a weak, wind-tossed figure—John was no weather vane of popular opinion.

Reformed readers notice the Lord’s sovereign care for His forerunner’s reputation and for the flock’s discernment. Jesus Himself frames the Baptist’s identity under the Father’s purpose.

Matthew 11:8

8What then did you go out to see? A man dressed in soft clothing? Behold, those who wear soft clothing are in kings' houses.

Arminian

Jesus next rules out luxury. Soft clothing belongs in kings’ houses, not in John’s austere calling. The crowds went out to a rugged herald, not a palace chaplain.

Arminian pastoral reading honors John’s costly faithfulness as a pattern of response to God’s call. Grace does not erase hardship; it enables steadfast witness when ease would be easier.

Reformed

The contrast is sharp: John’s rough vocation versus courtly comfort. Those who sought him did not find a man shaped by royal favor but by prophetic commission.

Reformed comfort: God often advances His kingdom through servants stripped of worldly soft clothing. Providence placed John in the wilderness, not in Herod’s halls, for the sake of the word he carried.

Matthew 11:9

9What then did you go out to see? A prophet? Yes, I tell you, and more than a prophet.

Arminian

Jesus affirms what the people rightly sensed—John is a prophet—and then raises the claim: “more than a prophet.” The forerunner stands at a unique hinge in redemptive history.

Classical Arminians rejoice that God gives clear light about His servants so hearers may receive them. Revelation about John is meant to be welcomed; grace enables the crowd to honor the prophet God has sent.

Reformed

Christ’s “Yes… and more” is authoritative christological framing. John is not merely one prophet among many; he occupies a singular place as the immediate herald of the Messiah.

Reformed emphasis: Jesus alone defines John’s greatness. The crowds’ opinions are corrected and elevated by the Lord’s own verdict within the Father’s unfolding plan.

Matthew 11:10

10This is he of whom it is written, “‘Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way before you.’

Arminian

Jesus identifies John with Malachi’s messenger sent “before your face” to prepare the way. Scripture’s promise meets a living person the crowds have already heard.

Arminians stress that fulfilled prophecy invites trust and response: God keeps His word and now summons hearers to prepare for the Lord the messenger announced. Assisting grace enables that preparation of heart.

Reformed

Matthew’s fulfillment logic continues: what was written is realized in John. The messenger is sent by God before the Lord’s face—divine initiative marking the path of the Messiah.

Reformed doxology: prophecy is not open-ended chance. The Lord who promised the forerunner also appointed him, so that the way of Christ advances according to Scripture.

Matthew 11:11

11Truly, I say to you, among those born of women there has arisen no one greater than John the Baptist. Yet the one who is least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he.

Arminian

Jesus crowns John with unmatched greatness “among those born of women,” then startles with a kingdom comparison: the least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he. Privilege turns on belonging to the new age John announced.

Classical Arminians hear both honor for John and an open invitation into kingdom life. The greater status is not earned by ranking but received by grace-enabled entrance into the reign John prepared others to welcome.

Reformed

John’s greatness is real and Jesus-certified; yet kingdom citizenship under the Messiah’s inaugurated reign surpasses even the forerunner’s Old-covenant height. The “least” in the kingdom stands in a richer redemptive position.

Reformed comfort: greatness in God’s economy is measured by Christ’s kingdom gift, not by human stature alone. Those brought into the kingdom share a privilege John pointed toward but did not yet fully inhabit.

Key contrast Both exalt John and the kingdom’s greater privilege. Arminians typically stress kingdom greatness as grace-enabled belonging offered to those who enter by faith; Reformed readers typically stress the greater standing of those God effectually brings into the inaugurated kingdom under Christ.

Matthew 11:12

12From the days of John the Baptist until now the kingdom of heaven has suffered violence, and the violent take it by force.

Arminian

Jesus describes the kingdom’s arrival as contested and urgent—“suffered violence,” with the forceful seizing it. From John’s days, the kingdom presses in and people press toward it with costly zeal.

Arminian pastoral reading often hears a summons to earnest pursuit: grace awakens holy urgency so hearers strive to enter, not drift. The kingdom is not taken by soft curiosity but by Spirit-enabled resolve.

Reformed

The saying is dense and debated, yet its force is clear: since John’s ministry the kingdom’s advance has been marked by conflict and forceful response—hostility against it, and also ardent pressing into it.

Reformed preaching holds both realities under providence: the kingdom advances amid opposition, and God draws people into it with a vigor that worldly ease never produces.

Matthew 11:13

13For all the Prophets and the Law prophesied until John,

Arminian

John stands as the hinge: Law and Prophets pointed forward “until John.” Promise reaches its threshold in the Baptist’s ministry.

Classical Arminians rejoice that God has spoken consistently and now calls hearers to recognize the moment. Scripture’s trajectory invites a living response to the One John heralds.

Reformed

Redemptive history is periodized by Jesus Himself: the prophetic witness of Law and Prophets runs up to John. He is the climactic forerunner, not an afterthought.

Reformed emphasis: God’s revelation unfolds on schedule. The same Lord who spoke through the Prophets appointed John’s hour as the hinge into the Messiah’s public work.

Matthew 11:14

14and if you are willing to accept it, he is Elijah who is to come.

Arminian

Jesus identifies John as the promised Elijah—yet frames it with “if you are willing to accept it.” Recognition is not automatic; it asks for a receptive heart.

Classical Arminians hear a genuine conditional invitation: God reveals John’s Elijah role and enables hearers to receive that hard, Scripture-shaped claim. Willingness is real, and assisting grace makes acceptance possible.

Reformed

John is Elijah “who is to come”—Malachi’s hope fulfilled in the forerunner’s person and mission. Jesus states the truth and notes the need for willing reception.

Reformed pastoral reading: the willingness to accept is itself among the gifts God works in those He enlightens. Christ discloses John’s identity; the Father grants ears that welcome the disclosure.

Key contrast Both confess John as the Elijah who was to come. Arminians typically emphasize the sincere call to willingly accept that identity, enabled by assisting grace; Reformed readers typically emphasize that those who accept do so because God effectually grants the willingness to receive Christ’s word.

Matthew 11:15

15He who has ears to hear, let him hear.

Arminian

Jesus ends with a classic summons: those who have ears must use them. Revelation about John and the kingdom is not for spectators; it demands hearing that leads to faith.

Classical Arminians treat the call as earnest and universal in address. Prevenient grace restores the capacity to hear; the responsibility to listen remains real. Blessed are those who receive what God has spoken.

Reformed

“Ears to hear” is gift language as well as command. Jesus calls for hearing even as He implies that true hearing is granted, not assumed.

Reformed comfort and warning together: the word goes out publicly, yet saving hearing is God’s work in the heart. Those given ears hear—and are summoned to heed what they have been given.

Key contrast Both press the urgency of hearing Jesus’ word about John. Arminians typically stress a real call to hear, enabled by prevenient grace for all; Reformed readers typically stress that effectual hearing is God’s gift to those He opens to receive the truth.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
