Passage

Matthew 11

Verses 25–27 · ESV · Voices

After pronouncing woes on unrepentant cities (vv. 20–24), Jesus turns from indictment to thanksgiving. He praises the Father—Lord of heaven and earth—for a revelatory will that hides kingdom truth from the self-wise and discloses it to “little children,” then speaks of the Son’s exclusive knowledge of the Father and of those to whom the Son chooses to reveal Him.

Matthew 11:25

25At that time Jesus declared, “I thank you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that you have hidden these things from the wise and understanding and revealed them to little children;

Arminian

Jesus thanks the Father not for arbitrary cruelty but for a pattern of grace: kingdom truth is not captured by human cleverness. “Wise and understanding” names those who lean on status and insight; “little children” names the humble who receive. Revelation meets lowliness.

Classical Arminians hear both judgment and mercy. Hiding is often read as God’s just response to prideful resistance—truth withheld from those who will not bow—while revealing to little children displays prevenient grace that enables the humble to welcome what God shows. The thanksgiving is pastoral: come as a child, not as a master of the mystery.

Reformed

Christ’s thanksgiving places revelation under the Father’s sovereign lordship. “Lord of heaven and earth” frames both hiding and revealing as divine acts, not accidents of human psychology alone. The wise are not robbed of a right; God freely discloses Himself as He wills.

Reformed readers hear election’s contour in the verbs: the Father has hidden and has revealed. Saving knowledge of “these things” is gift, not achievement. The church’s comfort is that God still opens eyes of “little children” according to His purpose, even when the learned remain closed.

Key contrast Both honor Jesus’ thanksgiving and the call to childlike receptivity. Arminians typically stress hiding as judgment on resisted light and revealing as grace that enables the humble to receive; Reformed readers typically stress the Father’s sovereign determination of who sees, so that revelation itself is an electing gift, not merely an offered opportunity.

Matthew 11:26

26yes, Father, for such was your gracious will.

Arminian

Jesus doubles the thanksgiving: the Father’s “gracious will” stands behind this way of revealing. Grace is not cold decree but fatherly kindness that delights to give light to the lowly.

Arminian pastoral reading keeps “gracious” warm and sincere. God’s will to reveal to little children coheres with a love that genuinely desires people to come; assisting grace still summons the proud to become childlike and receive what God freely shows.

Reformed

The Son ratifies the Father’s good pleasure. “Gracious will” (or “good pleasure”) names not reluctant permission but delighted purpose—God’s own eudokia behind the pattern of hiding and revealing.

Reformed comfort: when revelation seems uneven, the church does not accuse God of injustice. The Son praises the Father’s will; disciples learn to worship rather than demand that saving light be distributed on human terms.

Matthew 11:27

27All things have been handed over to me by my Father, and no one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son chooses to reveal him.”

Arminian

The Son’s unique knowledge of the Father and the Father’s of the Son is mutual and exclusive—then widened: the Son reveals the Father to “anyone” He chooses. Classical Arminians gladly confess Christ’s mediatorial authority: no one comes to the Father apart from the Son’s revealing work.

Yet “chooses to reveal” need not mean an arbitrary narrowing of love. Many Arminian readers hear the Son’s free, personal agency in making the Father known—revelation given through gospel proclamation and Spirit-enabled illumination that can be received or resisted. The verse magnifies Christ’s lordship without canceling the sincere call that follows in vv. 28–30.

Reformed

All things handed over to the Son, exclusive mutual knowledge between Father and Son, and revelation only to those the Son chooses—this is high Christology and sovereign grace. Saving knowledge of God is not a human climb; it is the Son’s gift.

Reformed confession: the Son’s choice to reveal is particular and effectual. Those who truly know the Father do so because the Son has willed to disclose Him. Assurance follows: if you know the Father in Christ, it is because He chose to make Him known—not because you outpaced others in insight.

Key contrast Both confess that the Son alone mediates true knowledge of the Father. Arminians typically read the Son’s choosing as free revelatory agency within a grace that sincerely enables response and may still be resisted; Reformed readers typically read it as the Son’s particular, effectual determination of who comes to know the Father—revelation that successfully gives what it intends.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on after pronouncing woes on unrepentant cities—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 11:25–27.

Arminian Notable voices

“Jesus answering - This word does not always imply, that something had been spoken, to which an answer is now made. It often means no more than the speaking in reference to some action or circumstance preceding. The following words Christ speaks in reference to the case of the cities above mentioned: I thank thee - That is, I acknowledge and joyfully adore the justice and mercy of thy dispensations: Because thou hast hid - That is, because thou hast suffered these things to be hid from men, who are in other respects wise and prudent, while thou hast discovered them to those of the weakest understanding, to them who are only wise to Godward.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 11:25–27)

Reformed Notable voices

“Though the Hebrew verb, answer, (nh,) is frequently employed even in the commencement of a discourse, yet in this passage I consider it to be emphatic; for it was from the present occurrence that Christ took occasion to speak. This is more fully confirmed by the words of Luke, that in the same hour Jesus rejoiced in spirit Whence came that rejoicing?”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 11:25–27)
