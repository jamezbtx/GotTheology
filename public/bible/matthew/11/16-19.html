Passage

Matthew 11

Verses 16–19 · ESV · Voices

After honoring John before the crowds (vv. 7–15), Jesus turns to diagnose “this generation”: like quarrelsome children in the marketplaces, it refuses both John’s austere call and the Son of Man’s table fellowship—yet wisdom is justified by her deeds.

Matthew 11:16

16“But to what shall I compare this generation? It is like children sitting in the marketplaces and calling to their playmates,

Arminian

Jesus searches for a fitting picture of “this generation” and lands on marketplace children calling to playmates. The comparison is pastoral and piercing: the issue is not lack of light but a stubborn, peevish spirit that will not join the game offered.

Classical Arminians hear a real moral diagnosis. Grace has approached this generation through prophets and now through the Messiah; their likeness to spoiled children names culpable resistance, not innocent confusion. The summons still stands for hearers who will answer rather than sit and complain.

Reformed

Christ’s rhetorical question introduces a judicial parable. “This generation” is measured against marketplace children who dictate terms and sulk when others will not play by their rules.

Reformed readers hear the Lord’s sovereign insight into hearts. Jesus Himself interprets the crowd’s posture under the Father’s purpose: hardness shows itself as childish control, not as a mere shortage of evidence. The diagnosis prepares the double charge that follows.

Key contrast Both treat the marketplace image as a fair indictment of peevish refusal. Arminians typically stress a generation that truly could have responded to grace yet freely prefers to sit and dictate terms; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s judicial reading of hardness inside God’s purpose, exposing control that masks unbelief.

Matthew 11:17

17“‘We played the flute for you, and you did not dance; we sang a dirge, and you did not mourn.’

Arminian

The children’s complaint is double: flute music brought no dance, and a dirge brought no mourning. Whatever tune is offered, the playmates will not join. The generation mirrors that contradiction—no approach satisfies.

Arminian pastoral reading hears a warning against selective hearing. God may approach in joy or in solemn call; prevenient grace enables either response. To refuse both is not discernment but a heart that will not yield.

Reformed

The couplet exposes impossible standards. Dance and mourning are both demanded in turn, and both refused. Jesus will apply the image to John and Himself: austerity and festivity alike are rejected.

Reformed emphasis: the problem is not the “tune” God sends but the will that will not mourn or rejoice under His word. Providence has varied the messengers; unbelief invents reasons to dismiss them all.

Matthew 11:18

18For John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say, ‘He has a demon.’

Arminian

John’s ascetic witness—“neither eating nor drinking”—was a living dirge of repentance. Instead of mourning with him, this generation slanders him: “He has a demon.” Severity is labeled madness rather than received as a call to turn.

Classical Arminians honor John’s costly obedience as grace-enabled faithfulness and treat the charge as free, culpable distortion. Light came in a hard form; hearers remain responsible for twisting it into accusation.

Reformed

Matthew explains the dirge half of the parable: John’s abstinence was rejected with demonic slander. The forerunner’s God-appointed austerity becomes, in unbelieving mouths, proof of possession.

Reformed comfort and warning meet: God sent John in that manner under His purpose, and hardness answered with blasphemous explanation. The verdict reveals the heart more than it explains the prophet.

Matthew 11:19

19The Son of Man came eating and drinking, and they say, ‘Look at him! A glutton and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!’ Yet wisdom is justified by her deeds.”

Arminian

Where John brought the dirge, the Son of Man brings the flute—eating, drinking, and table fellowship with tax collectors and sinners. The same generation now cries gluttony and drunkenness. No style of approach escapes their scorn; yet Jesus ends with hope: “wisdom is justified by her deeds.”

Classical Arminians hear both indictment and invitation. Christ’s open fellowship is sincere mercy toward the unlikely; the smear does not cancel the offer. Wisdom’s deeds—transformed lives, mercy received, truth lived—vindicate God’s way and summon hearers to recognize and trust rather than invent new insults.

Reformed

The flute half falls on Jesus Himself: the Son of Man’s eating and drinking, including friendship with tax collectors and sinners, is mocked as excess. Unbelief rejects festivity as surely as it rejected John’s fast.

Reformed climax: “wisdom is justified by her deeds.” God’s wise purpose in sending both the austere herald and the feasting Son will stand vindicated by what that wisdom accomplishes. Christ’s works and the fruit of His mission answer the slander; the Judge of hearts needs no leave from a fickle generation.

Key contrast Both see a generation that damns John for severity and Jesus for joy, and both rest in wisdom’s vindication. Arminians typically emphasize sincere, varied approaches of grace freely refused, with wisdom’s deeds inviting recognition; Reformed readers typically emphasize judicial exposure of contradictory unbelief and God’s wise purpose vindicated by Christ’s effective deeds.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on after honoring John before the crowds—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 11:16–19.

Arminian Notable voices

“This generation - That is, the men of this age. They are like those froward children of whom their fellows complain, that they will be pleased no way. John came neither eating nor drinking - In a rigorous austere way, like Elijah. And they say, He hath a devil - Is melancholy, from the influence of an evil spirit.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 11:16–19)

Reformed Notable voices

“This part is left out by Matthew, though it throws no small light on the connection of the words; for it was this circumstance which gave rise to Christ's expostulation, when he perceived that the scribes persisted so obstinately in despising God. The substance of this passage is, that the common people and the publicans gave glory to God; while the Scribes, flattering themselves with confidence in their own knowledge, cared little for what Christ said.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 11:16–19)
