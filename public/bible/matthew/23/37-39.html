Passage

Matthew 23

Verses 37–39 · ESV · Voices

After the woes, Jesus laments Jerusalem: prophetic killers whom He longed to gather as a hen gathers her brood—yet they were not willing. The house is left desolate until the city blesses the Coming One.

Matthew 23:37

37"O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it! How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!

Arminian

Double cry: Jerusalem, killer of prophets. “How often would I have gathered… and you were not willing!” Desire and resistance stand together—hen’s wings offered, brood refusing.

Classical Arminians hear sincere, repeated divine willingness met by real human unwillingness. Grace yearns and gathers; the city’s “not willing” is culpable, not scripted theater. The image invites trust under Christ’s wings still.

Reformed

Jesus’ lament names prophet-killing and His oft-willingness to gather. The hen image shows tender purpose; “you were not willing” records Israel’s resistance.

Reformed readers hold Christ’s genuine compassion with God’s sovereign counsel: the lament is true affection and true indictment. Unwillingness is their sin; gathering of the remnant proceeds by effectual grace even as judgment nears.

Key contrast Both take “would I… you were not willing” with full emotional and moral weight. Arminians typically emphasize a sincere, resistible gathering desire; Reformed readers typically emphasize true compassion and true human guilt within God’s sovereign purpose to save His people and judge persistent unbelief.

Matthew 23:38

38See, your house is left to you desolate.

Arminian

“See”—desolation announced. The house (temple/city) left to them empty of His protecting presence.

Arminian grief: refused wings lead to barren house. The warning aims at repentance before emptiness becomes final for that generation.

Reformed

Desolate house—judicial abandonment language. God withdraws the shelter they refused.

Reformed sobriety: Ichabod hangs over the house by Christ’s word. Desolation is righteous consequence within redemptive history’s turn.

Matthew 23:39

39For I tell you, you will not see me again, until you say, 'Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord.'"

Arminian

Sight of Jesus paused until the Psalm 118 blessing. Hope remains: a future confession of the Coming One.

Classical Arminians hear both severity and open future—Israel yet called to bless the One who comes. Grace keeps a door toward recognition and welcome.

Reformed

No seeing until the blessed confession. Psalm 118 frames return and recognition.

Reformed hope: God reserves a day when the city (and His people) truly honors the Coming One. Christ’s absence and return sit under the Father’s timetable; the blessing will be real when God grants it.

Key contrast Both hear judgment now and hope tied to blessing the Coming One. Arminians typically emphasize a real future invitation to confess Him; Reformed readers typically emphasize the same hope secured in God’s sovereign timing of recognition and return.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus laments Jerusalem: prophetic killers whom He longed to gather as a hen gathers her brood—yet they were not—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 23:37–39.

Arminian Notable voices

“These, as well as their phylacteries, the Pharisees affected to wear broader and larger than other men. The Jewish rabbis were also called father and master, by their several disciples, whom they required, To believe implicitly what they affirmed, without asking any further reason; To obey implicitly what they enjoined, without seeking further authority.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 23:37–39)

Reformed Notable voices

“For you build the sepulchers of the prophets. An unfounded opinion is entertained by some, that the scribes are here reproved for superstition, in foolishly honoring the deceased prophets by splendid sepulchers, as the Papists now transfer the honor of God to departed saints, and even are so perverse as to adore their images.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 23:37–39)
