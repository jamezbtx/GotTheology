Passage

Matthew 23

Verses 1–12 · ESV

Still in the temple courts during Passion Week, Jesus turns from sparring with leaders to address the crowds and His disciples. Before the seven woes, He names the scribes’ and Pharisees’ teaching seat, exposes hypocrisy and honor-seeking, and sets the church’s posture: one Teacher, one Father, greatness as service, exaltation through humility.

Matthew 23:1

1Then Jesus said to the crowds and to his disciples,

Arminian

Jesus widens the audience: crowds and disciples together. Grace teaches publicly so many may hear and turn.

Classical Arminians hear a pastoral warning aimed at followers, not only foes. Light is given so hearers can refuse hypocrisy and walk in truth.

Reformed

Matthew marks a deliberate address to crowds and disciples. The Lord teaches His people in the hearing of the city.

Reformed readers see Christ’s prophetic office: He speaks what the Father gives for this hour. The coming woes are framed first as discipleship instruction under His authority.

Matthew 23:2

2"The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses' seat,

Arminian

“Moses’ seat” names an official teaching posture. Jesus acknowledges a real teaching office even while He will soon expose abuse.

Arminian pastoral care: respect for Scripture’s teaching is not canceled by corrupt teachers. God still addresses His people through the Law’s witness; hearers are responsible to receive what is true.

Reformed

Jesus recognizes the scribes and Pharisees in Moses’ seat—an authorized teaching place tied to the Law.

Reformed emphasis: God ordered Israel’s instruction under Moses. The seat’s dignity is real; the occupants’ fidelity will be weighed. Christ’s word interprets both office and abuse.

Matthew 23:3

3so do and observe whatever they tell you, but not the works they do. For they preach, but do not practice.

Arminian

Do what they teach when it is Moses’ word; do not copy their works. Preaching without practice is hypocrisy that grace still names and calls to repent.

Classical Arminians stress real moral agency: hearers can and must distinguish true teaching from false living. The summons is sincere—obey truth, refuse imitation of empty religion.

Reformed

Jesus separates their teaching (as it conveys Moses) from their works. The charge is plain: they preach but do not practice.

Reformed sobriety: God’s Law remains binding; hypocrisy does not erase the command. Christ guards His flock by unmasking inconsistency while upholding the Word they mishandle.

Key contrast Both affirm obeying true Mosaic instruction while rejecting hypocritical example. Arminians typically emphasize the hearer’s responsible discernment and free refusal of empty religion; Reformed readers typically emphasize Christ’s authority to uphold the Law and expose hypocrisy within God’s ordered teaching economy.

Matthew 23:4

4They tie up heavy burdens, hard to bear, and lay them on people's shoulders, but they themselves are not willing to move them with their finger.

Arminian

Heavy burdens on others, no finger lifted to help—legal load without mercy. This contrasts Jesus’ own easy yoke (11:28–30).

Arminian preaching hears grace’s heart: God does not crush with empty loads. True holiness is enabled and accompanied; hypocrisy demands what it will not bear.

Reformed

Burden-binding without help paints false shepherds. The Law’s weight is twisted into crushing tradition while the teachers stay untouched.

Reformed comfort: Christ alone lifts what hypocrites pile on. God’s righteousness is not this fingerless severity; the Good Shepherd carries His own.

Matthew 23:5

5They do all their deeds to be seen by others. For they make their phylacteries broad and their fringes long,

Arminian

Motive is exposed: deeds “to be seen by others.” Broad phylacteries and long fringes turn devotion into display.

Classical Arminians warn that grace searches the heart. Public piety can be real or theatrical; God invites a sincere turn from audience-religion to love of the Father who sees in secret.

Reformed

All deeds aimed at visibility. Enlarged signs of piety become props for human praise.

Reformed diagnosis: the heart’s idol is glory from people (cf. Matt 6). God unmasks it; true fear of the Lord reorders worship away from the marketplace gaze.

Matthew 23:6

6and they love the place of honor at feasts and the best seats in the synagogues

Arminian

Love of honor seats reveals disordered desire. Status at feasts and synagogues competes with love of God and neighbor.

Arminian pastoral edge: grace frees from craving the best seat. A real choice remains—pursue recognition or pursue the kingdom’s lowly path.

Reformed

They love the places of honor. Preference for rank unmasks the heart’s treasure.

Reformed reading: providence will reverse seats. God records this love of status as evidence in the indictment Christ is building before the woes.

Matthew 23:7

7and greetings in the marketplaces and being called rabbi by others.

Arminian

Marketplace greetings and the title “rabbi” feed the same hunger for recognition. Teaching office becomes a brand.

Classical Arminians urge teachers to receive titles as service, not fuel. Grace can retrain ambition; the call is to refuse living for applause.

Reformed

Greetings and the rabbi-title complete the honor pattern. Public esteem becomes the currency of religion.

Reformed gravity: Christ names the love of being called teacher. The coming correction (vv. 8–10) will relocate authority in God and His Christ alone.

Matthew 23:8

8But you are not to be called rabbi, for you have one teacher, and you are all brothers.

Arminian

Disciples must not chase the rabbi-title. One Teacher; all are brothers. Equality under Christ guards the community from status games.

Arminian warmth: grace creates sibling fellowship. Shared learning under Jesus invites mutual humility rather than ranked gurus.

Reformed

One Teacher; all brothers. Christ’s unique teaching office relativizes human titles among His people.

Reformed confession: the church’s unity is grounded in the one Lord who teaches. Human instructors serve under Him; brotherhood is the given shape of the body.

Key contrast Both reject status-seeking under religious titles. Arminians typically emphasize grace-formed brotherhood that freely refuses guru culture; Reformed readers typically emphasize Christ’s exclusive teaching lordship that defines and secures the church’s equality.

Matthew 23:9

9And call no man your father on earth, for you have one Father, who is in heaven.

Arminian

Earthly “father” as spiritual ultimate is forbidden. One Father in heaven claims exclusive reverence.

Classical Arminians hear a call to redirect dependence: honor parents and elders rightly, but do not transfer divine Fatherhood to any man. Grace invites living under the heavenly Father’s name.

Reformed

One Father in heaven. Jesus protects the Father’s unique glory from earthly rivals in spiritual allegiance.

Reformed emphasis: God’s fatherhood is not shared as ultimate source. Human fathers and pastors are secondary; the church confesses one Father who begets, adopts, and rules.

Matthew 23:10

10Neither be called instructors, for you have one instructor, the Christ.

Arminian

Nor chase the instructor-title. The Christ is the one Instructor. Disciples teach only as His students.

Arminian pastoral note: ministry gifts remain, but ambition for the master-label must die. Grace enables teachers to point away from themselves to Jesus.

Reformed

One instructor: the Christ. All human instruction is derivative.

Reformed comfort: Christ rules His church as Teacher. The Spirit applies His word; earthly instructors are stewards, never rivals to the Son’s chair.

Matthew 23:11

11The greatest among you shall be your servant.

Arminian

Greatness is redefined as service. The path up is the path down into serving the brothers.

Classical Arminians urge a real embrace of servanthood—grace enables choosing the towel over the throne in daily relationships.

Reformed

Greatness = servant. Jesus restates the kingdom’s upside-down measure (cf. 20:26).

Reformed formation: God assigns true greatness in the shape of the Servant King. Status in the body is measured by lowly usefulness under Christ’s rule.

Matthew 23:12

12Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted."

Arminian

Self-exaltation meets humbling; self-humbling meets exaltation. God opposes pride and lifts the lowly—a living warning and promise.

Classical Arminians press the moral urgency: refuse pride now. Grace invites humble repentance that God delights to honor; the proverb is not fatalism but a call.

Reformed

Divine reversal: the self-exalted are humbled; the humble are exalted. God Himself executes the exchange.

Reformed confession: providence and judgment secure this pattern. Christ’s people trust the Father to humble and to lift in His time—assurance that honor-seeking religion will not finally stand.

Key contrast Both take the reversal as certain under God. Arminians typically emphasize a sincere summons to humble oneself in response to grace; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s sovereign humbling and exalting as the sure ordering of pride and lowliness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
