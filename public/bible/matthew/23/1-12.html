Passage

Matthew 23

Verses 1–12 · WEB · Voices

Still in the temple courts during Passion Week, Jesus turns from sparring with leaders to address the crowds and His disciples. Before the seven woes, He names the scribes’ and Pharisees’ teaching seat, exposes hypocrisy and honor-seeking, and sets the church’s posture: one Teacher, one Father, greatness as service, exaltation through humility.

Matthew 23:1

1Then Jesus spoke to the multitudes and to his disciples,

Arminian

Jesus widens the audience: crowds and disciples together. Grace teaches publicly so many may hear and turn.

What comes through is a pastoral warning aimed at followers, not only foes. Light is given so hearers can refuse hypocrisy and walk in truth.

Reformed

Matthew marks a deliberate address to crowds and disciples. The Lord teaches His people in the hearing of the city.

Reformed readers see Christ’s prophetic office: He speaks what the Father gives for this hour. The coming woes are framed first as discipleship instruction under His authority.

Matthew 23:2

2saying, “The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses’ seat.

Arminian

“Moses’ seat” names an official teaching posture. Jesus acknowledges a teaching office even while He will soon expose abuse.

Respect for Scripture’s teaching is not canceled by corrupt teachers. God still addresses His people through the Law’s witness; hearers are responsible to receive what is true.

Reformed

Jesus recognizes the scribes and Pharisees in Moses’ seat—an authorized teaching place tied to the Law.

God ordered Israel’s instruction under Moses. The seat’s dignity is not in doubt; the occupants’ fidelity will be weighed. Christ’s word interprets both office and abuse.

Matthew 23:3

3All things therefore whatever they tell you to observe, observe and do, but don’t do their works; for they say, and don’t do.

Arminian

Do what they teach when it is Moses’ word; do not copy their works. Preaching without practice is hypocrisy that grace still names and calls to repent.

Arminian interpreters stress moral agency: hearers can and must distinguish true teaching from false living. The summons is sincere—obey truth, refuse imitation of empty religion.

Reformed

Jesus separates their teaching (as it conveys Moses) from their works. The charge is plain: they preach but do not practice.

God’s Law remains binding; hypocrisy does not erase the command. Christ guards His flock by unmasking inconsistency while upholding the Word they mishandle.

Key contrast Both affirm obeying true Mosaic instruction while rejecting hypocritical example. Arminians emphasize the hearer’s responsible discernment and free refusal of empty religion; Reformed Christians typically emphasize Christ’s authority to uphold the Law and expose hypocrisy within God’s ordered teaching economy.

Matthew 23:4

4For they bind heavy burdens that are grievous to be borne, and lay them on men’s shoulders; but they themselves will not lift a finger to help them.

Arminian

Heavy burdens on others, no finger lifted to help—legal load without mercy. This contrasts Jesus’ own easy yoke (11:28–30).

Arminian preaching hears grace’s heart: God does not crush with empty loads. True holiness is enabled and accompanied; hypocrisy demands what it will not bear.

Reformed

Burden-binding without help paints false shepherds. The Law’s weight is twisted into crushing tradition while the teachers stay untouched.

Christ alone lifts what hypocrites pile on. God’s righteousness is not this fingerless severity; the Good Shepherd carries His own.

Matthew 23:5

5But they do all their works to be seen by men. They make their phylacteries broad and enlarge the fringes of their garments,

Arminian

Motive is exposed: deeds “to be seen by men.” Broad phylacteries and long fringes turn devotion into display.

Wesleyan Arminians warn that grace searches the heart. Public piety can be true or theatrical; God invites a sincere turn from audience-religion to love of the Father who sees in secret.

Reformed

All deeds aimed at visibility. Enlarged signs of piety become props for human praise.

The heart’s idol is glory from people (cf. Matt 6). God unmasks it; true fear of the Lord reorders worship away from the marketplace gaze.

Matthew 23:6

6and love the place of honor at feasts, the best seats in the synagogues,

Arminian

Love of honor seats reveals disordered desire. Status at feasts and synagogues competes with love of God and neighbor.

Grace frees from craving the best seat. A choice remains—pursue recognition or pursue the kingdom’s lowly path.

Reformed

They love the places of honor. Preference for rank unmasks the heart’s treasure.

Providence will reverse seats. God records this love of status as evidence in the indictment Christ is building before the woes.

Matthew 23:7

7the salutations in the marketplaces, and to be called ‘Rabbi, Rabbi’ by men.

Arminian

Marketplace greetings and the title “rabbi” feed the same hunger for recognition. Teaching office becomes a brand.

Classical Arminians urge teachers to receive titles as service, not fuel. Grace can retrain ambition; the call is to refuse living for applause.

Reformed

Greetings and the rabbi-title complete the honor pattern. Public esteem becomes the currency of religion.

Christ names the love of being called teacher. The coming correction (vv. 8–10) will relocate authority in God and His Christ alone.

Matthew 23:8

8But you are not to be called ‘Rabbi’, for one is your teacher, the Christ, and all of you are brothers.

Arminian

Disciples must not chase the rabbi-title. One Teacher; all are brothers. Equality under Christ guards the community from status games.

Grace creates sibling fellowship. Shared learning under Jesus invites mutual humility rather than ranked gurus.

Reformed

Christ’s unique teaching office relativizes human titles among His people.

The church’s unity is grounded in the one Lord who teaches. Human instructors serve under Him; brotherhood is the given shape of the body.

Key contrast Both reject status-seeking under religious titles. Arminians highlight grace-formed brotherhood that freely refuses guru culture; Reformed readers stress Christ’s exclusive teaching lordship that defines and secures the church’s equality.

Matthew 23:9

9Call no man on the earth your father, for one is your Father, he who is in heaven.

Arminian

Earthly “father” as spiritual ultimate is forbidden. One Father in heaven claims exclusive reverence.

Wesley’s heirs hear a call to redirect dependence: honor parents and elders rightly, but do not transfer divine Fatherhood to any man. Grace invites living under the heavenly Father’s name.

Reformed

Jesus protects the Father’s unique glory from earthly rivals in spiritual allegiance.

God’s fatherhood is not shared as ultimate source. Human fathers and pastors are secondary; the church confesses one Father who begets, adopts, and rules.

Matthew 23:10

10Neither be called masters, for one is your master, the Christ.

Arminian

Nor chase the instructor-title. The Christ is the one Instructor. Disciples teach only as His students.

Ministry gifts remain, but ambition for the master-label must die. Grace enables teachers to point away from themselves to Jesus.

Reformed

The Christ is their one instructor. All human instruction is derivative.

Christ rules His church as Teacher. The Spirit applies His word; earthly instructors are stewards, never rivals to the Son’s chair.

Matthew 23:11

11But he who is greatest among you will be your servant.

Arminian

Greatness is redefined as service. The path up is the path down into serving the brothers.

Classical Arminians urge an embrace of servanthood—grace enables choosing the towel over the throne in daily relationships.

Reformed

Greatness means being a servant. Jesus restates the kingdom’s upside-down measure (cf. 20:26).

God assigns true greatness in the shape of the Servant King. Status in the body is measured by lowly usefulness under Christ’s rule.

Matthew 23:12

12Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted.

Arminian

Self-exaltation meets humbling; self-humbling meets exaltation. God opposes pride and lifts the lowly—a living warning and promise.

Classical Arminians press the moral urgency: refuse pride now. Grace invites humble repentance that God delights to honor; the proverb is not fatalism but a call.

Reformed

Divine reversal: the self-exalted are humbled; the humble are exalted. God Himself executes the exchange.

Providence and judgment secure this pattern. Christ’s people trust the Father to humble and to lift in His time—assurance that honor-seeking religion will not finally stand.

Key contrast Both take the reversal as certain under God. Arminians stress a sincere summons to humble oneself in response to grace; Reformed interpreters emphasize God’s sovereign humbling and exalting as the sure ordering of pride and lowliness.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic Arminian and Reformed comment on Matthew 23:1–12, in the writers’ own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Then - Leaving all converse with his adversaries, whom he now left to the hardness of their hearts. The scribes sit in the chair of Moses - That is, read and expound the law of Moses, and are their appointed teachers. All things therefore - Which they read out of the law, and enforce therefrom.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 23:1–12)

Reformed Notable voices

“This warning was highly useful, that, amidst contentions and the noise of combats, amidst the trouble and confusion of public affairs, amidst the destruction of proper and lawful order, the authority of the word of God might remain entire. The design of Christ was, that the people might not, in consequence of being offended at the vices of the scribes, throw away reverence for the Law.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 23:1–12)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
