Passage

Matthew 14

Verses 34–36 · ESV · Voices

After the storm stills and the disciples worship Jesus as the Son of God (14:22–33), they land at Gennesaret. Recognition spreads through the region: the sick are brought, the fringe of His garment is sought, and all who touch are made well—mercy continuing on shore after glory on the sea.

Matthew 14:34

34And when they had crossed over, they came to land at Gennesaret.

Arminian

The crossing ends at Gennesaret. Storm, fear, and confession give way to solid ground—and a new field for mercy. Jesus does not linger only in private glory with the Twelve; He arrives where people live.

Classical Arminians hear a gracious landing. The same Lord who called Peter on the water now places Himself within reach of a region. Hearers remain responsible to recognize and seek Him when He comes near.

Reformed

Matthew’s “crossed over… Gennesaret” closes the sea narrative under the same providence that ordered the boat. The Son who rules wind and wave now steps onto appointed shore.

Reformed comfort: geography serves the kingdom. Landing is not random afterthought; the Father’s purpose carries Christ from mountain prayer through storm to a shoreline where many sick will meet His power.

Matthew 14:35

35And when the men of that place recognized him, they sent around to all that region and brought to him all who were sick

Arminian

Recognition sparks mission: the men send around the region and bring all who were sick. Faith that recognizes Jesus becomes love that carries neighbors to Him. No one is left as a private secret.

Classical Arminians stress responsible, grace-enabled response. Seeing Christ rightly moves people to act—informing others and bringing the needy. Assisting grace enables both recognition and this urgent charity; refusing to bring the sick would be a real failure of love.

Reformed

They “recognized him,” then the region hears and the sick are gathered. Recognition is gift and summons: God opens eyes, and the shoreline becomes a hospital under the Messiah’s feet.

Reformed emphasis: Christ is known because God makes Him known, and the flock’s movement serves His purpose. Bringing “all who were sick” displays the wideness of the King’s compassion already at work in this place.

Key contrast Both praise recognition that becomes neighbor-love. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled responsibility to recognize Christ and actively bring the sick; Reformed readers typically stress that recognition and the regional gathering unfold under God’s effectual purpose as the Messiah draws the needy to Himself.

Matthew 14:36

36and implored him that they might only touch the fringe of his garment. And as many as touched it were made well.

Arminian

They ask little—“only touch the fringe of his garment”—and receive much: as many as touched were made well. Humble faith seeks contact with Jesus; healing answers that trust without requiring spectacle.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere invitation embodied in Christ’s nearness. The fringe is not magic; faith reaches for the Person. Grace enables the touch, and hearers remain called to come—wholeness is offered to those who truly seek Him.

Reformed

“Only touch” and “as many as touched… were made well” show both humility and certainty. Virtue is not in cloth as charm but in the Son who heals whom He will—and here He heals them all who touch.

Reformed gospel: the fringe points beyond itself to Christ’s person and power (cf. 9:20–22). Perfect healing is His gift. The chapter that began with a tetrarch’s haunted dread ends with a shoreline of effectual mercy under the Lord the disciples have confessed.

Key contrast Both deny superstition and confess real healing through contact with Jesus. Arminians typically emphasize humble, grace-enabled faith that truly reaches for Christ and receives; Reformed readers typically emphasize the Son’s sovereign power that effectually makes well all who touch, crowning the chapter’s revelation of who He is.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on after the storm stills and the disciples worship Jesus as the Son of God (14:22–33), they land at Gennesaret—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 14:34–36.

Arminian Notable voices

“The land of Gennesaret - It was from this country that the sea or lake of Gennesaret had its name. In this district, on the western side of the lake, were the cities of Capernaum and Tiberias. The men of that place had knowledge of him - i.e. They knew him again.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the New Testament (on Matt 14:34–36)

Reformed Notable voices

“They came into the country of Gennesareth. The Evangelists give that designation to the country which borrowed its name from the lake, though it is uncertain if it was not rather the name of the country that was bestowed on the lake; but that is a matter of little consequence.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 14:34–36)
