Passage

Matthew 14

Verses 22–33 · WEB · Voices

After the five thousand eat and twelve baskets remain (14:13–21), Jesus immediately sends the disciples ahead by boat, dismisses the crowds, and prays alone on the mountain. In the night storm He walks on the sea, steadies their fear—“It is I”—receives Peter’s venture of faith, rescues him when fear sinks him, stills the wind, and hears the boat’s worship: “You are truly the Son of God.”

Matthew 14:22

22Immediately Jesus made the disciples get into the boat and go ahead of him to the other side, while he sent the multitudes away.

Arminian

“Immediately” after the feast, Jesus “made” the disciples get into the boat and go ahead while He dismisses the crowds. Obedience is prompt and concrete: leave the miracle’s glow, enter the boat, cross without Him visible beside them.

Wesleyan Arminians hear an earnest summons under assisting grace. Disciples are responsible to go when He sends—even when the path looks lonely. Grace enables the step into the boat; delaying for comfort would still be disobedience.

Reformed

Matthew’s “immediately” and “made” show Christ’s lordship over His own. The feeding’s joy does not set the agenda; Jesus orders the Twelve into the boat and clears the shore Himself.

Reformed readers hear providence already shaping the next lesson. The boat trip is not random logistics but the theater the Son appoints—where storm, fear, and recognition of His deity will unfold under the Father’s purpose.

Matthew 14:23

23After he had sent the multitudes away, he went up into the mountain by himself to pray. When evening had come, he was there alone.

Arminian

Crowds dismissed, Jesus goes up the mountain by Himself to pray. Evening finds Him alone—communion with the Father before the night’s trial for the disciples. Prayer is not escape from mission; it is the Son’s chosen priority.

Grace invites the same pattern. After ministry, withdraw to pray; solitude with God strengthens obedience for what follows. The disciples’ boat journey will need a Lord who has first sought the Father’s face.

Reformed

Jesus’ mountain prayer after dismissing the crowds reveals the Son’s communion with the Father amid the mission. Alone at evening, He is not abandoned; He is engaged in holy fellowship that precedes the sea epiphany.

The Lord who feeds multitudes also prays. Providence does not replace prayer; the Son’s intercession and dependence mark the path God has set—toward a night when He will walk the waves He rules.

Matthew 14:24

24But the boat was now in the middle of the sea, distressed by the waves, for the wind was contrary.

Arminian

Distance, beating waves, and a contrary wind describe struggle for disciples who obeyed. Obedience did not cancel the storm; the boat is far from land and hard pressed.

Arminian interpreters refuse to spiritualize away hardship. Grace had sent them; grace will meet them—but the middle of the sea still summons faith under pressure. Responsibility includes clinging to Christ’s word when circumstances contradict comfort.

Reformed

Matthew paints providence’s severity: far from land, beaten by waves, wind against them. The Lord who ordered the crossing also times the adversity that will unveil His glory.

The storm is not outside God’s care. Disciples suffer as sent people, not forgotten ones. The contrary wind prepares the stage for the Son who walks on what threatens them.

Matthew 14:25

25In the fourth watch of the night, Jesus came to them, walking on the sea.

Arminian

In the fourth watch—late, weary hours—Jesus comes, walking on the sea. He does not wait for calm water; He approaches them in the crisis itself. Presence is grace going first toward fearful disciples.

Arminian reading rejoices that Christ seeks His own. The summons of His coming still calls for recognition and trust. Night and waves do not bar the Lord who answers prayer with nearness.

Reformed

Christ’s arrival in the fourth watch is timed sovereignty: He comes when the night is deepest, walking on the sea as Lord over creation.

The One who prayed on the mountain now strides the waters He made. Providence includes the hour of His appearing; the storm cannot hinder the Son’s approach to the boat He sent ahead.

Matthew 14:26

26When the disciples saw him walking on the sea, they were troubled, saying, “It’s a ghost!” and they cried out for fear.

Arminian

Seeing Him, they misread glory as a ghost and cry out in fear. True disciples can still panic when the unfamiliar form of Christ’s coming meets exhausted nerves.

Classical Arminians name fear honestly without excusing unbelief. Grace had given enough of Jesus’ character that they might have hoped—yet terror wins the moment. The cry of fear becomes the opening for His word of peace.

Reformed

Matthew records terrified misidentification: “Troubled, saying, It’s a ghost!” Even those who have just seen loaves multiplied can misconstrue the Lord approaching on the waves.

Weakness in disciples does not annul election’s comfort or the Lord’s purpose. Fear is not softened; it is also the dark backdrop against which Jesus’ self-revelation will shine.

Matthew 14:27

27But immediately Jesus spoke to them, saying, “Cheer up! It is I! Don’t be afraid.”

Arminian

Jesus answers fear with immediate speech: “Cheer up! It is I! Don’t be afraid.” His presence and word invite courage. “It is I” (ego eimi) reassures by identifying the One they know—not a phantom.

Grace meets panic with a sincere call to trust. Disciples remain responsible to receive comfort rather than cling to terror. The same voice that sent them now steadies them mid-storm.

Reformed

Christ’s “immediately” matches their cry with His word. “Cheer up! It is I” is revelation and command—identity that stills fear because the Speaker is Lord.

Jesus does not merely offer advice; He effectually addresses His own. The “I am” resonance points beyond a ghost story to the One who walks the sea by right, under the Father’s purpose to reveal Himself.

Matthew 14:28

28Peter answered him and said, “Lord, if it is you, command me to come to you on the waters.”

Arminian

Peter answers with both address and condition: “Lord, if it is you, command me to come to you on the waters.” He wants Jesus’ word before he steps—faith seeking a clear commission rather than presumption.

Arminian interpreters hear a grace-enabled request. Peter’s “if” is testing recognition, not cynical unbelief; he asks for a command he intends to obey. Responsibility includes asking Christ to authorize risky discipleship and then answering when He speaks.

Reformed

Peter’s reply hangs on Jesus’ identity and word: only if it is the Lord, and only if He commands. The disciple will not invent a miracle; he waits for the Son’s imperative.

Even bold Peter depends on Christ’s initiating word. Faith that walks on water must be summoned by the One who rules the sea. Providence will grant the command—and expose how frail that faith remains without continual looking to Jesus.

Key contrast Both honor Peter’s desire for Jesus’ authorizing word before he steps. Arminians stress a grace-enabled, responsible request that seeks an earnest command to obey; Reformed commentators stress that only Christ’s sovereign word can initiate such a walk—Peter’s venture hangs entirely on the Lord who commands.

Matthew 14:29

29He said, “Come!” Peter stepped down from the boat and walked on the waters to come to Jesus.

Arminian

One word—“Come”—and Peter leaves the boat, walks on the water, and comes toward Jesus. Faith acts on the command; the impossible becomes path under Christ’s permission and power.

Classical Arminians celebrate obedient risk. Assisting grace enables the step out; Peter remains responsible to keep coming. Walking toward Jesus is authentic discipleship, not a stunt—until fear interrupts what faith began.

Reformed

Jesus’ “Come” is effectual for the moment: Peter actually walks on the water toward Him. The Son’s word creates what it requires; the sea holds because Christ commands.

This walk is gift, not self-generated heroism. Peter comes only because Jesus spoke. The miracle magnifies the Speaker; the disciple’s footing is entirely borrowed from the Lord’s power.

Matthew 14:30

30But when he saw that the wind was strong, he was afraid, and beginning to sink, he cried out, saying, “Lord, save me!”

Arminian

Seeing the wind, Peter fears, begins to sink, and cries, “Lord, save me.” Faith that started toward Jesus falters when circumstances dominate the gaze. Yet the cry is right: he turns again to the only Savior.

True disciples can mix faith and fear. Grace had enabled the walk; Peter remains accountable for looking away—and still welcome to cry for rescue. Sinking does not have to be the last word when the Lord is near.

Reformed

Matthew traces the collapse: wind seen, fear risen, sinking begun, then the desperate plea. Little faith is still faith that knows whom to call—“Lord, save me.”

God often lets His own feel the wind’s strength so pride cannot claim the walk. The sink is discipleship struggle under providence; the cry proves that Christ, not self-confidence, is the refuge.

Key contrast Both confess free faith mixed with fear and a right cry for rescue. Arminians stress Peter’s responsible failure of gaze and grace-enabled turn back to Jesus; Calvinists stress frail faith exposed under providence—sinking humbles, and the saving cry still rests on the Lord’s mercy alone.

Matthew 14:31

31Immediately Jesus stretched out his hand, took hold of him, and said to him, “You of little faith, why did you doubt?”

Arminian

Jesus immediately reaches, takes hold, and speaks: “You of little faith, why did you doubt?” Rescue precedes the rebuke; the hand is faster than the sermon. Little faith is still faith—rebuked so it may grow, not crushed.

The emphasis is on both mercy and accountability. Christ saves the sinking disciple; the question “why did you doubt?” assumes Peter could and should have kept trusting the One who said “Come.” Grace restores and corrects together.

Reformed

Christ’s hand is immediate and effectual—He takes hold before explaining. “Little faith” names weakness without denying that Peter belongs to Him; the question exposes doubt’s folly in the presence of the Lord of the sea.

Comfort: salvation here is Jesus’ grip, not Peter’s recovery of nerve. The Son rescues His own and then catechizes them. Doubt is no illusion; it is also answered by sovereign mercy that holds what fear cannot hold.

Key contrast Both see immediate rescue plus a searching rebuke of little faith. Arminians typically emphasize a grace-restored disciple accountable for doubt after a serious command to trust; Reformed readers stress effectual saving grip—Christ holds Peter first, and little faith is upheld by sovereign mercy even while doubt is named.

Matthew 14:32

32When they got up into the boat, the wind ceased.

Arminian

Once they are in the boat, the wind ceases. Christ’s presence aboard ends what beat the boat when He seemed absent. Peace follows the Lord into the vessel His word had sent out.

Invite Jesus into the crisis you cannot master. Grace that walked the waves also stills them; disciples remain called to welcome His lordship rather than manage the storm alone.

Reformed

The wind’s cessation is timed to their entry with Jesus. Creation obeys its Lord; the storm has served its purpose and ends at His pleasure.

Providence wrote both the contrary wind and its silence. The same Son who walked the sea now sits in the boat, and nature’s unrest yields—so the disciples may move from panic to worship.

Matthew 14:33

33Those who were in the boat came and worshiped him, saying, “You are truly the Son of God!”

Arminian

Those in the boat worship Him and confess, “You are truly the Son of God.” Fear has become reverence; the storm’s end becomes a sanctuary. Right response to revealed glory is worship and clear confession.

Classical Arminians urge the same: grace has shown Jesus’ identity; disciples are responsible to yield in adoration. The confession is not bare orthodoxy—it is bowed hearts answering light they have received.

Reformed

Matthew climaxes with worship and the confession of divine sonship. The One who walked the sea, saved Peter, and stilled the wind is truly the Son of God—acknowledged by those He kept through the night.

This confession is gift after revelation. God has opened their mouths to say what the miracle displayed. Worship is the fitting end of providence’s lesson—the boat becomes a place where the Son is honored as He is.

Key contrast Both receive the boat’s worship and Son-of-God confession as the right climax. Arminians stress a responsible, grace-enabled response of adoration to revealed light; Reformed readers stress that such confession is granted after sovereign self-revelation—God opens mouths to honor the Son His providence has displayed.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on Jesus immediately sends the disciples ahead by boat, dismisses the crowds, and prays alone on the mountain, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“He constrained his disciples - Who were unwilling to leave him. In the evening - Learned men say the Jews reckoned two evenings; the first beginning at three in the afternoon, the second, at sunset. The fourth watch - The Jews (as well as the Romans) usually divided the night into four watches, of three hours each. The first watch began at six, the second at nine, the third at twelve, the fourth at three in the morning.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 14:22–33)

Reformed Notable voices

“And immediately Jesus constrained his disciples They must have been constrained; for they would never, of their own accord, have left him, and gone to the other side. Now in this they testify their great veneration for him, when, contrary to their own opinions, they yield to his command and obey it.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 14:22–33)
