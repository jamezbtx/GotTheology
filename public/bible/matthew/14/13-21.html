Passage

Matthew 14

Verses 13–21 · ESV · Voices

After John’s disciples bury him and tell Jesus (14:1–12), Jesus withdraws by boat to a desolate place—yet the crowds follow on foot. Evening brings the disciples’ scarcity report, five loaves and two fish placed in His hands, a blessing, broken bread, satisfied multitudes, and twelve baskets left over.

Matthew 14:13

13Now when Jesus heard this, he withdrew from there in a boat to a desolate place by himself. But when the crowds heard it, they followed him on foot from the towns.

Arminian

Hearing of John’s death, Jesus seeks solitude by boat in a desolate place. Grief and withdrawal are real; the Messiah is not untouched by the forerunner’s blood. Yet when the crowds hear, they follow on foot from the towns—hunger for Him outrunning His boat.

Classical Arminians hear both Jesus’ human need for space and the crowd’s responsible pursuit. Grace still draws seekers across the shore; following on foot is costly attention to the One John announced. Desolation does not cancel the summons to come.

Reformed

“When Jesus heard this” links the feeding to John’s martyrdom. Withdrawal is not retreat from the Father’s plan but a movement inside it—toward a wilderness theater where the Shepherd will feed His people.

Reformed readers notice providence in the chase: crowds hear and follow, not by accident, but under the Lord who gathers a flock even when His servant seeks solitude. The desolate place will become a table of the kingdom.

Matthew 14:14

14When he went ashore he saw a great crowd, and he had compassion on them and healed their sick.

Arminian

Ashore, Jesus sees a great crowd—and compassion moves Him to heal their sick before any bread is broken. Pity is not abstract; it acts. The needy are met with real mercy that invites trust.

Classical Arminians stress that Christ’s compassion sincerely addresses those before Him. Healing is grace going first; the crowd remains summoned to receive the Healer, not merely His gifts. Soft hearts answer compassion with faith; hardness walks away still accountable.

Reformed

Matthew names the spring of Jesus’ action: He “had compassion” and healed. Messianic pity is holy and effectual—the sick are actually made well under His care.

Reformed comfort: the same Lord who withdrew in grief now spends Himself on the crowd by sovereign mercy. Compassion here is not a mere feeling debated in the abstract; it is the King’s heart expressed in power, advancing the Father’s purpose for this multitude.

Key contrast Both confess real, costly compassion that heals. Arminians typically stress compassion as grace that sincerely meets and summons the crowd to responsive faith; Reformed readers typically stress the Messiah’s sovereign pity that effectually heals, unfolding inside the Father’s purpose for this hour.

Matthew 14:15

15Now when it was evening, the disciples came to him and said, “This is a desolate place, and the day is now over; send the crowds away to go into the villages and buy food for themselves.”

Arminian

Evening exposes limits the disciples can see: desolate place, day over, hungry crowds. Their counsel is practical—“send the crowds away… buy food for themselves.” Care for logistics is not cruelty; it is incomplete faith.

Arminian pastoral reading honors honest assessment without baptizing unbelief. Grace invites disciples to bring scarcity to Jesus rather than dismiss the needy. Responsibility includes refusing the easy exit when the Lord is still present.

Reformed

The disciples speak facts Jesus already knows: wilderness, evening, empty hands. Their solution is dispersal and purchase—human calculation managing what looks impossible.

Reformed diagnosis: true need is named, yet the counsel still underestimates the One who stands among them. Providence has timed scarcity to evening so that Christ’s provision, not village markets, will feed the people.

Matthew 14:16

16But Jesus said, “They need not go away; you give them something to eat.”

Arminian

Jesus refuses dismissal: “They need not go away; you give them something to eat.” The command puts the disciples inside the mercy, not on the sidelines. Scarcity becomes a call to obedient participation.

Classical Arminians hear a real commission under assisting grace. The Lord who commands also enables; disciples are responsible to act in trust rather than outsource the hungry. “You give” is vocation, not a trick question.

Reformed

Christ’s word reverses the disciples’ plan. The crowds stay; the Twelve must feed them—yet the command will prove that He alone supplies what He requires through their hands.

Reformed emphasis: Jesus’ imperative is creative, not bare demand. He binds the disciples to the miracle so they learn dependence. What He orders, He will furnish; their “you give” rests on His power, not their pantry.

Key contrast Both hear a real word that keeps the crowds and involves the disciples. Arminians typically stress a grace-enabled commission the disciples should obey in faith; Reformed readers typically stress that Christ’s command creates the path—He will effectually supply through them what He requires of them.

Matthew 14:17

17They said to him, “We have only five loaves here and two fish.”

Arminian

The inventory is honest and small: five loaves, two fish. Confession of “only” is the right starting place—provided it leads to Christ, not to despair.

Arminian pastoral counsel: grace does not ask us to pretend abundance we lack. Bring the little you have; refuse the lie that small means useless. Responsibility includes naming need and still offering what is in hand.

Reformed

“Only” is the disciples’ theology of the moment—arithmetic without the Messiah. Matthew records the meager count so the coming surplus will glorify Jesus alone.

Reformed reading: God often reduces His people to “only” so that pride cannot claim the feast. Scarcity is real; it is also the stage providence sets for the Son’s multiplying word.

Matthew 14:18

18And he said, “Bring them here to me.”

Arminian

Jesus’ answer is simple: “Bring them here to me.” The little must pass into His hands before it feeds anyone. Offering precedes multiplying.

Classical Arminians rejoice in this invitation. Grace enables the surrender of what we clutch; discipleship means entrusting loaves and fish—and livelihoods—to the Lord who asks for them. Nothing is too small to place before Him.

Reformed

All resources must come to Christ first. “Bring them here to me” recenters the crisis on His person; the disciples’ inventory becomes His to bless.

Reformed comfort: the Son claims what His people hold. Provision flows from His lordship. The path from scarcity to surplus runs through hands that yield everything to Jesus.

Matthew 14:19

19Then he ordered the crowds to sit down on the grass, and taking the five loaves and the two fish, he looked up to heaven and said a blessing. Then he broke the loaves and gave them to the disciples, and the disciples gave them to the crowds.

Arminian

Order replaces scramble: crowds sit on the grass. Jesus takes, looks up, blesses, breaks, and gives to the disciples, who give to the crowds. The pattern quietly echoes the later supper language—gift received from heaven and shared.

Arminian application: the church learns to receive from Christ and pass along what He gives. Grace makes both receiving and distributing possible; disciples remain responsible stewards, not independent bakers. Looking up keeps the feast centered on the Father.

Reformed

Jesus hosts the meal: He orders the seating, takes the food, blesses heavenward, breaks, and distributes through the Twelve. Take–bless–break–give foreshadows the Lord’s table without collapsing the scenes into one.

Reformed emphasis: Christ is the giver; the disciples are channels. The Father is acknowledged; the Son multiplies; the church learns that every true feast begins with His blessing and His broken loaf, under providence that feeds a wilderness people.

Matthew 14:20

20And they all ate and were satisfied. And they took up twelve baskets full of the broken pieces left over.

Arminian

“All ate and were satisfied”—not sampled, but filled. Twelve baskets of broken pieces remain. Little offered in faith becomes more than enough in Christ’s hands.

Classical Arminians celebrate abundance that answers real need without wasteful pride. Grace invites trust that God multiplies what disciples bring; leftovers teach generosity and careful gathering, not anxiety that mercy will run dry.

Reformed

Satisfaction is total, and surplus is counted: twelve baskets—suggestive of Israel’s fullness under the Messiah’s care. The miracle’s math glorifies Jesus, not the original five-and-two.

Reformed doxology: providence and power meet in leftover baskets. God wastes nothing of His Son’s gift; the same Lord who fed manna in the wilderness now feeds this crowd, proving that little is much when He appoints the feast.

Key contrast Both confess that Christ turns little into satisfying abundance with leftovers. Arminians typically stress faith that brings the small offering and receives multiplied mercy; Reformed readers typically stress sovereign providence—Jesus effectually creates surplus so the glory rests on Him alone.

Matthew 14:21

21And those who ate were about five thousand men, besides women and children.

Arminian

Matthew closes with scale: about five thousand men, besides women and children. The census of mercy is larger than the headline number. No one who stayed at Jesus’ table went uncounted in the story’s compassion.

Arminian pastoral joy: the gospel’s provision is wide enough for households, not only for a male tally. Grace still summons crowds to sit and receive; the feeding displays a Lord who cares for whole families under the open sky.

Reformed

The final count seals the wonder. Thousands upon thousands ate because the Son willed to feed them. Women and children are named so the church remembers the breadth of His care.

Reformed comfort after John’s death: Herod’s banquet ended in a platter of blood; Jesus’ wilderness meal ends in satisfied multitudes. The kingdom’s King provides under the Father’s purpose—abundance where the world offered only violence.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus withdraws by boat to a desolate place—yet the crowds follow on foot—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 14:13–21.

Arminian Notable voices

“Jesus withdrew into a desert place - To avoid Herod: Because of the multitude pressing upon him, Mark vi, xxxii, and To talk with his disciples, newly returned from their progress, Luke ix, x, apart - From all but his disciples.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 14:13–21)

Reformed Notable voices

“John, who relates the same narrative, does not mention the reason why Jesus crossed over to the opposite bank, (6: 5.) Mark and Luke differ somewhat from Matthew; for they describe the occasion of the journey to have been to give some repose to his disciples, after that they had returned from their embassy. But there is no contradiction here; for it is possible that he intended to withdraw his disciples into a desert place, in order that he might be more at leisure to train them for higher labors, and that, about the same time, an additional reason arose out of the death of John.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 14:13–21)
