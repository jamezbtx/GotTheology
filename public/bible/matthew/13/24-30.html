Passage

Matthew 13

Verses 24–30 · ESV

Jesus sets another kingdom parable before the crowds: good seed sown in a field, an enemy’s weeds mixed among the wheat, and a master’s patience until harvest. Premature uprooting is refused; final separation is reserved for the reapers.

Matthew 13:24

24He put another parable before them, saying, “The kingdom of heaven may be compared to a man who sowed good seed in his field,

Arminian

Another kingdom picture: a man sows good seed in his field. The kingdom’s advance begins with genuine good—seed meant for life, field under the sower’s care.

Classical Arminians hear sincere goodness in God’s planting. The parable will introduce evil’s intrusion without blaming the sower’s seed; grace that plants also invites trust in the Master’s eventual justice.

Reformed

Christ frames the kingdom by a sower’s good seed. The field belongs to the man who plants; goodness is primary, not afterthought.

Reformed framing: the Son of Man’s kingdom work starts with His own planting. What follows—weeds, patience, harvest—unfolds under His ownership and purpose.

Matthew 13:25

25but while his men were sleeping, his enemy came and sowed weeds among the wheat and went away.

Arminian

While workers sleep, an enemy slips in weeds among the wheat and leaves. Evil is parasitic and opportunistic—mixed into what was planted good.

Arminian pastoral realism: the church lives in a contested field. Grace does not deny the enemy’s malice; hearers are called to wakefulness without panic, trusting the Master more than their own night watch.

Reformed

Enemy action explains the mixture: weeds appear because an adversary sowed them, not because the master’s seed failed.

Reformed sobriety: providence allows the field’s complexity for a time. Satan’s counterfeit sowing is real; God’s ownership of the field is not cancelled by nocturnal sabotage.

Matthew 13:26

26So when the plants came up and bore grain, then the weeds appeared also.

Arminian

Growth reveals mixture: grain appears, and weeds show themselves too. Early stages can look alike; fruiting time exposes difference.

Classical Arminians urge patient discernment. Grace grows true wheat; imitation also springs up. The church must not confuse every green shoot with harvest-ready righteousness.

Reformed

Appearance follows growth. Weeds become visible as the crop matures—timing that frustrates hasty judgment.

Reformed note: God orders the disclosure of difference. The kingdom’s present form includes mixture until the appointed revealing.

Matthew 13:27

27And the servants of the master of the house came and said to him, ‘Master, did you not sow good seed in your field? How then does it have weeds?’

Arminian

Servants are puzzled: good seed was sown—whence the weeds? Honest ministry asks hard questions when evil appears beside the good.

Arminian tone: bring confusion to the Master, not to despair. Grace allows lament and inquiry; the field’s problem does not mean the sower lied about His seed.

Reformed

The servants’ question assumes the master’s goodness and seeks explanation. Theology begins with who sowed, not with panic about weeds.

Reformed comfort: the Master’s character stands. Questions about evil in the field are answered by His word, not by rewriting the goodness of His planting.

Matthew 13:28

28He said to them, ‘An enemy has done this.’ So the servants said to him, ‘Then do you want us to go and gather them?’

Arminian

The master names the cause: an enemy. Servants immediately offer zealous weeding—gather them now.

Classical Arminians recognize holy impatience against evil, yet the parable will restrain premature purge. Grace that hates weeds also teaches patience toward a mixed field until God’s timing.

Reformed

Diagnosis first: enemy work. The servants’ zeal to gather is natural—and about to be corrected.

Reformed wisdom: identifying Satan’s work does not authorize every human solution. The Master retains the strategy for His field.

Matthew 13:29

29But he said, ‘No, lest in gathering the weeds you root up the wheat along with them.

Arminian

The master refuses: premature gathering risks uprooting wheat with weeds. Discernment is imperfect; zeal can harm the true crop.

Arminian pastoral caution: grace calls for careful love in church discipline and cultural conflict. Separate evil without crushing weak believers; leave final sorting to the Lord of the harvest.

Reformed

“No” protects the wheat. Human reapers now would damage what God intends to keep.

Reformed gravity: the church’s present authority is real yet limited. Final, perfect separation belongs to the end; interim patience is obedience, not indifference.

Key contrast Both affirm patience that refuses reckless purge. Arminians typically stress responsible, grace-shaped care not to harm true believers while opposing evil; Reformed readers typically stress God’s sovereign timing for final separation and the church’s limited mandate before harvest.

Matthew 13:30

30Let both grow together until the harvest, and at harvest time I will tell the reapers, “Gather the weeds first and bind them in bundles to be burned, but gather the wheat into my barn.”’”

Arminian

Both grow until harvest; then reapers gather weeds for burning and wheat for the barn. Delay is not denial—judgment and gathering are certain.

Classical Arminians hold urgency and patience together: grace still invites all to become wheat by faith; the coming fire warns that refusal is not forever tolerated. Present mixture does not cancel future accountability.

Reformed

The Master sets the timetable and commands the reapers. Burning and barn are His appointments at harvest.

Reformed assurance: history moves toward ordered separation under Christ’s word. Wheat is gathered because it is His; weeds face judgment because evil’s end is sure—not because servants forced an early cleanup.

Key contrast Both confess a real future judgment that separates weeds and wheat. Arminians typically emphasize sincere present invitation alongside certain judgment; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s decree of final separation and the security of the wheat He will gather into His barn.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
