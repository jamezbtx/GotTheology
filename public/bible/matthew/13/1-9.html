Passage

Matthew 13

Verses 1–9 · ESV · Voices

On the same day as the conflicts of chapter 12, Jesus leaves the house, sits beside the sea, and teaches great crowds from a boat. Matthew opens a concentrated block of kingdom teaching in parables—beginning with the Sower—where hearing the word becomes the hinge of discipleship.

Matthew 13:1

1That same day Jesus went out of the house and sat beside the sea.

Arminian

Matthew ties the parable discourse to the very day of rising opposition. Jesus does not retreat from ministry; He moves to the shoreline where more people can gather and hear.

Classical Arminians notice grace that keeps offering the word in public. The Teacher places Himself where crowds may come—revelation still going out, inviting hearers to receive rather than withdraw into private circles alone.

Reformed

“That same day” situates the parables under providence after controversy. Jesus leaves the house and takes a seat beside the sea—still under the Father’s purpose, still the authorized herald of the kingdom.

Reformed readers hear sovereignty in the movement: the same Lord who faced rejection now shapes how the crowds will hear. The setting is not accidental; it prepares the Word’s public sowing.

Matthew 13:2

2And great crowds gathered about him, so that he got into a boat and sat down. And the whole crowd stood on the beach.

Arminian

Crowds press in so densely that Jesus teaches from a boat while they stand on the beach. The scene is open and accessible—many can hear the same word at once.

Arminian pastoral reading stresses genuine invitation to a mixed multitude. Grace draws near through preached truth; hearers remain responsible for how they receive what is offered to all who stand within earshot.

Reformed

The throng forces a practical arrangement: Jesus sits in the boat; the whole crowd stands on the shore. Providence orders even the acoustics of ministry so that many hear the kingdom word together.

Reformed comfort: Christ is not overwhelmed by numbers. He remains Master of the moment, seating Himself to sow the word as the appointed means by which God addresses the gathered people.

Matthew 13:3

3And he told them many things in parables, saying: “A sower went out to sow.

Arminian

Jesus begins a string of parables with a simple picture: a sower goes out to sow. The kingdom word is cast abroad—seed meant to land, take root, and live.

Classical Arminians hear prevenient and assisting grace in the going-out of the sower. The word is sincerely offered; the parable will press hearers to respond without denying that grace enables that response.

Reformed

Parabolic teaching is Jesus’ chosen mode here. He opens with the sower’s action—seed going out—before any soil is named, so the primacy of the word stands first.

Reformed emphasis: the Word is God’s appointed means. The sower’s going out is not random chatter; it is the kingdom’s advance under Christ’s authority, preparing the question of how hearts receive what God sends.

Matthew 13:4

4And as he sowed, some seeds fell along the path, and the birds came and devoured them.

Arminian

Some seed falls on the hard path and is promptly taken. The word can be heard outwardly and yet never enter—lost before it roots.

Arminian accent: this is a sober warning about responsible hearing. Assisting grace enables soft reception; hardness and neglect remain real dangers for those who refuse to take the word in.

Reformed

Path-soil pictures the word snatched before it can live. Birds devour what never penetrates—an image later tied to the evil one’s work against the uncomprehending heart.

Reformed preaching holds both the enemy’s hostility and God’s sovereignty: the same seed is sown, yet some never receive it. The Word remains the means; fruitless hearing exposes a heart not opened to life.

Key contrast Both treat path-soil as tragic non-reception of the same sown word. Arminians typically stress prevenient grace that enables a genuine response and human responsibility for hardness; Reformed readers typically stress God’s sovereign giving of understanding and the Word as means that does not return empty of purpose even when some refuse.

Matthew 13:5

5Other seeds fell on rocky ground, where they did not have much soil, and immediately they sprang up, since they had no depth of soil,

Arminian

Rocky ground yields a quick green shoot—immediate springing up without depth. Initial enthusiasm is not the same as rooted faith.

Classical Arminians hear a pastoral caution: grace invites lasting reception, not merely a burst of interest. Hearers are called to welcome the word into the deep places of life, assisted by grace that can sustain more than a moment’s joy.

Reformed

Shallow soil produces speed without substance. The plant appears at once because there is little earth to slow or steady it—an image of quick profession without root.

Reformed vigilance: visible response is not identical to saving endurance. Providence and the Word test what only looks alive; depth belongs to those in whom God plants lasting life.

Matthew 13:6

6but when the sun rose they were scorched. And since they had no root, they withered away.

Arminian

Sun and scorching expose the rootless plant; what sprang up quickly withers. Trouble reveals whether the word was merely tasted or truly taken in.

Arminian pastoral reading refuses despair and presumption alike. Grace enables perseverance; hearers remain responsible not to abandon the word when heat comes. The warning is real because a lasting response is truly offered and truly needed.

Reformed

Heat does not create the failure; it discloses no root. Withering follows when profession meets pressure and finds nothing beneath.

Reformed comfort and gravity together: God knows those He has rooted by the Word. Temporary growth is not effectual calling unto endurance; true fruit abides because the Lord establishes the heart.

Key contrast Both see scorching as the test that unmasks shallow reception. Arminians typically emphasize assisting grace and a real call to persevere under trial; Reformed readers typically emphasize that lasting root is God’s gift in effectual calling, so withering exposes a work that never went deep by His saving power.

Matthew 13:7

7Other seeds fell among thorns, and the thorns grew up and choked them.

Arminian

Thorns grow up with the seed and choke it. Competing loves—cares, riches, and the world’s pull—can smother a hearing that began.

Classical Arminians stress responsible clearing of the heart’s garden. Prevenient grace enables repentance from divided loyalties; the parable urgently invites hearers not to let rival growth strangle the word.

Reformed

Thorny ground is not empty ground—life is present, yet crowded out. The word is choked when other growth claims the soil’s strength.

Reformed diagnosis: providence allows the exposure of divided hearts. The Word as means still lands; fruit fails where competing desires rule. God alone makes a heart in which the word is not finally smothered.

Matthew 13:8

8Other seeds fell on good soil and produced grain, some a hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty.

Arminian

Good soil receives the seed and yields grain—abundant, yet varied in measure. Fruitfulness is the mark of true hearing that takes the word in and lets it live.

Arminian joy: grace enables understanding and lasting response, and the harvest is real. The same sower’s word, sincerely offered, bears life where it is welcomed; differing yields still honor one gracious seed.

Reformed

Good soil is defined by produce: grain in hundred-, sixty-, and thirtyfold measures. Variety of yield does not cancel unity of gift—the seed is the same kingdom word.

Reformed doxology: fruitful hearing is God’s work through the Word. The harvest displays effectual reception under sovereignty; differing measures remind the church that fruit is grace, not identical outward success.

Key contrast Both celebrate good-soil fruit as true hearing of the sown word. Arminians typically credit prevenient/assisting grace that enables a free, responsible reception resulting in harvest; Reformed readers typically credit God’s sovereign making of good soil and effectual blessing of the Word so that fruit certainly comes where He gives life.

Matthew 13:9

9He who has ears, let him hear.”

Arminian

Jesus closes with a searching summons: ears are for hearing—really hearing. The parable is not entertainment; it is a call to receive the word with understanding and endurance.

Classical Arminians hear a genuine invitation addressed to hearers who may respond. Prevenient grace grants the capacity to hear; the command “let him hear” presses responsible obedience without emptying grace of its power.

Reformed

The charge assumes both capacity language (“who has ears”) and urgent duty (“let him hear”). Christ demands the hearing the parable describes—fruitful, rooted, unchoked.

Reformed confession: spiritual hearing is God’s gift, and the Word is His means. Those who truly hear do so because the Lord opens ears; the summons still stands as the church’s call under His sovereign mercies.

Key contrast Both treat the closing line as a real summons to fruitful hearing. Arminians typically hear a sincere, grace-enabled call that hearers may receive or refuse; Reformed readers typically hear a command that God effectually fulfills in those He gives ears to hear by the Word.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on on the same day as the conflicts of chapter 12, Jesus leaves the house, sits beside the sea—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 13:1–9.

Arminian Notable voices

“He went into the vessel - Which constantly waited upon him, while he was on the sea coast. In parables - The word is here taken in its proper sense, for apt similes or comparisons. This way of speaking, extremely common in the eastern countries, drew and fixed the attention of many, and occasioned the truths delivered to sink the deeper into humble and serious hearers.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 13:1–9)

Reformed Notable voices

“And great multitudes were gathered together to him. It is not without good reason that the Evangelists begin with informing us that, a vast multitude had assembled, and that when Christ beheld them, he was led to compare his doctrine to seed That multitude had been collected from various places: all were held in suspense; all were alike eager to hear, but not equally desirous to receive instruction.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 13:1–9)
