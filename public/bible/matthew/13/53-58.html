Passage

Matthew 13

Verses 53–58 · WEB · Voices

After the parable discourse, Jesus returns to His hometown synagogue. Familiarity breeds offense: neighbors name His family, stumble over His wisdom and works, and unbelief limits the mighty works done there.

Matthew 13:53

53When Jesus had finished these parables, he departed from there.

Arminian

Matthew closes the discourse: finished parables, departure. Teaching moves on into costly hometown mission.

Hearing parables is not the end; responding where you live is next. Grace that taught by the sea now walks toward Nazareth’s test.

Reformed

A Matthean formula marks completion. Jesus proceeds under the Father’s next appointment.

The Word’s schedule continues. Parables give way to a providence that will expose hometown unbelief.

Matthew 13:54

54Coming into his own country, he taught them in their synagogue, so that they were astonished and said, “Where did this man get this wisdom and these mighty works?

Arminian

Hometown synagogue, astonishment, and a “where from?” about wisdom and works. Wonder without trust is already forming.

Amazement can coexist with resistance. Grace sets wisdom and works before them; they must receive the Person, not only the spectacle.

Reformed

Jesus teaches in “their” synagogue and stuns them. The question of origin already hints at refusal.

Revelation can astonish and still be rejected. Providence places maximal light in familiar streets.

Matthew 13:55

55Isn’t this the carpenter’s son? Isn’t his mother called Mary, and his brothers James, Joses, Simon, and Judas?

Arminian

They reduce Jesus to family inventory: carpenter’s son, Mary, named brothers. Familiarity becomes a weapon against faith.

Do not let ordinary ties blind you to glory. Grace may come through the near-at-hand; refusal is still refusal.

Reformed

Pedigree talk replaces confession. They know the household and miss the Son.

Natural knowledge of Jesus’ family cannot produce saving knowledge of His person. Flesh and blood still fail.

Matthew 13:56

56Aren’t all of his sisters with us? Where then did this man get all of these things?”

Arminian

Sisters “with us” intensify the hometown claim, then the baffled “where then?” They cannot fit Jesus into their small map.

Limited categories reject unlimited Christ. Ask for light rather than force Him into what you already think you know.

Reformed

The question of source becomes accusation by implication. Local presence of family is treated as disproof of divine mission.

Unbelief invents geography arguments against revelation. God is not barred by Nazareth’s familiarity.

Matthew 13:57

57They were offended by him. But Jesus said to them, “A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country and in his own house.”

Arminian

Offense replaces astonishment. Jesus names the proverb of the dishonored hometown prophet.

The verse presses a tragic, culpable stumble: grace stood among them, and they took offense. The door of honor was refused.

Reformed

Scandal is the verdict of the town. Jesus interprets it with a prophet’s proverb under divine mission.

Rejection in Nazareth does not undo His honor before the Father. Providence includes even hometown contempt.

Key contrast Both treat the offense as guilt, not a scheduling accident. Arminians typically stress resistible refusal of near grace; Calvinists stress God’s purpose advancing through rejection and the moral blindness of unbelief.

Matthew 13:58

58He didn’t do many mighty works there because of their unbelief.

Arminian

Few mighty works “because of their unbelief.” Unbelief constrains what Jesus will do among them—not from weakness, but from holy refusal to indulge a scoffing town.

Faith matters for receiving. Grace is not magic for the hard-hearted; unbelief can forfeit blessings God was free to give.

Reformed

Matthew states the cause: their unbelief. Jesus’ restraint is purposeful, not impotent.

God is not obligated to multiply signs for a hostile people. Unbelief is both judgment and explanation under His sovereign hand.

Key contrast Both link limited mighty works to unbelief. Arminians stress that resisting grace forfeits blessings sincerely available; Reformed readers typically highlight God’s sovereign withholding of signs from a people judged in unbelief.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic Arminian and Reformed comment on Matthew 13:53–58, in the writers’ own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“He departed thence - He crossed the lake from Capernaum: and came once more into his own country - Nazareth: but with no better success than he had had there before. Whence hath HE - Many texts are not understood, for want of knowing the proper emphasis; and others are utterly misunderstood, by placing the emphasis wrong.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 13:53–58)

Reformed Notable voices

“Matthew does not mean, that immediately after delivering these discourses, he came into his own country; for it is evident from Mark, that some interval of time elapsed. But the meaning is, that after having taught for some time in Judea, he returned again to the Galileans, but did not receive from them kind treatment.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 13:53–58)
