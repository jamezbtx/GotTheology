Passage

Matthew 13

Verses 53–58 · ESV

After the parable discourse, Jesus returns to His hometown synagogue. Familiarity breeds offense: neighbors name His family, stumble over His wisdom and works, and unbelief limits the mighty works done there.

Matthew 13:53

53And when Jesus had finished these parables, he went away from there,

Arminian

Matthew closes the discourse: finished parables, departure. Teaching moves on into costly hometown mission.

Arminian realism: hearing parables is not the end; responding where you live is next. Grace that taught by the sea now walks toward Nazareth’s test.

Reformed

A Matthean formula marks completion. Jesus proceeds under the Father’s next appointment.

Reformed framing: the Word’s schedule continues. Parables give way to a providence that will expose hometown unbelief.

Matthew 13:54

54and coming to his hometown he taught them in their synagogue, so that they were astonished, and said, “Where did this man get this wisdom and these mighty works?

Arminian

Hometown synagogue, astonishment, and a “where from?” about wisdom and works. Wonder without trust is already forming.

Arminian caution: amazement can coexist with resistance. Grace sets wisdom and works before them; they must receive the Person, not only the spectacle.

Reformed

Jesus teaches in “their” synagogue and stuns them. The question of origin already hints at refusal.

Reformed note: revelation can astonish and still be rejected. Providence places maximal light in familiar streets.

Matthew 13:55

55Is not this the carpenter's son? Is not his mother called Mary? And are not his brothers James and Joseph and Simon and Judas?

Arminian

They reduce Jesus to family inventory: carpenter’s son, Mary, named brothers. Familiarity becomes a weapon against faith.

Arminian warning: do not let ordinary ties blind you to glory. Grace may come through the near-at-hand; refusal is still refusal.

Reformed

Pedigree talk replaces confession. They know the household and miss the Son.

Reformed sobriety: natural knowledge of Jesus’ family cannot produce saving knowledge of His person. Flesh and blood still fail.

Matthew 13:56

56And are not all his sisters with us? Where then did this man get all these things?”

Arminian

Sisters “with us” intensify the hometown claim, then the baffled “where then?” They cannot fit Jesus into their small map.

Arminian pastoral point: limited categories reject unlimited Christ. Ask for light rather than force Him into what you already think you know.

Reformed

The question of source becomes accusation by implication. Local presence of family is treated as disproof of divine mission.

Reformed diagnosis: unbelief invents geography arguments against revelation. God is not barred by Nazareth’s familiarity.

Matthew 13:57

57And they took offense at him. But Jesus said to them, “A prophet is not without honor except in his hometown and in his own household.”

Arminian

Offense replaces astonishment. Jesus names the proverb of the dishonored hometown prophet.

Classical Arminians hear a tragic, culpable stumble: grace stood among them, and they took offense. The door of honor was refused.

Reformed

Scandal is the verdict of the town. Jesus interprets it with a prophet’s proverb under divine mission.

Reformed comfort: rejection in Nazareth does not cancel His honor before the Father. Providence includes even hometown contempt.

Key contrast Both treat the offense as real guilt, not a scheduling accident. Arminians typically stress resistible refusal of near grace; Reformed readers typically stress God’s purpose advancing through rejection and the moral blindness of unbelief.

Matthew 13:58

58And he did not do many mighty works there, because of their unbelief.

Arminian

Few mighty works “because of their unbelief.” Unbelief constrains what Jesus will do among them—not from weakness, but from holy refusal to indulge a scoffing town.

Arminian gravity: faith matters for receiving. Grace is not magic for the hard-hearted; unbelief can forfeit blessings God was free to give.

Reformed

Matthew states the cause: their unbelief. Jesus’ restraint is purposeful, not impotent.

Reformed confession: God is not obligated to multiply signs for a hostile people. Unbelief is both judgment and explanation under His sovereign hand.

Key contrast Both link limited mighty works to unbelief. Arminians typically emphasize that resisting grace forfeits blessings sincerely available; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s sovereign withholding of signs from a people judged in unbelief.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
