Passage

Matthew 13

Verses 31–35 · ESV

Jesus adds twin kingdom images—mustard seed and leaven—then Matthew explains His parable method as fulfillment of prophetic speech that utters what has been hidden.

Matthew 13:31

31He put another parable before them, saying, “The kingdom of heaven is like a grain of mustard seed that a man took and sowed in his field.

Arminian

The kingdom is likened to a mustard seed a man took and sowed. Small beginnings are not failures; they are the kingdom’s chosen scale.

Arminian encouragement: do not despise tiny starts. Grace often works through what looks insignificant so that hearers may trust the Sower rather than spectacle.

Reformed

Christ chooses a seed image again: taken and sown in a field. The kingdom’s advance is under the man’s intentional act.

Reformed hope: God authors small starts that belong to His purpose. The field is not random; the sowing is appointed.

Matthew 13:32

32It is the smallest of all seeds, but when it has grown it is larger than all the garden plants and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and make nests in its branches.”

Arminian

Smallest seed, surprising growth, shelter for birds. The kingdom outgrows its first appearance and becomes refuge.

Classical Arminians hear an invitation to patient faith: receive the word now, even when results look slight. Grace grows what we cannot inflate by force.

Reformed

Disproportion is the point: least seed, great plant, nesting branches. God delights to magnify His kingdom beyond human scale.

Reformed confidence: growth is God’s doing. The tree’s size and hospitality display His purpose, not human branding.

Key contrast Both celebrate surprising kingdom growth from small beginnings. Arminians typically stress patient, grace-enabled trust in the word sown; Reformed readers typically stress God’s sovereign power to enlarge what He plants.

Matthew 13:33

33He told them another parable. “The kingdom of heaven is like leaven that a woman took and hid in three measures of flour, till it was all leavened.”

Arminian

Leaven hidden in flour works until all is leavened. The kingdom’s influence can be quiet, pervasive, and thorough.

Arminian pastoral reading: grace transforms from within as the word is received. Hidden does not mean inactive; it means steadily changing the whole.

Reformed

A woman hides leaven in a large batch until everything is changed. The kingdom’s work is often unseen before it is total.

Reformed emphasis: God effects thorough change by His appointed means. The “till” points to a sure outcome under His hand.

Matthew 13:34

34All these things Jesus said to the crowds in parables; indeed, he said nothing to them without a parable.

Arminian

Matthew summarizes: Jesus spoke these things to the crowds in parables—indeed, nothing without a parable. The method is consistent, not occasional.

Arminian note: revelation is still being offered publicly. Parables keep inviting hearers to lean in, ask, and receive understanding by grace.

Reformed

The evangelist stresses Jesus’ settled parabolic address to the crowds. Form matches mission.

Reformed framing: Christ’s speech is purposeful under Scripture’s plan. He is not experimenting; He is fulfilling a pattern.

Matthew 13:35

35This was to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet: “I will open my mouth in parables; I will utter what has been hidden since the foundation of the world.”

Arminian

Parables fulfill prophetic speech: opening the mouth to utter what was hidden. Jesus discloses mysteries in a form that both reveals and searches hearts.

Arminian wonder: what was hidden is now uttered. Grace brings light into history; hearers are called to receive what God has made known in the Son.

Reformed

Matthew cites fulfillment: Jesus’ parables enact the prophet’s line about uttering hidden things from the foundation.

Reformed doxology: revelation is historically timed by God. What was hidden is spoken now because the Son has come according to plan.

Key contrast Both treat parable-speech as fulfilled revelation, not mere storytelling. Arminians typically emphasize the uttered word as sincere light inviting reception; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s sovereign disclosure on His timetable through the Messiah.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
