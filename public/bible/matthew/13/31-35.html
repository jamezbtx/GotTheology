Passage

Matthew 13

Verses 31–35 · WEB · Voices

Jesus adds twin kingdom images—mustard seed and leaven—then Matthew explains His parable method as fulfillment of prophetic speech that utters what has been hidden.

Matthew 13:31

31He set another parable before them, saying, “The Kingdom of Heaven is like a grain of mustard seed which a man took, and sowed in his field,

Arminian

The kingdom is likened to a mustard seed a man took and sowed. Small beginnings are not failures; they are the kingdom’s chosen scale.

Do not despise tiny starts. Grace often works through what looks insignificant so that hearers may trust the Sower rather than spectacle.

Reformed

Christ chooses a seed image again: taken and sown in a field. The kingdom’s advance is under the man’s intentional act.

God authors small starts that belong to His purpose. The field is not random; the sowing is appointed.

Matthew 13:32

32which indeed is smaller than all seeds. But when it is grown, it is greater than the herbs and becomes a tree, so that the birds of the air come and lodge in its branches.”

Arminian

Smallest seed, surprising growth, shelter for birds. The kingdom outgrows its first appearance and becomes refuge.

Wesley’s heirs hear an invitation to patient faith: receive the word now, even when results look slight. Grace grows what we cannot inflate by force.

Reformed

Disproportion is the point: least seed, great plant, nesting branches. God delights to magnify His kingdom beyond human scale.

Growth is God’s doing. The tree’s size and hospitality display His purpose, not human branding.

Key contrast Both celebrate surprising kingdom growth from small beginnings. Arminians stress patient, grace-enabled trust in the word sown; Reformed commentators often emphasize God’s sovereign power to enlarge what He plants.

Matthew 13:33

33He spoke another parable to them. “The Kingdom of Heaven is like yeast which a woman took and hid in three measures of meal, until it was all leavened.”

Arminian

Leaven hidden in flour works until all is leavened. The kingdom’s influence can be quiet, pervasive, and thorough.

Grace transforms from within as the word is received. Hidden does not mean inactive; it means steadily changing the whole.

Reformed

A woman hides leaven in a large batch until everything is changed. The kingdom’s work is often unseen before it is total.

God effects thorough change by His appointed means. The “until” points to a sure outcome under His hand.

Matthew 13:34

34Jesus spoke all these things in parables to the multitudes; and without a parable, he didn’t speak to them,

Arminian

Matthew summarizes: Jesus spoke these things to the crowds in parables—indeed, nothing without a parable. The method is consistent, not occasional.

Revelation is still being offered publicly. Parables keep inviting hearers to lean in, ask, and receive understanding by grace.

Reformed

The evangelist stresses Jesus’ settled parabolic address to the crowds. Form matches mission.

Christ’s speech is purposeful under Scripture’s plan. He is not experimenting; He is fulfilling a pattern.

Matthew 13:35

35that it might be fulfilled which was spoken through the prophet, saying, “I will open my mouth in parables; I will utter things hidden from the foundation of the world.”

Arminian

Parables fulfill prophetic speech: opening the mouth to utter what was hidden. Jesus discloses mysteries in a form that both reveals and searches hearts.

What was hidden is now uttered. Grace brings light into history; hearers are called to receive what God has made known in the Son.

Reformed

Matthew cites fulfillment: Jesus’ parables enact the prophet’s line about uttering hidden things from the foundation.

Revelation is historically timed by God. What was hidden is spoken now because the Son has come according to plan.

Key contrast Both treat parable-speech as fulfilled revelation, not mere storytelling. Arminians typically highlight the uttered word as sincere light inviting reception; Calvinists typically highlight God’s sovereign disclosure on His timetable through the Messiah.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

The mustard seed, the leaven, and why Jesus speaks in parables: older Arminian and Reformed writers, each in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“He proposed to them another parable - The former parables relate chiefly to unfruitful hearers; these that follow, to those who bear good fruit. The kingdom of heaven - Both the Gospel dispensation, and the inward kingdom. The least - That is, one of the least: a way of speaking extremely common among the Jews.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 13:31–35)

Reformed Notable voices

“All these things Jesus spoke in parables. Though Mark expressly says, that Christ spoke the word to them as they were able to bear it, yet I think it probable that he continued to employ parables, not so much for the purpose of instruction, as to keep the attention of his hearers awake till a more convenient time.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 13:31–35)
