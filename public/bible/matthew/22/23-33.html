Passage

Matthew 22

Verses 23–33 · WEB · Voices

Sadducees, who deny the resurrection, pose a levirate-marriage riddle to discredit life after death. Jesus corrects their view of the age to come, grounds hope in God’s power and the Scriptures, and silences them before the listening crowds.

Matthew 22:23

23On that day Sadducees (those who say that there is no resurrection) came to him. They asked him,

Arminian

Same day, new challengers: Sadducees who deny resurrection. Unbelief takes doctrinal form and still approaches Jesus with a question.

Classical Arminians see grace meeting error with truth. Denial of the resurrection is not left unchallenged; Christ will call them back to Scripture and to God’s power.

Reformed

Matthew flags their dogma: no resurrection. The question is framed by a settled denial.

Reformed readers note providence in the timing—same day, successive tests. God brings error into the light so the Son may teach the hope of the age to come.

Matthew 22:24

24saying, “Teacher, Moses said, ‘If a man dies, having no children, his brother shall marry his wife and raise up offspring for his brother.’

Arminian

They cite Moses on levirate marriage (Deut 25). Scripture is quoted—yet aimed to undermine hope, not to seek truth.

Handling Moses without humble faith can weaponize the text. Grace still honors the Law’s intent while refusing cynical use of it.

Reformed

They open with “Moses said,” wrapping denial in biblical language.

Scripture cited is not Scripture believed. God will vindicate Moses by showing what the Word actually implies about the living God and the dead.

Matthew 22:25

25Now there were with us seven brothers. The first married and died, and having no offspring left his wife to his brother.

Arminian

The hypothetical begins: seven brothers, the first dies childless, widow passed on. A pastoral law becomes a puzzle piece in their trap.

The accent falls on how human grief (death, barrenness) can be twisted into skepticism. Jesus will reclaim the story for hope, not ridicule.

Reformed

Case study starts with the first brother’s death and the widow’s transfer.

Even their invented household sits under God’s truth. The details they stack will not overturn what God has spoken about life beyond the grave.

Matthew 22:26

26In the same way, the second also, and the third, to the seventh.

Arminian

The pattern repeats through seven—exaggeration designed to make resurrection look absurd.

Multiplying hard cases does not erase God’s promise. Faith answers difficulties by trusting God’s power, not by denying the age to come.

Reformed

Second, third, through the seventh—the riddle piles up to force a supposed contradiction.

Quantity of brothers cannot outargue God’s Word. The trap’s cleverness is no match for the Teacher who authored life.

Matthew 22:27

27After them all, the woman died.

Arminian

Finally the woman dies—every earthly tie in the story ends in the grave. Their point is that death seems to erase marriage’s obligations.

Arminians agree death is historical and grievous, yet refuse to let the grave have the last word when God speaks of resurrection life.

Reformed

Last of all, she dies. The earthly story closes.

Reformed hope already leans forward: the God of the living is about to redefine what death can and cannot end.

Matthew 22:28

28In the resurrection therefore, whose wife will she be of the seven? For they all had her.”

Arminian

The punch line assumes resurrection only to mock it: whose wife of the seven? They treat the age to come as a mere extension of present marriage rules.

Honest questions about eternity are welcome; loaded questions that shrink God’s power are not. Jesus will correct the premise, not dodge the people.

Reformed

“Whose wife will she be?” is the snare’s spring. They grant the word “resurrection” only to dismantle it.

False premises breed false dilemmas. Christ will expose both their ignorance of Scripture and their low view of God’s power.

Key contrast Both reject the Sadducees’ mocking premise. Arminians emphasize a sincere invitation to trust God’s power beyond earthly analogies; Reformed interpreters stress the same correction as Christ defending revealed truth against willful unbelief.

Matthew 22:29

29But Jesus answered them, “You are mistaken, not knowing the Scriptures, nor the power of God.

Arminian

Jesus answers plainly: “You are mistaken, not knowing.” The twin roots are ignorance of Scripture and of God’s power. Error is diagnosable and correctable by returning to both.

Classical Arminians urge teachability: grace exposes wrong so people may learn. Knowing the Bible and trusting God’s power belong together in living faith.

Reformed

Direct verdict: wrong—neither Scriptures nor God’s power known. Christ names the doctrinal and spiritual failure.

True knowledge is gift and duty. God reveals Himself in the Word; denying resurrection is refusal of that revelation and of divine power.

Key contrast Both receive Jesus’ diagnosis as authoritative. Arminians emphasize correctable ignorance that grace summons back to Scripture and trust; Reformed interpreters highlight the same verdict as exposing unbelief before the God who speaks and raises the dead.

Matthew 22:30

30For in the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like God’s angels in heaven.

Arminian

Resurrection life is not a replay of earthly marriage: no marrying or giving in marriage; likeness to angels in that respect. Continuity of persons, discontinuity of institutions.

God does not scrap love or identity; He transforms the order of the age to come. Hope is richer than the Sadducees imagined, not thinner.

Reformed

Jesus defines the resurrection state: neither marry nor given in marriage, but like angels in heaven (in not marrying).

God orders the new age. Marriage’s earthly calling gives way to a greater communion; the Creator’s power remakes life without being bound to present structures.

Matthew 22:31

31But concerning the resurrection of the dead, haven’t you read that which was spoken to you by God, saying,

Arminian

He turns them to what God said—to them. Scripture is personal address: “spoken to you.” Resurrection hope is not speculation but revelation.

Classical Arminians love this: God speaks so people may believe. Reading rightly is part of responding to grace.

Reformed

“Have you not read… spoken to you by God” presses covenantal Scripture. God Himself is the speaker.

Revelation settles the debate. The Sadducees’ canon is turned against their denial; God has already spoken life beyond the grave.

Matthew 22:32

32‘I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob’? God is not the God of the dead, but of the living.”

Arminian

Exodus 3:6 in the present tense—“I am”—grounds living relationship with the patriarchs. God is not the God of the dead, but of the living; therefore they live to Him.

The God who covenants keeps His people alive to Himself. Faith trusts this living God and the resurrection His power secures.

Reformed

Covenant Name and present “I am” prove the patriarchs live to God. He is God of the living.

God’s faithfulness to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob entails their life before Him—and by Christ’s word, the resurrection of the dead. Election’s God does not abandon His own to nonexistence.

Key contrast Both cling to Exodus 3 as resurrection proof. Arminians typically emphasize the living God’s covenantal faithfulness inviting trust; Reformed readers emphasize the same text as God’s sovereign self-revelation securing the life of His people.

Matthew 22:33

33When the multitudes heard it, they were astonished at his teaching.

Arminian

The crowd is astonished. Public teaching lands with force; Scripture and power, rightly held together, awaken wonder.

Astonishment can become faith. Grace used this debate so many hearers might move from surprise toward trust in the God of the living.

Reformed

Astonishment fills the crowd. Christ’s teaching silences the Sadducees and impresses the listeners.

God grants clarity through His Son. The week’s contests serve revelation—truth publicly vindicated on the way to the cross and empty tomb.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

On sadducees, who deny the resurrection, pose a levirate-marriage riddle to discredit life after death, a few classic voices from each tradition.

Arminian Notable voices

“Now there were with us seven brethren — this story seems to have been a kind of common-place objection, which no doubt they brought upon all occasions. Ye err, not knowing the Scriptures - Which plainly assert a resurrection. Nor the power of God - Which is well able to effect it.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 22:23–33)

Reformed Notable voices

“The same day came to him the Sadducees. We see here how Satan brings together all the ungodly, who in other respects differ widely from each other, to attack the truth of God. For, though deadly strife existed between these two sects, yet they conspire together against Christ; so that the Pharisees are not displeased to have their own doctrine attacked in the person of Christ.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 22:23–33)
