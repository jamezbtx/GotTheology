Passage

Matthew 22

Verses 15–22 · ESV · Voices

Pharisees and Herodians try to trap Jesus on the poll tax. His answer—“render to Caesar… and to God”—exposes bad faith, affirms civil duty without surrendering worship, and leaves opponents marveling as Passion Week tension rises.

Matthew 22:15

15Then the Pharisees went and plotted how to entangle him in his words.

Arminian

Opposition turns tactical: they plot to entangle Jesus in speech. Hostile intent meets the Teacher who still speaks truth in public.

Classical Arminians note that light keeps shining amid schemes. Grace does not withdraw from contested spaces; Jesus remains available to be heard rightly even when hearers aim to trap Him.

Reformed

Matthew names the plot: Pharisees seek to entangle Him in His words. Human conspiracy advances toward the cross under a darker wisdom than theirs.

Reformed readers see providence over malice. Their plotting is real sin, yet it cannot outrun the Father’s purpose for the Son in this week.

Matthew 22:16

16And they sent their disciples to him, along with the Herodians, saying, "Teacher, we know that you are true and teach the way of God truthfully, and you do not care about anyone's opinion, for you are not swayed by appearances.

Arminian

Flattery opens the trap: they call Him true, God’s truthful teacher, unmoved by appearances. The compliments are accurate in form, hollow in motive.

Arminian pastoral caution: grace can be praised with the lips while the heart resists. True teaching still deserves honest approach, not staged praise that softens a snare.

Reformed

Disciples of the Pharisees arrive with Herodians—an odd alliance—armed with polished flattery.

Reformed sobriety: God is not fooled by speech. Their confession that Jesus teaches God’s way “truthfully” will rebound; the One they flatter is the Truth who will unmask them.

Matthew 22:17

17Tell us, then, what you think. Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?"

Arminian

The question forces a binary: lawful to pay Caesar’s tax, or not? Either answer seems to cost Him—Rome or the crowd.

Classical Arminians hear a real moral query buried under bad faith. God’s law addresses civic life; Jesus will answer without surrendering the kingdom’s claim on the conscience.

Reformed

“Is it lawful…?” is designed as a no-win. Politics and piety are weaponized against the Messiah.

Reformed confidence: no dilemma escapes Christ’s wisdom. The question He is asked sits inside the hour God appointed for revealing His lordship over Caesar and conscience.

Matthew 22:18

18But Jesus, aware of their malice, said, "Why put me to the test, you hypocrites?

Arminian

Jesus knows their malice and names the test: hypocrisy. Grace includes exposing false motives so repentance remains possible.

Arminian pastoral edge: the Lord still addresses them—“why put me to the test?”—a searching word that invites honesty rather than only condemnation.

Reformed

Awareness of malice precedes the answer. He labels them hypocrites—truth-telling judgment.

Reformed emphasis: Christ’s knowledge is perfect; their hearts are open before Him. The rebuke is just and serves the revealing work of this Passion week.

Key contrast Both affirm Jesus’ true discernment of malice. Arminians typically stress a searching rebuke that still summons honesty; Reformed readers typically stress the same holy exposure as certain knowledge advancing God’s purpose in the confrontation.

Matthew 22:19

19Show me the coin for the tax." And they brought him a denarius.

Arminian

He asks for the tax coin; they produce a denarius. The object lesson begins with what they already handle.

Classical Arminians note ordinary means: Jesus teaches with a coin in hand. Grace meets people in the currency of their daily compromises and calls for clear thinking.

Reformed

“Show me the coin” turns their trap into a classroom. They bring the denarius without delay.

Reformed reading: even the props of the test serve the Teacher. God orders the moment so the image on the coin will preach.

Matthew 22:20

20And Jesus said to them, "Whose likeness and inscription is this?"

Arminian

Whose likeness and inscription? He draws the answer from them. Image-language echoes Genesis: coins bear Caesar’s image; people bear God’s.

Arminian teaching loves this hinge: rightful claims follow likeness. Civic tokens and divine image both matter—neither cancels the other.

Reformed

The question is precise: likeness and inscription. Authority is tied to whose image marks the object.

Reformed theology hears creation and providence: Caesar’s coin, God’s image-bearers. Christ frames the whole ethic before He states the duty.

Matthew 22:21

21They said, "Caesar's." Then he said to them, "Therefore render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's."

Arminian

They concede “Caesar’s”; Jesus replies with the double render: give Caesar his due, and God His. Civil obligation stands; worship and ultimate allegiance remain God’s alone.

Classical Arminians stress responsible freedom under grace: believers may pay tax without idolizing the state, and must yield themselves—image-bearers—to God. Both duties are real.

Reformed

“Render to Caesar… and to God” is kingdom wisdom in one sentence. Earthly authority receives its limited due; God receives what is inalienably His.

Reformed confession: Christ upholds ordered spheres under God’s sovereignty. Paying the tax does not shrink God’s claim; rendering to God relativizes Caesar without anarchic revolt.

Key contrast Both receive the double command as true. Arminians typically emphasize believers’ real responsibility in both civic duty and wholehearted yielding to God; Reformed readers typically emphasize the same duties under Christ’s lordship and God’s sovereign ordering of earthly authority.

Matthew 22:22

22When they heard it, they marveled. And they left him and went away.

Arminian

They marvel—and leave. Amazement without repentance is possible; the trap fails, yet hearts may still walk away.

Arminian pastoral note: marveling at Jesus’ wisdom is not the same as trusting Him. Grace gave a clarifying word; the next step is to stay and obey, not merely exit impressed.

Reformed

Marvel, then departure. The plot collapses under His answer.

Reformed sobriety: God frustrates the snare and still leaves room for later hardening. Their exit does not undo Christ’s victory in the exchange; the week moves on toward the cross He came to bear.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Pharisees and Herodians try to trap Jesus on the poll tax—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 22:15–22.

Arminian Notable voices

“The Herodians were a set of men peculiarly attached to Herod, and consequently zealous for the interest of the Roman government, which was the main support of the dignity and royalty of his family. Thou regardest not the person of men - Thou favourest no man for his riches or greatness.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 22:15–22)

Reformed Notable voices

“That they might entrap him in his words. The Pharisees, perceiving that all their other attempts against Christ had been fruitless, at length concluded that the best and most expeditious method of destroying him was, to deliver him to the governor, as a seditious person and a disturber of the peace.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 22:15–22)
