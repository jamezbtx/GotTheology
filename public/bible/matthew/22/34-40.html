Passage

Matthew 22

Verses 34–40 · ESV · Voices

After Jesus silences the Sadducees, Pharisees test Him with the greatest-commandment question. He answers with whole-person love for God (Deut 6:5) and neighbor-love like self (Lev 19:18), hanging all the Law and the Prophets on these two.

Matthew 22:34

34But when the Pharisees heard that he had silenced the Sadducees, they gathered together.

Arminian

News of the Sadducees’ silence draws the Pharisees together. Another test is forming; Jesus remains the public Teacher they cannot ignore.

Classical Arminians hear grace still available in contested moments. Gathering to challenge Him does not cancel the call to hear and obey the word He is about to give.

Reformed

Pharisees assemble because the Sadducees were silenced. Rival schools unite in testing the Messiah.

Reformed readers see providence steering the day’s contests. God orders successive confrontations so the greatest commandment is taught in the open before Passion Week deepens.

Matthew 22:35

35And one of them, a lawyer, asked him a question to test him.

Arminian

A lawyer asks—to test Him. Expertise in the Law becomes a probe rather than a quest for truth.

Arminian pastoral caution: knowing Torah is not the same as loving God. Grace invites the tester to become a hearer; the question can still lead to life if motives turn.

Reformed

The question is framed as a test. Legal skill serves a trap.

Reformed sobriety: Christ is not surprised. Even hostile inquiry becomes the occasion for perfect summary of the Law under the Son’s authority.

Matthew 22:36

36"Teacher, which is the great commandment in the Law?"

Arminian

Which commandment is great? The Law’s weight is on the table. Ranking can clarify—or become a game that misses the heart.

Classical Arminians welcome the question when it seeks to love God truly. Jesus will answer with commands that claim the whole person and the neighbor.

Reformed

“Which is the great commandment?” presses priority inside Torah.

Reformed confidence: the Law’s center is not left to rabbinic guesswork. The Son will name what God requires first and second, with full scriptural weight.

Matthew 22:37

37And he said to him, "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind.

Arminian

Jesus cites Deuteronomy 6:5: love God with all heart, soul, and mind. The first and great call is whole-person devotion—not partial religious performance.

Classical Arminians stress grace-enabled love that is real and responsible. God commands what He also enables; the believer is summoned to love Him sincerely with the whole self.

Reformed

Shema love, expanded with “mind,” claims every faculty for Yahweh. The great commandment is Godward before it is social.

Reformed confession: such love is the fruit of God’s prior love and Spirit’s work. The Law’s height drives us to grace and defines the holiness God works in His people.

Key contrast Both bow to whole-person love for God as the great command. Arminians typically emphasize a real, grace-enabled response of the whole self; Reformed readers typically emphasize the same command as God’s righteous requirement fulfilled in believers by sovereign grace.

Matthew 22:38

38This is the great and first commandment.

Arminian

Great and first: love for God holds priority. Neighbor-love will follow, but it never replaces the vertical claim.

Arminian preaching keeps the order clear so ethics stay worshipful—love of people flows from love of God, not the reverse.

Reformed

Jesus ranks without apology: this is great and first.

Reformed order of piety: God’s glory and love for Him stand before every secondary duty. The Law’s hierarchy is revealed by the Lawgiver’s Son.

Matthew 22:39

39And a second is like it: You shall love your neighbor as yourself.

Arminian

Second and “like it”: Leviticus 19:18. Neighbor-love matches the seriousness of God-love in moral weight, without erasing the first’s primacy.

Classical Arminians urge concrete mercy. Grace that claims the heart must reach the neighbor; “as yourself” sets an active, measurable care.

Reformed

The second commandment is like the first—neighbor-love as yourself. Horizontal duty is tethered to the vertical.

Reformed ethics: love of neighbor is not optional add-on but covenant obedience flowing from love of God. The Spirit writes both on the renewed heart.

Matthew 22:40

40On these two commandments depend all the Law and the Prophets."

Arminian

All the Law and the Prophets hang on these two. Scripture’s moral vision coheres in love for God and neighbor—summary without subtraction.

Arminian pastoral hope: complexity finds clarity. Living faith aims at this double love as the shape of obedience grace produces.

Reformed

“Depend” (hang) means the whole canon’s ethical load rests here. Law and Prophets are not rival systems.

Reformed unity of Scripture: God interprets His own Word through the Son. Every statute and prophetic call finds its center in this double love—fulfilled perfectly in Christ and formed in His people.

Key contrast Both receive the double summary as the Law’s center. Arminians typically stress these two as the living aim of grace-enabled obedience; Reformed readers typically stress the same center as God’s revealed moral will fulfilled in Christ and worked in the elect.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Pharisees test Him with the greatest-commandment question—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 22:34–40.

Arminian Notable voices

“A scribe asking him a question, trying him - Not, as it seems, with any ill design: but barely to make a further trial of that wisdom, which he had shown in silencing the Sadducees.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 22:34–40)

Reformed Notable voices

“According to Mark, the preface is inserted, that Jehovah alone is the God of Israel; by which words God supports the authority of his law in two ways. For, first, it ought to be a powerful excitement to the worship of God, when we are fully convinced that we worship the actual Creator of heaven and earth, because indifference is naturally produced by doubt; and, secondly, because it is a pleasing inducement to love him, when he freely adopts us as his people.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 22:34–40)
