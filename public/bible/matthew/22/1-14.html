Passage

Matthew 22

Verses 1–14 · ESV · Voices

Still in the temple courts after the vineyard parable, Jesus answers hostile leaders with another kingdom parable: a royal wedding feast. Refusal, judgment, wide invitation, and the wedding garment press both the sincerity of the call and the seriousness of coming on the King’s terms.

Matthew 22:1

1And again Jesus spoke to them in parables, saying,

Arminian

Jesus speaks again in parables to the same audience. Grace keeps teaching even after rejection; the King does not stop inviting with truth.

Classical Arminians hear patient, repeated address. Parables both reveal and search hearts—light offered so hearers may turn and live.

Reformed

Matthew marks another parabolic word from Jesus. The teaching continues under the Father’s purpose, not as a failed Plan A.

Reformed readers see sovereign instruction: Christ speaks what must be spoken in this hour. The parable will expose unbelief and display the kingdom’s true shape.

Matthew 22:2

2"The kingdom of heaven may be compared to a king who gave a wedding feast for his son,

Arminian

The kingdom is pictured as a king’s wedding feast for his son—joy, honor, and relationship at the center. God hosts; the Son is celebrated.

Arminian pastoral warmth: the gospel is invitation to feast, not mere duty. Grace sets a table and summons guests to glad communion with the Son.

Reformed

Kingdom = royal wedding for the Son. The feast is the King’s initiative and the Son’s honor.

Reformed confession: God designs the celebration and names its Guest of honor. Salvation history is the Father glorifying the Son by gathering a people to the banquet He prepared.

Matthew 22:3

3and sent his servants to call those who were invited to the wedding feast, but they would not come.

Arminian

Servants call those already invited—yet “they would not come.” Refusal is real: grace extends a genuine summons that can be resisted.

Classical Arminians stress the tragedy of willful rejection. The call is sincere; the guests’ “would not” is culpable, not a charade. God desires their presence at the feast.

Reformed

The call goes out; the invited refuse. Scripture records both the sending and the “would not.”

Reformed reading holds human responsibility inside providence: their refusal is their own sin, and it unfolds within God’s larger purpose for the Son’s feast. Unbelief never surprises the King.

Key contrast Both treat the refusal as real guilt. Arminians typically emphasize a sincere call that guests truly can and do resist; Reformed readers typically emphasize the same culpable refusal within God’s sovereign ordering of the parable’s outcome.

Matthew 22:4

4Again he sent other servants, saying, 'Tell those who are invited, "See, I have prepared my dinner, my oxen and my fat calves have been slaughtered, and everything is ready. Come to the wedding feast."'

Arminian

A second wave of servants intensifies the plea: everything is ready—come. Grace persists with richer detail and fresh messengers.

Arminian preaching hears God’s heart in the repeated “Come.” Readiness is on the King’s side; the only barrier named here is the guests’ response.

Reformed

Again the King sends. The feast’s readiness is complete—oxen, fat calves, dinner prepared.

Reformed comfort: God’s provision does not wait on human worthiness. The message is full and free in content; the King’s table is already set by His decree and generosity.

Matthew 22:5

5But they paid no attention and went off, one to his farm, another to his business,

Arminian

Indifference, not only violence, rejects the feast—farm and business crowd out the King’s joy. Ordinary cares can choke a real invitation.

Classical Arminians warn pastorally: grace can be neglected. The call stands; distracted hearts still bear responsibility for walking away.

Reformed

They “paid no attention” and returned to farm and business. Worldly absorption unmasks misplaced loves.

Reformed sobriety: providence exposes what rules the heart. Even quiet refusal serves the parable’s judgment theme—the invited prove unworthy by what they treasure.

Matthew 22:6

6while the rest seized his servants, treated them shamefully, and killed them.

Arminian

Others escalate from neglect to violence against the messengers. Rejecting the feast becomes hostility toward the King who sent them.

Arminian reading keeps agency clear: this cruelty is chosen evil. Grace sent servants; hearers answered with shame and murder—deepening the case for repentance.

Reformed

Seizure, shame, and killing of servants echo Israel’s treatment of the prophets and foreshadow the Passion path.

Reformed eyes see history inside the story: God knew this hostility; the servants’ suffering is not outside His plan for the Son’s kingdom and the city’s coming judgment.

Matthew 22:7

7The king was angry, and he sent his troops and destroyed those murderers and burned their city.

Arminian

Royal anger answers murder. Judgment is moral, not arbitrary—destruction falls on those who killed the messengers.

Classical Arminians affirm that rejected grace does not erase justice. The King who invites also judges; love’s feast does not cancel holiness.

Reformed

Anger, troops, burned city—severe judgment under the King’s authority. Many hear an allusion to Jerusalem’s fall.

Reformed confession: wrath is righteous and purposeful. God’s holiness vindicates His servants and His Son; judgment advances the kingdom story the parable tells.

Key contrast Both affirm just judgment after violent refusal. Arminians typically stress judgment as the moral consequence of resisting a sincere invitation; Reformed readers typically stress the same justice as God’s holy, sovereign response woven into redemptive history.

Matthew 22:8

8Then he said to his servants, 'The wedding feast is ready, but those invited were not worthy.

Arminian

Worthiness here is measured by response: the invited proved unworthy by refusal. The feast remains ready—God’s generosity is not canceled.

Arminian pastoral note: unworthiness is not about social class but about rejecting the call. Grace still seeks guests who will come.

Reformed

The feast is ready; the first invitees are declared “not worthy.” Worth tracks with reception of the King’s summons.

Reformed emphasis: God’s evaluation stands. Their unworthiness is real, and it clears the narrative for the wider gathering God intends.

Matthew 22:9

9Go therefore to the main roads and invite to the wedding feast as many as you find.'

Arminian

The mission widens: main roads, “as many as you find.” The invitation goes beyond the first list to whoever will hear.

Classical Arminians rejoice in expansive gospel offer. Grace is free for all who are found and called; the servants’ task is urgent, sincere invitation.

Reformed

“Go therefore” launches a broader summons to the roads. The King authorizes a wide hunt for guests.

Reformed mission: God opens the feast to the nations in His time. The servants invite because the King commands; the breadth of the call displays His purpose to fill the hall.

Matthew 22:10

10And those servants went out into the roads and gathered all whom they found, both bad and good. So the wedding hall was filled with guests.

Arminian

Bad and good alike are gathered; the hall fills. Grace does not wait for moral polish before inviting—yet filling the hall is not the final word of the parable.

Arminian hope: the open roads mean real opportunity. Mixed company at the feast still leaves room for personal response to the King’s inspection.

Reformed

Servants gather “both bad and good”; the hall is filled. God’s gathering includes the unlikely.

Reformed wonder: the King gets a full hall by His sending. The mix of guests sets up the garment scene—membership in the crowd is not the same as acceptance on the King’s terms.

Matthew 22:11

11"But when the king came in to look at the guests, he saw there a man who had no wedding garment.

Arminian

The King inspects. One guest lacks a wedding garment—presence without proper honor. Grace invites widely; coming still requires the King’s provision and fitting response.

Classical Arminians urge self-examination: sitting among guests is not enough. Receive what the feast requires—often heard as Christ’s righteousness and a life that answers the call.

Reformed

Royal inspection discovers a man without a wedding garment. External inclusion meets the King’s holy standard.

Reformed preaching: God sees. The garment (commonly read as the righteousness God provides) is not optional décor; the King’s look distinguishes true guests from pretenders.

Matthew 22:12

12And he said to him, 'Friend, how did you get in here without a wedding garment?' And he was speechless.

Arminian

“Friend” still addresses him, yet the question exposes presumption. Speechlessness signals no excuse—he knew, or should have known, the feast’s terms.

Arminian pastoral edge: grace leaves people without a defense when they refuse the garment. The silence is accountability, not divine trapping.

Reformed

The King asks; the man is speechless. No plea overturns the missing garment.

Reformed gravity: on that day excuses fail. God’s interrogation is just; silence confirms that entry without the appointed covering cannot stand.

Matthew 22:13

13Then the king said to the attendants, 'Bind him hand and foot and cast him into the outer darkness. In that place there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.'

Arminian

Binding and outer darkness show that nearness to the feast without the garment ends in exclusion. Weeping and gnashing underline real loss.

Classical Arminians warn without glee: the same King who invites also excludes the unclad guest. Persevering, garment-clad faith is the needed response to grace.

Reformed

Judgment language matches Jesus’ other kingdom warnings: outer darkness, weeping, gnashing of teeth.

Reformed confession: final exclusion is the King’s verdict. Holiness and mercy meet—the feast is joy for the clothed, and the unclothed face just banishment.

Matthew 22:14

14For many are called, but few are chosen."

Arminian

Many hear the call; few are “chosen” in the sense the parable dramatizes—few prove to be true feast-guests who come and remain on the King’s terms.

Classical Arminians typically read “called” as the wide, sincere invitation and “chosen” as those who freely respond and persevere in fitting faith (the garment), not a narrowing of the offer’s sincerity.

Reformed

Jesus seals the parable: many called, few chosen. The wide summons and the smaller company of the truly accepted stand together in His summary.

Reformed readers typically hear effectual grace and election in “chosen”: God’s calling gathers many hearers, and His choosing secures those who truly belong at the Son’s feast.

Key contrast Both take Jesus’ summary with full weight. Arminians typically emphasize a wide, sincere call and “chosen” as those who respond and endure in the garment of true faith; Reformed readers typically emphasize the same wide outward call alongside God’s sovereign choosing of those who finally feast with the Son.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus answers hostile leaders with another kingdom parable: a royal wedding feast—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 22:1–14.

Arminian Notable voices

“Jesus answering, spake - That is, spake with reference to what had just past. A king, who made a marriage feast for his son - So did God, when he brought his first - begotten into the world. Them that were invited - Namely, the Jews. One to his farm, another to his merchandise - One must mind what he has; another, gain what he wants.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 22:1–14)

Reformed Notable voices

“Though Matthew relates this parable among other discourses which were delivered by Christ about the time of the last Passover, yet as he does not specify any particular time, and as Luke expressly affirms that Christ delivered this discourse while he sat at table in the house of a Pharisee, I have thought it better to follow this order. The design which Matthew had in view was, to point out the reasons why the scribes were excited to the highest pitch of fury; and therefore he properly placed it in the midst of those discourses which were hateful to them, and interwove it with those discourses, without attending to the order of time.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 22:1–14)
