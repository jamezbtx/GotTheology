Passage

Matthew 22

Verses 41–46 · ESV · Voices

Jesus turns the examination on the Pharisees: whose son is the Christ? Psalm 110:1 shows David calling the Messiah “Lord,” so the Son of David is more than a mere descendant. No one dares ask Him another question.

Matthew 22:41

41Now while the Pharisees were gathered together, Jesus asked them a question,

Arminian

While they are still gathered, Jesus asks. The tested One becomes the examiner—grace that seeks their confession of the Messiah’s true identity.

Classical Arminians hear invitation in the question. He draws them toward fuller light about who the Christ is, not merely to win a debate.

Reformed

Christ seizes the moment of their gathering. The initiative shifts to the Son’s question.

Reformed reading: God turns the tables on schedule. Revelation presses forward; their silence later will confirm the force of what He is about to show from Psalm 110.

Matthew 22:42

42saying, "What do you think about the Christ? Whose son is he?" They said to him, "The son of David."

Arminian

“Whose son is the Christ?” They answer rightly as far as it goes: Son of David. Messianic hope tied to David’s line is true—and incomplete without Lordship.

Arminian pastoral note: partial orthodoxy needs growth. Grace affirms Davidic descent and presses them to see more than ancestry.

Reformed

He asks what they think about the Christ; they reply “the son of David.” Correct covenant expectation.

Reformed emphasis: Scripture’s promise of David’s son stands. Jesus will deepen it—Messiah is David’s son and David’s Lord by God’s design.

Matthew 22:43

43He said to them, "How is it then that David, in the Spirit, calls him Lord, saying,

Arminian

David, “in the Spirit,” calls Him Lord. Inspiration matters: the psalm is Spirit-given, not mere poetry. The Messiah’s identity rests on God’s Word.

Classical Arminians urge Spirit-taught Scripture reading. Grace opens Psalm 110 so hearers may confess Christ’s lordship freely and truly.

Reformed

“In the Spirit” marks David’s speech as God-breathed prophecy. The argument hangs on inspired Scripture.

Reformed bedrock: the Spirit spoke through David. Christ’s lordship over David is not later invention but revelatory fact embedded in the psalm.

Matthew 22:44

44"'The Lord said to my Lord, "Sit at my right hand, until I put your enemies under your feet"'?"

Arminian

Psalm 110:1: Yahweh speaks to David’s Lord—seat at the right hand, enemies underfoot. Exaltation and victory belong to the Messiah.

Arminian preaching: the risen Christ invites trust and allegiance. Enemies subdued is promise; sitting at God’s right hand summons present worship, not only future hope.

Reformed

Double “Lord”: Yahweh addresses David’s Lord with session at the right hand and promised conquest.

Reformed confession: this is the Messiah’s divine-royal status and certain triumph. God’s decree seats the Son and guarantees His enemies’ subjection.

Key contrast Both receive Psalm 110 as Messianic exaltation. Arminians typically emphasize the summons to trust and worship the enthroned Lord; Reformed readers typically emphasize the same text as God’s sovereign decree seating Christ and securing His victory.

Matthew 22:45

45If then David calls him Lord, how is he his son?"

Arminian

If David calls Him Lord, how is He David’s son? Both are true: descent and superior Lordship. The riddle pushes beyond a merely human heir.

Classical Arminians welcome the tension as revelation: Jesus is Son of David and Lord—worthy of the faith the Pharisees still withhold.

Reformed

The question locks the dilemma: Lord and son together. Genealogy alone cannot explain Psalm 110.

Reformed christology in seed form: the Messiah shares David’s line and transcends David as Lord. God has spoken; the Christ must be confessed accordingly.

Key contrast Both hold Son-of-David and Lord together. Arminians typically stress the call to confess this fuller Christ in living faith; Reformed readers typically stress the same identity as God-revealed truth that silences inadequate Messianic reductions.

Matthew 22:46

46And no one was able to answer him a word, nor from that day did anyone dare to ask him any more questions.

Arminian

No answer; no more questions from that day. Silence can be conviction’s doorway—or mere retreat from an uncomfortable Lord.

Arminian pastoral edge: grace gave clarity about the Christ. The right response is not only stopped mouths but opened faith in David’s Son and Lord.

Reformed

They cannot answer; questioning ceases. Christ’s wisdom closes the trap cycle.

Reformed gravity: God ends the examination on His Son’s terms. Silence does not equal salvation, yet it marks the public defeat of their tests as the path to the cross continues under divine purpose.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus turns the examination on the Pharisees: whose son is the Christ?—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 22:41–46.

Arminian Notable voices

“How doth David then by the Spirit - By inspiration, call him Lord? If he be merely the son (or descendant) of David? If he be, as you suppose, a mere man, the son of a man? The Lord said to my Lord - This his dominion, to which David himself was subject, shows both the heavenly majesty of the king, and the nature of his kingdom.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 22:41–46)

Reformed Notable voices

“Mark and Luke express more clearly the reason why Christ put this question. It was because there prevailed among the scribes an erroneous opinion, that the promised Redeemer would be one of David's sons and successors, who would bring along with him nothing more elevated than human nature.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 22:41–46)
