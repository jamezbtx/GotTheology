Passage

Matthew 27

Verses 45–56 · ESV

From the sixth to the ninth hour darkness covers the land. Jesus cries the forsaken cry of Psalm 22, yields up His spirit, the temple curtain tears, earth shakes, saints are raised, and a centurion confesses the Son of God while women watch from a distance.

Matthew 27:45

45Now from the sixth hour there was darkness over all the land until the ninth hour.

Arminian

Midday darkness—creation itself seems to mourn. The cross is not a private execution; the land is marked.

Arminian pastoral: God gives signs that summon attention. Grace uses wonder to call; darkness still requires a response of faith in the Crucified.

Reformed

Darkness over the land: cosmic witness to the hour. Providence clothes Golgotha in night at noon.

Reformed awe: nature serves the Passion narrative. The Creator’s death-work is signaled in the creaturely sky.

Matthew 27:46

46And about the ninth hour Jesus cried out with a loud voice, saying, “Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?” that is, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”

Arminian

The loud cry quotes Psalm 22: real anguish, not playacting. The Holy One tastes forsakenness in our place.

Classical Arminians stress sincere, costly atonement—Christ truly suffers for sinners whom grace invites to trust Him. The cry is not theater; it is the depth of redeeming love that enables faith rather than coerces it.

Reformed

Psalm 22 on the lips of the Son: forsakenness under judgment, yet still “My God.” The cry is covenantal anguish, not atheism.

Reformed heart of the cross: the cup is drained. Penal suffering and unbroken person of the Son hold together. He is forsaken in our stead according to the Father’s will, that the forsaken may be brought near.

Key contrast Both refuse a mild cross. Arminians typically emphasize Christ’s real, loving self-giving that sincerely calls sinners to faith; Reformed readers typically emphasize appointed substitution under the Father’s just will that will not fail to save His people.

Matthew 27:47

47And some of the bystanders, hearing it, said, “This man is calling Elijah.”

Arminian

They mishear Eli as Elijah. Even the cry of Scripture is garbled by unbelief.

Arminian note: proximity to the cross does not equal understanding. Grace opens ears; without it, holy words become gossip.

Reformed

Mishearing at the cross: Elijah instead of Eli. Darkness of mind matches darkness of sky.

Reformed note: human confusion cannot mute what Jesus actually prayed. The Word stands.

Matthew 27:48

48And one of them at once ran and took a sponge, filled it with sour wine, and put it on a reed and gave it to him to drink.

Arminian

A sponge of sour wine on a reed—small mercy or further staging? Matthew records the act without flattery.

Arminian application: mixed motives often surround holy suffering. Grace still calls bystanders from curiosity to faith.

Reformed

Sour wine offered: detail under providence, echoing Scripture’s thirst scenes.

Reformed holding: even a reed and sponge sit inside the fulfilled hour.

Matthew 27:49

49But the others said, “Wait, let us see whether Elijah will come to save him.”

Arminian

Wait—let us see: spectators want a show of rescue. Faith becomes watching for spectacle.

Arminian warning: delaying for signs can be unbelief dressed as patience. Grace points to the Christ already saving by staying.

Reformed

Elijah-watch as entertainment. They miss the salvation happening in the cry itself.

Reformed sobriety: no Elijah show. Deliverance will be resurrection, not their demanded stunt.

Matthew 27:50

50And Jesus cried out again with a loud voice and yielded up his spirit.

Arminian

Loud voice, then yielded spirit—death is real, and also given. He lays down life; it is not merely ripped away.

Arminian gospel: willing death for sinners. Grace invites trust in the One who yielded Himself, not in our climbing down from guilt.

Reformed

Yielded up His spirit: authority in dying. The Son’s life is not stolen at random.

Reformed assurance: obedient death under the Father. The Spirit is yielded when the work of that hour is complete.

Matthew 27:51

51And behold, the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom. And the earth shook, and the rocks were split.

Arminian

Curtain torn from top to bottom—access language in fabric. Earth shakes; rocks split. God interprets the death with holy violence against barriers.

Arminian pastoral: the way to God is opened in Christ for all who come. Grace invites bold approach; do not sew the curtain back with self-made religion.

Reformed

Torn top to bottom: God acts from above. Earthquake signs the verdict of heaven on the cross.

Reformed confidence: atonement opens the holy place. The barrier falls by God’s hand, not human ladders.

Matthew 27:52

52The tombs also were opened. And many bodies of the saints who had fallen asleep were raised,

Arminian

Tombs open; sleeping saints are raised—firstfruits atmosphere around the Crucified. Death’s reign cracks.

Arminian hope: the cross unleashes life. Grace that raises is offered in the gospel to those who believe.

Reformed

Opened tombs: the death of Jesus shakes Sheol’s gates. Matthew ties resurrection power to this death.

Reformed hope: the Crucified is already Lord over death’s domain. Providence previews Easter in the quake.

Matthew 27:53

53and coming out of the tombs after his resurrection they went into the holy city and appeared to many.

Arminian

Matthew times their appearance after His resurrection—Christ first. Witnesses in the holy city multiply testimony.

Arminian note: God gives evidence that calls for faith. Appearances invite responsible trust, not coerce robots.

Reformed

After His resurrection they appear: order preserved—Christ the firstfruits logic.

Reformed clarity: Jesus’ rising governs theirs. The holy city receives signs that the age to come has broken in.

Matthew 27:54

54When the centurion and those who were with him, keeping watch over Jesus, saw the earthquake and what took place, they were filled with awe and said, “Truly this was the Son of God!”

Arminian

A Gentile watch confesses: Truly this was the Son of God. The title mocked earlier is spoken in awe.

Arminian joy: grace reaches unexpected mouths. The confession is a real response to light given; hearers may likewise confess.

Reformed

Centurion’s confession from the guard post. Providence draws praise from Rome’s own ranks.

Reformed comfort: the Son is acknowledged even at the cross. God opens eyes according to mercy; the confession stands as true.

Key contrast Both treasure the confession. Arminians typically stress enabled, responsible response to revealed awe; Reformed readers typically stress God’s merciful opening of Gentile lips amid the appointed Passion.

Matthew 27:55

55There were also many women there, looking on from a distance, who had followed Jesus from Galilee, ministering to him,

Arminian

Women watch from a distance—faithful presence when many disciples fled. Ministry from Galilee continues at the cross.

Arminian encouragement: grace sustains quiet fidelity. Distance may be forced by danger; love still looks on.

Reformed

Women as witnesses: Matthew prepares Easter testimony. Providence keeps observers at the death.

Reformed honor: God often preserves the story through overlooked servants. Their watching is part of the plan of witness.

Matthew 27:56

56among whom were Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of James and Joseph and the mother of the sons of Zebedee.

Arminian

Named women: Mary Magdalene, the other Mary, Zebedee’s wife. Love with names attached.

Arminian pastoral: discipleship is personal. Grace calls particular people to particular courage at the cross.

Reformed

Names recorded under the cross. Scripture remembers who stayed near.

Reformed note: God writes witnesses into the Passion so the church may know this death was public, attested, and unto hope.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the forsaken cry, the yielded spirit, and the opened way through Christ’s death—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside the darkness, the torn curtain, and the centurion’s confession.

Arminian Notable voices

“Now they are justified freely by his grace, through the redemption that is in Jesus Christ. Him God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood.… Now hath Christ taken away the curse of the law, being made a curse for us… blotting out the handwriting that was against us… nailing it to his cross.”
John Wesley · Sermon 1, “Salvation by Faith”
“I know, the saints who will be placed before the tribunal of the Divine Justice, have had Faith, and through Faith have performed good Works. But… they appear and stand before God with this confidence… that God has set forth his Son Jesus Christ as a propitiation through Faith in his blood… I do not read, that Christ is constituted a propitiation through Works… that we may also be justified by Works.”
Jacobus Arminius · Works, Vol. 1 (on justification / faith and works)

Reformed Notable voices

“Nor is it by hypocrisy, or by assuming a character, that he complains of having been forsaken by the Father.… the inward sadness of his soul was so powerful and violent, that it forced him to break out into a cry.… having undertaken to be our surety, he resolved actually to undergo in our room the judgment of God.… Though the perception of the flesh would have led him to dread destruction, still in his heart faith remained firm… before stating the temptation, he begins by saying that he betakes himself to God as his God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 27:46)
“What do you understand by the word “suffered”? That during his whole life on earth, but especially at the end, Christ sustained in body and soul the wrath of God against the sin of the whole human race. This he did in order that, by his suffering as the only atoning sacrifice, he might deliver us, body and soul, from eternal condemnation, and gain for us God’s grace, righteousness, and eternal life.”
Heidelberg Catechism · Q. 37 (1563)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
