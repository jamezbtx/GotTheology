Passage

Matthew 27

Verses 27–31 · ESV

After Barabbas is released and Jesus is scourged and delivered to be crucified (v. 26), the governor’s soldiers take Jesus into the headquarters, mock His kingship with robe, thorns, and reed, then lead Him away to the cross.

Matthew 27:27

27Then the soldiers of the governor took Jesus into the governor's headquarters, and they gathered the whole battalion before him.

Arminian

Soldiers take Jesus inside; a whole battalion gathers for spectacle. The Passion becomes barracks entertainment. Roman power stacks the room against one bound Man.

Classical Arminians hear culpable cruelty under light enough to know better. Grace does not force reverence from the battalion; their mockery is chosen abuse, and hearers are warned not to treat the King as sport.

Reformed

Headquarters and battalion: public military scorn before the public cross. The Son enters the heart of Gentile power as predicted paths converge.

Reformed holding: their gathering is wicked, and providence still governs the hour. God is not the author of their contempt; He is not absent from the room where His Son is shamed.

Matthew 27:28

28And they stripped him and put a scarlet robe on him,

Arminian

Stripped, then draped in scarlet—mock royal dress. They costume the King to laugh at Him. Shame is deliberate, not accidental.

Arminian pastoral accent: Jesus yields to humiliation rather than summon force. Grace invites disciples to a Lord who bore disgrace; we remain responsible for whether we honor or mimic the mockers’ heart.

Reformed

Scarlet on the stripped Christ: parody of empire’s purple. Matthew lets the irony stand without needing the soldiers to understand it.

Reformed comfort: the robe of mockery cannot unmake true kingship. Their costume serves a story God is writing toward the cross, while their guilt stays entirely theirs.

Matthew 27:29

29and twisting together a crown of thorns, they put it on his head and put a reed in his right hand. And kneeling before him, they mocked him, saying, “Hail, King of the Jews!”

Arminian

Thorns for a crown, a reed for a scepter, kneeling for a joke: Hail, King of the Jews! Words of homage become weapons. The truth of His kingship is spoken as insult.

Arminian edge: lips can say “King” without bowing in faith. Grace still offers the real crown afterward—Christ exalted—while this verse shows how freely people can scorn the One who would save them.

Reformed

Every prop preaches against their intention: thorns, reed, hail. They kneel in mockery; the church will kneel in truth.

Reformed doxology in grief: God turns their satire into involuntary witness. “King of the Jews” is more accurate than they know; providence harvests even blasphemous hail without authoring their spit and blows.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat the mockery as empty theater. Arminians typically stress free, accountable scorn and a sincere call to true homage; Reformed readers typically stress that God overrules their parody into witness while leaving the soldiers fully guilty.

Matthew 27:30

30And they spit on him and took the reed and struck him on the head.

Arminian

Spit and strikes with the reed: contempt turns physical. The “scepter” becomes a club. Matthew does not soften the violence.

Arminian tenderness and warning: the suffering Christ still summons trust, not revenge fantasies. Grace enables following a struck Savior; joining the spit—in word or heart—remains our responsible sin to confess and flee.

Reformed

Spit and blows land on the anointed head. The battalion’s sport intensifies before the march to Golgotha.

Reformed sobriety: every strike is unjust, and none escapes the Father’s knowledge. The Servant bears what sinners deal out; God’s purpose for atonement is not canceled by their cruelty.

Matthew 27:31

31And when they had mocked him, they stripped him of the robe and put his own clothes on him and led him away to crucify him.

Arminian

Mockery ends; His own clothes return; they lead Him away to crucify Him. The game is over; the execution remains. Jesus goes as the Lamb led, not as a failed revolutionary dragged by surprise.

Arminian pastoral close: receive the led Christ in living faith. Grace still offers this scorned King to sinners who turn from the battalion’s laughter to the Savior’s cross—freely, sincerely, responsibly.

Reformed

Robe off, own clothes on, led to crucify: the Passion moves from headquarters to the road. Mockery was a station, not the destination.

Reformed comfort: “led him away to crucify him” fulfills the delivering theme under God’s counsel and human guilt together. The church confesses a Lord who was led without the Father’s will being overturned, and without the soldiers’ sin being excused.

Key contrast Both see willing path and real guilt in the leading away. Arminians typically emphasize Jesus’ knowing self-giving and a sincere summons to trust the mocked Christ; Reformed readers typically emphasize that this leading advances the appointed crucifixion without making God the author of the battalion’s abuse.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
