Passage

Matthew 27

Verses 11–14 · ESV

Bound and delivered to Pilate (vv. 1–2), Jesus now stands before the Roman governor. Pilate asks about kingship; the chief priests and elders heap accusations; Jesus answers little, and the governor is greatly amazed.

Matthew 27:11

11Now Jesus stood before the governor, and the governor asked him, “Are you the King of the Jews?” Jesus said, “You have said so.”

Arminian

Jesus stands before Pilate as accused, not as agitator. The question is political: King of the Jews? His reply—“You have said so”—neither flatters Rome nor denies the truth of His identity.

Classical Arminians hear a willing witness under pressure. Grace that draws does not force a theatrical defense; Jesus speaks enough for faith to rest on, and leaves hearers responsible for what they do with that word.

Reformed

The King stands before a lesser throne. “You have said so” affirms without the swagger of earthly revolt. Matthew places Jesus inside Roman process so the cross will be public judgment, not private violence.

Reformed comfort: the Son’s quiet confession advances the Father’s purpose. Pilate’s question is real; Christ’s kingship is deeper than the governor can see, and providence is not stalled by the courtroom.

Key contrast Both confess Jesus’ true kingship under interrogation. Arminians typically stress His free, knowing witness and a sincere summons to receive that word; Reformed readers typically stress that this exchange unfolds under the Father’s appointed path to the cross without excusing Pilate’s later injustice.

Matthew 27:12

12But when he was accused by the chief priests and elders, he gave no answer.

Arminian

Accusation piles up; Jesus is silent. He will not trade charge for charge with leaders who have already resolved His death. Silence here is strength, not confusion.

Arminian pastoral accent: the Lamb yields Himself; hearers remain free and accountable. Grace invites trust in a Savior who refuses self-justifying sparring, not in a Messiah who wins by out-arguing envy.

Reformed

No answer meets their accusations. Isaiah’s pattern of the silent Servant sits behind the scene. Their words fly; His mission holds steady.

Reformed holding of both: their accusing is culpable, and God’s purpose moves forward. Silence does not mean defeat; it means the cross will answer more loudly than a courtroom rebuttal.

Matthew 27:13

13Then Pilate said to him, “Do you not hear how many things they testify against you?”

Arminian

Pilate presses: Do you not hear? The governor expects self-defense. Many testimonies hang in the air; Jesus still will not scramble to clear His name on their terms.

Arminian note: opportunity for a different reply remains real in the moment, yet Jesus chooses the path of the Passion. Grace still calls Pilate—and every hearer—to reckon with this righteous Man rather than manage a case file.

Reformed

Pilate hears volume; Jesus hears mission. The question assumes that quantity of charges should compel speech. It does not.

Reformed sobriety: human process keeps rolling while the appointed hour approaches. God is not the author of false testimony, yet this interrogation sits inside the delivering-up already named.

Matthew 27:14

14But he gave him no answer, not even to a single charge, so that the governor was greatly amazed.

Arminian

Not even to a single charge—total restraint. Pilate is greatly amazed; silence this complete is unnatural in a capital hearing. The Holy One will not scramble for acquittal by Rome’s rules.

Arminian pastoral close: amazement is not yet faith. Grace invites Pilate—and us—beyond curiosity to trust the silent Christ. Responsibility remains: do not merely marvel at composure; receive the King.

Reformed

Amazement marks the governor; resolve marks the Son. Single-charge silence fulfills the Servant’s way and clears the path toward the sentence the priests desire.

Reformed comfort: Christ is not cornered into muteness by chance. His restraint serves redemption’s timetable. Pilate’s wonder does not save him; the church confesses a Lord whose silence was purposeful under the Father’s will.

Key contrast Both refuse a panicked, self-saving Jesus. Arminians typically emphasize knowing self-restraint and a sincere call beyond mere amazement to faith; Reformed readers typically emphasize purposeful silence under providence that advances the cross without excusing false accusers or a wavering judge.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
