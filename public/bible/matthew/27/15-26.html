Sample passage

Matthew 27

Verses 15–26 · ESV

After Jesus’ silence before Pilate (vv. 11–14), the feast custom of releasing a prisoner sets the stage. Pilate offers Barabbas or Jesus; the crowd, stirred by the priests, chooses Barabbas and demands crucifixion. Pilate washes his hands, the people accept bloodguilt, and Jesus is scourged and delivered to be crucified.

Matthew 27:15

15Now at the feast the governor was accustomed to release for the crowd any one prisoner whom they wanted.

Arminian

Custom opens a door: one prisoner freed by crowd choice. Pilate will use the feast mercy as political relief. Human preference will soon speak with terrible clarity.

Classical Arminians note real alternatives placed before the people. Grace that has shone in Jesus’ works still leaves the crowd responsible for whom they ask to release.

Reformed

Feast custom and Roman pragmatism meet. A release is offered; the Passion will still reach its appointed end.

Reformed holding: the custom is historical and the choice will be culpable. God’s purpose is not hostage to a governor’s holiday habit, yet the people’s request will be their own.

Matthew 27:16

16And they had then a notorious prisoner called Barabbas.

Arminian

Barabbas is notorious—known for crime, not for righteousness. The contrast with Jesus is already sharp before names are put to a vote.

Arminian pastoral edge: sinners often prefer a familiar rebel to a holy King. Grace still invites a better choice; notoriety is not destiny until the heart refuses the Christ.

Reformed

A notorious man stands ready for exchange. Scripture sets the stage without romance: the guilty and the Innocent will be weighed by a crowd.

Reformed sobriety: providence can use even a notorious name in the drama of substitution themes the gospel will later proclaim, without making God the author of the crowd’s envy-driven demand.

Matthew 27:17

17So when they had gathered, Pilate said to them, “Whom do you want me to release for you: Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ?”

Arminian

Pilate frames the choice: Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ. The title “Christ” is spoken in public. A real alternative is set before gathered people.

Arminian accent: the gospel often arrives as decision under light. Grace enables a turn toward Jesus; refusing Him for a notorious man remains accountable freedom, not forced fate.

Reformed

Two names, one release. Pilate hopes the crowd will choose the less dangerous option for his politics.

Reformed reading: the question is genuine in history, and the outcome will serve ends deeper than Pilate intends. “Christ” is named; human preference will soon reveal the heart.

Key contrast Both take the offered choice seriously. Arminians typically stress a sincere alternative under light and accountable preference; Reformed readers typically stress that this public choice still falls inside God’s redemptive purpose without excusing the crowd’s coming rejection.

Matthew 27:18

18For he knew that it was out of envy that they had delivered him up.

Arminian

Pilate knows the motive: envy. Delivery to Rome was not about justice. Even a pagan governor can see the priests’ heart.

Arminian warning: light resisted hardens into envy that hands over the righteous. Grace had confronted these leaders; their envy is chosen malice, not unavoidable script.

Reformed

Envy is named as the engine of the handover. Matthew lets Pilate’s insight stand: this is not a clean prosecution.

Reformed clarity: envy is their sin, and providence still carries Jesus toward the cross. God is not envious; He overrules envious hands without authoring their spite.

Matthew 27:19

19Besides, while he was sitting on the judgment seat, his wife sent word to him, “Have nothing to do with that righteous man, for I have suffered much because of him today in a dream.”

Arminian

A dream interrupts the judgment seat: that righteous man. Pilate’s wife warns him off. Another witness to Jesus’ innocence reaches the governor from his own household.

Arminian pastoral note: God can warn through unexpected voices. Grace enlarges the summons to do right; Pilate remains responsible for whether he heeds the warning or manages a riot instead.

Reformed

Even a dream testifies: righteous. Providence presses Pilate with knowledge beyond the priests’ brief.

Reformed comfort: innocence is underscored from multiple angles. The warning is real; it does not overturn the appointed path, and it leaves Pilate without excuse before God.

Matthew 27:20

20Now the chief priests and the elders persuaded the crowd to ask for Barabbas and destroy Jesus.

Arminian

Leaders persuade; the crowd is moved to ask for Barabbas and destroy Jesus. Influence is real, and so is the crowd’s yielding. Destruction is the aim, not mere detention.

Arminian application: religious persuasion can steer people away from the Christ. Grace still calls each hearer not to outsource conscience to envious counsel.

Reformed

Persuasion does its work. Priests and elders shape the request that will seal Jesus’ death sentence in popular cry.

Reformed holding of both: demagoguery is culpable, and the Passion advances. God is not the author of their persuasion; He is not surprised by its success.

Matthew 27:21

21The governor again said to them, “Which of the two do you want me to release for you?” And they said, “Barabbas.”

Arminian

Asked again, they answer: Barabbas. The notorious prisoner is preferred to the Christ. The choice is spoken in the open.

Arminian grief and urgency: under light, people can still choose badly. Grace had made Jesus known; selecting Barabbas is responsible rejection, not a forced line in a play.

Reformed

Barabbas is named by the crowd. The exchange motif sharpens: the guilty freed, the Righteous retained for death.

Reformed sobriety: their word is their own, and Scripture’s larger purpose is not thwarted. Substitution shadows gather without making God the author of their preference.

Matthew 27:22

22Pilate said to them, “Then what shall I do with Jesus who is called Christ?” They all said, “Let him be crucified!”

Arminian

Pilate pushes the question onto them: What shall I do with Jesus? The answer is unanimous and brutal: Crucify Him. “Christ” is still the title in the question; the cross is the crowd’s reply.

Arminian pastoral edge: every generation still faces what to do with Jesus. Grace invites faith; shouting for crucifixion shows how far refusal can go when envy leads.

Reformed

Crucify Him—Rome’s death for the One called Christ. The crowd supplies the sentence Pilate hesitates to own alone.

Reformed confidence: the cry is wicked, and the cross is the appointed altar. Human voices demand what God’s counsel had long framed; guilt and purpose meet without confusion of agents.

Key contrast Both hear a real rejection of the Christ. Arminians typically stress accountable persuasion and free, tragic preference against grace; Reformed readers typically stress that this cry advances the foreordained cross without excusing priests, crowd, or Pilate.

Matthew 27:23

23And he said, “Why? What evil has he done?” But they shouted all the more, “Let him be crucified!”

Arminian

Pilate asks for evil done; none is named. Volume replaces evidence: they shouted all the more. Injustice grows louder when it cannot answer “Why?”

Arminian warning: resisted light often doubles down. Grace still held out a righteous Man; the shout for the cross is willful, not compelled by missing facts alone.

Reformed

No evil is produced; the cry intensifies. Pilate’s question exposes the emptiness of the charge even as the sentence hardens.

Reformed reading: innocence is publicly unrefuted, yet the path to Golgotha continues. Providence does not need a true crime from Jesus; it overrules a false demand.

Matthew 27:24

24So when Pilate saw that he was gaining nothing, but rather that a riot was beginning, he took water and washed his hands before the crowd, saying, “I am innocent of this man's blood; see to it yourselves.”

Arminian

Fear of riot outweighs justice. Water and a speech—“I am innocent”—cannot cleanse a judge who yields the righteous. “See to it yourselves” echoes the priests’ cold counsel to Judas.

Arminian pastoral cut: ceremony is not repentance. Grace called Pilate to protect the innocent; washing hands while delivering Jesus is abdication, and he remains responsible.

Reformed

Handwashing is theater under pressure. Pilate claims innocence while surrendering bloodguilt management to the crowd.

Reformed sobriety: no water absolves him. God weighs governors as well as crowds. The Son will still be delivered; Pilate’s ritual does not rewrite providence or erase his compromise.

Matthew 27:25

25And all the people answered, “His blood be on us and on our children!”

Arminian

The people accept bloodguilt in dreadful words. Matthew records a corporate cry, not a racial curse for all time; it is this crowd’s self-imprecation in this hour.

Arminian pastoral care: treat the line with gravity and without antisemitic misuse. Grace still offers that same blood for cleansing to Jew and Gentile who trust Christ; this verse warns against owning rejection, not against any ethnicity.

Reformed

They claim the blood. The statement is historically specific and morally heavy. Scripture neither softens their words nor licenses hatred of later generations.

Reformed gospel irony: the blood they invoke in guilt is the blood God will offer for sinners. Providence turns even this cry toward a mercy they did not mean—without canceling the seriousness of their rejection that day.

Matthew 27:26

26Then he released for them Barabbas, and having scourged Jesus, delivered him to be crucified.

Arminian

Barabbas walks free; Jesus is scourged and delivered to crucifixion. The exchange is complete in history: the notorious released, the Christ handed over to death.

Arminian close: see both justice denied and love given. Grace calls us to the scourged Savior in living faith—not to envy’s verdict, and not to despair like Judas, but to trust the One delivered for sinners.

Reformed

Release, scourging, delivering: the sentence moves. “Delivered… to be crucified” continues the handing-over theme under God’s purpose and human guilt together.

Reformed comfort: Barabbas’s freedom is a dark mirror of a brighter exchange the gospel proclaims. Christ is delivered according to plan; scourging is real; the church confesses a Lord who was wounded by wicked hands inside the Father’s will.

Key contrast Both see a real exchange and real guilt. Arminians typically emphasize accountable choice for Barabbas and a sincere call to trust the delivered Christ; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s sovereign purpose in the delivering-over without making God the author of envy, riot, or Pilate’s cowardice.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
