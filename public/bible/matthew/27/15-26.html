Passage

Matthew 27

Verses 15–26 · WEB · Voices

After Jesus’ silence before Pilate (vv. 11–14), the feast custom of releasing a prisoner sets the stage. Pilate offers Barabbas or Jesus; the crowd, stirred by the priests, chooses Barabbas and demands crucifixion. Pilate washes his hands, the people accept bloodguilt, and Jesus is scourged and delivered to be crucified.

Matthew 27:15

15Now at the feast the governor was accustomed to release to the multitude one prisoner whom they desired.

Arminian

Custom opens a door: one prisoner freed by crowd choice. Pilate will use the feast mercy as political relief. Human preference will soon speak with terrible clarity.

Arminians note alternatives placed before the people. Grace that has shone in Jesus’ works still leaves the crowd responsible for whom they ask to release.

Reformed

Feast custom and Roman pragmatism meet. A release is offered; the Passion will still reach its appointed end.

The custom is historical and the choice will be culpable. God’s purpose is not hostage to a governor’s holiday habit, yet the people’s request will be their own.

Matthew 27:16

16They had then a notable prisoner called Barabbas.

Arminian

Barabbas is notorious—known for crime, not for righteousness. The contrast with Jesus is already sharp before names are put to a vote.

Sinners often prefer a familiar rebel to a holy King. Grace still invites a better choice; notoriety is not destiny until the heart refuses the Christ.

Reformed

A notorious man stands ready for exchange. Scripture sets the stage without romance: the guilty and the Innocent will be weighed by a crowd.

Providence can use even a notorious name in the drama of substitution themes the gospel will later proclaim, without making God the author of the crowd’s envy-driven demand.

Matthew 27:17

17When therefore they were gathered together, Pilate said to them, “Whom do you want me to release to you? Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ?”

Arminian

Pilate frames the choice: Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ. The title “Christ” is spoken in public. A free alternative is set before gathered people.

The gospel often arrives as decision under light. Grace enables a turn toward Jesus; refusing Him for a notorious man remains accountable freedom, not forced fate.

Reformed

Pilate offers two names for one release. Pilate hopes the crowd will choose the less dangerous option for his politics.

The question is authentic in history, and the outcome will serve ends deeper than Pilate intends. “Christ” is named; human preference will soon reveal the heart.

Key contrast Both take the offered choice seriously. Arminians press a sincere alternative under light and accountable preference; Reformed readers typically emphasize that this public choice still falls inside God’s redemptive purpose without excusing the crowd’s coming rejection.

Matthew 27:18

18For he knew that because of envy they had delivered him up.

Arminian

Pilate knows the motive: envy. Delivery to Rome was not about justice. Even a pagan governor can see the priests’ heart.

Light resisted hardens into envy that hands over the righteous. Grace had confronted these leaders; their envy is chosen malice, not unavoidable script.

Reformed

Envy is named as the engine of the handover. Matthew lets Pilate’s insight stand: this is not a clean prosecution.

Envy is their sin, and providence still carries Jesus toward the cross. God is not envious; He overrules envious hands without authoring their spite.

Matthew 27:19

19While he was sitting on the judgment seat, his wife sent to him, saying, “Have nothing to do with that righteous man, for I have suffered many things today in a dream because of him.”

Arminian

A dream interrupts the judgment seat: that righteous man. Pilate’s wife warns him off. Another witness to Jesus’ innocence reaches the governor from his own household.

God can warn through unexpected voices. Grace enlarges the summons to do right; Pilate remains responsible for whether he heeds the warning or manages a riot instead.

Reformed

Even a dream testifies: righteous. Providence presses Pilate with knowledge beyond the priests’ brief.

Comfort: innocence is underscored from multiple angles. The warning is no empty threat; it does not overturn the appointed path, and it leaves Pilate without excuse before God.

Matthew 27:20

20Now the chief priests and the elders persuaded the multitudes to ask for Barabbas and destroy Jesus.

Arminian

Leaders persuade; the crowd is moved to ask for Barabbas and destroy Jesus. Influence is no illusion, and so is the crowd’s yielding. Destruction is the aim, not mere detention.

Religious persuasion can steer people away from the Christ. Grace still calls each hearer not to outsource conscience to envious counsel.

Reformed

Persuasion does its work. Priests and elders shape the request that will seal Jesus’ death sentence in popular cry.

Demagoguery is culpable, and the Passion advances. God is not the author of their persuasion; He is not surprised by its success.

Matthew 27:21

21But the governor answered them, “Which of the two do you want me to release to you?” They said, “Barabbas!”

Arminian

Asked again, they answer: Barabbas. The notorious prisoner is preferred to the Christ. The choice is spoken in the open.

Under light, people can still choose badly. Grace had made Jesus known; selecting Barabbas is responsible rejection, not a forced line in a play.

Reformed

Barabbas is named by the crowd. The exchange motif sharpens: the guilty freed, the Righteous retained for death.

Their word is their own, and Scripture’s larger purpose is not thwarted. Substitution shadows gather without making God the author of their preference.

Matthew 27:22

22Pilate said to them, “What then shall I do to Jesus who is called Christ?” They all said to him, “Let him be crucified!”

Arminian

Pilate pushes the question onto them: What shall I do to Jesus? The answer is unanimous and brutal: Crucify Him. “Christ” is still the title in the question; the cross is the crowd’s reply.

Every generation still faces what to do with Jesus. Grace invites faith; shouting for crucifixion shows how far refusal can go when envy leads.

Reformed

Crucify Him—Rome’s death for the One called Christ. The crowd supplies the sentence Pilate hesitates to own alone.

The cry is wicked, and the cross is the appointed altar. Human voices demand what God’s counsel had long framed; guilt and purpose meet without confusion of agents.

Key contrast Both hear a rejection of the Christ. Arminians stress accountable persuasion and free, tragic preference against grace; Calvinists press that this cry advances the foreordained cross without excusing priests, crowd, or Pilate.

Matthew 27:23

23But the governor said, “Why? What evil has he done?” But they cried out exceedingly, saying, “Let him be crucified!”

Arminian

Pilate asks for evil done; none is named. Volume replaces evidence: they cried out exceedingly, saying. Injustice grows louder when it cannot answer “Why?”

Resisted light often doubles down. Grace still held out a righteous Man; the shout for the cross is willful, not compelled by missing facts alone.

Reformed

No evil is produced; the cry intensifies. Pilate’s question exposes the emptiness of the charge even as the sentence hardens.

Innocence is publicly unrefuted, yet the path to Golgotha continues. Providence does not need a true crime from Jesus; it overrules a false demand.

Matthew 27:24

24So when Pilate saw that nothing was being gained, but rather that a disturbance was starting, he took water and washed his hands before the multitude, saying, “I am innocent of the blood of this righteous person. You see to it.”

Arminian

Fear of riot outweighs justice. Water and a speech—“I am innocent”—cannot cleanse a judge who yields the righteous. “See to it” echoes the priests’ cold counsel to Judas.

Ceremony is not repentance. Grace called Pilate to protect the innocent; washing hands while delivering Jesus is abdication, and he remains responsible.

Reformed

Handwashing is theater under pressure. Pilate claims innocence while surrendering bloodguilt management to the crowd.

No water absolves him. God weighs governors as well as crowds. The Son will still be delivered; Pilate’s ritual does not rewrite providence or erase his compromise.

Matthew 27:25

25All the people answered, “May his blood be on us and on our children!”

Arminian

The people accept bloodguilt in dreadful words. Matthew records a corporate cry, not a racial curse for all time; it is this crowd’s self-imprecation in this hour.

Treat the line with gravity and without antisemitic misuse. Grace still offers that same blood for cleansing to Jew and Gentile who trust Christ; this verse warns against owning rejection, not against any ethnicity.

Reformed

They claim the blood. The statement is historically specific and morally heavy. Scripture neither softens their words nor licenses hatred of later generations.

The blood they invoke in guilt is the blood God will offer for sinners. Providence turns even this cry toward a mercy they did not mean—without canceling the seriousness of their rejection that day.

Matthew 27:26

26Then he released Barabbas to them, but Jesus he flogged and delivered to be crucified.

Arminian

Barabbas walks free; Jesus is scourged and delivered to crucifixion. The exchange is complete in history: the notorious released, the Christ handed over to death.

See both justice denied and love given. Grace calls us to the scourged Savior in living faith—not to envy’s verdict, and not to despair like Judas, but to trust the One delivered for sinners.

Reformed

Release, scourging, delivering: the sentence moves. “Delivered… to be crucified” continues the handing-over theme under God’s purpose and human guilt together.

Barabbas’s freedom is a dark mirror of a brighter exchange the gospel proclaims. Christ is delivered according to plan; scourging is not imaginary; the church confesses a Lord who was wounded by wicked hands inside the Father’s will.

Key contrast Both see an exchange and guilt. Arminians emphasize accountable choice for Barabbas and a sincere call to trust the delivered Christ; Reformed Christians highlight God’s sovereign purpose in the delivering-over without making God the author of envy, riot, or Pilate’s cowardice.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What earlier interpreters on each side wrote about Matthew 27:15–26.

Arminian Notable voices

“At every feast - Every year, at the feast of the passover. Mark xv, 6; Luke xxiii, 17; John xviii, 39. He knew that for envy they had delivered him - As well as from malice and revenge; they envied him, because the people magnified him. They all say, Let him be crucified - The punishment which Barabbas had deserved: and this probably made them think of it.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 27:15–26)

Reformed Notable voices

“Now the governor was wont at the festival Here is described to us, on the one hand, the insatiable cruelty of the priests, and, on the other, the furious obstinacy of the people; for both must have been seized with astonishing madness, when they were not satisfied with conspiring to put to death an innocent man, if they did not also, through hatred of him, release a robber. Thus wicked men after having once begun to fall, are driven headlong by Satan, so that they shrink from no crime, however detestable, but, blinded and stupefied, add sin to sin.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 27:15–26)
