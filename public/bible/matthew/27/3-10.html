Passage

Matthew 27

Verses 3–10 · ESV · Voices

As Jesus is condemned and handed to Pilate, Judas the betrayer is seized with remorse, returns the silver, and dies in despair; the priests buy the potter’s field, and Matthew records Scripture’s fulfillment.

Matthew 27:3

3Then when Judas, his betrayer, saw that Jesus was condemned, he changed his mind and brought back the thirty pieces of silver to the chief priests and the elders,

Arminian

Judas “changed his mind”—remorse rises when condemnation is irreversible. He returns the silver, but returning coin is not the same as returning to Christ in living faith.

Classical Arminians warn carefully here: grace had surrounded Judas; resisting that light ends in horror, not in forced damnation. Remorse can be real and still fall short of repentance that trusts the Savior. Flee early; do not manage betrayal until morning.

Reformed

The betrayer sees the fruit of his bargain and recoils. Scripture does not paint Judas as a puppet without conscience; it shows a guilty man under the weight of what he has done.

Reformed holding of both: Judas’s remorse is his own, and providence still advances. The silver returns; the path to the cross does not reverse. Sin remains sin; God’s purpose is not undone by a traitor’s panic.

Matthew 27:4

4saying, “I have sinned by betraying innocent blood.” They said, “What is that to us? See to it yourself.”

Arminian

Judas confesses: innocent blood. The priests shrug: What is that to us? Religious leaders who priced the Messiah now refuse pastoral care for the betrayer they used.

Arminian pastoral edge: true confession needs a merciful Christ, not cold religion. Grace still invites the sinner to Jesus; these priests point Judas to himself. “See to it yourself” is the counsel of despair, not the gospel.

Reformed

Innocent blood is named by the traitor himself. The council’s reply exposes hard hearts: they want the deed, not the burden of conscience.

Reformed sobriety: confession of fact is not yet faith. Judas speaks truth about Jesus’ innocence while priests wash their hands of pastoral duty. God’s justice will weigh both the betrayal and their callousness.

Key contrast Both treat Judas’s words as true about Jesus’ innocence and insufficient as saving turn. Arminians typically emphasize resisted grace and remorse short of faith in Christ; Reformed readers typically emphasize culpable despair under providence that still fulfills Scripture without excusing anyone.

Matthew 27:5

5And throwing down the pieces of silver into the temple, he departed, and he went and hanged himself.

Arminian

Silver hits the temple floor; Judas departs into death. Matthew records suicide without romance. Despair closes a path that confession alone did not open to hope.

Arminian warning and tenderness: the gospel still offers Christ to the guilty while breath remains; this verse shows how refusal and isolation can end. Do not hear fatalism—hear urgency. Turn to the living Lord, not merely away from the wages of sin.

Reformed

The end is tragic and sober. Scripture neither softens Judas’s guilt nor treats his death as atonement. Throwing silver cannot cleanse bloodguilt.

Reformed pastoral care: the cross ahead is the only sufficient covering. Judas’s hanging stands as a dread sign that remorse without Christ destroys. God’s purpose for the Passion continues; this death does not redeem him.

Matthew 27:6

6But the chief priests, taking the pieces of silver, said, “It is not lawful to put them into the treasury, since it is blood money.”

Arminian

Scruple without repentance: blood money must not enter the treasury, yet the same hands bargained for it. They parse lawfulness while ignoring the greater law of justice and mercy.

Arminian application: religion can be meticulous about coins and careless about Christ. Grace invites integrity of heart, not selective piety that keeps the ledger clean while the conscience stays hard.

Reformed

Irony sharpens: they admit the silver is blood money, then manage the optics. Legal concern for the treasury does not equal fear of God.

Reformed reading names hypocrisy under providence. Even their scruple will fund a field that memorializes guilt. God is not mocked; their “lawful” workaround still serves ends they do not control.

Matthew 27:7

7So they took counsel and bought with them the potter's field as a burial place for strangers.

Arminian

Counsel again: buy a field for strangers’ graves. Blood money becomes real estate. The priests tidy the problem without seeking the innocent One they condemned.

Arminian note: human planning after sin often aims at covering shame, not at conversion. Grace still calls leaders and people alike to Christ; a burial plot for others cannot bury their own accountability.

Reformed

The potter’s field enters the story by their counsel. Strangers will lie in ground purchased with the price of betrayal.

Reformed comfort in a dark scene: God weaves even this purchase into fulfillment. Their transaction is real and culpable; Scripture’s word is not thwarted by it.

Matthew 27:8

8Therefore that field has been called the Field of Blood to this day.

Arminian

Memory sticks: Field of Blood. The community names what the silver meant. History itself becomes a warning label.

Arminian pastoral use: God often lets consequences teach. Grace invites a better name—cleansed by Christ—yet this field reminds hearers that bargaining away the Lord leaves a stain people can still point to.

Reformed

“To this day” seals the memorial. Providence leaves a geographical sermon: blood money bought a graveyard.

Reformed sobriety: God records guilt in the landscape of the story. The field does not save; it testifies. The blood that cleanses will be Christ’s, shed openly, not silver thrown in the temple.

Matthew 27:9

9Then was fulfilled what had been spoken by the prophet Jeremiah, saying, “And they took the thirty pieces of silver, the price of him on whom a price had been set by some of the sons of Israel,

Arminian

Matthew hears Scripture fulfilled in the price set on Jesus. Prophetic word and human bargaining meet. The church is taught to read the Passion as promise kept, not chaos.

Arminian accent: fulfillment does not erase responsible actors. Sons of Israel set a price; grace had called them to honor the Messiah. Scripture coming true still leaves room—and obligation—for faith rather than sale.

Reformed

Fulfillment language governs the scene. The thirty pieces and the priced One stand under God’s prior word (drawing Zechariah’s imagery under Jeremiah’s prophetic frame as Matthew presents it).

Reformed confidence: the cross-path is not improvisation. Human pricing of the Shepherd fulfills what God spoke; Judas and the priests remain guilty agents inside that fulfillment.

Key contrast Both confess Scripture fulfilled in the priced Messiah. Arminians typically stress that fulfillment coexists with accountable refusal of grace; Reformed readers typically stress God’s sure word governing the price and the field without making God the author of betrayal.

Matthew 27:10

10and they gave them for the potter's field, as the Lord directed me.”

Arminian

The silver buys the field “as the Lord directed.” Matthew closes the Judas subplot by tying dirty money to directed fulfillment. The Lord is not surprised.

Arminian pastoral close: let the warning land, then look to Christ. Grace still offers the innocent blood Judas named—now about to be shed—for sinners who turn in living faith, not despair.

Reformed

“As the Lord directed me” places the purchase under divine speech. The potter’s field is not a loose end; it is word accomplished.

Reformed doxology in mourning: God directed the prophetic line that lands here. Betrayal’s wages mark a field; the Son goes to Pilate. Providence and gospel hold together for the church’s comfort and fear of the Lord.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on judas the betrayer is seized with remorse, returns the silver—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 27:3–10.

Arminian Notable voices

“Then Judas seeing that he was condemned - Which probably he thought Christ would have prevented by a miracle. - How easily could they digest innocent blood! It is not lawful (say they) to put it into the treasury - But very lawful to slay the innocent!”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 27:3–10)

Reformed Notable voices

“The high priest, with his council, after having examined him at an unseasonable hour of the night, finally resolve, at sunrise, to place him at the bar of the governor. By so doing, they observe the form of judicial proceedings, that they may not be suspected of undue haste, when they run to Pilate at an unusually early hour, as usually happens in cases of tumult.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 27:3–10)
