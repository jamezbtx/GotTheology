Sample passage

Matthew 27

Verses 1–2 · ESV

After the night trial and Peter’s denial (ch. 26), morning comes. The chief priests and elders take formal counsel to put Jesus to death, then bind Him and deliver Him over to Pontius Pilate the governor.

Matthew 27:1

1When morning came, all the chief priests and the elders of the people took counsel against Jesus to put him to death.

Arminian

Morning light does not soften their aim. The council that plotted by stealth now takes counsel to kill. Matthew shows deliberation, not a momentary flare of anger.

Classical Arminians hear accountable choice under revealed light. These leaders had heard Jesus’ teaching and seen His works; grace that invites does not erase the gravity of resolving His death. Human freedom remains real—and responsible.

Reformed

The Passion advances on schedule: morning, counsel, death as the stated end. What they feared to do during the feast (26:5) they now pursue in daylight.

Reformed readers hold both: culpable counsel and sovereign purpose. God is not the author of their murderous intent, yet this morning sits inside the delivering-up Jesus already named. Wicked hands move; the Father’s plan is not derailed.

Matthew 27:2

2And they bound him and led him away and delivered him over to Pilate the governor.

Arminian

Bound, led, delivered: the Holy One is handed to Rome. Religious leaders refuse to finish the deed alone; they enlist Gentile power. Jesus does not break free; He goes as the Lamb who yields Himself.

Arminian pastoral accent: the delivering is real history and a sincere call to trust the suffering Christ. Grace enables faith that follows a bound Savior rather than a Messiah of force. Hearers remain responsible for how they receive this path.

Reformed

“Delivered… over to Pilate” fulfills the language of handing over. Bound yet not defeated, Jesus enters the governor’s jurisdiction so the cross will be public and juridical, not a quiet lynching.

Reformed comfort: the Son is delivered according to purpose, not as a victim of chance. Pilate’s authority is real; Christ’s mission is deeper. The church confesses a Lord who was led away without His Father’s will being overturned.

Key contrast Both refuse a helpless accident. Arminians typically stress Jesus’ knowing self-giving and a sincere summons to trust the bound Christ; Reformed readers typically stress that this delivering-over advances the Father’s appointed purpose without excusing the priests’ guilt.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
