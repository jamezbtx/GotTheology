Passage

Matthew 27

Verses 32–44 · WEB · Voices

On the way to Golgotha Simon of Cyrene is compelled to carry the cross. Jesus is crucified between robbers, titled King of the Jews, and mocked by passersby and leaders who dare Him to come down from the cross.

Matthew 27:32

32As they came out, they found a man of Cyrene, Simon by name, and they compelled him to go with them, that he might carry his cross.

Arminian

Simon is compelled—unexpected cross-bearing. Ordinary travel meets the Passion. Matthew hints that following may mean shouldering what we did not choose.

Grace can conscript us into witness we did not plan. Simon’s compulsion is distinct from saving faith, yet it pictures discipleship’s cost under a sincere call to take up the cross.

Reformed

Simon of Cyrene enters by compulsion. Providence places a name on the road to Golgotha.

Even a forced carrier serves the hour God appointed. The cross moves forward; human schedules yield.

Matthew 27:33

33When they came to a place called “Golgotha”, that is to say, “The place of a skull,”

Arminian

The name itself preaches death. The King arrives at the place of execution.

Geography of shame becomes the place of redemption offered to all who will believe. Grace meets us at the skull-hill, not only in safe rooms.

Reformed

The Place of a Skull: appointed ground. Matthew translates so every reader feels the meaning.

This place is not accidental estate. The Son dies where the Father’s purpose and human cruelty intersect.

Matthew 27:34

34they gave him sour wine to drink mixed with gall. When he had tasted it, he would not drink.

Arminian

Wine mixed with gall—offered, tasted, refused. He will not dull the cup the Father gives.

Jesus chooses clarity in his suffering. Jesus chooses full obedience over numbed escape. Grace invites disciples to sober faithfulness, not evasion of costly love.

Reformed

He tastes and refuses. The Servant will drink another cup—wrath and obedience—without anesthetic escape.

Providence and piety meet in his refusal. Christ remains alert under the load He came to bear.

Matthew 27:35

35When they had crucified him, they divided his clothing among them, casting lots,

Arminian

Crucified—stated without spectacle prose. Then lots for garments: casual greed under a dying King.

Hearts can gamble while Christ bleeds. Grace still calls soldiers and spectators to look up and trust the Crucified.

Reformed

Garments divided by lots: Psalm 22’s shadow over the scene. Scripture’s pattern holds as nails hold.

Fulfillment in the details. Wicked casting of lots; sure Word of God.

Matthew 27:36

36and they sat and watched him there.

Arminian

They sit and watch. Guarding a crucified man as if He might still threaten their control.

Watching without worship is possible. Grace invites a different watch—beholding the Lamb in faith.

Reformed

Watch kept at the cross. Rome stays on duty; heaven’s purpose is not on pause.

Human vigilance cannot prevent what God has willed—nor can it stop the later empty tomb.

Matthew 27:37

37They set up over his head the accusation against him written, “THIS IS JESUS, THE KING OF THE JEWS.”

Arminian

The charge is the truth in hostile ink: King of the Jews. Empire means it as crime; the church hears confession.

Receive the title in faith. Grace enables allegiance to this King on the cross, not only a king of success.

Reformed

Title over His head: This is Jesus, the King of the Jews. Providence puts truth on the placard of scorn.

The charge is the gospel’s claim. God overrules the paperwork of death.

Matthew 27:38

38Then there were two robbers crucified with him, one on his right hand and one on the left.

Arminian

Numbered with transgressors—robbers on right and left. Holiness hung between guilt.

He is near sinners in death so sinners may draw near in life. Grace offers company with the Crucified to all who turn.

Reformed

Two robbers frame the King. Isaiah’s “with the transgressors” stands embodied.

Substitution’s shape appears even in the seating chart of Golgotha. The Innocent among the guilty by purpose.

Matthew 27:39

39Those who passed by blasphemed him, wagging their heads

Arminian

Passersby deride and wag heads. Casual blasphemy on the road. Suffering becomes entertainment.

Do not pass by the cross with a shrug. Grace calls for repentance, not tourism at Calvary.

Reformed

Head-wagging scorn fulfills the pattern of the righteous sufferer. Scripture foresaw contempt.

Their derision is free and guilty; God’s Word is not surprised.

Matthew 27:40

40and saying, “You who destroy the temple and build it in three days, save yourself! If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross!”

Arminian

Save yourself… come down: the old wilderness temptation in new clothes. Sonship is challenged to prove itself by escape.

Arminian readers find a temptation rejected. Grace strengthens fidelity. True Sonship stays on the cross for others rather than climbing down for applause.

Reformed

“If you are the Son of God” echoes the tempter. The demand is spectacle, not faith.

The Son stays. Saving Himself would abandon the purpose. He saves others by not coming down.

Key contrast Both refuse a cross-evacuating Messiah. Arminians press Jesus’ willing perseverance under a sincere call to trust that path; Reformed readers press the Father’s will that the Son remain until the work is finished.

Matthew 27:41

41Likewise the chief priests also mocking with the scribes, the Pharisees, and the elders, said,

Arminian

Religious leaders join the mockery. Shepherds become scorners at the cross.

Office does not equal faith. Grace still invites leaders to repent; mocking Christ while citing Scripture is deadly.

Reformed

Priests, scribes, elders: the council’s voice continues at Golgotha. Guilt is not only Gentile.

Leaders’ mockery is culpable; God’s purpose uses even their taunts as dark backdrop to the true King.

Matthew 27:42

42“He saved others, but he can’t save himself. If he is the King of Israel, let him come down from the cross now, and we will believe in him.

Arminian

He saved others—they admit the healings—yet demand self-salvation as the price of belief. Conditional faith that requires a climbed-down Christ is not faith.

Grace offers the Christ who stays. Believe the saving of others that cost Him Himself; do not bargain for a safer King.

Reformed

The taunt contains unintended gospel: He saved others. Inability to “save Himself” in their sense is the means of saving many.

They speak better than they know. The King of Israel reigns from the tree.

Matthew 27:43

43He trusts in God. Let God deliver him now, if he wants him; for he said, ‘I am the Son of God.’”

Arminian

They throw His trust back at Him. Psalm-shaped scorn questions whether the Father desires the Son.

When trust is mocked, grace still sustains. Jesus’ claimed Sonship is not disproved by the cross; it is displayed there.

Reformed

“He trusts in God” becomes a jeer. Heaven will answer—not by immediate rescue, but by resurrection.

Comfort: the Father does desire the Son. The deliverance comes on the third day, not on their timetable.

Matthew 27:44

44The robbers also who were crucified with him cast on him the same reproach.

Arminian

Even the dying revile Him (Matthew’s summary). Isolation is total at this moment.

Luke will show one robber turn; Matthew stresses the depth of scorn first. Grace can still reach a reviler before the end—do not delay.

Reformed

Robbers join the reviling. The circle of contempt closes.

The Servant is despised by all strata. Yet this is the hour appointed. Isolation on the cross is not abandonment of the plan.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

On the crucified King, scorn at Golgotha, and salvation that stays on the cross, a few classic voices from each tradition.

Arminian Notable voices

“Jesus Christ, the Savior of the world, died for all men and for every man, so that he has obtained for them all, by his death on the cross, redemption and the forgiveness of sins; yet that no one actually enjoys this forgiveness of sins except the believer.”
The Remonstrants · Five Articles of Remonstrance, Art. II (1610)
“The plain scriptural notion of justification is pardon, the forgiveness of sins… that act of God the Father whereby, for the sake of the propitiation made by the blood of his Son, he showeth forth his righteousness… by the remission of the sins that are past.… Plead thou, singly, the blood of the covenant, the ransom paid for thy proud, stubborn, sinful soul.”
John Wesley · Sermon 5, “Justification by Faith”

Reformed Notable voices

“But let us believe that Christ, though he might easily have done it, did not immediately deliver himself from death, but it was because he did not wish to deliver himself. And why did he for the time disregard his own safety, but because he cared more about the salvation of us all?”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 27:42)
“The Lord Jesus, by his perfect obedience and sacrifice of himself, which he through the eternal Spirit once offered up unto God, hath fully satisfied the justice of his Father; and purchased not only reconciliation, but an everlasting inheritance in the kingdom of heaven, for all those whom the Father hath given unto him.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · 8.5 (1647)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
