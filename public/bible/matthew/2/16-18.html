Sample passage

Matthew 2

Verses 16–18 · ESV

When Herod realizes the Magi will not return, he erupts in rage and slaughters the boys of Bethlehem; Matthew frames the horror with Jeremiah’s lament of Rachel weeping for her children.

Matthew 2:16

16Then Herod, when he saw that he had been tricked by the wise men, became furious, and he sent and killed all the male children in Bethlehem and in all that region who were two years old or under, according to the time that he had ascertained from the wise men.

Arminian

Matthew refuses to soften tyranny. Herod’s fury becomes policy: children die because a king will not yield the throne to God’s Christ.

Classical Arminians insist God is not the author of this evil. Human agents freely choose massacre; the gospel still names the crime. Grief and moral clarity belong together under a God who hates injustice and invites the world to the true King.

Reformed

The slaughter is wholly Herod’s wickedness—and it occurs within a providence that has already relocated the Messiah. God restrains evil from destroying the Son without making Herod’s sin His own doing.

Reformed pastoral hold: sovereignty is not a shrug over corpses. It is the assurance that tyrants do not finally write history, while their guilt remains undiluted.

Key contrast Both refuse to blame God for Herod’s murder. Arminians typically stress creaturely freedom and God’s non-causation of sin; Reformed theology typically stresses meticulous providence that limits evil’s reach to the Christ-child while never making God the author of Herod’s rage.

Matthew 2:17

17Then was fulfilled what was spoken by the prophet Jeremiah:

Arminian

Fulfillment language here is lament, not celebration. Scripture gives words for tears when the powerful crush the weak.

Shared pastoral gift: God does not gaslight the suffering. He speaks through Jeremiah so the church can weep truthfully and still hope.

Reformed

Even atrocity sits inside a canonical frame Matthew recognizes. “Fulfilled” does not mean God delighted in the killing; it means the pattern of Israel’s sorrow finds a terrible echo in Messiah’s infancy.

God’s Word interprets history without excusing its villains.

Matthew 2:18

18“A voice was heard in Ramah, weeping and loud lamentation, Rachel weeping for her children; she refused to be comforted, because they are no more.”

Arminian

Rachel’s voice refuses cheap comfort. Matthew honors mothers who will not pretend the loss is small. The incarnation enters a world of real blood and real cries.

Arminian hope does not silence lament: the same God who draws near in Christ will one day wipe tears—and until then, the church may weep with those who weep while pointing to the surviving King.

Reformed

Jeremiah 31 holds both exile’s grief and the promise of new covenant hope beyond it. Matthew cites the grief now; the wider chapter’s consolation still belongs to God’s people in Christ.

Reformed comfort in darkness: Rachel’s tears are heard, and they are not the last word in the story God is writing through His Son.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
