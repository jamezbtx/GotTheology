Passage

Matthew 12

Verses 43–45 · WEB · Voices

After the demand for a sign and Jesus’ reply from Jonah, Nineveh, and the queen of the South (12:38–42), He warns with a brief parable: an unclean spirit departs, returns to an empty house, and the last state is worse—applied to “this evil generation.”

Matthew 12:43

43“When an unclean spirit has gone out of a man, he passes through waterless places seeking rest, and doesn’t find it.

Arminian

Jesus pictures spiritual conflict: an unclean spirit leaves a person, wanders waterless places seeking rest, and finds none. Departure is not yet deliverance completed; restlessness marks the unclean realm.

Those in Wesley’s line hear a pastoral caution against settling for partial relief. Grace can free; hearers remain responsible to seek more than a temporary emptying. Shared confession: unclean spirits are adversaries, and leaving alone is not the same as being filled with Christ.

Reformed

The saying continues Jesus’ confrontation of an evil generation. Unclean spirits are neither myth nor equal rivals to God; they seek rest and find none apart from reclaiming a human “house.”

Christ’s word discloses the unseen with authority. The narrative is not speculative demonology for curiosity; it prepares a warning about empty religion under the sovereign Lord who alone binds the strong man and keeps His own.

Key contrast Both take the unclean spirit’s departure as incomplete as a final safety. Arminians commonly accent the hearer’s responsibility to pursue lasting freedom in Christ rather than temporary relief; Reformed readers commonly accent Christ’s authoritative unveiling of spiritual reality and the emptiness of rest apart from His keeping.

Matthew 12:44

44Then he says, ‘I will return into my house from which I came;’ and when he has come back, he finds it empty, swept, and put in order.

Arminian

The spirit claims the person as “my house” and returns to find it empty, swept, and put in order—cleaned yet unoccupied. Moral tidying without a new Master leaves the door open.

Reformation of habits is good and grace-enabled, but an empty house is perilous. Responsibility includes not only sweeping out evil but welcoming Christ to dwell. Both sides agree: outward order without inward occupancy is not saving rest.

Reformed

“Empty, swept, and put in order” is the tragedy of religion without regeneration’s Resident. The house looks improved; it is still vacant—and claimed by the returning enemy as home.

Moralism can rearrange furniture while the heart remains unindwelt by the Spirit. Christ’s people are temples He fills; empty order is not the gospel’s goal but a setup for worse bondage when the unclean returns.

Key contrast Both refuse to confuse a swept life with a saved life. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled responsibility to receive Christ as Occupant, not merely to reform behavior; Reformed interpreters highlight that without the Spirit’s indwelling the ordered house remains empty—and still claimed by the adversary.

Matthew 12:45

45Then he goes and takes with himself seven other spirits more evil than he is, and they enter in and dwell there. The last state of that man becomes worse than the first. Even so will it be also to this evil generation.”

Arminian

Seven spirits more evil enter and dwell; the last state becomes worse than the first. Jesus applies the picture to “this evil generation”—those who saw His works, demanded signs, and still refused Him.

Classical Arminians urge urgent response while light remains: partial reform that rejects the greater-than-Jonah leaves people more hardened. Grace invites true repentance and Spirit-filled dwelling; refusing that invitation is chosen peril. Shared hope and warning: only Christ occupying the heart prevents a worse end.

Reformed

The climax is judicial: worse last state, and so also for this evil generation. Exposure to Jesus without faith does not leave people neutral; light resisted deepens darkness.

The warning exposes empty religion’s trajectory under God’s just verdict, while the gospel answers the empty house—Christ binds the strong man, the Spirit indwells, and the last state of those in Him is not worse but secured. Assurance rests in the Occupant, not in a swept interior.

Key contrast Both take the worse last state with full seriousness for a generation that refuses Christ. Arminians commonly accent responsible repentance and the summons to be filled by grace rather than left empty; Reformed readers commonly accent judicial hardening after resisted light and the comfort that only Spirit-indwelt union with Christ secures a last state that is not worse.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Voices from both camps

Older voices from both traditions on Matthew 12:43–45.

Arminian Notable voices

“But how dreadful will be the consequence of their rejecting me? When the unclean spirit goeth out - Not willingly, but being compelled by one that is stronger than he. He walketh - Wanders up and down; through dry places - Barren, dreary, desolate; or places not yet watered with the Gospel: Seeking rest, and findeth none - How can he, while he carries with him his own hell?”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 12:43–45)

Reformed Notable voices

“But when the unclean spirit hath gone out. He speaks of scribes and hypocrites of a similar character, who, despising the grace of God, enter into a conspiracy with the devil. Against such persons he pronounces that punishment which their ingratitude deserves.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 12:43–45)
