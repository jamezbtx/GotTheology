Sample passage

Matthew 12

Verses 33–37 · ESV

After the Beelzebul charge and the warning about blasphemy against the Spirit (12:22–32), Jesus presses the Pharisees with tree-and-fruit logic: speech flows from the heart’s treasure, and on judgment day careless words will justify or condemn.

Matthew 12:33

33“Either make the tree good and its fruit good, or make the tree bad and its fruit bad, for the tree is known by its fruit.

Arminian

Jesus refuses a split verdict: good tree with bad fruit, or bad tree with good fruit. Nature and produce belong together; the tree is known by what it yields.

Classical Arminians hear both diagnosis and summons. Grace can renew a diseased tree so that good fruit becomes real; hearers remain responsible to stop pretending that bitter speech somehow grows from a healthy root. Shared ground: fruit reveals the tree.

Reformed

The proverb is christological confrontation, not a gardening tip. Make the tree good or make it bad—Jesus binds fruit to nature and exposes their charge against Him as incoherent.

Reformed accent: trees produce after their kind. Recognition by fruit is God’s appointed honesty about the heart. The saying prepares the sharper word that follows: evil mouths cannot suddenly speak as if the tree were sound.

Key contrast Both confess that fruit reveals the tree. Arminians commonly stress grace that can renew the tree and the hearer’s responsibility to bear good fruit; Reformed readers commonly stress that fruit exposes a nature already good or bad under God’s truthful verdict.

Matthew 12:34

34You brood of vipers! How can you speak good, when you are evil? For out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks.

Arminian

“Brood of vipers” is prophetic severity, not casual insult. The question is piercing: How can you speak good, when you are evil? Mouths overflow from the heart’s abundance.

Arminian pastoral reading: words are never neutral chatter; they disclose what we treasure. Grace enables a new heart and summons repentance of venomous speech. Shared confession: the tongue tells the truth about the inner life.

Reformed

Jesus names their lineage of deceit and states the impossibility: evil persons do not suddenly produce good speech. Abundance of the heart is the spring; the mouth is the stream.

Reformed diagnosis: total corruption of the heart explains religious lies as natural fruit, not accidental slips. Only a heart remade can overflow with good; until then, vipers’ speech matches vipers’ nature.

Key contrast Both tie speech to the heart’s overflow. Arminians typically emphasize responsible repentance and grace-enabled change of heart and tongue; Reformed readers typically emphasize the moral inability of an evil heart to speak truly good words until God remakes the spring.

Matthew 12:35

35The good person out of his good treasure brings forth good, and the evil person out of his evil treasure brings forth evil.

Arminian

Treasure language deepens the point: what is stored within is what is brought forth. Good and evil persons differ not only in occasional acts but in the storehouse from which words and deeds keep coming.

Classical Arminians urge examination of the treasure—and trust the Spirit who can fill the heart with good. Responsible discipleship means refusing to excuse evil speech as “just words” while claiming a good store within.

Reformed

Bring forth is harvest language. Good treasure yields good; evil treasure yields evil. The person and the store match.

Reformed comfort and cut: regeneration plants a good treasure; apart from it, the store remains evil. Christ’s people still fight remaining sin, yet the new heart’s true produce is good—and evil speech remains a serious alarm about what is still treasured.

Matthew 12:36

36I tell you, on the day of judgment people will give account for every careless word they speak,

Arminian

“I tell you” lifts the saying to solemn authority. Judgment day includes accounting for every careless word—not only spectacular blasphemies, but throwaway speech that reveals the heart.

Arminian urgency: take the warning to heart while mercy still invites change. Grace does not make words trivial; it makes repentance possible and obliges careful, loving speech before the Judge who hears all.

Reformed

The day of judgment is certain, and the docket includes careless words. Nothing spoken is lost to God’s memory or justice.

Reformed sobriety: final accountability is personal and exhaustive. The gospel does not cancel the warning; it drives sinners to the Christ who covers guilt and forms a people whose tongues He claims—still knowing that every word will be weighed.

Matthew 12:37

37for by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned.”

Arminian

Words will justify or condemn—not as a rival gospel of works, but as evidence that reveals faith or unbelief, a good treasure or an evil one. Speech participates in the verdict that discloses the person.

Classical Arminians hear a sober call: receive justifying grace in Christ, and let the mouth agree with a renewed heart. Shared hope: the same Lord who warns also saves; careless tongues need both pardon and transformation.

Reformed

Justify and condemn here are judicial outcomes tied to words as public fruit of the heart—not a second way of salvation beside Christ’s righteousness. On that day, speech will corroborate who we are before God.

Reformed gospel: sinners are justified by grace through faith in Christ alone; yet Jesus’ word stands—words will witness for or against us. Assurance rests in Him who justifies the ungodly, while holiness of speech marks those He has claimed.

Key contrast Both take the verdict by words with full seriousness. Arminians commonly accent words as grace-enabled evidence and responsible fruit that will justify or condemn in the open; Reformed readers commonly accent that justification is grounded in Christ while words serve as judicial evidence of the heart—comfort for the justified, warning where speech still exposes an evil treasure.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
