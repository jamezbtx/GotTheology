Sample passage

Matthew 12

Verses 1–8 · ESV

Following Jesus’ invitation to the weary (11:28–30), Matthew narrates a Sabbath controversy: the disciples pluck grain, the Pharisees accuse, and Jesus answers from Scripture—David, the temple priests, Hosea’s “mercy,” and His own lordship of the Sabbath.

Matthew 12:1

1At that time Jesus went through the grainfields on the Sabbath. His disciples were hungry, and they began to pluck heads of grain and to eat.

Arminian

The scene is ordinary and human: hunger on a holy day. The disciples’ need is not staged rebellion; Matthew invites sympathy before the dispute begins.

Classical Arminians gladly confess the Sabbath as God’s good gift, not a trap. Grace does not despise created need; the Lord who calls the weary also walks with hungry disciples. Human responsibility includes receiving rest and bread without turning either into a weapon.

Reformed

Matthew places the controversy under Jesus’ timing—“at that time”—so the Sabbath debate unfolds inside the Messiah’s mission, not as a freestanding ethics puzzle.

Reformed readers hear providence and christology together: the Lord of the Sabbath is already present with His people in their hunger. The day of rest is God’s gift; later verses will show that gift interpreted by the Son who gives it.

Matthew 12:2

2But when the Pharisees saw it, they said to him, “Look, your disciples are doing what is not lawful to do on the Sabbath.”

Arminian

The charge is sharp: “not lawful.” Zeal for the Sabbath is not evil in itself, but here it hardens into accusation that ignores hunger and misreads the Law’s intent.

Arminian pastoral reading warns against a conscience that polices others more eagerly than it shows mercy. Law remains good; weaponized tradition is not. Hearers are summoned to examine whether their “lawful” has become loveless.

Reformed

The Pharisees speak as guardians of orthodoxy, yet their “Look” is prosecution, not patient reading of Scripture. Matthew exposes how human fences can eclipse God’s purpose.

Reformed preaching honors the Law while diagnosing a deeper bondage: apart from Christ’s light, even religious seriousness can condemn the guiltless. The issue is not whether God gave Sabbath; it is whether these interpreters know the Giver.

Matthew 12:3

3He said to them, “Have you not read what David did when he was hungry, and those who were with him:

Arminian

Jesus answers with Scripture, not scoffing. “Have you not read” assumes the Pharisees know the text and calls them to read it again—David’s hunger and companionship included.

Classical Arminians treasure this pattern: God enlightens through His Word, and hearers remain responsible to receive that light. Scripture interprets Scripture so that mercy is not set against the Law but recovered from within it.

Reformed

Christ’s first move is canonical: He binds them to what is written. David’s story is not a loophole Jesus invents; it is revelation they should already know.

Reformed emphasis: the Son authoritatively opens the Scriptures. Illumination is needed because knowledge of the letter can still miss the Lord’s intent. The same God who gave the Law now speaks in the Son who fulfills it.

Matthew 12:4

4how he entered the house of God and ate the bread of the Presence, which it was not lawful for him to eat nor for those who were with him, but only for the priests?

Arminian

David’s act was irregular by ceremonial rule, yet Scripture does not brand him as lawless for meeting life-threatening need. Jesus legitimates compassion without mocking holiness.

Arminians often stress responsible, grace-enabled discernment: ritual boundaries matter, but they serve life and worship, not starve the needy. The gospel frees consciences from harsh casuistry while still honoring God’s house.

Reformed

Jesus grants the ceremonial point—“not lawful” in the ordinary sense—then shows that Scripture itself narrates an exception under David’s need. The Bible is not flat legalism.

Reformed reading: Christ teaches how to weigh Scripture’s own priorities. The bread of the Presence pointed beyond itself; hunger did not nullify holiness, but holiness was never meant to crush the anointed king and his men.

Matthew 12:5

5Or have you not read in the Law how on the Sabbath the priests in the temple profane the Sabbath and are guiltless?

Arminian

A second witness from the Law: temple service continues on the Sabbath, and the priests are “guiltless.” Work that serves God’s presence is not Sabbath-breaking.

Classical Arminian application often lands on vocation and mercy: some labors of love and worship rightly continue on the day of rest. Believers are called to wise obedience—resting from self-serving toil while remaining free to do what God’s house and neighbor require.

Reformed

Jesus argues from the Law against a narrow reading of the Law. Priestly “profanation” is holy work; therefore Sabbath holiness has an inner logic the Pharisees have missed.

Reformed confession hears continuity: God Himself ordered temple service that relativizes simplistic bans. The Sabbath is not abolished here; it is interpreted by the God who gave it, preparing the claim that Someone greater than the temple is present.

Matthew 12:6

6I tell you, something greater than the temple is here.

Arminian

Jesus claims a presence that surpasses the temple’s glory. If priestly service made Sabbath labor guiltless, how much more may His disciples walk freely with the One who is greater?

Arminians confess Christ’s supreme authority and the wideness of His merciful reign. The greater-than-temple claim invites every hearer to come to Him—not to a building alone, but to the living Lord who grants rest and calls for trusting discipleship.

Reformed

“I tell you” is royal speech. The temple was the apex of Israel’s worship geography; Jesus places Himself above it. Christology governs Sabbath ethics.

Reformed comfort: if the greater Temple is here, God’s people are not left to negotiate rest by human tradition. The Son’s presence defines holiness. Worship centers on Him who fulfills what the temple foreshadowed.

Matthew 12:7

7And if you had known what this means, ‘I desire mercy, and not sacrifice,’ you would not have condemned the guiltless.

Arminian

Hosea’s word (cf. Matt 9:13) exposes the heart of the Law: God wants mercy that reflects His own character, not sacrifice emptied of love. The disciples are “guiltless”; condemnation here is false judgment.

Classical Arminians often hear a summons to responsible compassion—grace enables and obliges mercy toward the needy. Assurance grows as we trust the God who delights in mercy; that same mercy shapes how we treat others, without turning the gospel into harsh ritual scorekeeping.

Reformed

Ignorance of Hosea is not a trivia gap; it is a spiritual failure that produces condemnation of the innocent. God prioritizes steadfast love over empty cult.

Reformed pastoral note: mercy is not antinomian license; it is the Law’s own center as fulfilled in Christ. Those justified by grace are freed from condemning spirits and taught to walk in the mercy they have received—guiltless in Him who covers them.

Key contrast Both refuse ritualism without mercy. Arminians commonly accent the believer’s grace-enabled responsibility to practice mercy and refrain from condemning; Reformed readers commonly accent that Hosea’s word exposes hearts and that the guiltless find safety in the Christ who fulfills mercy—assurance that then produces merciful living.

Matthew 12:8

8For the Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath.”

Arminian

The climax is personal authority: the Son of Man rules the Sabbath. Rest is not abolished; it is claimed by Jesus, who gives true rest to those who come to Him (11:28–30).

Classical Arminians receive this as gospel lordship that frees from Pharisaic bondage and calls for willing discipleship under Christ’s gentle yoke. The day remains gift; the Lord invites trust and obedience without fear-driven legalism.

Reformed

“Lord of the Sabbath” is a divine claim in narrative form. The Sabbath’s Maker stands among His people; therefore He alone interprets and fulfills the day.

Reformed gospel: Christ’s lordship is not a loophole for lawlessness but the ground of freedom—He kept, fulfilled, and reorients the Sabbath for His church. Rest is received from the reigning Son, not earned by fence-building.

Key contrast Shared confession: Jesus has real authority over the Sabbath. Arminians often emphasize His lordship as liberating call into free, grace-enabled obedience; Reformed theology often emphasizes His sovereign right to define Sabbath for His people as the One who effectually gives the rest the day promised.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
