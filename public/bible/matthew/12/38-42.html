Sample passage

Matthew 12

Verses 38–42 · ESV

After tree-and-fruit warnings and the accounting of careless words (12:33–37), some scribes and Pharisees answer Jesus by asking for a sign. He refuses spectacle to unbelief, promises only the sign of Jonah—His own death and rising—and warns that Nineveh and the queen of the South will condemn this generation, for something greater than Jonah and Solomon is here.

Matthew 12:38

38Then some of the scribes and Pharisees answered him, saying, “Teacher, we wish to see a sign from you.”

Arminian

The request sounds pious—“Teacher”—yet it comes after miracles already given and after hearts exposed by Jesus’ words. Wanting a sign can be honest seeking; here it functions as delay and demand, as if more proof could replace repentance.

Classical Arminians honor genuine longing for confirmation of faith, while warning that curiosity can mask resistance. Grace has already shone in deeds and speech; hearers remain responsible to receive the light they have rather than bargain for a sign on their own terms.

Reformed

Matthew’s “Then” ties the request to the preceding conflict. Scribes and Pharisees answer Him not with repentance but with a test: show us a sign. Religious titles do not equal faith.

Reformed reading: unbelief often asks for more evidence while ignoring the evidence already given. The Lord is not on trial before human courts; He will answer on His own terms, exposing an evil generation that seeks spectacle instead of submission.

Matthew 12:39

39But he answered them, “An evil and adulterous generation seeks for a sign, but no sign will be given to it except the sign of the prophet Jonah.

Arminian

Jesus names the generation “evil and adulterous”—spiritual unfaithfulness, not a slur for its own sake. Sign-seeking here is covenant betrayal: wanting God on demand while refusing the One already present.

Arminian pastoral urgency: the door is not slammed forever in this saying, but the path is narrowed. No custom sign will be granted to excuse unbelief; the sign of Jonah will be given. Hearers are summoned to repent and trust that appointed sign rather than negotiate for another.

Reformed

Christ diagnoses before He promises. An adulterous generation seeks a sign—not because God has been silent, but because hearts are faithless. The “no sign… except” is both judgment and mercy: He will not feed their demand, yet He will give Jonah’s sign.

Reformed accent: God remains free. Signs are His gift, not man’s leverage. The prophet Jonah is already Scripture’s type; Jesus binds this generation to that divine pattern rather than to their preferred miracle.

Key contrast Both refuse treating God as a vendor of proofs. Arminians commonly stress the hearer’s responsibility to repent and receive the sign God gives rather than demand another; Reformed readers commonly stress God’s sovereign freedom to withhold spectacle and appoint only the sign He chooses for an adulterous generation.

Matthew 12:40

40For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.

Arminian

Jesus treats Jonah’s three days as real history that prefigures His own burial and rising. The great sign is not fireworks in the sky but the Son of Man in the heart of the earth—and, by implication, His vindication beyond it.

Classical Arminians preach this as gospel invitation: the crucified and risen Lord is the sign God offers to all. Faith is responsible trust in that finished work; rejection of the resurrection sign is not a lack of evidence so much as a refusal of the Savior Scripture announced.

Reformed

Typology and christology meet: Jonah’s entombed ordeal points forward; the Son of Man will fulfill it in death and burial. “Will be” is certain prophecy from the Lord Himself.

Reformed comfort: God writes the sign into redemptive history before the demanders ask. The resurrection is not one option among miracles; it is the appointed proof of the Messiah, accomplished by the Father’s power according to the Scriptures—sufficient for faith, devastating to willful unbelief.

Key contrast Both confess the resurrection as the God-appointed sign of Jonah. Arminians often emphasize that this sign is sincerely offered and that hearers remain accountable to believe it; Reformed theology often emphasizes that God Himself appoints and accomplishes this sign as certain prophecy—proof that creates and confirms faith rather than a spectacle humans control.

Matthew 12:41

41The men of Nineveh will rise up at the judgment with this generation and condemn it, for they repented at the preaching of Jonah, and behold, something greater than Jonah is here.

Arminian

Gentile Nineveh repented at Jonah’s preaching; this generation has Someone greater than Jonah and still resists. At the judgment those repentant outsiders will stand as witnesses against privileged unbelief.

Arminian application: greater light brings greater responsibility. If pagans turned at a lesser prophet’s word, how urgent is repentance when the greater Preacher stands among us? Grace enables and calls; delay is dangerous when “something greater… is here.”

Reformed

Jesus grants historicity to Nineveh’s repentance and presses eschatological seriousness: they will rise and condemn. Privilege without faith is worse than hearing less and obeying more.

Reformed christology: “something greater than Jonah is here” is not mere comparison of talent but the presence of the Lord to whom Jonah pointed. Judgment exposes hearts that refused the greater One; the same Lord who warns also saves those He draws to Himself.

Key contrast Both take Nineveh’s repentance and the “greater than Jonah” claim with full weight. Arminians commonly accent privilege and responsibility—greater revelation obliges repentance now; Reformed readers commonly accent that the greater One is present by God’s purpose, so refusal is culpable hardness against the Christ Scripture promised, not a neutral wait for more light.

Matthew 12:42

42The queen of the South will rise up at the judgment with this generation and condemn it, for she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon, and behold, something greater than Solomon is here.”

Arminian

A second Gentile witness: the queen traveled from the ends of the earth for Solomon’s wisdom. This generation will not travel even the short distance of faith to the Wisdom standing before them—Someone greater than Solomon.

Classical Arminians hear a warm summons: seek Christ more eagerly than she sought Solomon. Wisdom is offered; hearers must come. Shared hope with Reformed confession: the greater-than-Solomon Lord still receives those who come to hear and believe.

Reformed

Solomon’s wisdom was proverbial gift from God; Jesus claims to surpass it in person. The queen’s costly journey condemns local indifference. Ends-of-the-earth hunger shames near-at-hand refusal.

Reformed gospel: Christ is Wisdom incarnate, greater than Israel’s wisest king. Judgment will vindicate Gentile seekers and expose covenant presumption. The church’s comfort is not that we outsmart Solomon, but that the greater Solomon has come—and effectually gathers a people who hear His voice.

Key contrast Both honor the queen’s seeking and Christ’s supremacy over Solomon. Arminians typically emphasize the responsible call to come and hear the greater Wisdom with eager faith; Reformed readers typically emphasize that the greater-than-Solomon One is God-with-us whose wisdom judges unbelief and gathers those the Father gives Him.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
