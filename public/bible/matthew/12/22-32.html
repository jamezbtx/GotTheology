Passage

Matthew 12

Verses 22–32 · ESV

After Sabbath controversy and Isaiah’s Servant song (12:1–21), Matthew narrates a Spirit-worked deliverance: Jesus heals a demon-oppressed man who was blind and mute. Crowds wonder about the Son of David; Pharisees attribute the exorcism to Beelzebul. Jesus answers with kingdom logic, the strong man bound, and a solemn warning about blasphemy against the Spirit.

Matthew 12:22

22Then a demon-oppressed man who was blind and mute was brought to him, and he healed him, so that the man spoke and saw.

Arminian

Compassion opens the unit: a man crushed by demonic oppression—blind and mute—is brought to Jesus and healed so that he speaks and sees. Deliverance is not abstract; sight and speech return as gifts of mercy.

Classical Arminians gladly confess Christ’s real power over evil and the human act of bringing the sufferer near. Grace meets helplessness; hearers are summoned to carry the broken to the Lord who still frees, without treating darkness as stronger than the Savior who heals.

Reformed

Matthew’s “Then” continues the Servant’s gentle yet powerful mission (12:15–21). The Spirit-anointed Servant does not merely teach; He undoes the devil’s grip so that a silenced, sightless man is restored.

Reformed comfort: Christ’s kingdom advances by sovereign rescue. Blindness and muteness under oppression are not final; the Lord who will soon speak of the Spirit’s work already displays that Spirit’s mercy in flesh-and-blood healing.

Matthew 12:23

23And all the people were amazed, and said, “Can this be the Son of David?”

Arminian

Amazement rises into a messianic question: “Can this be the Son of David?” The crowd senses Davidic hope without yet settling into firm confession. Wonder is a doorway, not yet discipleship.

Arminian pastoral reading honors this openness: God enlightens through mighty works, and people remain responsible to move from stunned curiosity toward trusting recognition of the Christ. Light invites a real answer—will we receive the Son of David, or only marvel and drift?

Reformed

The people’s question is the right category—Messiah from David’s line—yet it hangs unfinished. Matthew lets amazement stand before the Pharisees’ darker verdict.

Reformed emphasis: revelation presses a decision. Signs do not automatically create saving faith; they expose hearts. The same works that awaken hope in some will soon be blasphemed by others, under the God who opens eyes or leaves the resistant in their chosen darkness.

Matthew 12:24

24But when the Pharisees heard it, they said, “It is only by Beelzebul, the prince of demons, that this man casts out demons.”

Arminian

Hearing the Son-of-David murmur, the Pharisees answer with a settled smear: Jesus casts out demons only by Beelzebul, prince of demons. Mercy is relabeled as satanic. This is not honest doubt; it is willful misnaming of good.

Classical Arminians warn that light resisted can harden into active slander of grace. Religious leaders remain accountable for calling the Spirit’s liberating work the devil’s. Pastoral sobriety: examine whether zeal for “orthodoxy” has begun to hate what God plainly does in Christ.

Reformed

Matthew juxtaposes the crowd’s wonder with the Pharisees’ verdict. They do not deny the exorcism; they reassign its source to Beelzebul. Evil here is interpretive—stealing glory from God by crediting Satan.

Reformed diagnosis: apart from regenerating grace, even religious seriousness can invert reality. The plot to destroy Jesus (12:14) now speaks theology. What follows will name this as blasphemy against the Spirit—not a slip of the tongue, but a judicial hardness that calls holy power unclean.

Matthew 12:25

25Knowing their thoughts, he said to them, “Every kingdom divided against itself is laid waste, and no city or house divided against itself will stand.

Arminian

Jesus knows their thoughts before they finish speaking. He answers with public reason: a divided kingdom falls; a divided city or house cannot stand. Logic itself unmasks the charge as absurd.

Arminians treasure this pattern: Christ engages conscience with truth hearers can grasp. Grace does not bypass the mind; it summons responsible judgment. We are called to test accusations by coherence and Scripture, not by party loyalty.

Reformed

Omniscient insight meets forensic reply. Christ exposes the Pharisees’ hidden counsel and dismantles it with kingdom logic they cannot escape.

Reformed reading: the Son’s knowledge and wisdom are divine. He does not scramble for a defense; He reveals the bankruptcy of their thought-world. Truth stands because the Lord of truth speaks—and divided kingdoms, including Satan’s alleged self-war, cannot stand.

Matthew 12:26

26And if Satan casts out Satan, he is divided against himself. How then will his kingdom stand?

Arminian

The reductio is sharp: if Satan casts out Satan, his kingdom collapses. The Pharisees’ explanation requires the devil to sabotage his own rule—an incoherent story.

Classical Arminian application: God gives light sufficient to refute false charges, and hearers remain responsible to abandon absurd hostility. Honesty before evidence is part of repentance; clinging to a self-defeating narrative is chosen darkness, not forced ignorance.

Reformed

Jesus grants their premise only to destroy it. Satan has a kingdom; he does not dismantle it by casting himself out. The charge fails on its own terms.

Reformed emphasis: Christ speaks as One who knows the enemy’s realm and His own victory. The question “How then will his kingdom stand?” prepares the positive claim—Someone stronger is plundering Satan, not colluding with him.

Matthew 12:27

27And if I cast out demons by Beelzebul, by whom do your sons cast them out? Therefore they will be your judges.

Arminian

Jesus turns their standard back on them: if His exorcisms are Beelzebul’s work, by whom do “your sons” cast demons out? Consistency would condemn their own associates; therefore those sons become judges of the charge.

Arminians hear a call to fair-minded responsibility. Grace enables clear thinking; prejudice that excuses “our” works while damning Christ’s is exposed. Love of truth means applying one measure, not two.

Reformed

Christ uses an ad hominem that is just, not petty: their circle also practiced exorcism. Selective attribution reveals malice, not discernment.

Reformed pastoral note: the Judge of all already stands among them. Their “sons” will judge the hypocrisy of the charge—yet a deeper judgment follows when the Spirit’s work in Jesus is blasphemed. Consistency matters because God’s truth does.

Matthew 12:28

28But if it is by the Spirit of God that I cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you.

Arminian

The alternative is gospel: if Jesus casts out demons by the Spirit of God, then God’s kingdom has come upon them. The Spirit’s presence in Christ’s ministry means the reign of God has drawn near in saving power—and they must respond.

Classical Arminians stress this inaugurated kingdom as genuine offer and claim: the Spirit enables and summons repentance and faith. Kingdom arrival is not mere information; it places every hearer under responsibility to welcome the King rather than credit His works to hell.

Reformed

Pneumatology and eschatology meet: Spirit-worked exorcism means the kingdom “has come upon you.” God’s reign is not only future hope; it has invaded the present in the Son anointed by the Spirit.

Reformed confession: the kingdom’s coming is God’s sovereign act in Christ, not human achievement. Where the Spirit drives out demons through Jesus, God Himself has drawn near—comfort for the oppressed, and crisis for those who refuse the King.

Key contrast Both confess Spirit-empowered exorcism and real kingdom arrival in Jesus. Arminians commonly accent the kingdom’s coming as pressing a grace-enabled, responsible decision to receive the King; Reformed readers commonly accent that the kingdom has come upon them by God’s sovereign Spirit in Christ—an invasion that exposes hearts and effectually advances God’s saving reign.

Matthew 12:29

29Or how can someone enter a strong man’s house and plunder his goods, unless he first binds the strong man? Then indeed he may plunder his house.

Arminian

Jesus pictures Himself as the stronger One: Satan is the “strong man,” yet Christ binds him and plunders his house—freeing captives like the man just healed. Deliverance is conquest, not negotiation with darkness.

Arminian hope: the Stronger Man still liberates. Believers are called to trust His victory and participate in gathering the freed, confident that grace enables resistance to the devil without denying either human struggle or Christ’s decisive binding.

Reformed

The parable explains the exorcisms: Jesus has bound the strong man and now takes spoil. Satan’s house is being plundered because a greater power has already prevailed.

Reformed gospel: Christ’s binding is decisive. The church’s comfort is not that we outmuscle the devil, but that the Son has entered, bound, and despoiled. Every release of a captive displays the Stronger Man’s prior victory.

Matthew 12:30

30Whoever is not with me is against me, and whoever does not gather with me scatters.

Arminian

Neutrality collapses. Not being “with” Jesus is already “against” Him; not gathering with Him is scattering. The Beelzebul charge is not a third option—it is opposition dressed as discernment.

Classical Arminians hear a wide, urgent summons: grace calls every person into active allegiance—with Christ, gathering to Him. Hearers remain responsible to stop scattering by unbelief, slander, or pretended distance, and to join the Lord’s harvest work.

Reformed

Christ allows no safe middle. Allegiance is binary: with Him or against Him; gathering or scattering. The Pharisees’ “discernment” is exposed as hostility.

Reformed sobriety: the Son claims exclusive lordship over the conflict with Satan. Those effectually drawn gather with Him; those left in unbelief scatter—yet the call still rings out, and the church finds its identity in being on Christ’s side by grace alone.

Key contrast Shared confession: there is no neutral ground regarding Jesus. Arminians typically stress the responsible call to choose allegiance and gather with Him under enabling grace; Reformed theology typically stresses that true gathering is the fruit of Christ’s claim and the Spirit’s work—while unbelief’s “neutrality” is already opposition.

Matthew 12:31

31Therefore I tell you, every sin and blasphemy will be forgiven people, but the blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven.

Arminian

“Therefore” ties the warning to their charge: they have ascribed the Spirit’s work to Beelzebul. Jesus first widens mercy—“every sin and blasphemy will be forgiven”—then names the dread exception: blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven. This is not a random terror text; it answers settled, knowing rejection of the Spirit’s clear testimony in Christ.

Pastoral care (Arminian): do not scare tender consciences who fear a harsh thought has damned them—anxious longing for forgiveness is not this sin. Neither soft-pedal: persistent, willful repudiation of the Spirit’s convicting light, until one calls God’s holy work demonic, is real and ruinous. Flee to the Christ the Spirit honors while the day of grace remains.

Reformed

Christ’s “I tell you” is authoritative gospel and judgment together. The wideness of forgiveness (“every sin and blasphemy”) magnifies the gravity of the exception. Blasphemy against the Spirit, in this context, is the hardened verdict that the Spirit’s work in Jesus is satanic—a final-shaped refusal of the only light that saves.

Pastoral care (Reformed): the warning is severe because grace is real; it is not meant to torment believers who tremble at their sin. Those who mourn and seek Christ show the Spirit still at work. The unforgivable path is settled, judicial hardness that will not have the Spirit’s Christ—yet for the penitent, “every sin and blasphemy” still stands as promise.

Key contrast Both refuse scare-mongering and cheap comfort: the sin is real, and anxious seekers are not to be crushed. Arminians commonly describe blasphemy against the Spirit as persistent, responsible resistance to the Spirit’s prevenient and convicting grace until final repudiation; Reformed readers commonly describe it as settled hardness against the Spirit’s testimony to Christ, evidencing a heart not renewed—while assurance rests on the Spirit-wrought turning to Jesus, not on having never struggled.

Matthew 12:32

32And whoever speaks a word against the Son of Man will be forgiven, but whoever speaks against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven, either in this age or in the age to come.

Arminian

Jesus distinguishes speaking against the Son of Man—forgivable, as ignorance and later repentance show (cf. apostolic witness)—from speaking against the Holy Spirit, which will not be forgiven in this age or the age to come. The Spirit’s clear attestation of Jesus leaves no deeper appeal; to slander that light is to shut the door one refuses to open.

Classical Arminian pastoral word: keep the distinction. Many who once spoke wrongly of Christ later believed when the Spirit convicted them. But do not treat the Spirit’s witness as optional. While breath remains, turn toward the Son the Spirit glorifies; the warning’s “will not be forgiven” is meant to awaken, not to invent despair for those still willing to come.

Reformed

The contrast is pastoral and theological: a word against the Son of Man in His veiled humiliation may be forgiven; speaking against the Holy Spirit—rejecting His decisive witness to Jesus’ works—will not be forgiven in this age or the age to come. Eternity seals what settled unbelief has chosen against the Spirit’s light.

Reformed comfort and severity together: Peter cursed and denied, and was restored; the Pharisees’ Beelzebul verdict points toward a hardness that will not repent. If you fear you have committed this sin yet desire Christ’s mercy, that desire is not the profile of final blasphemy—come to the Son. The age to come holds no second gospel beyond the Spirit’s present testimony to Jesus.

Key contrast Both uphold Jesus’ solemn “not forgiven… in this age or in the age to come” without turning it into either panic or presumption. Arminians often emphasize ongoing responsibility to yield to the Spirit’s convicting work while forgiveness is offered; Reformed theology often emphasizes that final unforgiveness corresponds to a heart judicially set against the Spirit, while those effectually drawn to Christ find in the gospel’s wideness (vv. 31–32a) the assurance the warning itself cannot give.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the Spirit’s work, hardness of heart, and the unpardonable sin—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Jesus’ warning against blasphemy of the Spirit.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The blasphemy against the Spirit… is neither more nor less than the ascribing those miracles to the power of the devil, which Christ wrought by the power of the Holy Ghost… Whosoever speaketh against the Son of man… It shall be forgiven him—Upon his true repentance: But whosoever speaketh thus against the Holy Ghost, it shall not be forgiven, neither in this world nor in the world to come.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 12:31–32)

Reformed Notable voices

“Those persons sin and blaspheme against the Holy Spirit, who maliciously turn to his dishonor the perfections of God, which have been revealed to him by the Spirit… We need not then wonder, if for such sacrilege there is no hope of pardon; for they must be desperate who turn the only medicine of salvation into a deadly venom… God punishes contempt of his grace, by hardening the hearts of the reprobate, so that they never have any desire towards repentance.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 12:31)
“This much I do know about it; it is called a sin that is unto death, and as soon as ever a man commits it, a spiritual death steals over him, so that he never desires mercy, never is conscious of his guilt, and never wishes to find salvation by Jesus Christ… Now, my dear friends, you know that you have not come to that terrible state, because you are always restless about your soul’s salvation, and always wishing that by some means you might be saved.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · Sermon 2408, “Christ the Cure for Troubled Hearts”

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
