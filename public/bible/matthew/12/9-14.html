Passage

Matthew 12

Verses 9–14 · ESV

Continuing the Sabbath controversy (12:1–8), Jesus enters the synagogue, faces a trap question over a man with a withered hand, answers with mercy’s logic, heals, and draws the Pharisees into a plot to destroy Him.

Matthew 12:9

9He went on from there and entered their synagogue.

Arminian

Jesus does not retreat after the grainfield clash; He enters “their” synagogue—the place of worship where His critics hold influence. Presence itself is pastoral courage.

Classical Arminians notice the pattern of grace that keeps coming near: the Lord who claims the Sabbath also walks into contested space. Hearers are summoned to meet Him where Scripture is read, not only where comfort is easy.

Reformed

Matthew’s “from there” ties synagogue to the lordship claim just made. The Son of Man does not leave Sabbath theology in the field; He brings it into Israel’s gathered life.

Reformed reading hears providence and mission: Christ advances into the assembly God has ordered. The controversy that follows is not accidental; it unfolds under the Lord’s purposeful steps.

Matthew 12:10

10And a man was there with a withered hand. And they asked him, “Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath?”—so that they might accuse him.

Arminian

Need sits in plain sight—a withered hand—yet the question is prosecution, not pity. “Is it lawful?” is aimed to accuse, not to learn. Mercy is already being weighed against a trap.

Arminian pastoral warning: religious zeal can harden into a heart that uses a sufferer as bait. Grace enables real compassion; hearers remain responsible not to turn holy questions into weapons against love.

Reformed

Matthew exposes motive: the question is framed “so that they might accuse him.” Orthodoxy without charity becomes courtroom strategy.

Reformed diagnosis: apart from the Spirit’s softening, even Sabbath seriousness can prefer a charge over a cure. The Law’s Guardians miss the Law’s Giver standing before a broken man.

Matthew 12:11

11He said to them, “Which one of you who has a sheep, if it falls into a pit on the Sabbath, will not take hold of it and lift it out?

Arminian

Jesus argues from their own practice. A sheep in a pit draws immediate rescue; no one waits for Monday. He appeals to conscience they already live by.

Classical Arminians treasure this humane reasoning: God enlightens through common sense and Scripture together, and people remain accountable for the mercy they already show animals. The gospel does not invent compassion; it recovers it.

Reformed

Christ binds them with a reductio they cannot escape. Their own Sabbath mercy toward livestock condemns their hardness toward a man.

Reformed emphasis: the Son exposes inconsistency with authority. Revelation cuts through casuistry; the same Lord who gave the Sabbath also names what love requires when life is in the pit.

Matthew 12:12

12Of how much more value is a man than a sheep! So it is lawful to do good on the Sabbath.”

Arminian

Human worth towers over livestock. Therefore “it is lawful to do good”—healing is not Sabbath violation but Sabbath-fitting mercy. The day serves life, not cruelty.

Arminians hear a wide summons to responsible goodness: grace frees and obliges believers to relieve suffering without fear of loveless fences. Doing good is not optional charity; it is aligned with God’s heart.

Reformed

Jesus states the a fortiori and then the verdict: good-doing is lawful on the Sabbath. The day’s holiness is not emptied; it is filled with mercy that reflects the Creator’s valuation of persons.

Reformed comfort: Christ interprets the Law from within. The Sabbath is reclaimed for the good God always intended—rest that does not abandon the needy, under the Lord who defines the day.

Matthew 12:13

13Then he said to the man, “Stretch out your hand.” And the man stretched it out, and it was restored, healthy like the other.

Arminian

The command meets inability: a withered hand cannot stretch by nature. Yet Jesus speaks, the man acts, and restoration follows—healthy like the other. Word and response stand together.

Classical Arminians often see enabling grace in narrative form: the Lord’s call supplies what He requires, without erasing the man’s real stretching. Faith-shaped obedience is summoned and assisted; the healing magnifies both Christ’s power and a genuine human response.

Reformed

“Stretch out your hand” is an effective word. What the man could not do, he does under Christ’s command, and the hand is restored. Sovereign power and human action are not rivals here.

Reformed pastoral reading: the same Lord who creates life by speech restores the withered. The man’s stretching is real, yet it is fruit of the Savior’s effectual word—grace that heals, not mere advice waiting on unaided strength.

Key contrast Both confess Christ’s real power and the man’s real stretching. Arminians typically describe the command as grace that enables a free, assisted response; Reformed readers typically describe it as Christ’s effectual word that restores and draws forth the act it commands.

Matthew 12:14

14But the Pharisees went out and conspired against him, how to destroy him.

Arminian

Mercy answered with a murder plot. Seeing good done, they leave to conspire destruction. Hardness of heart is not a small theological footnote; it is tragic, responsible refusal of light.

Classical Arminians warn that grace resisted can deepen into hostility. The scene urges examination: will we celebrate Christ’s compassion, or cling to a righteousness that would rather silence Him than repent?

Reformed

Matthew closes the unit with conspiracy. The Sabbath’s Lord has done good; His opponents plan to destroy Him. Evil is unmasked by its hatred of mercy.

Reformed gospel sobriety: the cross is already foreshadowed. Yet the plot cannot overturn God’s purpose—the Son they seek to destroy is the One through whom salvation will be accomplished. Worship answers their conspiracy with trust in the Christ they reject.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
