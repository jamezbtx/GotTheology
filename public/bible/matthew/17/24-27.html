Passage

Matthew 17

Verses 24–27 · ESV

In Capernaum collectors ask Peter whether Jesus pays the two-drachma temple tax. Jesus teaches Peter that sons are free, yet provides a miraculous shekel from a fish’s mouth so as not to give offense—paying for Himself and for Peter.

Matthew 17:24

24When they came to Capernaum, the collectors of the two-drachma tax went up to Peter and said, “Does your teacher not pay the tax?”

Arminian

The question probes loyalty and practice. Collectors approach Peter, assuming the Teacher’s household pattern.

Arminian note: public witness includes ordinary obligations. How disciples answer shapes whether the gospel is needlessly obstructed.

Reformed

Temple tax collectors test the Capernaum base. The query is loaded: is Jesus inside Israel’s pious norms?

Reformed framing: Christ will answer on His terms. Identity as Son will interpret the tax before money appears.

Matthew 17:25

25He said, “Yes.” And when he came into the house, Jesus spoke to him first, saying, “What do you think, Simon? From whom do kings of the earth take toll or tax? From their sons or from others?”

Arminian

Peter answers yes; Jesus then catechizes him first—omniscience meeting the conversation Peter just had. The king-and-sons analogy prepares freedom.

Arminian pastoral feel: Jesus forms conscience by questions. Grace teaches freedom without training disciples in careless offense.

Reformed

Jesus initiates before Peter reports. Royal logic: kings tax others, not their own sons.

Reformed accent: the Son knows and leads. Theology of sonship precedes the coin; status in the kingdom interprets civic-religious dues.

Matthew 17:26

26And when he said, “From others,” Jesus said to him, “Then the sons are free.

Arminian

Peter supplies the obvious answer; Jesus draws the line: sons are free. Temple dues do not bind the Son as a mere subject.

Classical Arminians hear liberty in Christ without antinomian swagger. Freedom is real—and love will still choose careful paths for others’ sake.

Reformed

“The sons are free” is christological before it is ethical. Jesus claims household freedom under the Father’s kingship.

Reformed confession: the Son is not a debtor to the temple as outsiders are. Freedom is His by identity; any payment will be voluntary wisdom.

Key contrast Both affirm the Son’s freedom. Arminians typically move quickly to liberty exercised in love so as not to hinder others; Reformed readers typically root the freedom first in Christ’s unique sonship, then in wise condescension.

Matthew 17:27

27However, not to give offense to them, go to the sea and cast a hook and take the first fish that comes up, and when you open its mouth you will find a shekel. Take that and give it to them for me and for yourself.”

Arminian

Freedom yields to love: pay anyway, lest needless offense block the message. The fish-and-shekel sign supplies what Peter lacks and covers both Teacher and disciple.

Arminian application: Christian liberty is not a club. Grace frees us in Christ and frees us to serve—paying costs that protect witness and neighbor.

Reformed

“Not to give offense” governs the miracle. Providence puts a shekel in a fish’s mouth; Peter hooks provision for two.

Reformed wonder and ethics: the free Son chooses temporary accommodation, and the Father funds it. Christ’s kingdom advances without pointless scandal over a tax He did not owe.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
