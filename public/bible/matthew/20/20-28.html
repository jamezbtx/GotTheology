Passage

Matthew 20

Verses 20–28 · ESV · Voices

Right after the third passion prediction, the mother of Zebedee’s sons seeks thrones beside Jesus. He answers with the cup, the Father’s preparation of places, and a call to servant greatness—climaxing in the Son of Man who came to serve and give His life as a ransom for many.

Matthew 20:20

20Then the mother of the sons of Zebedee came up to him with her sons, and kneeling before him she asked him for something.

Arminian

Kneeling honor meets ambitious request. The mother approaches with James and John—devotion tangled with desire for status, and that right after talk of the cross.

Arminian pastoral note: grace draws people near Jesus, yet nearness does not instantly purify motives. Hearers remain responsible to examine what they ask when they kneel.

Reformed

Matthew places petition next to passion prediction. Even close followers can kneel and still aim at thrones.

Reformed realism: Christ will expose and reorder desire. The request that follows will meet His cup and the Father’s will, not flattery of family ambition.

Matthew 20:21

21And he said to her, “What do you want?” She said to him, “Say that these two sons of mine are to sit, one at your right hand and one at your left, in your kingdom.”

Arminian

Jesus draws the ask into the open: “What do you want?” The reply seeks the highest seats—right and left in the kingdom—for her sons.

Classical Arminians hear a real question that invites honesty. Grace can purify ambition when desire is named; the kingdom is not seized by maternal lobbying.

Reformed

The King questions before He answers. The request assumes places of honor are His to assign on demand.

Reformed accent: Christ will neither rubber-stamp nor crush with contempt alone—He will redefine greatness and refer the seats to the Father’s preparation.

Matthew 20:22

22Jesus answered, “You do not know what you are asking. Are you able to drink the cup that I am to drink?” They said to him, “We are able.”

Arminian

Ignorance is named: they ask for glory without grasping the cup. Jesus ties throne-talk to His suffering and asks if they can drink it; their “We are able” is eager and untested.

Arminian warmth with caution: grace calls disciples into cross-shaped fellowship. Willingness matters, yet self-confidence must learn the cup’s cost by following, not boasting.

Reformed

Christ diagnoses the request: they do not know what they ask. The cup is His appointed suffering; their quick “able” underestimates both the cup and themselves.

Reformed sobriety: sharing Christ’s cup is gift and calling under His lordship. He will grant participation in suffering according to His purpose—not according to their self-assessment.

Matthew 20:23

23He said to them, “You will drink my cup, but to sit at my right hand and at my left is not mine to grant, but it is for those for whom it has been prepared by my Father.”

Arminian

Two truths stand: they will drink His cup, and the seats are not a blank check from the Son’s hand—they are prepared by the Father. Cross-fellowship is promised; honorary thrones are not bargained.

Arminian pastoral reading: grace enables costly discipleship. Places in glory remain the Father’s wise appointment; ambition yields to trust rather than demand.

Reformed

Jesus promises cup-sharing and withholds seat-granting as if it were patronage. The places “prepared by my Father” rest in eternal purpose, not disciple negotiation.

Reformed confession: the Father’s preparation governs honor in the kingdom. The Son gladly submits the assignment of seats to that counsel while securing their path through His own cup.

Key contrast Both affirm cup-sharing and refuse throne-grabbing. Arminians typically stress grace that enables costly following while leaving honor to the Father’s wise appointment we must trust; Reformed readers typically stress the Father’s prior preparation of places as the decisive ground of kingdom honor.

Matthew 20:24

24And when the ten heard it, they were indignant at the two brothers.

Arminian

Indignation spreads: the ten resent the brothers’ bid. Rivalry multiplies when glory is the prize—anger at their ambition can hide matching desire.

Arminian pastoral edge: grace confronts faction in the church. Hearers must repent not only of grasping seats but of resenting those who grasped first.

Reformed

The circle fractures into offense. Hearing the request, the ten burn—not yet at pride in themselves, but at the brothers.

Reformed framing: Christ will gather them and rewrite greatness. Left alone, even apostolic hearts compete; He alone remakes the community’s honor code.

Matthew 20:25

25But Jesus called them to him and said, “You know that the rulers of the Gentiles lord it over them, and their great ones exercise authority over them.

Arminian

Jesus calls the whole group near and names the Gentile pattern: rulers lord it; great ones dominate. Worldly greatness is coercive authority.

Classical Arminians hear a clear contrast for the church’s choosing. Grace frees disciples to refuse that pattern; leadership among Jesus’ people must not copy pagan power.

Reformed

The Lord summons them and diagnoses the world’s greatness: domination dressed as rule. He assumes they “know” this model—and will forbid it among them.

Reformed accent: Christ defines His community against the nations’ hierarchy. Kingdom authority will be cross-shaped because the King Himself rejects Gentile lordship as the template.

Matthew 20:26

26It shall not be so among you. But whoever would be great among you must be your servant,

Arminian

A flat prohibition: “It shall not be so among you.” Greatness is redefined as service—the one who would be great must become servant.

Arminian preaching presses the will: grace enables a real turn from domination to serving. Status in Christ’s body grows by descending to meet needs.

Reformed

Kingdom order is spoken with authority: not so among you. Desire for greatness is redirected into servanthood.

Reformed comfort: Christ legislates a new measure. The church’s greatness is His gift expressed in service, protecting His people from celebrity power.

Matthew 20:27

27and whoever would be first among you must be your slave,

Arminian

The ladder flips again: first place means slave status—deeper than “servant.” Primacy among disciples looks like belonging to others’ good.

Arminian mission tone: assisting grace strengthens costly lowliness. Ambition for “first” must be converted into willing bondage to love.

Reformed

“Slave” intensifies the paradox. The race to be first becomes a race to the bottom in status for the sake of the body.

Reformed pastoral weight: this is the ethic of those united to a crucified Lord. He forms firstness as self-giving, not self-advancement.

Matthew 20:28

28even as the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.”

Arminian

The pattern and the power: the Son of Man came to serve and to give His life as a ransom for many. Leadership copies His mission; atonement is the deep gift beneath the ethic.

Arminian gospel: the ransom is sufficient and sincerely offered in the preaching of Christ. “Many” does not shrink the call—grace summons all to trust the Servant who gave Himself.

Reformed

Christology grounds ethics: He came not to be served but to serve, giving His life as a ransom for many. The cross is both example and accomplishment.

Reformed confession: the ransom secures a people—“many” as the effective fruit of His death. Servant greatness in the church flows from union with the One who actually bought them.

Key contrast Both adore the Servant who gives His life and call the church to cross-shaped greatness. Arminians typically stress the ransom’s sufficient provision sincerely offered to all who believe; Reformed readers typically stress the ransom’s effective securing of the “many” the Father gave the Son.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on right after the third passion prediction, the mother of Zebedee’s sons seeks thrones beside Jesus—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 20:20–28.

Arminian Notable voices

“Then came to him the mother of Zebedee's children - Considering what he had been just speaking, was ever any thing more unreasonable? Perhaps Zebedee himself was dead, or was not a follower of Christ. In thy kingdom - Still they expected a temporal kingdom.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 20:20–28)

Reformed Notable voices

“Then came to him the mother of Zebedee's children. This narrative contains a bright mirror of human vanity; for it shows that proper and holy zeal is often accompanied by ambition, or some other vice of the flesh, so that they who follow Christ have a different object in view from what they ought to have.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 20:20–28)
