Passage

Matthew 20

Verses 1–16 · ESV

After promising reward to those who left all to follow Him, Jesus tells the vineyard parable: a Master hires laborers at every hour and pays the last the same as the first. The point lands on grace, generosity, and the warning that the last will be first—and the first last.

Matthew 20:1

1For the kingdom of heaven is like a master of a house who went out early in the morning to hire laborers for his vineyard.

Arminian

Jesus opens with a kingdom likeness: a Master who seeks workers, not workers who invent the vineyard. “Early in the morning” shows initiative—grace goes out first.

Arminian warmth: the call is real and repeated through the day. Prevenient grace still seeks idle hands in the marketplace; hearers remain responsible to answer when hired.

Reformed

The kingdom is defined by the Master’s going out. Hiring is His act before any labor begins.

Reformed framing: divine calling precedes human toil. The vineyard belongs to the Lord; laborers enter by His summons, not by storming the gate on their own terms.

Matthew 20:2

2After agreeing with the laborers for a denarius a day, he sent them into his vineyard.

Arminian

A fair day’s wage is set by agreement. The first workers know exactly what justice looks like under the contract.

Arminian note: grace does not cheat. God keeps His word to those who respond early; the later surprise of generosity will not cancel this honesty.

Reformed

The denarius is ordinary hire—no inflated promise. Even the “first” stand under a gift framed as wage.

Reformed accent: what looks like debt is still the Master’s free arrangement. He owes no one a vineyard place; the agreement itself is kindness.

Matthew 20:3

3And going out about the third hour he saw others standing idle in the marketplace,

Arminian

About nine o’clock the Master finds more idle people. Idleness here is unemployed waiting, not celebrated leisure—souls without holy work.

Arminian pastoral push: grace keeps seeking. Standing unused is tragic when the vineyard still needs hands; the offer of work is mercy that summons a response.

Reformed

The Master’s second outing underscores persistence. He sees before they ask.

Reformed note: providence finds laborers where they stand. The marketplace of the world is not beyond the Lord’s hiring eye.

Matthew 20:4

4and to them he said, ‘You go into the vineyard too, and whatever is right I will give you.’

Arminian

No fixed sum—only trust: “whatever is right.” They go on the Master’s character, not a spreadsheet.

Arminian reading: faith trusts the Giver’s equity. Grace invites without haggling; those who enter do so by accepting His word as enough.

Reformed

Later hires rest on the Master’s promise of what is right, not on their bargaining power.

Reformed comfort: righteousness of payment is located in Him. The worker’s security is the Owner’s integrity, not a negotiated claim of merit.

Matthew 20:5

5So they went. Going out again about the sixth hour and the ninth hour, he did the same.

Arminian

Noon and mid-afternoon bring the same pattern: going out, hiring, sending. Opportunity keeps arriving while daylight lasts.

Arminian mission tone: no hour is too late for a sincere call. Grace multiplies invitations; delaying response remains a real risk while the day declines.

Reformed

Repeated outings display tireless initiative. Midday and ninth-hour calls are still the Master’s doing.

Reformed accent: effectual kindness spans the clock. Whom He wills to put in the vineyard, He fetches—even when human employers would wait for tomorrow.

Matthew 20:6

6And about the eleventh hour he went out and found others standing. And he said to them, ‘Why do you stand here idle all day?’

Arminian

Near day’s end He still finds idle standers and questions them. The “why” exposes wasted hours without denying a late chance.

Arminian seriousness: grace asks searching questions. Standing idle “all day” is accountable; yet the Master’s presence at the eleventh hour still offers entrance.

Reformed

Even the eleventh hour is not beneath His going out. The question reveals need; the finding reveals mercy.

Reformed pastoral weight: late calling is still sovereign kindness. No boast remains for those hired at dusk—only wonder that He came at all.

Matthew 20:7

7They said to him, ‘Because no one has hired us.’ He said to them, ‘You go into the vineyard too.’

Arminian

Their excuse is lack of hire, not refusal of work. He answers with inclusion: “You go … too.”

Arminian hope: those overlooked by men are still invited by Christ. The universal wideness of the call meets particular hearers who must actually go.

Reformed

Unhired does not mean unwanted by this Master. The command is simple and sufficient.

Reformed note: election of workers appears as personal address—“you … too.” Entrance is gift before it is achievement.

Matthew 20:8

8And when evening came, the owner of the vineyard said to his foreman, ‘Call the laborers and pay them their wages, beginning with the last, up to the first.’

Arminian

Evening is reckoning time. Pay begins with the last—an order that will expose hearts before it fills hands.

Arminian warning: grace rearranges status. Those who arrived late are not shamed; those who arrived early must not assume rank by clock-hours alone.

Reformed

The Owner directs payment sequence. Last-first order is deliberate pedagogy, not payroll accident.

Reformed emphasis: sovereignty shows in the line. The Lord of the vineyard sets both wage and order so that grace, not seniority, holds the center.

Matthew 20:9

9And when those hired about the eleventh hour came, each of them received a denarius.

Arminian

One hour’s work, a full day’s coin. Generosity lands first on the least expected.

Arminian joy: free grace can lavish equal life on late responders. The gift is not earned by duration; it is received by those who entered when called.

Reformed

The denarius to the last is pure surplus kindness relative to hours worked.

Reformed wonder: undeserved wage displays the Owner’s freedom. Eternal life’s “hire” is bounty, not a ledger of minutes.

Matthew 20:10

10Now when those hired first came, they thought they would receive more, but each of them also received a denarius.

Arminian

Expectation inflates: if the last got a denarius, surely we get more. Merit math collides with equal pay.

Arminian pastoral care: comparing callings breeds disappointment. Grace that saved us early does not owe us a premium for resenting those saved later.

Reformed

“They thought” is the hinge of the grievance. Assumption, not injustice, creates the crisis.

Reformed sobriety: flesh calculates desert. The parable unmasks how quickly even hired laborers drift from gratitude into entitlement.

Matthew 20:11

11And on receiving it they grumbled at the master of the house,

Arminian

Grumbling targets the Master, not the coin. The gift in hand becomes occasion for complaint.

Arminian warning: envy can spoil received grace. We remain responsible not to murmur when God is good to others as He has been good to us.

Reformed

Murmur against the householder is theological: it challenges the Giver’s character.

Reformed gravity: discontent with equal grace reveals a heart still measuring by works. The problem is not the denarius but the eye that begrudges.

Matthew 20:12

12saying, ‘These last worked only one hour, and you have made them equal to us who have borne the burden of the day and the scorching heat.’

Arminian

Their résumé is real—long hours, heat, burden—yet used as a weapon against equality. “You have made them equal” is the scandal of grace.

Arminian preaching: labor in the vineyard is good; boasting over latecomers is not. Grace enables enduring the heat without despising those hired at dusk.

Reformed

True hardship is claimed as superior merit. Equality under the Master feels like demotion.

Reformed note: the complaint assumes God must scale reward to visible toil. The parable refuses that economy without denying that early workers did sweat.

Matthew 20:13

13But he replied to one of them, ‘Friend, I am doing you no wrong. Did you not agree with me for a denarius?

Arminian

“Friend” plus “no wrong” plus the original agreement: justice toward the first is intact. The grievance is envy, not theft.

Arminian clarity: God defrauds no one who trusts His promise. Grace to another never subtracts what was pledged to you; refuse the evil eye.

Reformed

The Master answers personally and calmly. Contract kept; character vindicated.

Reformed comfort: divine righteousness and divine generosity are not rivals. He can be just to the first and free toward the last at once.

Matthew 20:14

14Take what belongs to you and go. I choose to give to this last worker as I give to you.

Arminian

“Take … and go” ends the dispute; “I choose to give” names the heart of the parable. The last receive by the Master’s will to give, not by hours logged.

Arminian accent: free grace is offered widely and given generously—yet the early worker is still told to receive his own without bitterness. Opportunity and envy both press responsibility: take the gift, release the comparison.

Reformed

Choice language is unmistakable: “I choose to give.” The Owner’s freedom, not the laborer’s leverage, decides the late equal wage.

Reformed accent: sovereign generosity stands in the open. Effectual kindness to the last is the Master’s good pleasure; the first are not wronged when He displays it.

Key contrast Shared ground: the first are paid fairly, and the last are paid by sheer generosity. Arminians typically stress the Master’s free offer and our responsibility to receive without envy; Reformed readers typically stress the Master’s sovereign freedom to give effectually as He chooses—while both refuse merit-grumbling.

Matthew 20:15

15Am I not allowed to do what I choose with what belongs to me? Or do you begrudge my generosity?’

Arminian

Two questions: ownership rights, and the evil eye of envy. “Begrudge my generosity” exposes the moral failure—resenting goodness itself.

Arminian pastoral edge: grace that saves us also forbids jealousy of grace to others. The call was open enough to hire us; we must not slam the door of joy when others are hired and paid.

Reformed

Divine freedom and human envy face off. What belongs to God, God may distribute.

Reformed emphasis: sovereignty is not tyranny here but liberality. The “evil eye” is unbelief’s allergy to unearned kindness; grace rebukes it without apology.

Key contrast Both hear God’s right to be generous and humanity’s temptation to envy. Arminians typically frame the warning as resistible sin against shared, offered grace; Reformed readers typically frame it as revolt against the Owner’s free, discriminating bounty—while both call envy of grace a spiritual disaster.

Matthew 20:16

16So the last will be first, and the first last.

Arminian

The proverb seals the parable: kingdom order overturns seniority boasting. Last-first is grace’s public verdict against merit theater.

Arminian conclusion: many hear the hire-call; those who obey enter the joy. Do not bank on being “first” in time or toil—respond, labor gratefully, and celebrate every late denarius God gives.

Reformed

Last-first is the kingdom’s settled word after the Master’s defense of His freedom. Precedence is grace’s to assign.

Reformed conclusion: God calls freely whom He pleases and orders reward by His wisdom. Early laborers run with humility; late laborers marvel—both live by undeserved wage.

Key contrast Shared ground: status in the kingdom is not secured by clocking seniority. Arminians typically hear last-first as warning and invitation—obey the call, refuse envy, trust free grace offered; Reformed readers typically hear it as the fruit of sovereign calling and divine freedom in rewarding—while both renounce a wages-of-merit gospel.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the vineyard, last and first, and the Master’s freedom—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Jesus’ parable.

Arminian Notable voices

“Is it not lawful for me to do what I will with my own?—Yea, doubtless, to give either to Jew or Gentile a reward infinitely greater than he deserves. … Art thou envious, because I am gracious?”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 20:15)
“As eternal life is the free gift of God, he has a right to give it in whatever proportions, at whatever times, and on whatever conditions he pleases. … Eternal life is offered to both through the blood of the cross; and there is room enough in heaven for all.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Matt 20:13–15)

Reformed Notable voices

“Men have no right to complain of the bounty of God, when he honors unworthy persons by large rewards beyond what they deserve. … Since God defrauds no man of a just hire, He is at liberty to bestow on those whom He has lately called an undeserved reward.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 20:8–15)
“It was not a transaction of a mercenary sort, but a display of free favour; and so the great quality of sovereignty comes in as to the very order of payment. … The Lord will take care that, in the transactions of his grace, his sovereignty as well as his goodness shall be conspicuous.”
C. H. Spurgeon · The Gospel of the Kingdom (on Matt 20:8)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
