Passage

Matthew 20

Verses 29–34 · ESV · Voices

Leaving Jericho with a great crowd, Jesus meets two blind men who cry out for mercy as Son of David. Rebuked by the crowd, they cry louder; He stops, asks what they want, touches their eyes in pity, and they see and follow Him.

Matthew 20:29

29And as they went out of Jericho, a great crowd followed him.

Arminian

The road out of Jericho is crowded with followers. Public movement surrounds Jesus as He continues toward Jerusalem.

Arminian pastoral note: proximity of many does not guarantee sight. Grace will soon meet need on the roadside while the throng keeps walking.

Reformed

Matthew sets the scene: exit from Jericho, a great crowd in tow. The King’s path still runs under His timing toward the city of the cross.

Reformed framing: providence places mercy beside the road. What happens next is not interruption of the plan but part of the Son of David’s work.

Matthew 20:30

30And behold, there were two blind men sitting by the roadside, and when they heard that Jesus was passing by, they cried out, “Lord, have mercy on us, Son of David!”

Arminian

“Behold” highlights two blind men who hear and cry: “Lord, have mercy… Son of David!” Faith speaks through darkness—Messianic title plus plea for mercy.

Classical Arminians hear a real cry grace enables. Light reaches them as news of Jesus passing; they must not stay silent when mercy is near.

Reformed

Sightless men confess more truly than many with eyes: Lord, Son of David, mercy. Hearing becomes petition.

Reformed accent: God often draws true confession from the needy. The title they shout is theologically right; the King they name is the One appointed to save.

Matthew 20:31

31The crowd rebuked them, telling them to be silent, but they cried out all the more, “Lord, have mercy on us, Son of David!”

Arminian

The crowd tries to hush mercy-seekers. The blind men cry out all the more—the same confession, louder. Persistence marks their plea.

Arminian warmth: grace that awakens cry also sustains it against social pressure. Hearers are summoned to keep calling on the Son of David when voices tell them to be quiet.

Reformed

Human rebuke meets stubborn petition. Louder crying after silencing attempts shows need that will not take the crowd’s veto as final.

Reformed pastoral reading: Christ will soon override the hush. Persistent cry here is not self-salvation; it is the Spirit-kept insistence of those the Lord is about to heal.

Matthew 20:32

32And stopping, Jesus called them and said, “What do you want me to do for you?”

Arminian

Jesus stops—the King pauses for the overlooked—and calls them with a question that honors their agency: “What do you want me to do for you?”

Arminian pastoral edge: grace invites specific trust. He draws desire into the open so faith can name its need before His power.

Reformed

Stopping and calling are His initiatives. The question does not signal ignorance but draws their request into spoken dependence.

Reformed comfort: the Son of David rules the moment. Crowd pressure does not set His pace; He halts and summons because mercy is His to give.

Matthew 20:33

33They said to him, “Lord, let our eyes be opened.”

Arminian

The ask is clear and fitting: “Lord, let our eyes be opened.” They bring blindness to the only One who can heal it.

Classical Arminians urge simple, direct faith. Grace has brought them to speech; they must ask for what only Jesus can do—and expect His kindness.

Reformed

Petition matches need: opened eyes from the Lord. No bargain, no rival therapy—only appeal to His power.

Reformed note: saving and healing mercy begin with His lordship confessed. They ask because He has drawn the ask; the gift will answer the word they speak.

Matthew 20:34

34And Jesus in pity touched their eyes, and immediately they recovered their sight and followed him.

Arminian

Pity moves His hand: He touches, sight returns at once, and they follow. Mercy felt becomes discipleship walked.

Arminian mission tone: the Lord who answers persistent cry also claims the newly seeing. Grace that opens eyes calls for a life of following the Healer.

Reformed

Compassion, touch, immediate sight, and following—Matthew’s chain shows sovereign mercy in motion. Pity is not soft sentiment but the King’s effective kindness.

Reformed confession: He opens what only He can open. Recovered sight is gift; following is fruit. The Son of David creates both the vision and the path behind Him.

Key contrast Both celebrate mercy that hears the blind and opens their eyes. Arminians typically stress persistent, grace-enabled crying that Jesus sincerely answers; Reformed readers typically stress the Lord’s sovereign pity that effectually opens eyes and draws the healed to follow.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on leaving Jericho with a great crowd, Jesus meets two blind men who cry out for mercy as Son of David—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 20:29–34.

Arminian Notable voices

“Behold two blind men cried out - St. Mark and St. Luke mention only one of them, blind Bartimeus. He was far the more eminent of the two, and, as it seems, spoke for both. The multitude charged them to hold their peace - And so they will all who begin to cry after the Son of David.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 20:29–34)

Reformed Notable voices

“And while they were departing from Jericho. Osiander has resolved to display his ingenuity by making four blind men out of one. But nothing can be more frivolous than this supposition. Having observed that the Evangelists differ in a few expressions, he imagined that one blind man received sight when they were entering into the city, and that the second, and other two, received sight when Christ was departing from it.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 20:29–34)
