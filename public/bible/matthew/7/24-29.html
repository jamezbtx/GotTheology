Passage

Matthew 7

Verses 24–29 · ESV · Voices

The Sermon on the Mount closes with two builders—hearing-and-doing versus hearing-without-doing—then Matthew’s narrator notes the crowds’ astonishment: Jesus taught with authority, not as their scribes.

Matthew 7:24

24“Everyone then who hears these words of mine and does them will be like a wise man who built his house on the rock.

Arminian

Wisdom is hearing plus doing. Jesus’ words are the rock; obedience is how one builds upon them. The invitation is universal in form—“everyone” who hears and does.

Classical Arminians treasure responsible response: grace enables putting the sermon into practice. Shared ground: Christ’s teaching is the only sure foundation for a life that lasts.

Reformed

“These words of mine” claim unique authority. The wise builder is defined by obedient hearing. Rock is Christ’s word received in living faith.

Reformed accent: such doing is the fruit of a heart made wise by God. The parable does not teach self-salvation; it displays genuine discipleship that grace produces.

Matthew 7:25

25And the rain fell, and the floods came, and the winds blew and beat on that house, but it did not fall, because it had been founded on the rock.

Arminian

Storms come to both houses. Stability is not the absence of trial but foundation. The rock-built house stands because it was founded rightly—on Jesus’ words obeyed.

Arminian encouragement: persevering obedience under grace prepares you for rains, floods, and winds. The promise is endurance, not ease.

Reformed

Providence sends weather that tests foundations. The house stands “because” of the rock—cause named. Trial reveals what grace has built.

Reformed comfort: security rests on the foundation, not on the builder’s swagger. Christ’s word upheld in faith holds when judgment-winds blow.

Matthew 7:26

26And everyone who hears these words of mine and does not do them will be like a foolish man who built his house on the sand.

Arminian

Hearing without doing is folly. Same sermon, opposite response: sand instead of rock. Exposure to truth without obedience is spiritually dangerous.

Classical Arminians press accountability: you may hear and refuse to practice. Grace invites doing; folly is a real choice against wisdom.

Reformed

Folly is not lack of information but refusal to obey. Sand looks workable until the storm. Mere hearers build lives that cannot last.

Reformed warning: common grace may give hearing; saving grace produces doing. The contrast exposes empty profession inside the crowd that listened.

Matthew 7:27

27And the rain fell, and the floods came, and the winds blew and beat against that house, and it fell, and great was the fall of it.”

Arminian

Identical storm, opposite end: collapse, and “great was the fall.” Jesus does not soften the tragedy of a life that heard Him and did not obey. The sermon ends with pastoral alarm meant to save.

Arminian plea: build on the rock now—hear and do by the grace God supplies. Shared confession: the fall of sand-built religion is catastrophic and unnecessary if one turns to obey Christ.

Reformed

The fall is great because the claim was great and the foundation false. Final judgment and life’s crises alike unmask sand. Jesus loves His hearers enough to terrify them out of folly.

Reformed close: only the rock holds. Assurance belongs to those founded on Christ’s words in Spirit-wrought obedience; the parable drives us to Him, not to panic self-construction.

Key contrast Both require hearing and doing Jesus’ words. Arminians commonly stress grace-enabled obedient response versus foolish refusal; Reformed readers commonly stress Spirit-produced obedience on Christ the rock, with storms revealing true foundations.

Matthew 7:28

28And when Jesus finished these sayings, the crowds were astonished at his teaching,

Arminian

The sermon ends; amazement begins. Crowds feel the weight of “these sayings”—ethics, warnings, promises, and the claim of Lordship. Astonishment is a fitting first response to hearing Jesus.

Classical Arminians note that amazement must mature into obedient faith. Grace that arrests attention also calls for entering the narrow gate. Shared ground: Jesus’ teaching is not ordinary religious advice.

Reformed

Matthew marks completion: Jesus finished these sayings. Crowd astonishment is historical and theological—God has spoken in His Son with unique force.

Reformed readers see providence preparing the next scenes of authority in deed. Wonder is gift; saving faith is still required beyond the crowd’s awe.

Matthew 7:29

29for he was teaching them as one who had authority, and not as their scribes.

Arminian

The reason for astonishment is authority. Scribes leaned on tradition’s citations; Jesus speaks as the Lawgiver fulfilled and the Judge who will say “I never knew you.” Hearers must decide what to do with that claim.

Arminian pastoral close: authority invites trust and obedience freely rendered under grace. You are summoned to build on His words, not merely to admire them. Shared confession: Jesus’ authority surpasses every secondary teacher.

Reformed

Authority versus scribal manner is Matthew’s key. Jesus does not merely interpret Torah; He authoritatively declares the Father’s will and His own lordship.

Reformed confession: this is the voice of the incarnate Son. The contrast with scribes magnifies Christ alone. Crowds may marvel; the elect are brought to submit—yet the text leaves every reader under the same authoritative Word.

Key contrast Both marvel at Jesus’ unique authority over against the scribes. Arminians often stress the summons to grace-enabled obedient response to that authority; Reformed readers often stress the divine Son’s sovereign right to command and the Spirit’s work producing true submission.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on The Sermon on the Mount closes with two builders—hearing-and-doing versus hearing-without-doing—then Matthew’s—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 7:24–29.

Arminian Notable voices

“He taught them - The multitudes, as one having authority - With a dignity and majesty peculiar to himself as the great Lawgiver, and with the demonstration and power of the Spirit: and not as the scribes - Who only expounded the law of another; and that in a lifeless, ineffectual manner.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 7:24–29)

Reformed Notable voices

“Every one, therefore, who heareth As it is often difficult to distinguish the true professors of the Gospel from the false, Christ shows, by a beautiful comparison, where the main difference lies. He represents two houses, one of which was built without a foundation, while the other was well-founded.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 7:24–29)
