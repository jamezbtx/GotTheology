Passage

Matthew 7

Verses 21–23 · WEB · Voices

Jesus presses past religious speech and even impressive ministry claims to the heart of kingdom entry: doing the Father’s will—and the dread verdict for workers of lawlessness He never knew.

Matthew 7:21

21“Not everyone who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter into the Kingdom of Heaven, but he who does the will of my Father who is in heaven.

Arminian

Orthodox address—“Lord, Lord”—is not enough. Entry belongs to those who do the Father’s will. Jesus claims to decide kingdom entrance and ties it to obedient discipleship, not empty confession.

Arminians find a sincere call to living faith that obeys. Grace enables doing the Father’s will; lip-service without obedience is a damning refusal. Jesus is Lord, and lordship means obedience.

Reformed

Saying “Lord” without doing the Father’s will is empty religion. Jesus Himself is the gatekeeper of the kingdom; confession must be joined to obedience that grace produces.

True faith works. Doing the Father’s will is not a second ladder beside Christ; it grows from knowing Him—yet Jesus will not flatter fruitless orthodoxy.

Matthew 7:22

22Many will tell me in that day, ‘Lord, Lord, didn’t we prophesy in your name, in your name cast out demons, and in your name do many mighty works?’

Arminian

“That day” is judgment day. Many will plead ministry credentials—prophecy, exorcism, miracles—all “in your name.” Impressive activity can coexist with a heart Jesus does not own.

Do not trust gifts or results as proof of saving relationship. Grace calls for obedient trust, not résumé religion. Shared confession: ministry success differs from being known by Christ.

Reformed

Eschatological surprise: many claim Jesus’ name over powerful works. Charisma is not regeneration. The plea assumes that spectacular service settles the case; Jesus will show it does not.

Gifts can be present where saving grace is not. Providence may allow mighty works; only new birth and persevering faith mark those Christ knows.

Matthew 7:23

23Then I will tell them, ‘I never knew you. Depart from me, you who work iniquity.’

Arminian

The verdict is relational and moral: “I never knew you,” and workers of lawlessness. Jesus speaks as Judge. Lawless practice under religious cover is exposed.

Knowing Christ in living faith matters more than naming Him in ministry. The warning invites self-examination and renewed obedience while mercy still calls. Common confession: departure from Christ is the ultimate loss.

Reformed

“I never knew you” is devastating covenant language—no saving relationship, despite the name on the résumé. Lawlessness names the true allegiance of their works.

Reformed assurance for the humble: those Christ knows, He keeps. The terror of this verse is for false profession; the comfort is that being known by Jesus, not our mighty works, is the ground of hope.

Key contrast Both insist that empty “Lord, Lord” and even mighty works cannot replace doing the Father’s will in a living relationship with Jesus. Arminians commonly stress grace-enabled obedient faith against résumé religion; Reformed readers commonly stress that being known by Christ—and the fruit that follows—is the decisive reality His judgment reveals.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Doing the Father’s will, not merely saying ‘Lord, Lord’: older Arminian and Reformed writers, each in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Not every one - That is, no one that saith, Lord, Lord - That makes a mere profession of me and my religion, shall enter - Whatever their false teachers may assure them to the contrary: He that doth the will of my Father - as I have now declared it. Observe: every thing short of this is only saying, Lord, Lord.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 7:21–23)

Reformed Notable voices

“Not every one that saith to me, Lord, Lord. Christ extends his discourse farther: for he speaks not only of false prophets, who rush upon the flock to tear and devour, but of hirelings, who insinuate themselves, under fair appearances, as pastors, though they have no feeling of piety.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 7:21–23)
