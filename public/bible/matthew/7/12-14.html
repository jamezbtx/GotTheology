Passage

Matthew 7

Verses 12–14 · ESV · Voices

Jesus gathers the sermon’s ethic into the Golden Rule, then presses the urgency of the two ways: the narrow gate that leads to life and the wide, easy road that leads to destruction.

Matthew 7:12

12“So whatever you wish that others would do to you, do also to them, for this is the Law and the Prophets.

Arminian

The Golden Rule is positive and active: not merely avoid harm, but do the good you desire to receive. Jesus ties it to the whole Law and Prophets—love of neighbor summarized for daily life.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere, grace-enabled command. Believers can, under assisting grace, choose empathy and initiative in love. Shared confession: the Father who gives good gifts forms a people who give good treatment to others.

Reformed

“So” links the rule to the Father’s generosity just taught. Having received mercy, disciples mirror toward others the care they long to receive. This is Torah’s heart, not a freestanding ethic of self-interest.

Reformed readers stress that such love is fruit of a new heart. The Law and the Prophets find their summary in Christ-shaped neighbor-love—obedience flowing from grace, not a ladder to earn the Father’s favor.

Key contrast Both treat the Golden Rule as the Law and Prophets in practical form. Arminians commonly stress grace-enabled, responsible love toward neighbors; Reformed readers commonly stress Spirit-wrought obedience that mirrors the Father’s prior generosity.

Matthew 7:13

13“Enter by the narrow gate. For the gate is wide and the way is easy that leads to destruction, and those who enter by it are many.

Arminian

The imperative is personal and urgent: Enter. The wide gate and easy way are popular precisely because they cost little repentance. Many walk them—toward destruction, not life.

Classical Arminians hear a real invitation that can be refused. Grace enables entering; the will is summoned to leave the easy crowd-path. Shared ground: destruction is real, and popularity is no proof of safety.

Reformed

Jesus diagnoses the default of the fallen world: the broad road is crowded because the flesh loves ease. Destruction is the end of the unexamined majority path.

Reformed accent: the warning is merciful severity. God’s Word exposes the lie that numbers equal truth. The many on the wide way underscore our need of sovereign grace to leave what feels natural.

Matthew 7:14

14For the gate is narrow and the way is hard that leads to life, and those who find it are few.

Arminian

Narrow gate and hard way lead to life. Few find it—not because God delights in exclusion, but because costly discipleship is refused by many. Finding implies seeking that grace makes possible.

Arminian pastoral hope: you may enter; the gate is open in Christ. Persevering on the hard way is grace-enabled obedience, not self-salvation. Shared confession: life is found only through this narrow way.

Reformed

Hardness and fewness do not make the gate unfair; they tell the truth about sin and the cost of following Jesus. Life is the gift at the end of the narrow path He opens.

Reformed assurance: those who find it find because the Shepherd leads. Election does not cancel the call to enter; it guarantees a people who will. The hard way is still the way of grace.

Key contrast Both urge entering the narrow gate that leads to life. Arminians often stress a genuine, grace-enabled choice against the easy majority path; Reformed readers often stress that finding the hard way is itself God’s saving work amid a fallen preference for ease.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus gathers the sermon’s ethic into the Golden Rule—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 7:12–14.

Arminian Notable voices

“The strait gate - The holiness described in the foregoing chapters. Wide is the gate, and many there are that go in through it - They need not seek for this; they come to it of course. Many go in through it, because strait is the other gate - Therefore they do not care for it; they like a wider gate.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 7:12–14)

Reformed Notable voices

“All things whatsoever you would wish The word therefore (oun) is superfluous, as we often find such particles occurring, and without any addition to the sense, in detached sentences. I have already said, that Matthew does not give here a single discourse, but a summary of doctrine collected out of many sermons.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 7:12–14)
