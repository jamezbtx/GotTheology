Passage

Matthew 7

Verses 15–20 · ESV

On the narrow way, Jesus warns of false prophets: charming exteriors, predatory hearts, and a simple test—fruit over fleece. Disciples must discern by what a life and teaching actually produce.

Matthew 7:15

15“Beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep’s clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves.

Arminian

“Beware” is pastoral alertness, not paranoia. False prophets look like sheep—familiar, religious, even comforting—yet inwardly they devour. Appearance is not enough; character and teaching must be tested.

Classical Arminians hear a call to responsible discernment. Grace does not replace vigilance; it enables it. Shared ground: the flock needs protection from wolves dressed as helpers.

Reformed

Jesus assumes the church will face deceivers. Sheep’s clothing names the danger of religious camouflage; wolves name the intent to consume.

Reformed accent: Christ shepherds His people by Word and Spirit. Beware is covenant care—He warns so that His sheep will not be naive about counterfeit voices.

Matthew 7:16

16You will recognize them by their fruits. Are grapes gathered from thornbushes, or figs from thistles?

Arminian

Fruit is the public test. Grapes do not grow on thorns; figs do not come from thistles. Over time, teaching and life reveal the tree. Discernment is patient and concrete.

Arminian reading: you can and should learn to recognize fruit. Grace sharpens sight; believers are responsible not to excuse bad fruit for the sake of impressive fleece.

Reformed

Recognition by fruit is Jesus’ appointed criterion. Nature produces after its kind; so do prophets. Rhetoric and costume cannot permanently hide a thornbush heart.

Reformed pastoral note: fruit includes doctrine and life under the Word. The test protects the church from charm without truth and truth-claims without holiness.

Matthew 7:17

17So, every healthy tree bears good fruit, but the diseased tree bears bad fruit.

Arminian

Healthy and diseased trees differ in what they produce. Jesus ties inner condition to outward result. Good fruit is expected from a sound tree; bad fruit exposes disease.

Classical Arminians stress that grace renews the tree so that good fruit becomes possible and real. Holiness is not optional decoration; it is evidence of a living faith.

Reformed

The proverb is moral and spiritual: trees bear according to their nature. A healthy tree’s good fruit and a diseased tree’s bad fruit are not accidents.

Reformed accent: new birth makes a healthy tree. Good fruit is the Spirit’s produce, not a ladder by which we climb into favor—yet fruitlessness remains a grave warning.

Matthew 7:18

18A healthy tree cannot bear bad fruit, nor can a diseased tree bear good fruit.

Arminian

Jesus speaks in strong cannot-language about settled natures. Habitual bad fruit contradicts a claim of health. The saying presses honesty about what our lives keep producing.

Arminian pastoral care: if fruit is bad, seek the Physician. Grace can heal a diseased tree; denial only prolongs harm to self and flock.

Reformed

Cannot marks incompatibility of natures. Counterfeit goodness may mimic for a time; the settled yield of a diseased tree is bad fruit.

Reformed comfort: regeneration changes the tree. Where Christ makes healthy, good fruit follows—imperfect yet real—because the Spirit does not leave His work barren.

Matthew 7:19

19Every tree that does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire.

Arminian

Judgment language is clear: fruitless trees face fire. Jesus warns to awaken, not to entertain despair. The axe and fire call for repentance while there is time.

Arminian urgency: heed the warning; turn and bear fruit worthy of repentance. Grace invites change before the cutting comes.

Reformed

Eschatological seriousness: unfruitful trees are cut down and burned. Jesus echoes John the Baptist’s axe at the root—kingdom judgment is real.

Reformed sobriety: final judgment vindicates the difference fruit reveals. The warning belongs to the gospel’s honesty; the remedy is Christ, who makes trees alive.

Matthew 7:20

20Thus you will recognize them by their fruits.

Arminian

The section closes where it opened: recognition by fruits. Disciples are not left guessing; they are given a workable, pastoral test.

Arminian hope and duty: practice fruit-watching with humility and courage. Shared confession: fleece lies; fruit tells.

Reformed

Thus seals the criterion. The church’s safety is not cynicism but fruit-discernment under Christ’s Word.

Reformed assurance: the Shepherd who warns also keeps. Recognition by fruits is how His people stay on the narrow way among wolves.

Key contrast Both insist on fruit as the test of prophets. Arminians commonly stress grace-enabled, responsible discernment; Reformed readers commonly stress fruit as evidence of a nature remade—or exposed—under Christ’s sovereign care of the flock.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
