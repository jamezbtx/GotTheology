Passage

Matthew 7

Verses 1–5 · ESV · Voices

Still in the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus turns from anxiety and treasure to relationships in the kingdom community: a warning against hypocritical judgment, paired with the call to clear-eyed self-examination before helping a brother.

Matthew 7:1

1“Judge not, that you be not judged.

Arminian

Jesus does not ban all moral discernment; He forbids the condemning spirit that puts the self in God’s seat. The warning is pastoral: refuse censorious judgment, or you invite the same measure upon yourself.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere, grace-enabled call. Believers can, under assisting grace, refuse harsh verdicts and choose mercy. Shared ground: God alone is the final Judge, and hypocritical condemnation empties community of love.

Reformed

“Judge not” confronts the heart that loves to condemn. The saying is not moral relativism; it is a rebuke of self-exalting judgment that forgets our own need of mercy.

Reformed readers stress that true humility before the Judge is fruit of grace. Those justified by Christ are freed from playing God over their neighbors—and still called to careful, charitable discernment under His Word.

Key contrast Both reject censorious hypocrisy and affirm God as Judge. Arminians often stress grace-enabled refusal of harsh verdicts; Reformed readers often stress humility as the fruit of justifying grace that ends self-exalting judgment.

Matthew 7:2

2For with the judgment you pronounce you will be judged, and with the measure you use it will be measured to you.

Arminian

The measure we use returns upon us. Jesus ties relational ethics to accountability: harshness breeds harshness; mercy invites mercy. This is not karma but the Father’s moral order among disciples.

Arminian pastoral emphasis: you are responsible for the measure you choose. Grace enables a generous measure; refusal of mercy is a real, accountable choice against the way of the kingdom.

Reformed

Jesus names a divine reciprocity of measure. God is not mocked; the spirit of judgment we cultivate is the spirit by which we ourselves will be assessed.

Reformed comfort and warning: the Judge is just. Those who have received mercy are to extend it. The measure language presses sanctification—new hearts learning the generosity of the gospel.

Matthew 7:3

3Why do you see the speck that is in your brother’s eye, but do not notice the log that is in your own eye?

Arminian

The comic image exposes our bias: we magnify a brother’s small fault and ignore our own large one. Self-blindness is the disease; noticing the speck without noticing the log is not love.

Classical Arminians linger on responsible self-examination. Grace helps us see our own sin truly; we are called to look inward first, not to weaponize others’ flaws.

Reformed

Speck and log unmask the flesh’s talent for selective sight. We are expert at others’ sins and amateurs at our own—unless grace opens our eyes.

Reformed accent: the log is not a minor oversight. Remaining sin and self-righteousness distort vision; the Spirit must convict before we can help a brother well.

Matthew 7:4

4Or how can you say to your brother, ‘Let me take the speck out of your eye,’ when there is the log in your own eye?

Arminian

Offering to “help” while unhelped ourselves is hypocrisy dressed as care. Jesus questions the credibility of unrepentant correctors. Shared confession: brotherly care requires integrity.

Arminian reading treasures sincerity: you may truly desire to help, but you must first deal with your own obstruction. Grace enables honest repentance that makes help safe and humble.

Reformed

The offer to remove a speck while carrying a log is spiritual theater. Jesus exposes the contradiction so that correction, when it comes, will be from cleared sight, not pride.

Reformed pastoral note: church discipline and mutual exhortation remain; they must flow from hearts first humbled under the Word, not from a log-eyed superiority.

Matthew 7:5

5You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother’s eye.

Arminian

Order matters: first the log, then the speck. Jesus does not forbid helping a brother; He requires clear sight and repentant integrity first. The goal is restored vision that can serve in love.

Arminian hope: by grace you can repent of the log and then gently aid your brother. Mutual care remains; hypocrisy is what dies. Shared ground: clear-eyed, humble help is kingdom community at its best.

Reformed

“Hypocrite” is Jesus’ sharp pastoral word. Priority is self-repentance; only then comes clarity for brotherly help. Judgment without self-judgment is banned; restored vision for gentle restoration is commanded.

Reformed assurance: the Spirit who convicts of the log also forms a people who restore with meekness. Clear sight is gift and calling—fruit of grace that still takes speck-removal seriously.

Key contrast Both affirm self-examination before brotherly correction. Arminians commonly stress grace-enabled repentance that frees us to help; Reformed readers commonly stress Spirit-wrought humility that makes clear-sighted restoration possible.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus turns from anxiety and treasure to relationships in the kingdom community: a warning against hypocritical—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 7:1–5.

Arminian Notable voices

“Judge not - any man without full, clear, certain knowledge, without absolute necessity, without tender love. With what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you - Awful words! So we may, as it were, choose for ourselves, whether God shall be severe or merciful to us.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 7:1–5)

Reformed Notable voices

“Judge not These words of Christ do not contain an absolute prohibition from judging, but are intended to cure a disease, which appears to be natural to us all. We see how all flatter themselves, and every man passes a severe censure on others.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 7:1–5)
