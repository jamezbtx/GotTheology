Sample passage

Matthew 7

Verses 7–11 · ESV

After warnings about hypocritical judgment and holy things cast before the hostile, Jesus opens the door of prayer: ask, seek, knock—and trusts the Father’s goodness by comparing earthly parents’ gifts to heaven’s far greater generosity.

Matthew 7:7

7“Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you.

Arminian

Three imperatives invite persistent, sincere prayer. Jesus assumes disciples will ask; the promise matches the plea. This is not magic; it is filial trust in a giving Father.

Classical Arminians hear a genuine invitation. Grace makes asking possible; the will is called to keep seeking. Shared ground: prayer is the disciple’s privilege before a Father who hears.

Reformed

Ask, seek, knock—ascending intensity of dependent prayer. The promises are certain because the Father is faithful, not because the asker is strong.

Reformed readers hear sovereignty and invitation together: God ordains the means of asking as surely as the gifts He gives. Prayer is commanded dependence, not leverage over God.

Matthew 7:8

8For everyone who asks receives, and the one who seeks finds, and to the one who knocks it will be opened.

Arminian

“Everyone who asks” widens the promise to all who come in faith. Receiving, finding, and opening are the Father’s sure responses to sincere petition aligned with His will.

Arminian pastoral reading: no sincere seeker is turned away as unworthy of approach. Responsibility to ask remains; the Father’s generosity meets those who come.

Reformed

Universal language (“everyone who asks”) describes the certain pattern for those who come as children—not a blank check for every craving, but a trustworthy Father to every true petitioner.

Reformed accent: the ones who ask are the ones grace has drawn to ask. Receiving is covenant care; the open door is the Father’s initiative matching His children’s cries.

Matthew 7:9

9Or which one of you, if his son asks him for bread, will give him a stone?

Arminian

Jesus argues from the lesser to the greater: even flawed parents do not mock a hungry child with a stone. How much more the heavenly Father.

Arminian warmth: you may trust the Father’s heart. Asking for needful good is safe; He does not bait-and-switch His children.

Reformed

The analogy assumes ordinary parental care as a pale mirror of God’s. A stone for bread would be cruelty; the Father’s character forbids such mockery.

Reformed comfort: God’s fatherhood is not projection of ours—ours is the faint echo. His gifts are wise and good because He is.

Matthew 7:10

10Or if he asks for a fish, will give him a serpent?

Arminian

Fish and serpent deepen the point: a good father does not answer a wholesome request with harm. Jesus trains confidence, not suspicion, toward the Father’s answers.

Classical Arminians stress that believers can approach without fear of divine cruelty. Grace teaches us to ask for good and to trust His wisdom when He gives differently than we imagined.

Reformed

Serpent for fish would be paternal malice. Jesus rules that out for the Father. Delays and denials of our lesser wants are never traps; they are the wiser love of heaven.

Reformed pastoral note: providence may withhold what we ask and still give what is good. The analogy protects trust even when the gift is not the one we named.

Matthew 7:11

11If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him!

Arminian

Jesus is frank: we are “evil,” yet still give good gifts. How much more the holy Father. The “how much more” is the sermon’s pastoral climax for prayer—generosity scaled to God’s character.

Arminian reading treasures both honesty about sin and boldness in asking. Grace enables approach; the Father gives “good things” to those who ask. Shared confession: heaven’s Father outgives every earthly parent.

Reformed

“You… who are evil” is total honesty about fallen parents—and then a fortiori praise of the Father. Good gifts to asking children are certain because of who He is.

Reformed assurance: the Father in heaven gives good things—supremely, what Luke’s parallel names as the Holy Spirit’s gift-realm—according to His wise love. Asking is the appointed path; the Giver is better than we dare hope.

Key contrast Both ground prayer in the Father’s goodness. Arminians commonly stress the sincere invitation and grace-enabled asking of all who come; Reformed readers commonly stress the Father’s certain generosity toward those His grace draws to ask, including His wise reshaping of our requests.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
