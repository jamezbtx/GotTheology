Passage

Matthew 15

Verses 1–9 · ESV

Pharisees and scribes arrive from Jerusalem and accuse Jesus’ disciples of breaking the tradition of the elders by eating with unwashed hands. Jesus turns the charge: their Corban practice voids God’s command to honor father and mother, and Isaiah’s word exposes lip-service worship that teaches human commandments as doctrine.

Matthew 15:1

1Then Pharisees and scribes came to Jesus from Jerusalem and said,

Arminian

Leaders travel from the holy city to confront Jesus. Official religion arrives with a question already loaded against Him and His disciples.

Classical Arminians hear a real test of hearts. Grace has brought truth near Jerusalem’s teachers; they remain responsible for how they receive or resist the One standing before them.

Reformed

Matthew’s “from Jerusalem” marks weighty opposition. The center of Israel’s religious authority opens a conflict that will run toward the cross.

Reformed framing: this meeting is not random friction. The Father’s purpose includes exposing tradition that smothers His word, even when the challenge comes from the capital.

Matthew 15:2

2“Why do your disciples break the tradition of the elders? For they do not wash their hands when they eat.”

Arminian

The charge is tradition, not Moses: unwashed hands at meals. Human custom is treated as if it were God’s fence around purity.

Arminian pastoral caution: religious habits can feel holy while sidelining God’s actual commands. Hearers are called to weigh tradition by Scripture, not to baptize every elder custom as binding.

Reformed

“Tradition of the elders” is named as the standard. Ritual handwashing becomes the measure of faithfulness in their mouths.

Reformed diagnosis: when man-made fences become law, the conscience is captured. Christ will reclaim the Word’s supremacy over inherited rules that pose as piety.

Matthew 15:3

3He answered them, “And why do you break the commandment of God for the sake of your tradition?

Arminian

Jesus answers charge with charge: they break God’s commandment for tradition’s sake. The question is not anti-tradition in the abstract; it is anti-substituting custom for obedience.

Classical Arminians stress a sincere summons back to God’s revealed will. Grace exposes the swap so hearers can repent of elevating human rules over divine command.

Reformed

Christ’s counter-question unveils the real breach. Tradition is not neutral here; it is the tool by which God’s commandment is broken.

Reformed emphasis: the Son judges religion by the Word. Where custom cancels command, He names the guilt and will not flatter the system.

Key contrast Both reject tradition that overrides God’s command. Arminians typically stress a grace-enabled call to repent of that swap and obey Scripture; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s authoritative exposure of a system already voiding the Word under God’s judicial purpose.

Matthew 15:4

4For God commanded, ‘Honor your father and your mother,’ and, ‘Whoever reviles father or mother must surely die.’

Arminian

Jesus cites God’s own voice: honor father and mother, and the severe word against reviling parents. Fifth-commandment love is not optional piety.

Arminian application: grace restores weight to family honor. Disciples are responsible to keep God’s commands in ordinary relationships, not only in ritual correctness.

Reformed

Two texts from the Law ground the rebuke. God commanded; the issue is divine authority, not rabbinic preference.

Reformed comfort and warning: the Law still reveals God’s holy will. Christ upholds the commandment even as He will fulfill all righteousness for His people.

Matthew 15:5

5But you say, ‘If anyone tells his father or his mother, “What you would have gained from me is given to God,”

Arminian

“But you say” introduces a religious loophole: dedicating support “to God” so parents are denied. Sacred language covers neglect.

Classical Arminians name this as accountable hypocrisy. Grace does not bless vows that starve parents while sounding devout. Love of God never cancels love of neighbor.

Reformed

Human speech (“you say”) stands against God’s command. Corban-style dedication becomes a pious excuse to withhold honor.

Reformed reading: false worship invents escapes from duty. Christ exposes how religion can be weaponized against the very people the Law protects.

Matthew 15:6

6he need not honor his father.’ So for the sake of your tradition you have made void the word of God.

Arminian

The punch line: tradition makes void the word of God. What God spoke is emptied so custom can stand.

Arminian pastoral grief: this is resistible light refused. Hearers who cling to loopholes remain responsible for silencing Scripture in practice.

Reformed

“Made void” is judicial clarity. Tradition is not merely mistaken; it nullifies God’s word for the sake of the system.

Reformed emphasis: Christ guards the Word’s integrity. Where human religion cancels divine speech, the Messiah names the void and will plant what the Father plants.

Key contrast Both confess that tradition can nullify Scripture. Arminians typically stress moral responsibility to stop voiding God’s word and return to obedience; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s verdict that such religion already stands condemned as making void what God has spoken.

Matthew 15:7

7You hypocrites! Well did Isaiah prophesy of you, when he said:

Arminian

“Hypocrites” is not a slur for disagreement; it names a split between claim and heart. Isaiah already mapped this people.

Classical Arminians hear a warning meant to wake. Prophetic words still call for repentance; grace invites a return from play-acting worship to true fear of God.

Reformed

Jesus ties His hearers to Isaiah’s oracle. The prophet’s “you” lands on this generation’s leaders.

Reformed note: Scripture interprets Scripture, and Christ applies it with authority. Hypocrisy is unmasked by the Word that searches hearts.

Matthew 15:8

8“‘This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me;

Arminian

Lips near, heart far: worship can be verbally correct and relationally empty. God wants the heart, not flattery.

Arminian summons: assisting grace softens hearts to draw near in truth. Hearers remain called to close the gap between speech and love.

Reformed

Isaiah’s diagnosis is heart-distance under lip-honor. External religion without inward nearness is vanity before God.

Reformed comfort: only God can bring far hearts near. The same Lord who exposes distance will create true worshipers by His Spirit.

Key contrast Both insist God requires the heart, not mere words. Arminians typically emphasize a grace-enabled turning of the heart toward God; Reformed readers typically emphasize that nearness of heart is God’s regenerating gift, not the fruit of unaided resolve.

Matthew 15:9

9in vain do they worship me, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men.’”

Arminian

Vain worship teaches human commandments as if they were divine doctrines. The classroom of tradition displaces God’s curriculum.

Classical Arminians urge reformation of teaching: measure every “must” by Scripture. Grace enables teachers and hearers to refuse man-made burdens posed as gospel.

Reformed

Worship is “in vain” when men’s commands are taught as doctrine. The issue is authority: whose word binds the conscience?

Reformed doxology: Christ restores the Word to the center. Empty worship is judged so that true fear of God may rise where His commandments alone rule as doctrine.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
