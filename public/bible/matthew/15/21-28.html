Passage

Matthew 15

Verses 21–28 · ESV · Voices

Jesus withdraws to the district of Tyre and Sidon. A Canaanite woman cries for mercy to the Son of David for her demon-oppressed daughter. After silence, a word about Israel’s lost sheep, and a hard saying about children’s bread, her faith answers with crumbs from the masters’ table. Jesus praises her great faith; her daughter is healed instantly.

Matthew 15:21

21And Jesus went away from there and withdrew to the district of Tyre and Sidon.

Arminian

Jesus withdraws northwest toward Tyre and Sidon. Gentile coasts become the next stage after conflict with Jerusalem’s teachers.

Arminian mission tone: the Lord places Himself where outsiders can find Him. Grace crosses borders; hearers in every district remain summoned to seek mercy.

Reformed

Withdrawal is purposeful geography. The Son moves into a Gentile district under the Father’s plan, not as aimless retreat.

Reformed framing: Tyre and Sidon will host a revelation of faith that shames much of Israel’s privilege. Providence orders the map of mercy.

Matthew 15:22

22And behold, a Canaanite woman from that region came out and was crying, “Have mercy on me, O Lord, Son of David; my daughter is severely oppressed by a demon.”

Arminian

A Canaanite woman comes crying: mercy, Lord, Son of David. She uses Israel’s royal title for a daughter’s demonic oppression.

Classical Arminians hear remarkable light. Grace has taught her who Jesus is; she responds with persistent petition. Maternal love and true faith meet in one cry.

Reformed

Matthew’s “behold” flags wonder: a Canaanite names Jesus Lord and Son of David. Faith speaks Israel’s confession from outside Israel’s house.

Reformed note: such confession is gift. The Spirit opens Gentile lips to the Messiah before the bread of the children is formally extended.

Matthew 15:23

23But he did not answer her a word. And his disciples came and begged him, saying, “Send her away, for she is crying out after us.”

Arminian

Jesus’ silence is real and hard. The disciples want dismissal; her crying annoys their journey.

Arminian pastoral reading: silence is not absence of compassion. Faith that keeps asking under quiet skies is still meeting a gracious Lord who will speak.

Reformed

No word yet. Disciples prefer an exit over engagement. Christ’s delay serves a larger unveiling of faith.

Reformed comfort: the Lord’s timing includes silence that deepens petition. What looks like neglect is often the forge of greater trust.

Matthew 15:24

24He answered, “I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.”

Arminian

Jesus names His mission priority: the lost sheep of Israel. The gospel’s order is not racism; it is covenantal sequence that still leaves room for Gentile mercy.

Classical Arminians hold both: Israel first, and a sincere wideness that will bless the nations. This woman is not lied to; she is placed inside a real redemptive order that grace is already stretching.

Reformed

The Son states His sentness: Israel’s lost sheep. Messianic mission has an appointed first audience.

Reformed emphasis: Christ’s words are true, not a bluff. Yet within that priority the Father has prepared a Gentile faith that will receive crumbs as feast—and later the nations in fullness.

Key contrast Both affirm Israel’s priority in Jesus’ earthly mission and coming mercy for Gentiles. Arminians typically stress a sincere, grace-enabled invitation that this woman rightly presses into; Reformed readers typically stress the Messiah’s ordered mission and the Father’s sovereign gift of faith that pierces that order without denying it.

Matthew 15:25

25But she came and knelt before him, saying, “Lord, help me.”

Arminian

She advances, kneels, and shortens the prayer: “Lord, help me.” Persistence becomes worship.

Arminian beauty: faith does not quit when the first answers are hard. Grace sustains kneeling; the will truly seeks help from Jesus alone.

Reformed

Nearer and lower: she kneels. The plea is pure dependence—“help me.”

Reformed reading: true faith is humble and Christward. The Lord who seemed distant is the only helper she names.

Matthew 15:26

26And he answered, “It is not right to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs.”

Arminian

The saying is severe: children’s bread is not for dogs. Household order is pictured; Gentiles stand outside as “dogs” in the proverb’s starkness.

Classical Arminians do not soften the hardness, yet hear a test that faith may answer. Grace enables her to receive the word without rage and to find an opening inside it.

Reformed

Jesus speaks the barrier in household language. Israel’s children have first claim on the bread of the kingdom.

Reformed weight: the distinction is real in redemptive history. The wonder is not that He said it, but that faith will bow under it and still cling to Him.

Key contrast Both refuse to pretend the saying was soft. Arminians typically hear a severe test that grace enables her to answer without despair; Reformed readers typically hear truthful mission order under which God will magnify a faith He Himself sustains.

Matthew 15:27

27She said, “Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters' table.”

Arminian

“Yes, Lord”—she accepts the premise, then finds mercy in the crumbs. Humility and wit meet trust: even dogs eat under the masters’ table.

Arminian triumph of faith: she does not demand the children’s portion; she pleads for overflow. Grace-taught faith agrees with Jesus and still expects kindness.

Reformed

Her “yes” submits; her “yet” believes. Crumbs from the masters’ table are enough if they come from this Master.

Reformed doxology: faith argues from Jesus’ own metaphor toward hope. The logic is given by God; the answer will prove crumbs to be a feast of power.

Key contrast Both praise her humble, ingenious trust. Arminians typically stress a free, grace-enabled response that agrees with Christ and seeks mercy; Reformed readers typically stress faith as God’s gift arguing rightly from Christ’s word toward certain help.

Matthew 15:28

28Then Jesus answered her, “O woman, great is your faith! Let it be done for you as you desire.” And her daughter was healed instantly.

Arminian

Jesus praises “great” faith and grants her desire; the daughter is healed instantly. Faith is honored as real trust that receives.

Classical Arminians rejoice: the Lord’s commendation takes her faith seriously as a living response under grace. Gentile seekers are truly welcomed when they cling to the Son of David.

Reformed

Christ names her faith great and effectually heals. Desire aligned with trust meets the Son’s sovereign “let it be done.”

Reformed gospel: great faith is recognized because God wrought it; instant healing displays the King’s authority beyond Israel’s borders already.

Key contrast Both confess great faith and instant mercy. Arminians typically emphasize faith as grace-enabled trust that truly receives Christ’s gift; Reformed readers typically emphasize that such great faith and the healing are God’s effectual work through the Son who wills the daughter well.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on faith, mercy, and the nations—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside the Canaanite woman’s plea and Jesus’ praise of her faith.

Arminian Notable voices

“Saving faith… is a divine evidence and conviction, not only that God was in Christ, reconciling the world unto himself, but also that Christ loved me, and gave himself for me. It is by this faith… that we receive Christ.”
John Wesley · Sermon 43, “The Scripture Way of Salvation”
“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)

Reformed Notable voices

“This woman is a mother… She does not cease to pray… Faith is not always equally vigorous; but… when it is tried, it shines more brightly.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 15:22–28)
“Faith is the gift of God… We are not sufficient of ourselves to think anything as of ourselves; but our sufficiency is of God.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “Faith: What Is It? How Can It Be Obtained?” (echoing Eph 2:8; 2 Cor 3:5)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
