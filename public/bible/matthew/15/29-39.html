Passage

Matthew 15

Verses 29–39 · WEB · Voices

From the coast Jesus returns beside the Sea of Galilee, sits on the mountain, and heals crowds of lame, blind, crippled, mute, and many others; they glorify the God of Israel. He then has compassion on a three-day crowd with nothing to eat, multiplies seven loaves and a few fish, satisfies about four thousand men, in addition to women and children, gathers seven baskets of leftovers, and departs by boat into the borders of Magdala.

Matthew 15:29

29Jesus departed from there and came near to the sea of Galilee; and he went up on the mountain and sat there.

Arminian

Jesus walks the Galilean shore and sits on the mountain—the Teacher’s posture that invites approach.

The seated Lord is accessible. Grace draws crowds to the mountain; coming remains a response.

Reformed

Return to Galilee and ascent to the mountain place Him as host of mercy. Sitting signals authority ready to receive the needy.

The Lord who praised Gentile faith now gathers a multitude under providence beside the sea.

Matthew 15:30

30Great multitudes came to him, having with them the lame, blind, mute, maimed, and many others, and they put them down at his feet. He healed them,

Arminian

Crowds bring every kind of brokenness and place sufferers at His feet. Healing answers the placement.

Arminians see faith’s charity: neighbors carried to Jesus. Grace enables both bringing and receiving; none are healed by staying away.

Reformed

The catalog of misery meets the Messiah’s power. Laid at His feet, they are healed—effectual compassion in many forms.

Christ is sufficient for the full inventory of human ruin. The mountain becomes a hospital under His wordless and spoken authority.

Matthew 15:31

31so that the multitude wondered when they saw the mute speaking, the injured healed, the lame walking, and the blind seeing—and they glorified the God of Israel.

Arminian

Wonder rises into doxology: they glorify the God of Israel. Healings reverse Isaiah’s hopes in living color.

Right seeing leads to right praise. Grace opens mouths once mute and hearts once dull to honor Israel’s God.

Reformed

Signs produce wonder and glory to Israel’s God. Even in a mixed crowd, Yahweh is praised for Messiah’s works.

Miracles serve worship. The Father is glorified as the Son restores bodies according to promised kingdom power.

Key contrast Both see healings that end in glory to the God of Israel. Arminians press grace-enabled wonder that rightly turns to praise; Reformed commentators stress God’s effectual display of messianic power that secures doxology.

Matthew 15:32

32Jesus summoned his disciples and said, “I have compassion on the multitude, because they have continued with me now three days and have nothing to eat. I don’t want to send them away fasting, or they might faint on the way.”

Arminian

Compassion speaks after three days: He will not send them away hungry. Bodily need matters to the Lord of hearts.

Arminian interpreters hear sincere pity that acts. Disciples are drawn into that mercy; refusing the hungry would contradict the Master’s heart.

Reformed

Jesus interprets His own motive: compassion, duration, danger of fainting. Unwillingness to dismiss them hungry is holy resolve.

The Shepherd’s pity is purposeful. Providence has timed scarcity so His provision—not their planning—will feed them.

Key contrast Both confess compassion that refuses to dismiss the hungry. Arminians typically press a sincere mercy that summons disciples to participate; Reformed readers stress the Son’s sovereign pity unfolding the Father’s provision for this crowd.

Matthew 15:33

33The disciples said to him, “Where could we get so many loaves in a deserted place as to satisfy so great a multitude?”

Arminian

The disciples see desolation and scale, not the Lord who just healed multitudes. Memory of earlier feeding is slow.

Weak faith still asks “where?” Grace invites them to bring the question to Jesus rather than despair.

Reformed

Arithmetic without Christ: no bakery in the wilderness for so many. Their question forgets the Feeder already among them.

Desolation is the stage God chooses. Human “where” will meet divine “how many loaves do you have?”

Matthew 15:34

34Jesus said to them, “How many loaves do you have?” They said, “Seven, and a few small fish.”

Arminian

Jesus asks for inventory: seven loaves, a few small fish. Little is named so it can be offered.

Bring what you have. Grace multiplies surrender, not pretended abundance.

Reformed

The question pulls their resources into His hands. Seven and a few are enough when He commands the feast.

Christ inventories in order to claim. Scarcity confessed becomes the raw material of miracle.

Matthew 15:35

35He commanded the multitude to sit down on the ground;

Arminian

Order precedes abundance. Sitting is trust that food is coming from His hands.

Obey the seating before you see the baskets. Grace enables resting under His direction.

Reformed

He directs; they sit. The King hosts before He multiplies.

Providence includes the posture of waiting. The ground becomes a tablecloth under His command.

Matthew 15:36

36and he took the seven loaves and the fish. He gave thanks and broke them, and gave to the disciples, and the disciples to the multitudes.

Arminian

Take, thank, break, give—through disciples to crowds. The church learns to receive and distribute.

Disciples are responsible channels. Grace makes both gratitude and generous passing-on possible.

Reformed

Thanksgiving rises; broken loaves move through the Twelve. Christ remains the giver; they are hands.

Word and thanks center the meal. What He breaks, He multiplies through servants He appoints.

Key contrast Both see Christ feeding through thankful breaking and disciples’ hands. Arminians underline grace-enabled stewardship in the distribution; Reformed interpreters underline the Son’s effectual provision that uses disciples as instruments.

Matthew 15:37

37They all ate and were filled. They took up seven baskets full of the broken pieces that were left over.

Arminian

All eat and are satisfied; seven baskets remain. Compassion did not ration meager bites.

Trust the Lord with need and gather leftovers wisely. Mercy is abundant without being wasteful.

Reformed

Satisfaction is complete; surplus is counted in seven baskets—echoing the seven loaves in reverse glory.

The math praises Jesus. He creates more than enough so faith remembers who fed the wilderness crowd.

Key contrast Both confess full satisfaction with leftover abundance. Arminians emphasize faith that receives multiplied mercy; Reformed readers emphasize sovereign provision that effectually fills and overflows.

Matthew 15:38

38Those who ate were four thousand men, in addition to women and children.

Arminian

The census of mercy is larger than the headline.

Jesus cares for whole families on the mountain, not only a male tally.

Reformed

The number seals the scale of the sign. Women and children are included in the Spirit’s record of His care.

Comfort: the King’s table is wide. Providence counted every eater under the Messiah’s feast.

Matthew 15:39

39Then he sent away the multitudes, got into the boat, and came into the borders of Magdala.

Arminian

Crowds are sent away; Jesus boats toward Magadan. The feast ends in ordered departure, not clinging chaos.

Receive the gift, then follow His next leading. Grace for today includes releasing the mountain when He moves on.

Reformed

Dismissal and departure close the scene under His control. Magadan is the next appointed shore.

The Shepherd feeds, then leads onward. Chapter 15’s mercies travel with Him toward new confrontations and clearer confession.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on sits on the mountain, and heals crowds of lame, blind, crippled, mute, and many others; they glorify the God of Israel, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“The sea of Galilee - The Jews gave the name of seas to all large lakes. This was a hundred furlongs long, and forty broad. It lay on the borders of Galilee, and the city of Tiberias stood on its western shore. It was likewise styled the lake of Gennesareth: perhaps a corruption of Cinnereth, the name by which it was anciently called, Num. xxxiv, 11.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 15:29–39)

Reformed Notable voices

“Though it is unquestionably the same journey of Christ, on his return from the neighborhood of Sidon, that is related by Matthew and by Mark, yet in some points they do not quite agree. It is of little moment that the one says he came to the borders of Magdala, and the other, that he came to the coasts of Dalmanutha; for the cities were adjacent, being situated on the lake of Gennesareth, and we need not wonder that the district which lay between them received both names.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 15:29–39)
