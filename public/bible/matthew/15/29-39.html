Passage

Matthew 15

Verses 29–39 · ESV · Voices

From the coast Jesus returns beside the Sea of Galilee, sits on the mountain, and heals crowds of lame, blind, crippled, mute, and many others; they glorify the God of Israel. He then has compassion on a three-day crowd with nothing to eat, multiplies seven loaves and a few fish, satisfies about four thousand men besides women and children, gathers seven baskets of leftovers, and departs by boat to the region of Magadan.

Matthew 15:29

29Jesus went on from there and walked beside the Sea of Galilee. And he went up on the mountain and sat down there.

Arminian

Jesus walks the Galilean shore and sits on the mountain—the Teacher’s posture that invites approach.

Arminian welcome: the seated Lord is accessible. Grace draws crowds to the mountain; coming remains a real response.

Reformed

Return to Galilee and ascent to the mountain place Him as host of mercy. Sitting signals authority ready to receive the needy.

Reformed framing: the same Lord who praised Gentile faith now gathers a multitude under providence beside the sea.

Matthew 15:30

30And great crowds came to him, bringing with them the lame, the blind, the crippled, the mute, and many others, and they put them at his feet, and he healed them,

Arminian

Crowds bring every kind of brokenness and place sufferers at His feet. Healing answers the placement.

Classical Arminians see faith’s charity: neighbors carried to Jesus. Grace enables both bringing and receiving; none are healed by staying away.

Reformed

The catalog of misery meets the Messiah’s power. Laid at His feet, they are healed—effectual compassion in many forms.

Reformed comfort: Christ is sufficient for the full inventory of human ruin. The mountain becomes a hospital under His wordless and spoken authority.

Matthew 15:31

31so that the crowd wondered, when they saw the mute speaking, the crippled healthy, the lame walking, and the blind seeing. And they glorified the God of Israel.

Arminian

Wonder rises into doxology: they glorify the God of Israel. Healings reverse Isaiah’s hopes in living color.

Arminian joy: right seeing leads to right praise. Grace opens mouths once mute and hearts once dull to honor Israel’s God.

Reformed

Signs produce wonder and glory to Israel’s God. Even in a mixed crowd, Yahweh is praised for Messiah’s works.

Reformed emphasis: miracles serve worship. The Father is glorified as the Son restores bodies according to promised kingdom power.

Key contrast Both see healings that end in glory to the God of Israel. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled wonder that rightly turns to praise; Reformed readers typically stress God’s effectual display of messianic power that secures doxology.

Matthew 15:32

32Then Jesus called his disciples to him and said, “I have compassion on the crowd because they have been with me now three days and have nothing to eat. And I am unwilling to send them away hungry, lest they faint on the way.”

Arminian

Compassion speaks after three days: He will not send them away hungry. Bodily need matters to the Lord of hearts.

Classical Arminians hear sincere pity that acts. Disciples are drawn into that mercy; refusing the hungry would contradict the Master’s heart.

Reformed

Jesus interprets His own motive: compassion, duration, danger of fainting. Unwillingness to dismiss them hungry is holy resolve.

Reformed note: the Shepherd’s pity is purposeful. Providence has timed scarcity so His provision—not their planning—will feed them.

Key contrast Both confess real compassion that refuses to dismiss the hungry. Arminians typically stress a sincere mercy that summons disciples to participate; Reformed readers typically stress the Son’s sovereign pity unfolding the Father’s provision for this crowd.

Matthew 15:33

33And the disciples said to him, “Where are we to get enough bread in such a desolate place to feed so great a crowd?”

Arminian

The disciples see desolation and scale, not the Lord who just healed multitudes. Memory of earlier feeding is slow.

Arminian honesty: weak faith still asks “where?” Grace invites them to bring the question to Jesus rather than despair.

Reformed

Arithmetic without Christ: no bakery in the wilderness for so many. Their question forgets the Feeder already among them.

Reformed diagnosis: desolation is the stage God chooses. Human “where” will meet divine “how many loaves do you have?”

Matthew 15:34

34And Jesus said to them, “How many loaves do you have?” They said, “Seven, and a few small fish.”

Arminian

Jesus asks for inventory: seven loaves, a few small fish. Little is named so it can be offered.

Arminian pattern: bring what you have. Grace multiplies surrender, not pretended abundance.

Reformed

The question pulls their resources into His hands. Seven and a few are enough when He commands the feast.

Reformed emphasis: Christ inventories in order to claim. Scarcity confessed becomes the raw material of miracle.

Matthew 15:35

35And directing the crowd to sit down on the ground,

Arminian

Order precedes abundance. Sitting is trust that food is coming from His hands.

Arminian discipleship: obey the seating before you see the baskets. Grace enables resting under His direction.

Reformed

He directs; they sit. The King hosts before He multiplies.

Reformed calm: providence includes the posture of waiting. The ground becomes a tablecloth under His command.

Matthew 15:36

36he took the seven loaves and the fish, and having given thanks he broke them and gave them to the disciples, and the disciples gave them to the crowds.

Arminian

Take, thank, break, give—through disciples to crowds. The church learns to receive and distribute.

Classical Arminians see vocation: disciples are responsible channels. Grace makes both gratitude and generous passing-on possible.

Reformed

Thanksgiving rises; broken loaves move through the Twelve. Christ remains the giver; they are hands.

Reformed pattern: Word and thanks center the meal. What He breaks, He multiplies through servants He appoints.

Key contrast Both see Christ feeding through thankful breaking and disciples’ hands. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled stewardship in the distribution; Reformed readers typically stress the Son’s effectual provision that uses disciples as instruments.

Matthew 15:37

37And they all ate and were satisfied. And they took up seven baskets full of the broken pieces left over.

Arminian

All eat and are satisfied; seven baskets remain. Compassion did not ration meager bites.

Arminian gratitude: trust the Lord with need and gather leftovers wisely. Mercy is abundant without being wasteful.

Reformed

Satisfaction is complete; surplus is counted in seven baskets—echoing the seven loaves in reverse glory.

Reformed doxology: the math praises Jesus. He creates more than enough so faith remembers who fed the wilderness crowd.

Key contrast Both confess full satisfaction with leftover abundance. Arminians typically emphasize faith that receives multiplied mercy; Reformed readers typically emphasize sovereign provision that effectually fills and overflows.

Matthew 15:38

38Those who ate were four thousand men, besides women and children.

Arminian

Four thousand men, besides women and children: households fed. The census of mercy is larger than the headline.

Arminian pastoral joy: Jesus cares for whole families on the mountain, not only a male tally.

Reformed

The number seals the scale of the sign. Women and children are included in the Spirit’s record of His care.

Reformed comfort: the King’s table is wide. Providence counted every eater under the Messiah’s feast.

Matthew 15:39

39And after sending away the crowds, he got into the boat and went to the region of Magadan.

Arminian

Crowds are sent away; Jesus boats toward Magadan. The feast ends in ordered departure, not clinging chaos.

Arminian wisdom: receive the gift, then follow His next leading. Grace for today includes releasing the mountain when He moves on.

Reformed

Dismissal and departure close the scene under His control. Magadan is the next appointed shore.

Reformed note: the Shepherd feeds, then leads onward. Chapter 15’s mercies travel with Him toward new confrontations and clearer confession.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on sits on the mountain, and heals crowds of lame, blind, crippled, mute, and many others; they glorify the God of Israel—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 15:29–39.

Arminian Notable voices

“The sea of Galilee - The Jews gave the name of seas to all large lakes. This was a hundred furlongs long, and forty broad. It lay on the borders of Galilee, and the city of Tiberias stood on its western shore. It was likewise styled the lake of Gennesareth: perhaps a corruption of Cinnereth, the name by which it was anciently called, Num. xxxiv, 11.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 15:29–39)

Reformed Notable voices

“Though it is unquestionably the same journey of Christ, on his return from the neighborhood of Sidon, that is related by Matthew and by Mark, yet in some points they do not quite agree. It is of little moment that the one says he came to the borders of Magdala, and the other, that he came to the coasts of Dalmanutha; for the cities were adjacent, being situated on the lake of Gennesareth, and we need not wonder that the district which lay between them received both names.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 15:29–39)
