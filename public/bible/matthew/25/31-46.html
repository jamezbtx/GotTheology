Passage

Matthew 25

Verses 31–46 · ESV · Voices

Jesus ends the discourse with the Son of Man in glory, angels, and a throne of judgment over all nations. Sheep and goats are separated; the King identifies with ‘the least of these’ in hunger, thirst, strangerhood, nakedness, sickness, and prison. Eternal punishment and eternal life close the chapter—mercy’s fruit revealed, not a rival gospel.

Matthew 25:31

31When the Son of Man comes in his glory, and all the angels with him, then he will sit on his glorious throne.

Arminian

Glory, angels, throne—the Son of Man as King-Judge. The meek teacher now sits in open majesty.

Arminian awe: the same Jesus who invites also judges. Grace makes the throne good news for those who trust Him.

Reformed

Parousia glory and judicial session. Christ alone occupies the glorious throne.

Reformed certainty: the Judge is the crucified and risen Son. History bends toward His bench.

Matthew 25:32

32Before him will be gathered all the nations, and he will separate people one from another as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats.

Arminian

All nations gathered; shepherd-separation of sheep and goats.

Arminian mission weight: the nations truly stand before Him. The gospel’s call matters because this sorting is real.

Reformed

Universal assembly, personal separation. Shepherd imagery makes the Judge intimate and decisive.

Reformed gravity: God distinguishes peoples one from another with shepherd certainty.

Matthew 25:33

33And he will place the sheep on his right, but the goats on the left.

Arminian

Right and left—honored place versus rejection’s side.

Arminian clarity: destination language is not metaphor only. Choose the Shepherd’s voice now.

Reformed

Placement is the King’s act before the sentences are spoken.

Reformed note: verdict begins in where He sets you.

Matthew 25:34

34Then the King will say to those on his right, 'Come, you who are blessed by my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world.

Arminian

Blessed by the Father, inherit a kingdom prepared from the foundation.

Arminian joy: inheritance language keeps salvation gift, even as works will be cited as evidence. Prepared kingdom means grace older than our charity.

Reformed

Father’s blessing, prepared kingdom, foundation of the world—election’s warmth under inheritance speech.

Reformed comfort: the kingdom is prepared before our works. Sheep receive what God planned.

Key contrast Both hear gift in ‘inherit’ and ‘prepared.’ Arminians typically hold preparation together with a real history of enabled mercy; Reformed readers typically stress eternal preparation and works as evidences, not purchase price.

Matthew 25:35

35For I was hungry and you gave me food, I was thirsty and you gave me drink, I was a stranger and you welcomed me,

Arminian

Hunger, thirst, stranger—met with food, drink, welcome.

Arminian discipleship: love of neighbor is not optional garnish. Grace that saves creates concrete mercy.

Reformed

The King lists embodied cares. Faith’s fruit wears ordinary kindness.

Reformed pattern: the righteous are known by mercies that mirror the King’s heart.

Matthew 25:36

36I was naked and you clothed me, I was sick and you visited me, I was in prison and you came to me.'

Arminian

Naked, sick, prison—clothing, visiting, coming near.

Arminian pastoral: costly presence is kingdom work. Grace pushes toward the overlooked.

Reformed

Visitation and clothing continue the evidence trail.

Reformed note: holiness is social as well as vertical—love of the least under Christ’s claim.

Matthew 25:37

37Then the righteous will answer him, saying, 'Lord, when did we see you hungry and feed you, or thirsty and give you drink?

Arminian

The righteous are surprised—they did not keep a scorecard of feeding the King.

Arminian beauty: true mercy is often unconscious of its greatness. Love is not self-advertising virtue signaling.

Reformed

Surprise protects against works-pride. The sheep weren’t trading for a throne.

Reformed insight: evidence can be real without being mercenary. God sees what humility forgot to tally.

Matthew 25:38

38And when did we see you a stranger and welcome you, or naked and clothe you?

Arminian

Stranger and naked likewise unnoticed in their own memory.

Arminian encouragement: ordinary welcome counts in the King’s ledger of love.

Reformed

The questions continue—unselfconscious righteousness.

Reformed tone: the Spirit’s fruit often feels like simply doing the next faithful thing.

Matthew 25:39

39And when did we see you sick or in prison and visit you?'

Arminian

Sick and prison visits also surprise them.

Arminian note: showing up in hard places is how faith works through love.

Reformed

Presence with the suffering is remembered in heaven even when forgotten on earth.

Reformed comfort: God wastes no cup of cold water given in the King’s name.

Matthew 25:40

40And the King will answer them, 'Truly, I say to you, as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me.'

Arminian

As you did it to the least of these my brothers, you did it to me.

Arminian center: Christ so identifies with His vulnerable people that mercy to them is mercy to Him. Grace teaches us to see Jesus in the least.

Reformed

The King’s ‘Truly’ binds care for His brothers to care for Himself.

Reformed center: union with Christ makes service to His people service to Him. Works are tokens of belonging, not the root of it.

Key contrast Shared ground: Christ is served in the least. Arminians typically emphasize love as the living outworking of a faith that can be resisted or embraced; Reformed readers typically emphasize Spirit-wrought evidence in the elect without making mercy the meritorious cause of justification.

Matthew 25:41

41Then he will say to those on his left, 'Depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels.

Arminian

Depart, cursed, eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels.

Classical Arminians refuse to blunt this: judgment is personal and everlasting. The warning summons repentance and mercy now.

Reformed

The left-hand sentence is as clear as the right-hand blessing. Fire prepared for the devil becomes the goats’ place by the King’s word.

Reformed solemnity: Jesus teaches real eternal judgment. Holiness and justice meet at this throne.

Matthew 25:42

42For I was hungry and you gave me no food, I was thirsty and you gave me no drink,

Arminian

No food, no drink—omissions named as guilt.

Arminian warning: neglect can damn as surely as open violence. Grace still opens hands while there is time.

Reformed

Sins of omission stand in the indictment.

Reformed clarity: failing to love is rebellion, not mere incompleteness.

Matthew 25:43

43I was a stranger and you did not welcome me, naked and you did not clothe me, sick and in prison and you did not visit me.'

Arminian

No welcome, clothing, or visit—absence where presence was owed.

Arminian grief: indifference to the least is indifference to Christ. Turn toward them today.

Reformed

The negative catalog mirrors the sheep’s positives.

Reformed weight: what we will not do reveals whom we will not serve.

Matthew 25:44

44Then they also will answer, saying, 'Lord, when did we see you hungry or thirsty or a stranger or naked or sick or in prison, and did not minister to you?'

Arminian

They too are surprised—claiming they never saw the King in need.

Arminian edge: blindness to the least is self-serving. Ask for eyes grace can give.

Reformed

Self-justifying surprise without fruit.

Reformed diagnosis: empty religion can miss Christ while using His title ‘Lord.’

Matthew 25:45

45Then he will answer them, saying, 'Truly, I say to you, as you did not do it to one of the least of these, you did not do it to me.'

Arminian

As you did not do it to the least, you did not do it to me.

Arminian solemnity: omission toward the least is omission toward Jesus. The identification cuts both ways.

Reformed

The same Truly binds neglect of the least to neglect of the King.

Reformed finality: Christ’s solidarity with His own makes lovelessness personal against Him.

Matthew 25:46

46And these will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life."

Arminian

Eternal punishment and eternal life—parallel forever-words.

Classical Arminians hold both destinies as real outcomes meant to move hearers toward Christ and concrete love now.

Reformed

Parallel ‘eternal’ seals the seriousness of both ends.

Reformed confession: Jesus teaches everlasting punishment and everlasting life. The Judge’s word stands.

Key contrast Both affirm everlasting outcomes. Arminians typically press the warning as a genuine summons to faith working through love; Reformed readers typically press the certainty of the King’s separation and the evidential role of mercy in those appointed to life.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on faith’s fruit, judgment, and love for the needy—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside the sheep and the goats.

Arminian Notable voices

“Faith working by love… is the essence… of all true religion… We know that the whole of religion is contained in the love of God and of our neighbour.”
John Wesley · Sermon 91, “On Charity”
“I know, the saints who will be placed before the tribunal of the Divine Justice, have had Faith, and through Faith have performed good Works. But… they appear and stand before God with this confidence… that God has set forth his Son Jesus Christ as a propitiation through Faith in his blood… I do not read, that Christ is constituted a propitiation through Works… that we may also be justified by Works.”
Jacobus Arminius · Works, Vol. 1 (on justification / faith and works)

Reformed Notable voices

“Christ does not say that the works are the cause of salvation, but the tokens… by which we may know who are the children of God… God will acknowledge those works as done to himself.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 25:35–40)
“Faith… is the alone instrument of justification; yet… is not alone in the person justified, but is ever accompanied with all other saving graces, and is no dead faith, but works by love.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · 11.2 (1647)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
