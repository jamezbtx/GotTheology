Passage

Matthew 25

Verses 14–30 · ESV

Jesus continues the readiness theme with a journeying master who entrusts unequal talents according to ability, then returns after a long time to settle accounts. Faithful servants enter the master’s joy; the fearful, idle servant loses even what he hid and is cast into outer darkness—stewardship under delayed return.

Matthew 25:14

14For it will be like a man going on a journey, who called his servants and entrusted to them his property.

Arminian

The kingdom is like a departing master entrusting property to servants—trust before absence.

Arminian frame: grace confers real responsibility. What we hold is gift meant to be used, not merely admired.

Reformed

Journey, calling, entrustment: Christ frames discipleship as stewardship under His lordship.

Reformed framing: every talent is the Master’s property first. Assignment precedes accounting.

Matthew 25:15

15To one he gave five talents, to another two, to another one, to each according to his ability. Then he went away.

Arminian

Gifts differ—five, two, one—each according to ability, then departure.

Arminian pastoral: unequal measures do not excuse unfaithfulness. Grace fits the trust to the servant and still expects faithful trading.

Reformed

Distribution is personal and measured. Ability is acknowledged; ownership remains the master’s.

Reformed comfort and gravity: God is not arbitrary in assignment, yet every servant remains accountable for what was given.

Matthew 25:16

16He who had received the five talents went at once and traded with them, and he made five talents more.

Arminian

Five-talent faithfulness acts at once and doubles the gift.

Arminian joy: grace energizes prompt obedience. Delay is not wisdom when the Master’s goods are in hand.

Reformed

Immediate trading marks the faithful steward.

Reformed pattern: effectual calling produces fruitful labor. The increase is still the master’s.

Matthew 25:17

17So also he who had the two talents made two talents more.

Arminian

Two talents likewise produce two more—same faithfulness, smaller scale.

Arminian encouragement: the standard is fidelity, not matching someone else’s five. Grace honors proportionate trust.

Reformed

Equal commendation will meet unequal starting points when fidelity matches the trust.

Reformed note: God weighs faithfulness relative to entrustment, not envy of another’s sum.

Matthew 25:18

18But he who had received the one talent went and dug in the ground and hid his master's money.

Arminian

One talent is buried—preservation without fruit, fear without faith.

Arminian warning: hiding gift can feel safe and still be disobedience. Grace calls the timid to risk obedience now.

Reformed

Burial looks careful and is rebellion against the purpose of the trust.

Reformed diagnosis: sloth can wear the mask of caution. God sees the unused gift.

Matthew 25:19

19Now after a long time the master of those servants came and settled accounts with them.

Arminian

After a long time, settlement comes. Delay does not cancel audit.

Arminian realism: long absence tests motives. Live as those who will give account when He returns.

Reformed

Long time, then settling. The master’s calendar governs.

Reformed certainty: delayed parousia is still certain judgment and reward.

Matthew 25:20

20And he who had received the five talents came forward, bringing five talents more, saying, 'Master, you delivered to me five talents; here, I have made five talents more.'

Arminian

The five-talent servant reports increase with open hands.

Arminian delight: grace-grown fruit is gladly shown. Boasting is displaced by ‘you delivered… I have made.’

Reformed

Accounting begins with gift acknowledged and fruit presented.

Reformed joy: stewards confess both source and outcome under the master’s eye.

Matthew 25:21

21His master said to him, 'Well done, good and faithful servant. You have been faithful over a little; I will set you over much. Enter into the joy of your master.'

Arminian

Well done—faithful over little, set over much, enter the master’s joy.

Arminian hope: the Master rewards real fidelity without turning reward into wages that purchase salvation. Joy is shared life with Him.

Reformed

Commendation, promotion, joy: the master’s verdict defines the servant’s future.

Reformed comfort: ‘Well done’ is gracious speech to stewards kept faithful. Entrance into joy is gift and goal.

Key contrast Both treasure the master’s ‘Well done.’ Arminians typically emphasize rewarded faithfulness enabled by resistible grace; Reformed readers typically emphasize perseverance and reward as fruits of the master’s keeping grace.

Matthew 25:22

22And he also who had the two talents came forward, saying, 'Master, you delivered to me two talents; here, I have made two talents more.'

Arminian

The two-talent servant echoes the same confession and fruit.

Arminian note: smaller trust, same gospel pattern—receive, trade, report.

Reformed

Parallel faithfulness under a smaller sum.

Reformed equality of praise will follow; the narrative already shows matching integrity.

Matthew 25:23

23His master said to him, 'Well done, good and faithful servant. You have been faithful over a little; I will set you over much. Enter into the joy of your master.'

Arminian

Identical ‘Well done’ and joy for the two-talent servant.

Arminian comfort: God is not impressed only by large portfolios. Faithful little is great in His joy.

Reformed

Same words, same joy. The master’s pleasure tracks fidelity, not raw totals.

Reformed assurance: unequal gifts, equal entrance into joy for the faithful.

Matthew 25:24

24He also who had received the one talent came forward, saying, 'Master, I knew you to be a hard man, reaping where you did not sow, and gathering where you scattered no seed,

Arminian

The one-talent servant’s theology of the master is hard and extractive—reaping where he did not sow.

Arminian grief: a distorted view of God breeds buried gifts. Grace invites a truer knowledge of the Master that frees obedience.

Reformed

Accusation against the master’s character precedes the excuse.

Reformed diagnosis: bad theology of God licenses disobedience. The heart’s picture of the Lord shapes the hands.

Matthew 25:25

25so I was afraid, and I went and hid your talent in the ground. Here, you have what is yours.'

Arminian

Fear hid the talent; ‘what is yours’ is returned unused.

Arminian plea: fear is understandable and still not faithfulness. Bring the gift into the open while bankers and fields remain.

Reformed

Fear without faith produces burial, not stewardship.

Reformed severity: returning the unused gift does not equal obedience. God required increase fitting the trust.

Matthew 25:26

26But his master answered him, 'You wicked and slothful servant! You knew that I reap where I have not sown and gather where I scattered no seed?

Arminian

Wicked and slothful—the master names the heart and the inactivity.

Arminian warning: the Master’s rebuke is meant to convert hearers before the settlement. Leave sloth; trust Him enough to trade.

Reformed

Wickedness and sloth are paired. Knowledge claimed (‘you knew’) becomes self-indictment.

Reformed gravity: the servant’s own words condemn him under the master’s justice.

Matthew 25:27

27Then you ought to have invested my money with the bankers, and at my coming I should have received what was my own with interest.

Arminian

Even bank interest would have been better than burial.

Arminian edge: minimal faithful risk beats pious inactivity. Grace stretches timid servants toward some real obedience.

Reformed

The master argues from the servant’s own premise: if I am hard, at least seek interest.

Reformed logic: excuses collapse. Some fruit was always required.

Matthew 25:28

28So take the talent from him and give it to him who has the ten talents.

Arminian

Talent transferred to the one with ten—stewardship reallocated.

Arminian sobriety: unused trust can be reassigned. Hold gift loosely as the Master’s, urgently as your charge.

Reformed

The master redistributes for fruitfulness.

Reformed sovereignty: gifts serve the master’s household, not the servant’s security blanket.

Matthew 25:29

29For to everyone who has will more be given, and he will have an abundance. But from the one who has not, even what he has will be taken away.

Arminian

Abundance to the one who has; loss for the one who has not.

Arminian reading: living faith tends to grow; barren possession shrinks. Receive and trade while grace enables.

Reformed

Kingdom economy under the master: fruitful trust expands; empty hands lose even appearance.

Reformed proverb-force: God gives increase to living stewardship and removes unused forms.

Key contrast Both reject idle preservation as faithfulness. Arminians typically urge enabled, responsible trading now; Reformed readers typically stress the master’s prerogative to give and take according to true fruit.

Matthew 25:30

30And cast the worthless servant into the outer darkness. In that place there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.'

Arminian

Worthless servant cast into outer darkness—weeping and gnashing.

Classical Arminians will not soften the end: this warning aims at repentance and faithful stewardship before that darkness.

Reformed

Outer darkness is the master’s verdict on worthless stewardship.

Reformed solemnity: Jesus’ judgment language is real. The warning magnifies the joy offered to faithful servants.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
