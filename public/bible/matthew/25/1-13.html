Passage

Matthew 25

Verses 1–13 · ESV · Voices

Still answering the disciples’ watchfulness questions from chapter 24, Jesus pictures the kingdom as ten virgins awaiting a delayed bridegroom. Wisdom and folly turn on oil ready before the midnight cry; those prepared enter the feast, the door shuts, and the refrain returns: watch, for you know neither the day nor the hour.

Matthew 25:1

1Then the kingdom of heaven will be like ten virgins who took their lamps and went to meet the bridegroom.

Arminian

The kingdom is likened to ten virgins going out to meet the bridegroom—shared anticipation, shared lamps.

Arminian pastoral frame: grace invites many into the waiting people. The issue will not be whether they started out, but whether they remain ready when He comes.

Reformed

Jesus opens with a kingdom simile: virgins, lamps, bridegroom. The church’s posture is expectant meeting, not casual drift.

Reformed framing: Christ sets the scene of His own return. Belonging to the wedding party is privilege under the Bridegroom’s timeline.

Matthew 25:2

2Five of them were foolish, and five were wise.

Arminian

The split is moral and spiritual, not social: five foolish, five wise.

Classical Arminians hear a real fork in the road. Grace enables wisdom; folly is a resistible path within the same company of waiters.

Reformed

Jesus divides the company before the delay is told. Wisdom and folly are already present among those who look alike.

Reformed note: outward association with the bridegroom’s people is not the same as saving readiness. God alone finally sorts wise from foolish.

Matthew 25:3

3For when the foolish took their lamps, they took no oil with them,

Arminian

Foolishness shows in omission: lamps without oil. Form without reserve.

Arminian warning: profession without the Spirit’s sustaining grace leaves the lamp empty. Take oil seriously while dealers are still open.

Reformed

The foolish take lamps yet skip oil—appearance without provision.

Reformed diagnosis: empty religion can carry religious gear and still lack what the night requires. God exposes counterfeit preparation.

Matthew 25:4

4but the wise took flasks of oil with their lamps.

Arminian

The wise bring flasks with the lamps—forethought matching hope.

Arminian encouragement: grace trains wise readiness. Oil is not earned cleverness; it is received provision for a long wait.

Reformed

Wisdom packs oil with the lamp. Hope plans for delay under the master’s timing.

Reformed comfort: God supplies what perseverance needs. The wise are not self-made; they are kept ready.

Matthew 25:5

5As the bridegroom was delayed, they all became drowsy and slept.

Arminian

Delay comes; all grow drowsy and sleep—wise and foolish alike feel the wait.

Arminian realism: waiting is hard. Grace does not deny weariness; it keeps oil present even when eyes close.

Reformed

The bridegroom’s delay tests the company. Sleep falls on both groups.

Reformed gravity: providence includes delay. The difference is not who never tires, but who already carries oil.

Matthew 25:6

6But at midnight there was a cry, 'Here is the bridegroom! Come out to meet him.'

Arminian

Midnight cry: the bridegroom is here—come out to meet him. Sudden summons after long quiet.

Arminian urgency: the cry still lands as a genuine call. Live ready so the announcement is joy, not scramble.

Reformed

The cry cuts the night. Hidden timing becomes public arrival.

Reformed certainty: the bridegroom’s hour is His. The church lives between promise and cry.

Matthew 25:7

7Then all those virgins rose and trimmed their lamps.

Arminian

All rise and trim lamps—shared scramble at the announcement.

Arminian note: activity at the last moment is not the same as oil stored earlier. Trimming cannot invent what was never carried.

Reformed

Trimming is the common response; oil is not.

Reformed clarity: crisis religion looks busy. Only prior provision meets the bridegroom.

Matthew 25:8

8And the foolish said to the wise, 'Give us some of your oil, for our lamps are going out.'

Arminian

The foolish beg oil from the wise—lamps going out expose emptiness.

Arminian grief: borrowed faith cannot substitute for one’s own trust. Ask dealers now; do not wait to borrow at midnight.

Reformed

Going-out lamps reveal the lack. The request admits crisis too late.

Reformed severity: saving readiness is not transferable stock. Each servant answers for the oil entrusted.

Matthew 25:9

9But the wise answered, saying, 'Since there will not be enough for us and for you, go rather to the dealers and buy for yourselves.'

Arminian

The wise refuse a share that would empty both; they point to the dealers.

Arminian pastoral edge: some helps cannot be lent at the door. Grace still points the empty-handed to the only place oil is sold—before the feast begins.

Reformed

Not enough for us and you—honesty, not cruelty. Dealers, not last-second transfer.

Reformed boundary: the wise cannot become oil for the foolish. Personal faith and Spirit-wrought readiness are not poolable at the threshold.

Matthew 25:10

10And while they were going to buy, the bridegroom came, and those who were ready went in with him to the marriage feast, and the door was shut.

Arminian

While they buy, the bridegroom comes; the ready enter; the door shuts.

Arminian solemnity: opportunity has a real end. The shut door is meant to wake hearers while it is still day.

Reformed

Readiness enters; delay-shopping misses the feast. The shut door is the Master’s act.

Reformed solemnity: Christ’s timing closes the invitation on His terms. Being found ready is the only safe place.

Key contrast Both treat the shut door as real. Arminians typically stress a sincere warning that summons present readiness; Reformed readers typically stress the Bridegroom’s sovereign hour that separates the prepared from the unprepared.

Matthew 25:11

11Afterward the other virgins came also, saying, 'Lord, lord, open to us.'

Arminian

Late knocks and ‘Lord, lord’—religious speech after exclusion.

Arminian grief: words alone do not reopen what readiness would have entered. Come while the door is open.

Reformed

Afterward is too late. ‘Lord, lord’ without prior readiness fails.

Reformed echo of Matthew 7: confession without known relationship is empty at the door.

Matthew 25:12

12But he answered, 'Truly, I say to you, I do not know you.'

Arminian

The answer is devastating: ‘I do not know you.’

Classical Arminians refuse to soften this: the Bridegroom’s knowledge is relational, not merely census. Grace still pleads—be known by Him now.

Reformed

Truly—I do not know you. Recognition, not résumé, decides entry.

Reformed weight: Christ’s knowledge of His own is decisive. The warning guards false assurance.

Key contrast Shared ground: entrance turns on being known by the Lord. Arminians typically press an enabled, personal response of ongoing readiness; Reformed readers typically emphasize Christ’s effectual knowledge of His people and the impossibility of last-minute counterfeit oil.

Matthew 25:13

13Watch therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour.

Arminian

Watch—because day and hour remain unknown.

Arminian close: watching is active faithfulness, not anxiety. Grace sustains oil until He comes.

Reformed

The parable lands in the same command as chapter 24: watch.

Reformed close: unknown timing is God’s gift to keep the church awake. Readiness is the wise response to a certain bridegroom and an unrevealed hour.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus pictures the kingdom as ten virgins awaiting a delayed bridegroom—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 25:1–13.

Arminian Notable voices

“Then shall the kingdom of heaven - That is, the candidates for it, be like ten virgins - The bridemaids on the wedding night were wont to go to the house where the bride was, with burning lamps or torches in their hands, to wait for the bride groom's coming. When he drew near, they went to meet him with their lamps, and to conduct him to the bride.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 25:1–13)

Reformed Notable voices

“By this term is meant the condition of the future Church, which was to be collected by the authority and direction of Christ. He employs this remarkable title, that believers may not deceive themselves by an erroneous opinion that they have arrived at absolute perfection.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 25:1–13)
