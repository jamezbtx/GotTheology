Passage

Matthew 16

Verses 24–28 · ESV · Voices

Jesus applies the cross to every disciple: deny self, take up the cross, follow Him. He contrasts saving and losing life, warns against gaining the world at the soul’s cost, and promises the Son of Man’s coming in glory—with a pledge that some present will see Him coming in His kingdom.

Matthew 16:24

24Then Jesus told his disciples, “If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross and follow me.

Arminian

Discipleship is voluntary in form (“if anyone would”) and costly in content. Self-denial and cross-bearing are the shape of following Jesus.

Arminian preaching: grace enables this path and sincerely calls all to it. The cross is not for apostles only; every believer is summoned to a living response of costly trust.

Reformed

Coming after Jesus means death to self-rule. The cross is not jewelry; it is an instrument of execution embraced daily in loyalty to Christ.

Reformed accent: those the Lord effectually calls He also conforms to the crucified path. Grace does not cancel the cost; it creates cross-bearing people.

Matthew 16:25

25For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake will find it.

Arminian

The paradox is gospel arithmetic: clutching life loses it; releasing life for Jesus finds it. The “for my sake” keeps martyrdom and ordinary obedience Christ-centered.

Classical Arminians urge a real choice under grace—self-preservation or Jesus. Enabling grace makes the better loss possible.

Reformed

Jesus defines true life by allegiance to Himself. Self-salvation is self-destruction; Christ-centered loss is gain.

Reformed comfort: the promise “will find it” is sure for those who belong to Him. Their losing is not random tragedy but appointed fellowship with the Son.

Matthew 16:26

26For what will it profit a man if he gains the whole world and forfeits his soul? Or what shall a man give in return for his soul?

Arminian

Profit language exposes false ledgers. The whole world is a bad bargain for a forfeited soul; no currency buys a soul back.

Arminian urgency: the warning is sincere for every hearer. Grace still invites repentance before the trade becomes final.

Reformed

Jesus prices the soul beyond markets. World-gain with soul-loss is infinite bankruptcy.

Reformed gravity: only Christ’s redemption answers “what in return?” The rhetorical questions drive sinners to the Savior who alone can keep the soul.

Matthew 16:27

27For the Son of Man is going to come with his angels in the glory of his Father, and then he will repay each person according to what he has done.

Arminian

The cross-path ends in glory and judgment. Deeds matter as the fruit and evidence of a life’s allegiance.

Arminian reading: repayment according to works fits a judgment that takes real human response seriously, while salvation remains by grace through faith—works as living faith’s harvest, not a rival currency.

Reformed

The glorious coming is certain. Repayment according to works displays the public evidence of hearts already united to Christ—or exposed as empty.

Reformed balance: justification is by faith; judgment according to works vindicates that faith’s reality. Grace produces the obedience Christ will own.

Matthew 16:28

28Truly, I say to you, there are some standing here who will not taste death until they see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom.”

Arminian

Jesus pledges a near sight of kingdom glory—often linked to the Transfiguration (ch. 17), resurrection power, or Pentecost advance. Hope is not postponed forever.

Arminian encouragement: God gives foretastes that strengthen costly following. Trust the promise; keep carrying the cross.

Reformed

“Truly” seals a near fulfillment within that generation’s experience—Transfiguration, risen reign, or kingdom breakthrough—without canceling the final coming of v. 27.

Reformed confidence: Christ keeps time. Some would see kingdom majesty soon; the church still waits for the last appearing in the same sure hope.

Key contrast Both read discipleship as costly and hope as certain. Arminians typically emphasize the universal call to cross-bearing under enabling grace; Reformed readers typically emphasize that those Christ calls He keeps on the cross-shaped path until glory.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus applies the cross to every disciple: deny self, take up the cross, follow Him—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 16:24–28.

Arminian Notable voices

“If any man be willing to come after me - None is forced; but if any will be a Christian, it must be on these terms, Let him deny himself, and take up his cross - A rule that can never be too much observed: let him in all things deny his own will, however pleasing, and do the will of God, however painful. Should we not consider all crosses, all things grievous to flesh and blood, as what they really are, as opportunities of embracing God's will at the expense of our own?”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 16:24–28)

Reformed Notable voices

“And Peter, taking him aside, began to rebuke him. It is a proof of the excessive zeal of Peter, that he reproves his Master; though it would appear that the respect he entertained for him was his reason for taking him aside, because he did not venture to reprove him in presence of others.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 16:24–28)
