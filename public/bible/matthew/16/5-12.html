Sample passage

Matthew 16

Verses 5–12 · ESV

Crossing the lake, the disciples fret about forgotten bread while Jesus warns them of the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees. He recalls the feedings, exposes their little faith, and clarifies that the danger is false teaching, not empty baskets.

Matthew 16:5

5When the disciples reached the other side, they had forgotten to bring any bread.

Arminian

A small forgetfulness opens the next lesson. The disciples arrive empty-handed, preoccupied with provision they can see.

Arminian pastoral reading notices ordinary human anxiety under grace: followers of Jesus still misplace attention. The Lord will not scold hunger; He will redirect trust.

Reformed

Matthew’s detail is deliberate: forgotten bread becomes the stage for a warning about doctrine.

Reformed preaching often marks providence in small scenes—the Lord uses even the disciples’ lapse to teach. Need becomes the classroom where Christ corrects faith.

Matthew 16:6

6Jesus said to them, “Watch and beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees.”

Arminian

“Watch and beware” is a living command. Leaven works quietly; so does corrupting teaching. Jesus protects His friends by naming the danger.

Classical Arminians hear enabling grace in the warning itself: God does not leave disciples to drift. He calls for vigilant, responsible discernment that grace makes possible.

Reformed

Christ shepherds by caution. Leaven is small, pervasive, and transforming—an apt image for teaching that spreads through a community.

Reformed emphasis: the Lord who feeds multitudes also guards His church’s mind. Truth and error are not equal options; Jesus commands separation from soul-spoiling doctrine.

Matthew 16:7

7And they began discussing it among themselves, saying, “We brought no bread.”

Arminian

They hear “leaven” and think lunch. Spiritual metaphor collapses into logistics. The miss is almost comic—and deeply human.

Arminian honesty: even sincere disciples can resist clarity by staying in the safer worry. Grace keeps addressing them anyway.

Reformed

Mishearing Jesus is not rare among His own. Their private conference fixes on scarcity rather than the Teacher’s intent.

The Reformed note is patience and realism: without illumination, even recent miracles fade from practical memory. Christ must explain Himself again.

Matthew 16:8

8But Jesus, aware of this, said, “O you of little faith, why are you discussing among yourselves the fact that you have no bread?

Arminian

He knows their talk and names the root: little faith, not merely empty packs. Faith here is trust that should already have learned from His generosity.

Classical Arminians treat “little faith” as a real but incomplete trust—grace has begun, yet the heart still shrinks. The rebuke invites growth, not despair.

Reformed

Omniscient kindness: Jesus answers the conversation they never voiced to Him. “Little faith” diagnoses unbelief’s scale, not its absence.

Reformed pastoral use: Christ preserves and corrects weak disciples. Their faith is His gift under repair, not a self-made strength that failed a test.

Matthew 16:9

9Do you not yet perceive? Do you not remember the five loaves for the five thousand, and how many baskets you gathered?

Arminian

Memory is a servant of faith. Jesus points them back to baskets already counted—evidence they handled with their own hands.

Arminian urgency: God has given enough light in experience and Word for a trusting response. Forgetting mercy is a moral failure grace still seeks to heal.

Reformed

“Not yet” marks delayed perception despite privilege. The feedings were not mere wonders; they were tutorials in the Lord’s sufficiency.

Reformed accent: remembrance is commanded because God has acted. Faith feeds on God’s deeds; unbelief edits the past until anxiety feels reasonable.

Matthew 16:10

10Or the seven loaves for the four thousand, and how many baskets you gathered?

Arminian

A second feeding doubles the witness. Abundance repeated should silence panic about bread.

Both traditions agree: Christ is a generous Lord. Arminians press the invitation to keep trusting the One who has already provided.

Reformed

Two banquets, two tallies of leftovers—Matthew’s Jesus insists the pattern is clear.

Sovereignty here is practical: the same Lord who multiplied loaves rules their present lack. Anxiety about bread insults a proven Provider.

Matthew 16:11

11How is it that you fail to understand that I did not speak about bread? Beware of the leaven of the Pharisees and Sadducees.”

Arminian

Jesus restates the warning without softening it. The issue is teaching that ferments unbelief and hypocrisy, not bakery yeast.

Arminian application: hearers remain responsible to understand and obey. Grace clarifies; disciples must not hide in confusion when Christ speaks plainly.

Reformed

Failure to understand is exposed, then healed by repetition. Christ will not let the metaphor stay muddled.

Reformed comfort: the Shepherd teaches until the point lands. Doctrine matters because false leaven ruins the loaf of the church’s faith.

Matthew 16:12

12Then they understood that he did not tell them to beware of the leaven of bread, but of the teaching of the Pharisees and Sadducees.

Arminian

Understanding finally dawns. The danger is doctrinal and spiritual—teachings that puff pride, demand signs, and miss the Messiah.

Classical Arminians celebrate this as grace-enabled insight that still involves real learning. God opens minds; disciples truly come to see.

Reformed

Matthew closes the scene with illumination granted. What they could not grasp, they now grasp because Jesus persisted.

Reformed doxology: Christ not only warns; He gives understanding. The church is kept from deadly teaching by the Lord’s effective instruction.

Key contrast Both prize Christ’s warning and the disciples’ eventual clarity. Arminians typically describe understanding as grace-enabled perception disciples must embrace; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s effective teaching that secures true understanding in His own.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
