Passage

Matthew 16

Verses 21–23 · ESV · Voices

Immediately after Peter’s confession, Jesus begins to show the necessity of His suffering, death, and resurrection. Peter rebukes Him; Jesus rebukes Peter as a satanic hindrance for setting his mind on human things rather than God’s.

Matthew 16:21

21From that time Jesus began to show his disciples that he must go to Jerusalem and suffer many things from the elders and chief priests and scribes, and be killed, and on the third day be raised.

Arminian

“Must” is the grammar of saving love: the cross is necessary, not accidental. Messiah’s path runs through suffering to resurrection.

Arminian gospel: this death is sufficient for the world and sincerely offered in the preaching of Christ crucified. Grace calls all to trust the risen Lord.

Reformed

From this hinge (“from that time”) Jesus unfolds the decree-shaped path: suffer, be killed, be raised. The cross is not Plan B.

Reformed confession: He dies for His people according to the Scriptures, and the third-day rising is as certain as the suffering. Providence and promise hold both.

Matthew 16:22

22And Peter took him aside and began to rebuke him, saying, “Far be it from you, Lord! This shall never happen to you.”

Arminian

The confessor becomes the corrector. Affection without understanding tries to protect Jesus from the very mission that saves.

Arminian caution: sincere love can still resist God’s revealed will. Grace must keep reshaping our preferences until we love the cross Jesus chooses.

Reformed

Peter’s rebuke is pious mutiny. “Lord” on the lips and “never” in the plan do not fit together.

Reformed realism: even newly illuminated disciples can become obstacles when they edit God’s redemptive script. Christ will not negotiate the cross.

Matthew 16:23

23But he turned and said to Peter, “Get behind me, Satan! You are a hindrance to me. For you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man.”

Arminian

The severity matches the danger. To pull Jesus from the cross is to echo the tempter. Mindset—“things of God” vs “things of man”—is the issue.

Classical Arminians hear a call to continual repentance of worldly thinking. Grace enables a new mind; disciples must yield to it again and again.

Reformed

Jesus names the satanic character of cross-avoidance. Peter is momentarily a skandalon—a trap in the path of redemption.

Reformed pastoral edge: Christ protects the atonement from friendly fire. He keeps the mission on course, and He will yet restore the disciple He now rebukes.

Key contrast Both refuse a cross-less Christ. Arminians typically stress the disciple’s ongoing, grace-enabled turning of the mind; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s unyielding resolve to fulfill the Father’s saving purpose for His people.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on immediately after Peter’s confession, Jesus begins to show the necessity of His suffering, death, and resurrection—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 16:21–23.

Arminian Notable voices

“From that time Jesus began to tell his disciples, that he must suffer many things - Perhaps this expression, began, always implied his entering on a set and solemn discourse. Hitherto he had mainly taught them only one point, That he was the Christ.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 16:21–23)

Reformed Notable voices

“And Peter, taking him aside, began to rebuke him. It is a proof of the excessive zeal of Peter, that he reproves his Master; though it would appear that the respect he entertained for him was his reason for taking him aside, because he did not venture to reprove him in presence of others.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 16:21–23)
