Passage

Matthew 26

Verses 14–16 · WEB · Voices

Immediately after the anointing at Bethany, Judas Iscariot goes to the chief priests, bargains to deliver Jesus for silver, and from then on seeks an opportunity to betray Him.

Matthew 26:14

14Then one of the twelve, who was called Judas Iscariot, went to the chief priests

Arminian

Matthew names the wound: one of the Twelve. Proximity to Jesus does not erase responsible choice. Judas goes—on his own feet—to the priests.

Arminian readers refuse fatalism here. Grace had placed Judas under Jesus’ teaching and kindness; resisting that light remains an accountable act. The tragedy is not that God forced betrayal, but that a disciple walked toward it.

Reformed

“One of the twelve” intensifies the horror without denying God’s counsel. Judas’s going is his own wickedness, yet it advances the delivering-up Jesus has already announced (v. 2).

Moral agency and divine purpose. Scripture never calls Judas’s sin good; it does show that the cross is not derailed by it.

Matthew 26:15

15and said, “What are you willing to give me if I deliver him to you?” So they weighed out for him thirty pieces of silver.

Arminian

The question is mercenary: What are you willing to give me? Betrayal is priced. Thirty pieces of silver (echoing prophetic shame) expose a heart that weighs Jesus against coin.

Grace invites love of Christ above gain; Judas freely bargains Him away. The offer of silver does not coerce; it meets a will already turning from the Lord.

Reformed

The price and the plot meet. The priests pay; Judas sells. Zechariah’s shadowed sum hangs over the scene as Scripture’s own commentary on despised shepherding.

Providence can use even a purchase of blood without authoring the greed. God remains just; Judas remains guilty; the silver will not buy peace.

Key contrast Both treat Judas as morally responsible, not a puppet to pity into innocence. Arminians emphasize resistible grace rejected for greed; Reformed readers highlight that this wicked bargain still falls under God’s purpose to deliver up the Son without excusing the sin.

Matthew 26:16

16From that time he sought opportunity to betray him.

Arminian

From that moment: settled seeking. Betrayal becomes a project. Matthew warns that a turn against Christ can harden into habit.

For prayer: today matters. Assisting grace still calls back the wanderer until the end; yet prolonged seeking of opportunity shows how refusal deepens. Flee early; do not manage sin as strategy.

Reformed

Opportunity-hunting marks a fixed course. Judas is not merely surprised into a bad hour; he watches for a door.

Remaining sin, if indulged, seeks occasions. The Lord of the Passover still reigns, and this seeking will meet the hour Jesus named—yet the seeker’s guilt stands.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

What earlier interpreters on each side wrote about Matthew 26:14–16.

Arminian Notable voices

“They bargained with him for thirty pieces of silver - (About three pounds fifteen shillings sterling; or sixteen dollars sixty- seven cents) the price of a slave, Exod. xxi, 32.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 26:14–16)

Reformed Notable voices

“Then one of the twelve, who was called Judas Iscariot. Christ's admonition was so far from being of any avail for softening the heart of Judas, or producing any change in it for the better, that he immediately went away, without any concern, to transact an infamous bargain with his enemies.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 26:14–16)
