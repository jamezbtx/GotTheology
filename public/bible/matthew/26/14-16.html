Passage

Matthew 26

Verses 14–16 · ESV · Voices

Immediately after the anointing at Bethany, Judas Iscariot goes to the chief priests, bargains to deliver Jesus for silver, and from then on seeks an opportunity to betray Him.

Matthew 26:14

14Then one of the twelve, whose name was Judas Iscariot, went to the chief priests

Arminian

Matthew names the wound: one of the Twelve. Proximity to Jesus does not erase responsible choice. Judas goes—on his own feet—to the priests.

Classical Arminians refuse fatalism here. Grace had placed Judas under Jesus’ teaching and kindness; resisting that light remains a real, accountable act. The tragedy is not that God forced betrayal, but that a disciple walked toward it.

Reformed

“One of the twelve” intensifies the horror without denying God’s counsel. Judas’s going is his own wickedness, yet it advances the delivering-up Jesus has already announced (v. 2).

Reformed holding of both: moral agency and divine purpose. Scripture never calls Judas’s sin good; it does show that the cross is not derailed by it.

Matthew 26:15

15and said, “What will you give me if I deliver him over to you?” And they paid him thirty pieces of silver.

Arminian

The question is mercenary: What will you give me? Betrayal is priced. Thirty pieces of silver (echoing prophetic shame) expose a heart that weighs Jesus against coin.

Arminian pastoral edge: grace invites love of Christ above gain; Judas freely bargains Him away. The offer of silver does not coerce; it meets a will already turning from the Lord.

Reformed

Price and plot meet. The priests pay; Judas sells. Zechariah’s shadowed sum hangs over the scene as Scripture’s own commentary on despised shepherding.

Reformed sobriety: providence can use even a purchase of blood without authoring the greed. God remains just; Judas remains guilty; the silver will not buy peace.

Key contrast Both treat Judas as morally responsible, not a puppet to pity into innocence. Arminians typically emphasize resistible grace rejected for greed; Reformed readers typically emphasize that this wicked bargain still falls under God’s purpose to deliver up the Son without excusing the sin.

Matthew 26:16

16And from that moment he sought an opportunity to betray him.

Arminian

From that moment: settled seeking. Betrayal becomes a project. Matthew warns that a turn against Christ can harden into habit.

Arminian application: today matters. Assisting grace still calls back the wanderer until the end; yet prolonged seeking of opportunity shows how refusal deepens. Flee early; do not manage sin as strategy.

Reformed

Opportunity-hunting marks a fixed course. Judas is not merely surprised into a bad hour; he watches for a door.

Reformed pastoral watchfulness: remaining sin, if indulged, seeks occasions. The Lord of the Passover still reigns, and this seeking will meet the hour Jesus named—yet the seeker’s guilt stands.

Reformed Notable voices

“Then one of the twelve, who was called Judas Iscariot. Christ's admonition was so far from being of any avail for softening the heart of Judas, or producing any change in it for the better, that he immediately went away, without any concern, to transact an infamous bargain with his enemies.”
