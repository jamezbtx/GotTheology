Passage

Matthew 26

Verses 17–25 · WEB · Voices

On the first day of Unleavened Bread the disciples ask where to prepare the Passover. Jesus directs them to a certain man in the city; they prepare the meal. At table He announces that one of them will betray Him, identifies the traitor by the dipped morsel exchange with Judas, and Judas answers with a feigned question.

Matthew 26:17

17Now on the first day of unleavened bread, the disciples came to Jesus, saying to him, “Where do you want us to prepare for you to eat the Passover?”

Arminian

The disciples come with a practical, devoted question: Where shall we prepare? They still want to serve Jesus’ Passover plans. Faith shows itself in ordinary obedience as the feast arrives.

Grace keeps inviting disciples into responsible care for worship. They ask; they are ready to act on His word.

Reformed

The feast calendar moves under God’s hand. Disciples seek Jesus’ will for preparation—right instinct on the edge of the Passion.

Even logistics belong to providence. The Passover they prepare is the stage for the Lamb God has appointed.

Matthew 26:18

18He said, “Go into the city to a certain person, and tell him, ‘The Teacher says, “My time is at hand. I will keep the Passover at your house with my disciples.”’”

Arminian

Jesus sends them with authority and transparency: “My time is at hand.” He is not trapped by surprise; He chooses the house and owns the hour.

Arminians catch a Savior who freely walks toward the cross. The “time” is embraced, not merely endured, and disciples are invited to keep Passover with Him in trust.

Reformed

“My time is at hand” is messianic self-knowledge. The Teacher commands a house and names the hour the Father has set.

Christ’s Passion is timed, not random. The Passover table is prepared because His appointed time has come.

Key contrast Both refuse a Jesus dragged unwillingly into an unknown hour. Arminians emphasize His free, grace-revealing embrace of the time; Reformed readers underline the Father’s appointed hour that the Son knowingly keeps.

Matthew 26:19

19The disciples did as Jesus commanded them, and they prepared the Passover.

Arminian

Simple obedience: they did as directed and prepared. On the brink of betrayal and cross, faithfulness still looks like following clear instructions.

Assisting grace enables everyday obedience. Grand crises do not excuse neglecting the next obedient step.

Reformed

Directed—and done. The meal is ready because Christ’s word is effective among His own.

Providence often advances through ordinary obedience. The table of the new covenant begins with disciples who simply prepare.

Matthew 26:20

20Now when evening had come, he was reclining at the table with the twelve disciples.

Arminian

Evening comes; Jesus reclines with the Twelve—including the one already bargaining. Table fellowship is still offered even toward the traitor.

Nearness to Christ is a gift that can be received or betrayed. Grace at the table does not coerce loyalty; it deepens responsibility.

Reformed

The Twelve are at table with the Lord of the feast. Presence and impending betrayal sit together under God’s purpose.

Jesus does not flee the company. He reclines, and the night unfolds according to the word He will speak.

Matthew 26:21

21As they were eating, he said, “Most certainly I tell you that one of you will betray me.”

Arminian

Jesus discloses the wound before it lands: one of you. The warning is public enough to search every conscience at the table.

Arminian readers hear a sincere alert. Grace gives light so that repentance remains possible and self-examination is summoned. Betrayal is announced as human agency, not excused as inevitability that removes guilt.

Reformed

The “Most certainly” carries prophetic certainty. Jesus knows the traitor’s path and still speaks it into the meal.

Foreknowledge and announcement do not cancel Judas’s guilt. Christ’s word both reveals and judges; the betrayal He names is still Judas’s sin.

Matthew 26:22

22They were exceedingly sorrowful, and each began to ask him, “It isn’t me, is it, Lord?”

Arminian

Sorrow and self-suspicion: Is it I, Lord? Healthy fear of falling marks disciples who know their weakness. They do not first point at others.

Grace humbles the disciples. Responsible souls ask the searching question rather than assume immunity. Better sorrowful honesty than proud certainty.

Reformed

Grief sweeps the table. One after another they probe their own hearts before the Lord.

Christ’s word produces holy trembling among the elect who remain. Self-distrust under Jesus’ gaze is safer than confidence in the flesh.

Matthew 26:23

23He answered, “He who dipped his hand with me in the dish will betray me.

Arminian

Betrayal from shared dish sharpens the treachery: friendship and food, then delivering over. Jesus identifies the circle without yet naming the man for all to seize.

Intimacy rejected is still culpable. Table grace resisted becomes deeper guilt, not lighter.

Reformed

Shared dish underlines covenant nearness violated. Psalm-shaped treachery shadows the meal.

Scripture’s pattern of betrayal from within God’s people finds its sharpest instance here, under the Messiah’s own hand.

Matthew 26:24

24The Son of Man goes even as it is written of him, but woe to that man through whom the Son of Man is betrayed! It would be better for that man if he had not been born.”

Arminian

Two truths stand: the Son of Man goes even as written, and woe to the betrayer. Scripture’s path does not erase Judas’s responsibility. The “woe” is moral and personal.

Arminians insist God is not the author of Judas’s sin. Prophecy describes what wicked freedom will do; it does not coerce the evil. The warning is grave: better never born than die in this betrayal unrepentant.

Reformed

“As it is written” and “woe” together are the classic Reformed pair: divine purpose and human culpability. The Son goes by Scripture; the betrayer remains under judgment.

God’s decree does not make evil good. Judas’s act fulfills what was written and still deserves the woe Jesus pronounces.

Key contrast Both refuse to pit Scripture’s plan against moral guilt. Arminians stress that prophecy does not coerce Judas and that the woe addresses responsible refusal of grace; Calvinists stress that the Son’s going is written and sure while the betrayer’s woe remains fully just.

Matthew 26:25

25Judas, who betrayed him, answered, “It isn’t me, is it, Rabbi?” He said to him, “You said it.”

Arminian

Judas mirrors the others’ question but says “Rabbi,” not “Lord”—and already intends betrayal. Jesus’ “You said it” unmasks him without theatrical force.

Religious speech can cover a turning heart. Grace still confronts; Judas is not left without light. The tragedy is persistence in the path he chose.

Reformed

The betrayer asks; the Lord answers with identifying assent. Hypocrisy meets omniscient mercy that still tells the truth.

Jesus’ word seals the exposure. Judas’s question does not hide him from the Son of Man who goes as written.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What earlier interpreters on each side wrote about Matthew 26:17–25.

Arminian Notable voices

“On the first day of unleavened bread - Being Thursday, the fourteenth day of the first month, Exod. xii, 6, 15. Mark xiv, 12 Luke xxii, 7 The Master saith, My time is at hand - That is, the time of my suffering. He that dippeth his hand with me in the dish - Which it seems Judas was doing at that very time.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 26:17–25)

Reformed Notable voices

“To render the treachery of Judas more detestable, he points out the aggravated baseness of it by this circumstance, that he was meditating the act of betraying him while he sat with him at the holy table. For if a stranger had done this, it would have been more easily endured; but that one of his intimate friends should form such a design, and — what is more — that, after having entered into an infamous bargain, he should be present at the sacred banquet, was incredibly monstrous.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 26:17–25)
