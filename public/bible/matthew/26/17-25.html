Passage

Matthew 26

Verses 17–25 · ESV

On the first day of Unleavened Bread the disciples ask where to prepare the Passover. Jesus directs them to a certain man in the city; they prepare the meal. At table He announces that one of them will betray Him, identifies the traitor by the dipped morsel exchange with Judas, and Judas answers with a feigned question.

Matthew 26:17

17Now on the first day of Unleavened Bread the disciples came to Jesus, saying, “Where will you have us prepare for you to eat the Passover?”

Arminian

The disciples come with a practical, devoted question: Where shall we prepare? They still want to serve Jesus’ Passover plans. Faith shows itself in ordinary obedience as the feast arrives.

Arminian warmth: grace keeps inviting disciples into responsible care for worship. They ask; they are ready to act on His word.

Reformed

The feast calendar moves under God’s hand. Disciples seek Jesus’ will for preparation—right instinct on the edge of the Passion.

Reformed note: even logistics belong to providence. The Passover they prepare is the stage for the Lamb God has appointed.

Matthew 26:18

18He said, “Go into the city to a certain man and say to him, ‘The Teacher says, My time is at hand. I will keep the Passover at your house with my disciples.’”

Arminian

Jesus sends them with authority and transparency: “My time is at hand.” He is not trapped by surprise; He chooses the house and owns the hour.

Classical Arminians hear a Savior who freely walks toward the cross. The “time” is embraced, not merely endured, and disciples are invited to keep Passover with Him in trust.

Reformed

“My time is at hand” is messianic self-knowledge. The Teacher commands a house and names the hour the Father has set.

Reformed comfort: Christ’s Passion is timed, not random. The Passover table is prepared because His appointed time has come.

Key contrast Both refuse a Jesus dragged unwillingly into an unknown hour. Arminians typically stress His free, grace-revealing embrace of the time; Reformed readers typically stress the Father’s appointed hour that the Son knowingly keeps.

Matthew 26:19

19And the disciples did as Jesus had directed them, and they prepared the Passover.

Arminian

Simple obedience: they did as directed and prepared. On the brink of betrayal and cross, faithfulness still looks like following clear instructions.

Arminian application: assisting grace enables everyday obedience. Grand crises do not excuse neglecting the next obedient step.

Reformed

Directed—and done. The meal is ready because Christ’s word is effective among His own.

Reformed pastoral: providence often advances through ordinary obedience. The table of the new covenant begins with disciples who simply prepare.

Matthew 26:20

20When it was evening, he reclined at table with the twelve.

Arminian

Evening comes; Jesus reclines with the Twelve—including the one already bargaining. Table fellowship is still offered even toward the traitor.

Arminian gravity: nearness to Christ is a real gift that can be received or betrayed. Grace at the table does not coerce loyalty; it deepens responsibility.

Reformed

The Twelve are at table with the Lord of the feast. Presence and impending betrayal sit together under God’s purpose.

Reformed wonder: Jesus does not flee the company. He reclines, and the night unfolds according to the word He will speak.

Matthew 26:21

21And as they were eating, he said, “Truly, I say to you, one of you will betray me.”

Arminian

Jesus discloses the wound before it lands: one of you. The warning is public enough to search every conscience at the table.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere alert. Grace gives light so that repentance remains possible and self-examination is summoned. Betrayal is announced as real human agency, not excused as inevitability that removes guilt.

Reformed

The “truly” carries prophetic certainty. Jesus knows the traitor’s path and still speaks it into the meal.

Reformed holding: foreknowledge and announcement do not cancel Judas’s guilt. Christ’s word both reveals and judges; the betrayal He names is still Judas’s sin.

Matthew 26:22

22And they were very sorrowful and began to say to him one after another, “Is it I, Lord?”

Arminian

Sorrow and self-suspicion: Is it I, Lord? Healthy fear of falling marks disciples who know their weakness. They do not first point at others.

Arminian pastoral: grace humbles. Responsible souls ask the searching question rather than assume immunity. Better sorrowful honesty than proud certainty.

Reformed

Grief sweeps the table. One after another they probe their own hearts before the Lord.

Reformed use: Christ’s word produces holy trembling among the elect who remain. Self-distrust under Jesus’ gaze is safer than confidence in the flesh.

Matthew 26:23

23He answered, “He who has dipped his hand in the dish with me will betray me.

Arminian

Betrayal from shared dish sharpens the treachery: friendship and food, then delivering over. Jesus identifies the circle without yet naming the man for all to seize.

Arminian note: intimacy rejected is still culpable. Table grace resisted becomes deeper guilt, not lighter.

Reformed

Shared dish underlines covenant nearness violated. Psalm-shaped treachery shadows the meal.

Reformed reading: Scripture’s pattern of betrayal from within God’s people finds its sharpest instance here, under the Messiah’s own hand.

Matthew 26:24

24The Son of Man goes as it is written of him, but woe to that man by whom the Son of Man is betrayed! It would have been better for that man if he had not been born.”

Arminian

Two truths stand: the Son of Man goes as written, and woe to the betrayer. Scripture’s path does not erase Judas’s responsibility. The “woe” is moral and personal.

Classical Arminians insist God is not the author of Judas’s sin. Prophecy describes what wicked freedom will do; it does not coerce the evil. The warning is real: better never born than die in this betrayal unrepentant.

Reformed

“As it is written” and “woe” together are the classic Reformed pair: divine purpose and human culpability. The Son goes by Scripture; the betrayer remains under judgment.

Reformed confession: God’s decree does not make evil good. Judas’s act fulfills what was written and still deserves the woe Jesus pronounces.

Key contrast Both refuse to pit Scripture’s plan against moral guilt. Arminians typically stress that prophecy does not coerce Judas and that the woe addresses responsible refusal of grace; Reformed readers typically stress that the Son’s going is written and sure while the betrayer’s woe remains fully just.

Matthew 26:25

25Judas, who would betray him, answered, “Is it I, Rabbi?” He said to him, “You have said so.”

Arminian

Judas mirrors the others’ question but says “Rabbi,” not “Lord”—and already intends betrayal. Jesus’ “You have said so” unmasks him without theatrical force.

Arminian warning: religious speech can cover a turning heart. Grace still confronts; Judas is not left without light. The tragedy is persistence in the path he chose.

Reformed

The betrayer asks; the Lord answers with identifying assent. Hypocrisy meets omniscient mercy that still tells the truth.

Reformed sobriety: Jesus’ word seals the exposure. Judas’s question does not hide him from the Son of Man who goes as written.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
