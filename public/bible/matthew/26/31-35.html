Passage

Matthew 26

Verses 31–35 · ESV

On the way Jesus cites Zechariah: the shepherd will be struck and the sheep scattered, yet promises to go before them to Galilee after He is raised. Peter vows loyalty even to death; Jesus foretells a threefold denial before the rooster crows; the others echo Peter’s boast.

Matthew 26:31

31Then Jesus said to them, “You will all fall away because of me this night. For it is written, ‘I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered.’

Arminian

Jesus predicts universal falling away “because of me this night,” grounding it in Scripture. Warning comes before failure—light given so they might watch.

Arminian reading: the prediction is real, yet they remain responsible. Grace that forewarns still calls them to vigilance; scattering will be their collapse under pressure, not an excuse.

Reformed

Zechariah’s word frames the night: shepherd struck, sheep scattered. Jesus applies Scripture to their coming failure.

Reformed comfort-in-severity: even their falling lies inside what is written. The strike is under God’s hand; their scatter is foreknown—and will not be the last word.

Matthew 26:32

32But after I am raised up, I will go before you to Galilee.”

Arminian

Failure is not final in Jesus’ sentence. Resurrection and Galilee reunion are promised before they fall. Hope is spoken in advance.

Arminian gospel: grace restores. The same Lord who warns of scattering invites them to meet Him again—sincere promise for repentant disciples.

Reformed

Raised up, then going before them: Christ bookends their collapse with His victory and leadership.

Reformed assurance: the Shepherd who is struck will rise and gather. Their future with Him rests on His raising, not on their overnight courage.

Matthew 26:33

33Peter answered him, “Though they all fall away because of you, I will never fall away.”

Arminian

Peter contrasts himself with the others: they may, I never. Self-confidence speaks louder than Jesus’ warning.

Arminian pastoral: free agents can boast beyond strength. Grace calls for humility; resisting the Lord’s clear word about weakness is itself a dangerous choice.

Reformed

Peter’s “never” collides with Jesus’ “you will all.” Fleshly resolve outruns dependent faith.

Reformed diagnosis: confidence in self, even loyal-sounding, is not the same as grace. The Lord will humble this boast for Peter’s good.

Matthew 26:34

34Jesus said to him, “Truly, I tell you, this very night, before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times.”

Arminian

Specific, timed, numbered: tonight, before the rooster, three denials. Jesus loves Peter enough to shatter illusion.

Classical Arminians hear both warning and space for later repentance (which will come with tears). The forecast does not force Peter’s words; it exposes where self-trust will lead if he does not watch.

Reformed

Prophetic precision meets Peter’s pride. The denial is foretold in detail.

Reformed holding: Jesus’ knowledge is perfect; Peter’s agency remains. The crowing dawn will confirm the Lord’s word and begin Peter’s restoration path.

Key contrast Both deny that prediction cancels responsibility. Arminians typically stress warning grace and Peter’s accountable collapse under resistible self-trust; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s certain foretelling within a purpose that will restore the disciple He keeps.

Matthew 26:35

35Peter said to him, “Even if I must die with you, I will not deny you!” And all the disciples said the same.

Arminian

Louder vow, wider chorus: all the disciples say the same. Sincerity of feeling is not the same as kept faith under fire.

Arminian counsel: mean your love for Christ, but lean on grace, not on volume. The night will test whether resolve was prayer-backed or self-powered.

Reformed

Group resolve echoes Peter. Matthew shows how shared confidence can still be fleshly.

Reformed pastoral: better to believe Jesus about our weakness than to out-sing His warning. He will keep His word—about their failure and about Galilee.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
