Passage

Matthew 26

Verses 26–30 · ESV · Voices

During the Passover meal Jesus institutes the Lord’s Supper: bread as His body, the cup of the covenant in His blood poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins. He points ahead to drinking new with them in His Father’s kingdom, then they sing a hymn and go out to the Mount of Olives. (Key text: default Voices now; passage-specific quotes later as assigned.)

Matthew 26:26

26Now as they were eating, Jesus took bread, and after blessing it broke it and gave it to the disciples, and said, “Take, eat; this is my body.”

Arminian

Jesus takes, blesses, breaks, and gives—then commands: Take, eat; this is my body. The gift is offered to disciples at the table for reception by faith.

Classical Arminians emphasize the sincere offer in the sacrament. Christ truly presents Himself crucified for sinners; grace enables a free taking and eating in faith, not a mechanical force. The broken bread summons trust in His self-giving.

Reformed

The Lord’s own hands interpret the meal. Blessing, breaking, giving, and the word “this is my body” bind sign to Christ’s body given for His people.

Reformed sacramentology (without settling every intramural debate here): Christ authoritatively attaches the bread to His body. The Supper is His gift and command, received by faith as He feeds those He has claimed.

Matthew 26:27

27And he took a cup, and when he had given thanks he gave it to them, saying, “Drink of it, all of you,

Arminian

Thanksgiving, then a cup given with “Drink of it, all of you.” The invitation is corporate and earnest—every disciple addressed.

Arminian accent: “all of you” fits a sincere gospel hospitality at the table. Grace enables each to drink in faith; none is told the cup is a fiction for some and reality for others in the outward call of the meal.

Reformed

Thanks and distribution come from Christ. “All of you” binds the Eleven (and the table company under His word) to one cup He interprets.

Reformed note: the command is Christ’s. Participation is discipleship under His lordship; the meaning of the cup follows in His next words.

Matthew 26:28

28for this is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins.

Arminian

The cup is covenant blood, poured out for many for forgiveness. Jesus centers salvation in His atoning death—blood that remits sins.

Classical Arminians gladly preach a real, sufficient atonement sincerely offered in the gospel, received by faith. “For many” magnifies the wide saving purpose of the cross without turning forgiveness into automatic possession apart from trusting Christ. The Supper seals what faith receives: sins forgiven through His blood.

Reformed

Covenant blood, poured out for many, for forgiveness—this is the heart of the meal. Jesus interprets His death as the covenant sacrifice that remits sins.

Reformed confession: the “many” are the people Christ effectively redeems; the cup proclaims a definite, powerful atonement. Forgiveness is not a vague mood but blood-bought covenant mercy.

Key contrast Both adore the blood of the covenant for forgiveness. Arminians typically stress a sufficient atonement sincerely offered and received by faith (“many” as the wide gospel scope); Reformed readers typically stress an effectual atonement for the many given to the Son, proclaimed and sealed in the Supper.

Matthew 26:29

29I tell you I will not drink again of this fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it new with you in my Father’s kingdom.”

Arminian

Jesus frames the cup with hope: a pause until the kingdom feast with them. The Supper looks back to the cross and forward to shared joy.

Arminian encouragement: the promise is personal (“with you”) and inviting. Grace sustains disciples through the dark night toward a real future table with Christ.

Reformed

Abstaining now, feasting then: eschatology saturates the cup. The Father’s kingdom will host new wine with His people.

Reformed hope: Christ’s vow is sure. The same Lord who pours out covenant blood guarantees the reunion banquet for those united to Him.

Matthew 26:30

30And when they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives.

Arminian

Hymn, then Olivet. Worship does not stop when the path turns toward Gethsemane. They go out with praise still on their lips.

Arminian discipleship: grace enables praise and obedience together. Singing is a fitting response before the trial of watching and praying.

Reformed

Psalm-shaped praise, then the mount. The story moves under God’s script toward arrest and cross.

Reformed cadence: Word, sacrament, song, and then the appointed place. Providence leads from table to olives without dropping the thread of Christ’s purpose.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the cup of the covenant in His blood poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 26:26–30.

Arminian Notable voices

“Jesus took the bread - the bread or cake, which the master of the family used to divide among them, after they had eaten the passover. The custom our Lord now transferred to a nobler use. This bread is, that is, signifies or represents my body, according to the style of the sacred writers.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 26:26–30)

Reformed Notable voices

“And while they were eating, Jesus took bread. I do not understand these words to mean that with the paschal supper was mixed this new and more excellent supper, but rather that an end was then put to the former banquet. This is still more clearly expressed by Luke, when he says that, Christ gave the cup after that he had supped; for it would have been absurd that one and the same mystery should be broken off by an interval of time.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 26:26–30)
