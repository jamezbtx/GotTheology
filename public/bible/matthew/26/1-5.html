Sample passage

Matthew 26

Verses 1–5 · ESV

After the Olivet Discourse (chs. 24–25), Matthew opens the Passion narrative. Jesus announces that Passover is two days away and that the Son of Man will be delivered up to crucifixion, while the chief priests and elders plot a stealthy arrest—yet delay it for fear of the people.

Matthew 26:1

1When Jesus had finished all these sayings, he said to his disciples,

Arminian

Matthew’s familiar bridge (“when Jesus had finished…”) turns from teaching about the end to the path of the cross. The disciples are still learners under a living Teacher who now speaks of His own delivering up.

Classical Arminians hear grace that keeps speaking before the crisis. Jesus does not leave His followers without light; He prepares them so they may trust and follow rather than stumble when the hour arrives.

Reformed

The narrative hinge is deliberate: finished discourse, then Passion. The same Lord who taught about the coming of the Son of Man now interprets His near suffering as that Son’s path.

Reformed readers hear providence already naming the story’s center. Christ’s word governs what follows; the plot of men will not outrun the purpose He announces.

Matthew 26:2

2“You know that after two days the Passover is coming, and the Son of Man will be delivered up to be crucified.”

Arminian

Jesus joins Passover timing to His own destiny: delivered up and crucified. He speaks plainly so the disciples may understand the cost of redemption and not invent a different Messiah.

Arminian accent: the announcement is a sincere, responsible summons to faith. Grace enables hearers to receive this hard word rather than redefine the kingdom away from the cross. The Lamb goes knowingly; disciples are invited to keep trusting Him.

Reformed

Christ names both calendar and cross. “Delivered up” and “crucified” are not accidents He merely foresees; they belong to the mission of the Son of Man under the Father’s will.

Reformed comfort and sobriety: the Passion is not chaos overtaking Jesus. He interprets the feast and His death together, so the church confesses a Savior who was not surprised by the hour appointed.

Key contrast Both refuse a Messiah who stumbles into death. Arminians typically stress Jesus’ knowing self-giving and a sincere call that enables disciples to trust the cross-shaped path; Reformed readers typically stress the Son of Man’s appointed delivering-up within the Father’s decree that will not fail.

Matthew 26:3

3Then the chief priests and the elders of the people gathered in the palace of the high priest, whose name was Caiaphas,

Arminian

While Jesus speaks of Passover and the cross, leaders gather under Caiaphas. Matthew shows religious power assembling against the Messiah—not ignorance alone, but deliberation.

Arminian pastoral note: human freedom is real and accountable. These leaders choose counsel against the light they have. Grace that invites does not erase the seriousness of resisting the Christ.

Reformed

The scene shifts to Caiaphas’s palace: priests and elders convene. Human plotting is on stage, yet it will serve ends they do not intend.

Reformed reading holds both: culpable counsel and sovereign overruling. God is not the author of their sin, yet their gathering falls inside a purpose that leads to the cross Christ has just named.

Matthew 26:4

4and plotted together in order to arrest Jesus by stealth and kill him.

Arminian

The plot is explicit: stealthy arrest and death. Matthew does not soften the moral gravity. Leaders who should shepherd Israel scheme to destroy the Holy One.

Classical Arminians underscore responsible evil. Prevenient light and prior revelation do not coerce; people may harden. The gospel still offers mercy even as this verse warns how far refusal can go.

Reformed

Stealth and murder are the council’s aim. Scripture records their intent without flattery.

Reformed preaching names their guilt and God’s rule together: wicked hands will seize Jesus, yet Acts will later say He was delivered up according to the definite plan of God. Sin remains sin; providence remains providence.

Matthew 26:5

5But they said, “Not during the feast, lest there be an uproar among the people.”

Arminian

Fear of the crowd, not fear of God, restrains their timetable. They want Jesus dead without a feast-time uproar. Politics and self-preservation shape their “wisdom.”

Arminian application: hearers remain free and responsible under social pressure. Grace invites a different courage—honoring Christ even when the crowd is loud. Their delay is still rebellion dressed as prudence.

Reformed

Their caveat (“not during the feast”) shows calculated caution. They hope to manage risk; they do not reckon with the Lord of the feast.

Reformed irony of providence: the very feast they fear becomes the setting God uses. Human caution cannot fence in God’s hour. What they postpone in fear, God appoints in wisdom for the Passover Lamb.

Key contrast Both expose fear of people over fear of God. Arminians often stress accountable choice under pressure and grace that enables a better response; Reformed readers often stress that even their delayed timetable cannot thwart the Passover purpose Christ has announced.

About this sample. Commentary here is written for product demonstration. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
