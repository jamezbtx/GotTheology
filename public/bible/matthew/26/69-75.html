Passage

Matthew 26

Verses 69–75 · ESV · Voices

In the courtyard a servant girl, then another, then bystanders confront Peter about being with Jesus. He denies with oath and curse that he does not know the man. The rooster crows; Peter remembers Jesus’ word, goes out, and weeps bitterly.

Matthew 26:69

69Now Peter was sitting outside in the courtyard. And a servant girl came up to him and said, “You also were with Jesus the Galilean.”

Arminian

Courtyard pressure begins with a servant girl’s observation: you were with Jesus. Small voice, large test.

Arminian realism: responsible faith is tried in ordinary conversations, not only in councils. Grace is needed for a clear yes.

Reformed

Peter sits; identification comes. The night Jesus foretold opens with a simple sentence.

Reformed note: providence uses an unlikely examiner. The word about three denials starts its clock.

Matthew 26:70

70But he denied it before them all, saying, “I do not know what you mean.”

Arminian

First denial: feigned confusion before them all. Fear edits loyalty in public.

Classical Arminians name this as real failure under resistible pressure—Peter could have confessed; he chose evasion. Grace still pursues the denier toward tears.

Reformed

Denial one: I do not know what you mean. The rock crumbles in speech.

Reformed honesty: elect disciples can fall grievously. Christ’s earlier word proves true; Peter’s restoration will also.

Matthew 26:71

71And when he went out to the entrance, another servant girl saw him, and she said to the bystanders, “This man was with Jesus of Nazareth.”

Arminian

He moves to the entrance; another girl tells bystanders. Escape relocates the test; witness spreads.

Arminian lesson: changing seats does not remove the call to own Christ. Responsibility follows him.

Reformed

Second recognition, wider audience. Nazareth’s Jesus is named again.

Reformed pacing: the foretold pattern unfolds step by step. No detail of Jesus’ warning is idle.

Matthew 26:72

72And again he denied it with an oath: “I do not know the man.”

Arminian

Denial with an oath: I do not know the man. Intensity rises; he will not say the Name with belonging.

Arminian grief: oath-backed falsehood compounds sin. Free agency deepens the wound; repentance will need matching depth.

Reformed

Oath denial. Peter invokes sacred bond to seal a lie about Jesus.

Reformed severity and hope: this is how far self-trust falls. The Lord who predicted it will still look him into life later.

Matthew 26:73

73After a little while the bystanders came up and said to Peter, “Certainly you too are one of them, for your accent betrays you.”

Arminian

Accent betrays him—identity leaks when words deny. Bystanders press certainty.

Arminian note: creation and history leave marks; pretending neutrality about Jesus often fails. Better truthful confession than thicker cover.

Reformed

Galilean speech exposes him. Providence uses even accent.

Reformed detail: the third wave arrives “after a little while,” matching Jesus’ timeline toward the rooster.

Matthew 26:74

74Then he began to invoke a curse on himself and to swear, “I do not know the man.” And immediately the rooster crowed.

Arminian

Curse and swear: peak denial—then the rooster. Nature itself preaches Jesus’ word.

Classical Arminians hear both Peter’s accountable climax of refusal and mercy in the crowing that stops the spiral. The sign is grace interrupting, not mere humiliation.

Reformed

Self-curse, oath, denial—then immediate crow. Prophecy punctuates the night.

Reformed certainty: Jesus’ “truly” stands. The rooster is the Lord’s appointed messenger to Peter’s conscience.

Key contrast Both treat the denials as real sin and the rooster as mercy-tinged fulfillment. Arminians typically stress Peter’s free, deepening failure and grace that interrupts toward repentance; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s sure word coming to pass and the keeping purpose that will restore this disciple.

Matthew 26:75

75And Peter remembered the saying of Jesus, “Before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times.” And he went out and wept bitterly.

Arminian

Memory of Jesus’ saying, then bitter tears. Repentance begins where the Lord’s word lands on a broken heart.

Arminian hope: weeping is not the end—Galilee still waits. Grace that enables later restoration already works in godly sorrow. No straw man of cheap tears; this is grief over denying the Lord.

Reformed

He remembers Jesus’ saying—then goes out weeping bitterly. The word does the wounding and the beginning of healing.

Reformed comfort: bitter tears under Christ’s true word are the path home. The Shepherd who foresaw the denials also foresaw this grief and the breakfast of restoration still to come.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on in the courtyard a servant girl, then another, then bystanders confront Peter about being with Jesus—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 26:69–75.

Arminian Notable voices

“He denied with an oath - To which possibly he was not unaccustomed, before our Lord called him. Surely thou art also one of them, for thy speech discovereth thee - Malchus might have brought a stronger proof than this. But such is the overruling providence of God, that the world, in the height of their zeal, commonly catch hold of the very weakest of all arguments against the children of God.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 26:69–75)

Reformed Notable voices

“Here we see that there is no necessity for a severe contest, or for many forces or implements of war, to overpower a man; for any man, who is not supported by the hand of God, will instantly fall by a slight gale or the rustling of a falling leaf. Peter undoubtedly was not less courageous than any of us, and he had already given no ordinary proof of his valor, though it was exercised in a rash and improper manner; and yet he does not wait until he is dragged before the tribunal of the high priest, or until his enemies attempt to put him to death by violence, but, terrified by a woman's voice, immediately denies his Master.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 26:69–75)
