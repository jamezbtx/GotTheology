Passage

Matthew 26

Verses 36–46 · WEB · Voices

At Gethsemane Jesus takes Peter, James, and John aside, grieves to the point of death, and thrice prays that the cup pass if possible—yet submits to the Father’s will. The disciples sleep despite His call to watch and pray; He returns the third time announcing the hour and the betrayer’s arrival. (Key text: default Voices now; passage-specific quotes later as assigned.)

Matthew 26:36

36Then Jesus came with them to a place called Gethsemane, and said to his disciples, “Sit here, while I go there and pray.”

Arminian

Gethsemane means “oil press.” Jesus stations most disciples and goes to pray. The Son faces the cup in communion with the Father, not in isolated stoicism.

He models dependent prayer. Grace invites us to the same posture—bring anguish to God rather than numb it.

Reformed

Place and purpose: Gethsemane for prayer. The Passion’s next step is communion with the Father.

Even the obedient Son prays. Providence does not cancel petition; it fills it with holy struggle.

Matthew 26:37

37He took with him Peter and the two sons of Zebedee, and began to be sorrowful and severely troubled.

Arminian

Inner circle witnesses sorrow and trouble. The Messiah’s humanity is not theater; grief is true.

Arminian readers refuse a docetic Jesus. The One who offers rest (11:28) now tastes crushing sorrow—so that His sympathy and sacrifice are true.

Reformed

Sorrow and trouble begin under the Father’s eye. The spotless Son feels the weight of the hour.

Christ’s anguish is not weakness that threatens the plan; it is the Holy One entering the depths appointed for our salvation.

Matthew 26:38

38Then Jesus said to them, “My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death. Stay here and watch with me.”

Arminian

Soul-sorrow unto death, plus a request: watch with me. Jesus seeks companionship in prayerful vigilance.

The ask is not imaginary. Disciples are responsible to stay awake with Him; grace enables watching, and sleep will be failure they own.

Reformed

He names death-deep sorrow and commands watchfulness. The Shepherd wants witnesses who pray.

Christ does not hide His grief from His own. His word exposes how much we need grace to watch even one hour.

Matthew 26:39

39He went forward a little, fell on his face, and prayed, saying, “My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass away from me; nevertheless, not what I desire, but what you desire.”

Arminian

Face to the ground: honest plea that the cup pass if possible, then full submission—“not what I desire, but what you desire.” Desire and obedience meet without sin.

Wesleyan Arminians treasure both the sincerity of the petition and the free, holy yielding of the Son. This is not reluctant coercion but willing alignment with the Father. The cup of wrath is faced in love; disciples learn to pray desire under “Your desire.”

Reformed

The cup is the appointed suffering; “if possible” and “Your desire” display perfect sonship. The Son’s human will bows to the Father without conflict of purpose in the Godhead.

Gethsemane seals that Calvary is the Father’s will embraced by the Son. Salvation’s cup is not accidental; it is consented under heaven’s decree.

Key contrast Both worship the Son’s submission. Arminians emphasize Jesus’ free, holy yielding and a model of sincere petition under God’s will; Reformed interpreters highlight that the cup is the Father’s appointed will which the Son effectually embraces for the redemption of His people.

Matthew 26:40

40He came to the disciples and found them sleeping, and said to Peter, “What, couldn’t you watch with me for one hour?

Arminian

Sleep meets sorrow. Jesus addresses Peter—the boaster—about one hour’s watch. The failure is personal and mild in tone, yet searching.

Responsible disciples can fail quickly. Grace still comes asking; repentance starts with admitting we did not watch.

Reformed

Found sleeping: the contrast with Jesus’ vigil is stark. Peter is named because his vow still echoes.

Christ’s question exposes flesh. One hour proves how much keeping depends on God, not on overnight zeal.

Matthew 26:41

41Watch and pray, that you don’t enter into temptation. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak.”

Arminian

Watch and pray—means of not entering temptation. Willing spirit, weak flesh: Jesus dignifies desire and diagnoses inability without excuse.

What comes through is an earnest command enabled by assisting grace. Prayer is the appointed way to resist; neglect is culpable. The verse refuses both perfectionism and fatalism.

Reformed

Watchfulness and prayer are Christ’s prescription against entering temptation. Willing spirit, weak flesh names the Christian’s conflict.

The command drives us to dependent prayer, not to self-trust. God supplies what He requires as we ask; sleeping flesh will otherwise fold.

Matthew 26:42

42Again, a second time he went away and prayed, saying, “My Father, if this cup can’t pass away from me unless I drink it, your desire be done.”

Arminian

Second prayer clarifies: if the cup cannot pass without His drinking, Your will be done. Resolve deepens through repeated prayer.

Persistence in prayer shapes willing obedience. Grace strengthens the yes without erasing the cost.

Reformed

The second petition assumes the cup may be necessary—and yields. “Your desire be done” is Gethsemane’s heart.

The Son drinks what the Father will not let pass. Redemption’s necessity is faced and accepted.

Matthew 26:43

43He came again and found them sleeping, for their eyes were heavy.

Arminian

Their eyes are heavy, and they fall asleep again. Weakness is understandable—and still failure to watch. Matthew is gentle and truthful.

Bodily limits are true; so is the call. Grace meets heavy eyes with further invitation, not instant discard.

Reformed

Heavy eyes explain without excusing. The narrative shows how quickly resolve sinks.

Jesus returns again. Their sleep does not undo His prayer or His purpose for them.

Matthew 26:44

44He left them again, went away, and prayed a third time, saying the same words.

Arminian

Jesus perseveres in prayer while they sleep. Importunity marks holy desire under the Father’s will.

Keep praying the costly yes. Grace sustains repetition when feelings do not invent new phrases.

Reformed

Triple prayer with the same words: steadfast submission. The Son finishes the vigil His disciples cannot.

Where they fail to watch, He prevails in prayer. Our hope rests on His faithfulness in the garden.

Matthew 26:45

45Then he came to his disciples and said to them, “Are you still sleeping and resting? Behold, the hour is at hand, and the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners.

Arminian

The hour is at hand; betrayal into sinners’ hands. Jesus wakes them into the crisis He has accepted.

The moment of decision arrives for Judas and for the watching church. Christ’s acceptance of the hour invites our trust, not our panic.

Reformed

Hour and betrayal announced as present. The Son of Man steps into sinners’ hands on schedule.

“At hand” is not chaos. What Jesus prayed through, He now meets as the appointed time.

Matthew 26:46

46Arise, let’s be going. Behold, he who betrays me is at hand.”

Arminian

Rise, let us be going—toward the betrayer, not away. Courage born of submitted will moves forward.

Obedience follows prayer. Grace that yielded in the garden now walks into the arrest without fleeing the Father’s path.

Reformed

Jesus goes forward to meet the betrayal. The Shepherd leads even here.

Christ is not cornered; He goes. The betrayer’s “at hand” meets the Son who has already said yes to the cup.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Older voices from both traditions on Matthew 26:36–46.

Arminian Notable voices

“Then cometh Jesus to a place called Gethsemane - That is, the valley of fatness. The garden probably had its name from its soil and situation, laying in some little valley between two of those many hills, the range of which constitutes the Mount of Olives.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 26:36–46)

Reformed Notable voices

“Mark and Matthew add a more minute description of the place. But Luke expresses what is still more to the purpose, that Christ came there according to his custom. Hence we infer, that he did not seek retirement for the purpose of concealing himself, but, as if he had made an assignation with his enemies, he presented himself to death.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 26:36–46)
