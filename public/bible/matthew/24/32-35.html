Passage

Matthew 24

Verses 32–35 · ESV · Voices

Jesus adds the fig-tree lesson: tender branch and leaves mean summer is near; likewise “all these things” mean He is near, at the gates. He seals it with a solemn “Truly” about this generation and the unmatched permanence of His words over heaven and earth.

Matthew 24:32

32"From the fig tree learn its lesson: as soon as its branch becomes tender and puts out its leaves, you know that summer is near.

Arminian

A simple agricultural lesson: tender branch and leaves mean summer is near. Jesus teaches readable signs without locking a calendar date.

Arminian pastoral tone: grace trains discernment. Disciples can learn the season’s nearness and respond wisely—watchfulness without date-setting panic.

Reformed

The fig tree’s lesson is analogical clarity. God built seasons into creation as a teacher.

Reformed reading: Christ authorizes natural observation as a parable for redemptive timing. Nearness is knowable in kind even when the hour stays hidden.

Matthew 24:33

33So also, when you see all these things, you know that he is near, at the very gates.

Arminian

Seeing “all these things” yields knowledge: He is near, at the gates. Recognition should move the will.

Classical Arminians urge responsive readiness. Nearness is not trivia; grace enables living as those who expect the Gate-stander soon.

Reformed

“All these things” signal His nearness at the gates. Jesus grants interpretive certainty at the level of season, not stopwatch.

Reformed assurance: God wants His church knowing the King is near. The gates image comforts sufferers and humbles scoffers.

Matthew 24:34

34Truly, I say to you, this generation will not pass away until all these things take place.

Arminian

Solemn “Truly”: this generation will not pass until these things occur. Jesus binds His honor to fulfillment.

Arminian seriousness: take His timeline language as trustworthy speech that calls for faith. Debates over “generation” must not blunt the summons to trust the Speaker.

Reformed

Christ’s amen ties fulfillment to “this generation” in His discourse sense. His word will not fail its referent.

Reformed confidence: whatever the precise scope scholars weigh, Jesus’ oath stands. God keeps what the Son swears; history bends to His Truly.

Key contrast Both take Jesus’ “Truly” as binding. Arminians typically emphasize trusting His promise as a call to present faith; Reformed readers typically emphasize the certainty of fulfillment under His sovereign word.

Matthew 24:35

35Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away."

Arminian

Creation may pass; His words will not. The most stable thing in the universe is what Jesus has said.

Classical Arminians rest here for ethics and hope: build life on His speech. Grace invites clinging to permanent words in a temporary world.

Reformed

Heaven and earth are temporary; Christ’s words are not. Revelation outlasts cosmos.

Reformed confession: the Son’s speech shares God’s permanence. Every prophecy and command in this discourse rests on a foundation firmer than sky and soil.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus adds the fig-tree lesson: tender branch and leaves mean summer is near; likewise “all these things” mean He is—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 24:32–35.

Arminian Notable voices

“Learn a parable - Our Lord having spoke of the signs preceding the two grand events, concerning which the apostles had inquired, begins here to speak of the time of them. And to the question proposed, ver. 3, concerning the time of the destruction of Jerusalem, he answers ver. Concerning the time of the end of the world, he answers chap. xxiv,”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 24:32–35)

Reformed Notable voices

“I do not suppose the meaning of this to be merely that, during the state of confusion which has been mentioned, there will be as evident a sign that the coming of Christ is nigh, as that by which we know with certainty that the summer is at hand, when the trees begin to grow green; but, in my opinion, Christ expresses something else. For as in winter the trees, contracted by the severity of the cold, show greater vigor, but in spring lose their toughness, and appear more feeble, and are even cleft asunder to open up passage for fresh twigs, so the afflictions by which, according to the perception of the flesh, the Church is softened, do not in any way impair its vigor.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 24:32–35)
