Passage

Matthew 24

Verses 1–14 · ESV · Voices

Leaving the temple after the woes of chapter 23, Jesus predicts its total ruin. On the Mount of Olives the disciples ask about timing, His coming, and the end of the age. He answers first with pastoral warnings: deception, wars, famines, earthquakes, persecution, cold love—and the gospel’s worldwide witness. These are birth pains, not yet the end; endurance and proclamation mark His people until the end comes.

Matthew 24:1

1Jesus left the temple and was going away, when his disciples came to point out to him the buildings of the temple.

Arminian

Jesus leaves the temple; the disciples linger on its buildings. Awe at stone and gold can miss the Lord’s departure.

Classical Arminians hear a gentle check on misplaced trust. Grace invites turning from sacred architecture to the departing Christ—eyes free to follow Him rather than cling to what looks permanent.

Reformed

Matthew joins exit and admiration: Jesus goes away while disciples showcase the temple’s grandeur.

Reformed readers see providence in the leave-taking. The house He has just judged (23:38) still dazzles His followers; the coming word will reorder their hope from buildings to the Son’s sure speech.

Matthew 24:2

2But he answered them, "You see all these, do you not? Truly, I say to you, there will not be left here one stone upon another that will not be thrown down."

Arminian

Jesus answers their sightseeing with a solemn “Truly”: not one stone left on another. The temple’s glory is temporary under judgment.

Arminian pastoral edge: God warns so hearers may repent and relocate trust. The prediction is mercy as well as doom—space to stop resting in what will fall and cling to the King who speaks.

Reformed

Christ’s “Truly” seals a total demolition: every stone thrown down. History will keep His word (A.D. 70 and the larger judgment pattern).

Reformed comfort: the Lord who rules nations also rules temples. What dazzles disciples cannot outlast His decree; faith rests on His promise, not on walls He has marked for ruin.

Key contrast Both take the temple’s fall as certain under Jesus’ word. Arminians typically emphasize a sincere warning that calls for relocated trust; Reformed readers typically emphasize Christ’s sovereign decree that history will fulfill.

Matthew 24:3

3As he sat on the Mount of Olives, the disciples came to him privately, saying, "Tell us, when will these things be, and what will be the sign of your coming and of the end of the age?"

Arminian

Privately on Olivet they ask three linked questions: when, what sign of His coming, and the end of the age. Honest discipleship seeks light from Jesus Himself.

Classical Arminians honor the ask: grace welcomes inquiring faith. Timing and signs are not forbidden topics when brought to the Teacher who answers without flattery or panic.

Reformed

The setting shifts to Olivet; the questions bind temple ruin, parousia, and age-end. Jesus will answer on His terms, not theirs alone.

Reformed gravity: God receives the private inquiry and orders the discourse. Revelation is gift; the disciples’ curiosity is gathered into Christ’s prophetic instruction for the church.

Matthew 24:4

4And Jesus answered them, "See that no one leads you astray.

Arminian

First word: watch against deception. Before calendars and catastrophes, Jesus guards their minds.

Arminian warmth: grace enables vigilance. Leading astray is a real danger; disciples are summoned to an active “see”—refuse seduction, stay near the true Christ.

Reformed

Jesus opens with a command: see that no one leads you astray. Discernment is the first gift of this prophecy.

Reformed emphasis: the Good Shepherd protects His flock by word. False Christs will come; His people are kept by His warning and by the Spirit who applies it.

Matthew 24:5

5For many will come in my name, saying, 'I am the Christ,' and they will lead many astray.

Arminian

Many claim His name and title—“I am the Christ”—and many follow. Counterfeit messiahs exploit longing for rescue.

Classical Arminians press responsibility: light is given to test claims by Jesus’ true word. Grace strengthens refusal of glamorous lies; leading astray is tragic, not inevitable for every hearer.

Reformed

False Christs multiply and succeed with many. The pattern is foreknown and foretold.

Reformed sobriety: deception is under Christ’s predictive lordship. The elect are not finally abandoned to impostors; the warning itself is a means of preservation.

Key contrast Both warn against false messiahs. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled discernment that freely refuses deception; Reformed readers typically stress Christ’s foretelling and preserving purpose amid widespread leading astray.

Matthew 24:6

6And you will hear of wars and rumors of wars. See that you are not alarmed, for this must take place, but the end is not yet.

Arminian

Wars and rumors will fill the ears—yet “see that you are not alarmed.” Turmoil is not automatic proof that the end has arrived.

Arminian pastoral care: grace steadies nerves. Believers can refuse panic, keep loving, and wait for clearer fulfillment rather than baptize every conflict as the finale.

Reformed

Conflict is certain (“must take place”) yet incomplete (“the end is not yet”). Providence includes war without collapsing history into the last hour.

Reformed comfort: God’s necessity and God’s patience meet. Disciples are commanded out of alarm into trust that the timetable remains in the Father’s hand.

Matthew 24:7

7For nation will rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom, and there will be famines and earthquakes in various places.

Arminian

International strife, famine, and earthquake mark the age in various places. Creation and politics groan together.

Classical Arminians urge mercy in the midst: such pains are calls to prayer, aid, and repentance—not excuses for cold hearts. Grace keeps mission alive while the earth shakes.

Reformed

Jesus catalogs geopolitical and creational upheaval. These are not random; they sit inside His foresight.

Reformed confession: the Lord of nations and nature speaks beforehand. Famines and quakes serve His larger purposes even while they wound—summons to fear God more than the tremor.

Matthew 24:8

8All these are but the beginning of the birth pains.

Arminian

“Birth pains” names real agony that is still only beginning. History moves toward delivery, not endless cycle.

Arminian hope: labor points to life. Grace invites endurance through early contractions without mistaking them for the final cry—or giving up before the birth.

Reformed

Birth-pain imagery frames the age: painful, purposeful, progressive under God.

Reformed reading: God likens judgment-and-hope history to labor He oversees. The beginning is real suffering; the end is His appointed outcome, not chaos without a midwife.

Matthew 24:9

9"Then they will deliver you up to tribulation and put you to death, and you will be hated by all nations for my name's sake.

Arminian

Tribulation, death, and worldwide hatred come “for my name’s sake.” Loyalty to Jesus costs.

Classical Arminians do not soften the call: grace enables costly faithfulness. Hatred is not failure of the gospel’s sincerity; it is the world’s answer that disciples may refuse to return in kind.

Reformed

Persecution is predicted as certain for Jesus’ name. The church’s suffering is not outside the plan.

Reformed courage: Christ’s name draws hatred and also holds His people. Martyrdom and tribulation advance under His lordship; the nations’ hate cannot cancel His claim.

Matthew 24:10

10And then many will fall away and betray one another and hate one another.

Arminian

Many fall away, betray, and hate. Pressure exposes shallow attachment and fractures community.

Arminian pastoral warning: falling away is a real danger Scripture names. Grace calls for persevering love now—refuse betrayal, keep soft hearts, cling to Christ before the test intensifies.

Reformed

Apostasy, betrayal, and mutual hate rise under pressure. Jesus is not surprised by failed profession.

Reformed gravity: God distinguishes enduring faith from temporary zeal. The prediction humbles pride and steadies the remnant who remain by His keeping power.

Matthew 24:11

11And many false prophets will arise and lead many astray.

Arminian

False prophets multiply alongside false Christs. Religious speech can still lead many astray.

Classical Arminians urge testing spirits by the gospel Jesus actually taught. Grace supplies Scripture and Spirit so hearers can refuse glamorous error.

Reformed

False prophecy is a standing feature of the last-days pattern Jesus describes. Many are led astray—yet not beyond His foresight.

Reformed protection: the true Prophet has spoken first. His church is guarded by the written word and by elders who hold the pattern of sound teaching.

Matthew 24:12

12And because lawlessness will be increased, the love of many will grow cold.

Arminian

Rising lawlessness chills love. Moral chaos does not only break laws; it freezes charity.

Arminian urgency: grace rekindles love. Disciples can and must resist coldness—practice mercy, forgive, keep the first love warm while iniquity multiplies around them.

Reformed

Lawlessness increases; many loves cool. Jesus diagnoses the age’s spiritual climate.

Reformed lament and hope: God names the chill without surrendering the remnant. Love that endures is His work in His people even when lawlessness abounds.

Matthew 24:13

13But the one who endures to the end will be saved.

Arminian

Endurance to the end is tied to salvation. The path is persevering faith, not a momentary spark.

Classical Arminians hear a living summons: continue in Christ. Grace enables endurance; the promise is for the one who does endure—not a bare slogan that cancels the call to keep going.

Reformed

Salvation belongs to the one who endures to the end. Perseverance is the mark of true faith.

Reformed confession: those God saves He keeps; endurance is evidence of His preserving grace. The verse comforts saints in tribulation and warns empty profession.

Key contrast Both require endurance unto salvation. Arminians typically emphasize a sincere, grace-enabled call to keep enduring; Reformed readers typically emphasize perseverance as the fruit and proof of God’s preserving grace.

Matthew 24:14

14And this gospel of the kingdom will be proclaimed throughout the whole world as a testimony to all nations, and then the end will come.

Arminian

The kingdom gospel goes worldwide as testimony to all nations—then the end. Mission is not optional filler before the finish.

Arminian missionary heartbeat: grace sincerely offers Christ to the nations. The church partners in proclamation; the end waits on a global witness God intends to be real and heard.

Reformed

Universal proclamation precedes the end. The gospel’s reach is certain under Christ’s word—“will be proclaimed.”

Reformed confidence: God will get His testimony to the nations. Mission rests on decree as well as duty; the end comes on schedule after the witness He ordained.

Key contrast Both tie the end to worldwide gospel witness. Arminians typically stress the church’s real, grace-empowered duty to proclaim to all nations; Reformed readers typically stress the certainty that God will accomplish that testimony before the end He has set.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on leaving the temple after the woes of chapter 23, Jesus predicts its total ruin—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 24:1–14.

Arminian Notable voices

“There shall not be left one stone upon another - This was most punctually fulfilled; for after the temple was burnt, Titus, the Roman general, ordered the very foundations of it to be dug up; after which the ground on which it stood was ploughed up by Turnus Rufus. As he sat on the Mount of Olives - Whence they had a full view of the temple.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 24:1–14)

Reformed Notable voices

“The disciples undoubtedly perceived that Christ was paying, as it were, his last adieu to the temple. It remained, therefor that he should erect a new temple far more magnificent, and that he should produce a more flourishing condition of the kingdom, as had been foretold by the Prophets; for he had nothing to do with that temple, in which every thing was opposed to him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 24:1–14)
