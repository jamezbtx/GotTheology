Passage

Matthew 4

Verses 18–22 · WEB · Voices

Walking by the Sea of Galilee, Jesus calls Simon Peter and Andrew from their nets, then James and John from their boat with Zebedee; all four leave immediately and follow Him.

Matthew 4:18

18Walking by the sea of Galilee, he saw two brothers: Simon, who is called Peter, and Andrew, his brother, casting a net into the sea; for they were fishermen.

Arminian

Jesus walks, sees, and finds brothers at ordinary work—casting nets. Discipleship begins in a workplace, not a seminary lobby.

The call meets people where they are. Grace notices fishermen; response will soon be asked in freedom under that grace.

Reformed

The King is walking and seeing. Simon and Andrew are located by providence at the nets when the word comes.

Vocation becomes the stage for calling. “He saw” is not casual glance; it is the Shepherd identifying those He will summon.

Matthew 4:19

19He said to them, “Come after me, and I will make you fishers for men.”

Arminian

“Come after me” is personal command; “I will make you” is promise. Jesus reshapes their trade into mission—people, not only fish.

Arminians find a serious invitation with enabling: follow, and He will form you. Both affirm: discipleship is Jesus’ making, not self-reinvention.

Reformed

Imperative and future meet: follow, and I will make. The Maker of fishers speaks first; transformation is His work.

Effectual calling is audible here. The same voice that commands also creates the new craft—missionaries formed by the King’s word.

Key contrast Both affirm Jesus’ initiative and the promise to remake them. Arminians emphasize a sincere, grace-enabled call they freely obey; Calvinists stress an effectual call that infallibly produces followers and fishers.

Matthew 4:20

20They immediately left their nets and followed him.

Arminian

Immediately—nets left, path taken. Delay would have been another sermon; obedience is the commentary.

James and John leave the boat and their father at once, and that is what responsive faith looks like. Grace that calls also enables leaving; responsibility remains to walk after Him without postponing forever.

Reformed

Immediacy marks the power of the call. Nets that defined them are released in a moment.

When the Shepherd calls, sheep follow. Leaving is evidence of a word that works—not mere advice filed for later.

Matthew 4:21

21Going on from there, he saw two other brothers, James the son of Zebedee, and John his brother, in the boat with Zebedee their father, mending their nets. He called them.

Arminian

Another pair, same pattern: He sees, He calls. James and John are mending nets with their father—family business interrupted by the King.

The call is personal and costly. Grace does not erase fathers and boats; it reorders loves so Christ comes first.

Reformed

Going on, seeing, calling—Jesus’ initiative repeats. Zebedee is present; the summons still lands on the sons.

The same sovereign voice that took Peter and Andrew now claims James and John. The church’s core is gathered by calling, not by committee.

Matthew 4:22

22They immediately left the boat and their father, and followed him.

Arminian

Boat and father left immediately. Loyalty to Jesus outranks livelihood and even family claims when they compete.

Arminian readers preach costly, willing discipleship under grace. The leave-taking is choice enabled by the call—shared with Reformed readers that following Jesus may rearrange every tie.

Reformed

Immediate departure from boat and father displays the call’s force. Priority shifts because the King has spoken.

Discipleship can mean leaving good gifts for a greater Lord. The Father who calls also holds families; yet when His Son summons, obedience does not negotiate forever.

Key contrast Both honor costly, immediate following. Arminians often emphasize grace-enabled free response that leaves nets and father; Reformed readers often emphasize effectual calling that produces such leaving as its fruit.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on walking by the Sea of Galilee, Jesus calls Simon Peter and Andrew from their nets, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“Simon called Peter, and Andrew his brother - Why did not Jesus Christ call some of the eminent Scribes or Pharisees to publish his Gospel, and not poor unlearned fishermen, without credit or authority? Because it was the kingdom of heaven they were to preach, and their teaching must come from above: besides, the conversion of sinners, though it be effected instrumentally by the preaching of the Gospel, yet the grand agent in it is the Spirit of God.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the New Testament (on Matt 4:18–22)

Reformed Notable voices

“As this history is placed by Luke after the two miracles, which we shall afterwards see, an opinion has commonly prevailed, that the miracle, which is here related by him, was performed some time after that they had been called by Christ. But the reason, which they allege, carries little weight: for no fixed and distinct order of dates was observed by the Evangelists in composing their narratives.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 4:18–22)
