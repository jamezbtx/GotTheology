Passage

Matthew 4

Verses 12–17 · ESV · Voices

Hearing of John’s arrest, Jesus withdraws to Galilee, settles in Capernaum by the sea in Zebulun and Naphtali, fulfills Isaiah’s light-for-darkness promise, and begins preaching: “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.”

Matthew 4:12

12Now when he heard that John had been arrested, he withdrew into Galilee.

Arminian

John’s arrest is news that moves Jesus. Withdrawal to Galilee is wisdom, not panic—the mission continues under pressure.

Arminian pastoral reading: God’s call advances even when heralds suffer. Hearers in hard places are still summoned; grace does not wait for safe politics.

Reformed

Providence times the shift. John’s silencing does not stall the kingdom; it relocates the voice to Galilee.

Reformed accent: Jesus’ withdrawal is purposeful under the Father’s plan. The Baptist’s arrest becomes the hinge on which public ministry in the north begins.

Matthew 4:13

13And leaving Nazareth he went and lived in Capernaum by the sea, in the territory of Zebulun and Naphtali,

Arminian

Nazareth is left; Capernaum by the sea becomes home base in Zebulun and Naphtali. Jesus plants Himself among ordinary lakeside life.

Classical Arminians note the nearness of the King: He dwells where people fish and trade. The invitation will be local and sincere—kingdom news in a working town.

Reformed

Geography is theology. Capernaum is chosen; tribal names recall Israel’s story and Isaiah’s map.

Reformed confidence: the Son’s address is not random. Settling by the sea places light where prophecy said darkness sat—mission plotted by Scripture.

Matthew 4:14

14so that what was spoken by the prophet Isaiah might be fulfilled:

Arminian

Matthew frames the move as fulfillment. Jesus’ address is Isaiah’s promise arriving—God keeps His word so people may believe and turn.

Arminian hope: prophecy becomes invitation. What God spoke, He now does in history so hearers can respond to the light.

Reformed

“So that… might be fulfilled” is Matthew’s signature of providence. The prophet’s line lands on schedule.

Reformed reading: Scripture’s Author moves the Messiah’s feet. Capernaum is fulfillment geography—God’s decree written beforehand and walked out in time.

Matthew 4:15

15“The land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali, the way of the sea, beyond the Jordan, Galilee of the Gentiles—

Arminian

Isaiah names borderlands and “Galilee of the Gentiles.” The light is aimed at mixed, overlooked places, not only the religious center.

Arminian mission tone: the gospel’s reach is wide. Grace that dawns in Gentile Galilee signals a sincere call that will not stay tribal.

Reformed

Border sea-road and Gentile Galilee sit in the quote. God had already mapped mercy to the margins.

Reformed accent: election’s Lord is also the Lord of nations in promise. The territories named are the stage He chose for the kingdom’s opening word.

Matthew 4:16

16the people dwelling in darkness have seen a great light, and for those dwelling in the region and shadow of death, on them a light has dawned.”

Arminian

Darkness and death’s shadow name the human condition; a great light dawns. Seeing the light is gift—and summons to walk in it.

Classical Arminians preach dawn as prevenient brightness: God shines so people may turn. Shared doxology: without Christ’s light, we sit in death’s region.

Reformed

Dwelling in darkness is not a neutral preference; it is bondage. Light dawns as God’s act before our climb.

Reformed comfort: illumination is grace. The great light is Christ Himself arriving—sovereign dawn for a people who could not invent daybreak.

Key contrast Both confess real darkness and Christ’s real light. Arminians typically stress a gracious, enabling light that sincerely invites all hearers to repent; Reformed readers typically stress effectual dawn—God shines to bring His people out of death’s shadow.

Matthew 4:17

17From that time Jesus began to preach, saying, “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.”

Arminian

From that time Jesus preaches John’s keynote as His own: repent, for the kingdom is near. The King Himself invites turning.

Arminian urgency: the call is sincere and for the hearers. Prevenient grace awakens; people remain responsible to repent. Kingdom nearness is mercy’s pressure—turn while the light has dawned.

Reformed

The kingdom’s nearness is God’s doing; repentance is the fitting human response under His reign. Jesus’ first Galilean sermon is royal summons.

Reformed accent: the King announces what He brings. Where His Word runs, He effectually gathers a repenting people—same gospel demand John preached, now from Messiah’s own lips.

Key contrast Both treat repentance as urgent under a near kingdom. Arminians typically stress a grace-enabled, resistible-in-principle call all should answer; Reformed readers typically stress God’s initiative in bringing the kingdom and effectually summoning His people to turn.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on repentance, kingdom nearness, and grace—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Jesus’ first Galilean summons.

Arminian Notable voices

“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)

Reformed Notable voices

“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
