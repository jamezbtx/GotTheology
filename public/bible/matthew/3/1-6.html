Passage

Matthew 3

Verses 1–6 · ESV

Years after the Nazareth settlement, Matthew jumps to John’s public ministry: a wilderness preacher calls Israel to repent because the kingdom of heaven is near, fulfills Isaiah’s voice, and baptizes confessing crowds in the Jordan.

Matthew 3:1

1In those days John the Baptist came preaching in the wilderness of Judea,

Arminian

Matthew opens the adult story with a preacher, not a palace. John appears in the wilderness of Judea with a word for the nation.

Classical Arminians often stress that God sends a sincere call through human heralds. The wilderness setting strips away religious comfort so the message can be heard. Shared ground: God prepares the way before the King arrives.

Reformed

“In those days” is providence, not accident. The Baptist arrives on schedule in the place God chose for a preparatory ministry.

Reformed accent: John’s appearing is God’s initiative. The wilderness preacher is a gift to Israel before the Son’s public work begins.

Matthew 3:2

2“Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.”

Arminian

The first word is repent, and the reason is nearness of the kingdom. Turn because the King is near, not merely because life is hard.

Arminian pastoral urgency: the call is real and for the hearers. Grace awakens; people remain responsible to turn. Both traditions preach that kingdom nearness demands a changed mind and life.

Reformed

Repentance is required because God’s reign has drawn near. The kingdom does not wait on human scheduling.

Reformed comfort and warning: God brings His kingdom; the call to repent is His sovereign summons. Where the Word runs, hearts are confronted with the King who is already advancing.

Key contrast Both treat repentance as urgent under a near kingdom. Arminians typically stress a sincere, grace-enabled call all hearers should answer; Reformed readers typically stress God’s initiative in bringing the kingdom and effectually summoning His people to turn.

Matthew 3:3

3For this is he who was spoken of by the prophet Isaiah when he said, “The voice of one crying in the wilderness: ‘Prepare the way of the Lord; make his paths straight.’”

Arminian

John is not inventing a brand; he is Isaiah’s voice. Preparation means clearing obstacles so the Lord may come.

Arminian warmth: God has long intended this invitation. Prophecy becomes preaching so people can prepare. Straight paths are something hearers are called to make under grace.

Reformed

Matthew ties John to written prophecy. The Lord has a way, and He sends a voice to announce it.

Reformed confidence: Scripture’s promise lands in history. The wilderness cry is God’s own plan unfolding so that the Lord Himself will walk among His people.

Matthew 3:4

4Now John wore a garment of camel's hair and a leather belt around his waist, and his food was locusts and wild honey.

Arminian

Camel’s hair, leather belt, locusts, and wild honey mark John as a prophet in Elijah’s line, not a court chaplain. His life matches his message of urgency.

Pastoral shared note: authenticity matters. A call to repent loses force when the messenger’s life is padded with the comforts he will not challenge.

Reformed

John’s austere dress and diet signal prophetic continuity. God equips His herald with a life that fits the word.

Reformed reading: even the messenger’s habits sit under providence. The focus remains the Word he carries, not a cult of personality.

Matthew 3:5

5Then Jerusalem and all Judea and all the region about the Jordan were going out to him,

Arminian

Crowds stream from city and countryside. The Word draws people out of ordinary routines toward repentance and baptism.

Arminian hope: when the gospel is preached with clarity, many may come. Drawing is gracious and wide in the narrative’s first movement—yet coming still requires response.

Reformed

A regional movement gathers at the Jordan. God is stirring Israel before the Messiah’s public debut.

Reformed accent: such going-out is not random revivalism alone; it is the Lord preparing a people by His Word through His prophet.

Matthew 3:6

6and they were baptized by him in the river Jordan, confessing their sins.

Arminian

Baptism joins confession. Immersion in the Jordan is not empty ritual; sins are named and turned from.

Classical Arminians press honesty here: grace invites confession, and people truly confess. Shared confession across traditions: forgiveness is sought by admitting guilt, not by hiding behind pedigree.

Reformed

Confession and baptism mark those who answer John’s call. Sin is acknowledged under the Word.

Reformed pastoral note: true repentance includes open confession. God grants a people who tell the truth about themselves as they await the mightier One still to come.

Key contrast Both honor baptism with confession as real turning. Arminians often emphasize sincere, grace-enabled response; Reformed readers often emphasize God’s preparatory work producing a confessing people under His Word.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
