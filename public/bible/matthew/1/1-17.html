Passage

Matthew 1

Verses 1–17 · ESV

Matthew opens his Gospel with the genealogy of Jesus Christ—tracing the Messiah from Abraham and David through exile to Joseph and Mary—so that readers see promise fulfilled and a Savior who belongs to Israel’s story.

Matthew 1:1

1The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham.

Arminian

Matthew announces Jesus as Messiah (“Christ”), rightful heir of David’s throne, and the seed promised to Abraham. The Gospel begins with God’s covenant faithfulness toward a people He has loved and invited into blessing.

Classical Arminians gladly confess that this title is God’s initiative: the Son comes as gift, not human invention. The same God who pledged himself to Abraham still seeks a worldwide family through the Messiah—grace that goes out and calls for a living response of faith.

Reformed

The opening line is covenantal and Christological: Jesus is the anointed King from David’s line and the promised offspring of Abraham. Matthew frames the whole Gospel as the story of God’s sworn commitments kept.

Reformed readers hear here the certainty of promise. What God pledged to the patriarchs and to David He brings to pass in His Son; the genealogy is not chance ancestry but the outworking of a purposeful decree that centers everything in Christ.

Matthew 1:2

2Abraham was the father of Isaac, and Isaac the father of Jacob, and Jacob the father of Judah and his brothers,

Arminian

The line begins with the fathers of Israel. God’s choosing of Abraham’s family was always aimed at blessing the nations (Gen 12), not at closing the door. Judah stands out among the brothers as the royal tribe—yet the mention of “his brothers” keeps the whole people in view.

Arminian pastoral emphasis: God’s saving plan moves through history by grace that gathers a people who trust Him. The story invites every reader into that same trust.

Reformed

Election in history is already visible: Isaac, not Ishmael; Jacob, not Esau; Judah among the sons. God orders the Messianic line according to His wise purpose.

Yet the brothers are named too—Israel as a people remains the theater of God’s faithfulness. The Reformed note is gratitude: the Christ who comes is the Lord of a story God has authored from the start.

Matthew 1:3

3and Judah the father of Perez and Zerah by Tamar, and Perez the father of Hezron, and Hezron the father of Ram,

Arminian

Tamar’s inclusion is startling and gracious. Her story (Gen 38) is tangled with injustice and human failure, yet God weaves her into the Messiah’s line. Matthew will not hide the scandals of grace.

Classical Arminians often linger here on God’s heart to redeem the marginalized and the morally compromised—not by ignoring sin, but by pursuing sinners with restorative mercy that can be received.

Reformed

Tamar in the royal genealogy displays providence that is not derailed by human sin. God fulfills His promise through a broken household, preserving the line He has appointed.

The Reformed comfort is similar in tone: grace is stronger than scandal. The Messiah’s pedigree already preaches that salvation is of the Lord, not of human purity or pedigree.

Matthew 1:4

4and Ram the father of Amminadab, and Amminadab the father of Nahshon, and Nahshon the father of Salmon,

Arminian

These quieter names bridge the wilderness generation toward the settlement of the land. God’s faithfulness often looks ordinary—generation after generation kept by grace.

Arminians and Reformed alike can preach patience here: the Messiah’s coming is prepared across centuries. Faith learns to trust God’s timing.

Reformed

Uncelebrated links still matter. God’s covenant purpose advances through people history barely remembers, yet each is necessary to the appointed line.

Sovereignty here is quiet and meticulous: the Lord of history wastes no generation in bringing His Son.

Matthew 1:5

5and Salmon the father of Boaz by Rahab, and Boaz the father of Obed by Ruth, and Obed the father of Jesse,

Arminian

Rahab the Canaanite and Ruth the Moabite stand in the Messiah’s ancestry. Outsiders are grafted into Israel’s hope by faith and covenant loyalty. Matthew signals that the Gospel will welcome the nations.

Classical Arminian reading stresses God’s sincere outreach beyond ethnic Israel: grace invites the foreigner who turns and trusts. Inclusion is not an afterthought; it is written into Jesus’ family tree.

Reformed

Rahab and Ruth embody Gentile inclusion according to God’s promise to Abraham. Their faith is real—and it is also the fruit of God’s drawing them into the covenant people from whom the Christ would come.

Reformed preaching gladly celebrates the same welcome: Christ is for Jew and Gentile. The difference of accent is usually not whether outsiders are loved, but that their coming rests on God’s effectual purpose and promise.

Matthew 1:6

6and Jesse the father of David the king. And David was the father of Solomon by the wife of Uriah,

Arminian

David the king arrives—the hinge of Messianic hope. Then Matthew names Solomon “by the wife of Uriah,” refusing to erase Bathsheba’s story or David’s sin. Grace does not romanticize the past.

Arminian pastoral note: God restores the repentant and still keeps His word. The throne promise stands, and so does the call to holiness; mercy never makes adultery light.

Reformed

Davidic kingship is essential to Matthew’s Christology. Naming “the wife of Uriah” confesses both royal glory and royal guilt—yet the promise of 2 Samuel 7 is not canceled by David’s failure.

Reformed assurance: God’s covenant with David is upheld by God’s fidelity. The greater Son of David will be the righteous King the line itself could not supply.

Matthew 1:7

7and Solomon the father of Rehoboam, and Rehoboam the father of Abijah, and Abijah the father of Asaph,

Arminian

The kingdom fractures under Rehoboam; Abijah’s reign is mixed. Still the line continues. God’s purpose moves through flawed kings without endorsing their folly.

Both traditions agree: political decline does not nullify Messianic hope. Faithfulness to the promise is God’s, even when rulers are not faithful.

Reformed

Matthew compresses royal history without softening it. The Davidic house declines, yet God preserves the seed He has chosen.

Providence here is persevering: the Messiah’s line is kept through judgment as well as blessing.

Matthew 1:8

8and Asaph the father of Jehoshaphat, and Jehoshaphat the father of Joram, and Joram the father of Uzziah,

Arminian

Reformers and compromisers sit side by side. Jehoshaphat sought the Lord; other names recall darker days. Matthew’s list is a moral mosaic, not a hall of fame.

The shared pastoral word: do not trust bloodline or office; trust the God who keeps sending light into dim generations.

Reformed

Genealogical telescoping (common in Matthew) still serves a theological end: God shepherds the line across reigns good and evil.

The Reformed accent falls on divine preservation of the Messianic promise amid the instability of Judah’s throne.

Matthew 1:9

9and Uzziah the father of Jotham, and Jotham the father of Ahaz, and Ahaz the father of Hezekiah,

Arminian

Ahaz’s unbelief and Hezekiah’s reform stand near each other. God’s dealings with Judah include both severe warning and surprising mercy.

Arminians often highlight how God continues to confront and invite—even stubborn kings are addressed by prophets. History remains a field of genuine moral response under grace.

Reformed

The same span shows judgment and revival under God’s hand. Isaiah’s ministry (looming behind Matthew’s later citations) addresses these very courts.

God’s sovereignty does not erase human responsibility in the kings; it guarantees that His redemptive purpose will not finally fail.

Matthew 1:10

10and Hezekiah the father of Manasseh, and Manasseh the father of Amos, and Amos the father of Josiah,

Arminian

Manasseh’s wickedness and Josiah’s renewal remind readers that grace can reclaim even the darkest chapters—and that revival is urgently needed. Matthew’s readers know how fragile reform can be.

The invitation stands: turn while there is time. God’s patience in the genealogy is itself a kind of gospel.

Reformed

Even Manasseh cannot erase the line God intends to keep. Josiah’s reforms shine, yet exile still comes—showing that national repentance, though precious, cannot itself secure final redemption.

That final security awaits the Christ to whom this list points.

Matthew 1:11

11and Josiah the father of Jechoniah and his brothers, at the time of the deportation to Babylon.

Arminian

Exile is named without flinching. Covenant unfaithfulness brings judgment; yet the story does not end in Babylon. God disciplines the people He still claims.

Arminian and Reformed preaching meet here in sober hope: judgment is real, and so is the God who can plant hope again beyond the rubble.

Reformed

The deportation is a theological marker: the throne is emptied under God’s righteous judgment, yet the Davidic hope is not discarded.

Matthew prepares us to see Jesus as the true King who arrives after the darkest national humiliation—promise surviving exile by God’s decree.

Matthew 1:12

12And after the deportation to Babylon: Jechoniah was the father of Shealtiel, and Shealtiel the father of Zerubbabel,

Arminian

“After the deportation” is a resurrection-shaped phrase for Israel’s story. Zerubbabel leads a remnant home; small beginnings become the stage for larger hope.

Grace after judgment encourages weary believers: failure is not the last word when God still speaks promise.

Reformed

Post-exilic names prove God’s fidelity under the curse of exile. The line continues because God willed it to continue.

Haggai and Zechariah’s word to Zerubbabel already pointed beyond a rebuilt temple to a greater glory—fulfilled in Christ.

Matthew 1:13

13and Zerubbabel the father of Abiud, and Abiud the father of Eliakim, and Eliakim the father of Azor,

Arminian

Obscure generations fill the long wait. Many saints live and die between promise and fulfillment without seeing the Messiah’s day.

Pastoral agreement across traditions: ordinary faithfulness in unnoticed years still matters in God’s economy.

Reformed

The hidden years are not empty years. God’s counsel advances when prophets are silent and names are unfamiliar.

Christ’s advent is therefore pure gift after long providence—not the climax of human achievement.

Matthew 1:14

14and Azor the father of Zadok, and Zadok the father of Achim, and Achim the father of Eliud,

Arminian

Again the quiet links. Matthew teaches patience and trust: God is at work when we cannot chart the next revival.

The genealogy trains hope that is steady rather than frantic.

Reformed

Each fathering is a kept thread in a tapestry God alone fully sees. The Reformed instinct is doxology: history is purposeful because God is purposeful.

Matthew 1:15

15and Eliud the father of Eleazar, and Eleazar the father of Matthan, and Matthan the father of Jacob,

Arminian

The list nears Joseph. Legal and familial ties will matter for Jesus’ place in David’s house, even as the virgin birth (vv. 18–25) will show that Jesus is more than a merely natural son.

Both traditions confess mystery approaching: God prepares a household for the incarnation.

Reformed

The approach to Joseph sets up legal Davidic descent. God arranges the earthly guardianship of His Son with the same care that preserved the line for centuries.

Nothing in the incarnation is accidental.

Matthew 1:16

16and Jacob the father of Joseph the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born, who is called Christ.

Arminian

Matthew carefully shifts grammar: Joseph is Mary’s husband; Jesus is born “of whom” (Mary). The virgin conception is already hinted. Jesus is truly Christ—anointed Savior—entering David’s house without ordinary human fathering.

Arminians and Reformed unite in worship here. Where they later nuance “his people” (v. 21), they agree that the incarnation is sheer grace toward sinners who could never climb to God.

Reformed

The wording protects both Jesus’ virginal conception and His rightful place through Joseph in David’s line. “Who is called Christ” is Matthew’s thesis statement in miniature.

God has brought the promised King. The genealogy’s goal is not ancestry for its own sake but the identification of Jesus as the Messiah appointed for the salvation God planned.

Matthew 1:17

17So all the generations from Abraham to David were fourteen generations, and from David to the deportation to Babylon fourteen generations, and from the deportation to Babylon to the Christ fourteen generations.

Arminian

Matthew’s threefold “fourteen” is patterned theology more than a modern census. Promise (Abraham), kingship (David), exile, and Christ structure Israel’s story toward hope.

Classical Arminians hear an open-armed climax: the Christ has come for the world God loves. The patterned history invites faith—God has acted; respond to the King.

Reformed

The schematic fourteens preach providence: history is ordered toward “the Christ.” Exile is not an interruption that escapes God’s plan; it is a movement within it.

Reformed readers stress the certainty of arrival. The Messiah appears on schedule according to God’s wisdom—fulfillment that grounds assurance, not bare chronology.

Key contrast On the genealogy itself both traditions largely agree—fulfillment, inclusion of Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Bathsheba, and Jesus as Abraham’s and David’s son. Differences are usually mild accents: Arminians often stress the wideness of the welcome written into the line; Reformed readers often stress the certainty of God’s ordered purpose bringing the Christ without fail.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
