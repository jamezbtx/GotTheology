Passage

Matthew 1

Verses 18–25 · ESV · Voices

After the genealogy, Matthew narrates Jesus’ birth: Mary’s virginal conception by the Holy Spirit, Joseph’s righteous struggle and obedience, the naming of Jesus as Savior, and the fulfillment of Isaiah’s Immanuel promise.

Matthew 1:18

18Now the birth of Jesus Christ took place in this way. When his mother Mary had been betrothed to Joseph, before they came together she was found to be with child from the Holy Spirit.

Arminian

Matthew insists the conception is “from the Holy Spirit”—the incarnation is God’s holy initiative, not human scheming. Mary is already “his mother”; the child is already named with Messianic dignity.

Classical Arminians emphasize that God moves first in salvation history and in personal salvation. The Spirit’s work creates a new beginning; human beings are then summoned to trust and obey that gift. Grace enables; it does not wait for moral achievement.

Reformed

The virgin conception is the Spirit’s creative act: the eternal Son takes true human nature without ordinary human fathering. Salvation begins in God’s holy action, not in human possibility.

Reformed confession joins the church’s historic faith here: Christ’s person is given by God. The same God who effectually brings the Son into the world effectually brings sinners to the Son—initiative all the way down.

Matthew 1:19

19And her husband Joseph, being a just man and unwilling to put her to shame, resolved to divorce her quietly.

Arminian

Joseph is “just”—righteous according to the law—and merciful. He will not expose Mary to public disgrace. Matthew honors a conscience that holds truth and tenderness together.

Arminian pastoral reading often notices responsible moral agency under pressure: Joseph deliberates, chooses restraint, and seeks a quiet path. Grace does not erase careful human judgment; it later redirects it (vv. 20–24).

Reformed

Joseph’s righteousness is real and commendable, yet it is not enough to interpret the miracle. Left to himself, even a just man would misread God’s work and step away from Mary.

Reformed preaching honors Joseph’s character while underlining need for revelation: God must speak if Joseph is to play his part in the redemptive plan.

Matthew 1:20

20But as he considered these things, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream, saying, “Joseph, son of David, do not fear to take Mary as your wife, for that which is conceived in her is from the Holy Spirit.

Arminian

Heaven interrupts Joseph’s fearful plans. Addressed as “son of David,” he is called into the royal story by a word that calms fear and clarifies the Spirit’s work.

Classical Arminians see here prevenient, assisting grace in narrative form: God enlightens, reassures, and enables a faithful response Joseph could not generate from suspicion alone. The command remains a genuine call to trust and act.

Reformed

God’s messenger does not merely advise; he reconstitutes Joseph’s understanding and summons him into obedience. “Son of David” ties Joseph to the legal line God is using for the Messiah.

Reformed emphasis: the Lord who conceived the child by the Spirit also effectually directs the guardian He has appointed. Revelation overcomes fear so that the promise advances.

Key contrast Both affirm God’s initiative and Joseph’s real obedience. Arminians typically describe the angelic word as grace that enables a free, faithful response; Reformed readers typically describe it as God’s effective word that secures Joseph’s part in a plan already certain.

Matthew 1:21

21She will bear a son, and you shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins.”

Arminian

“Jesus” (Yahweh saves) defines the child’s mission: rescue from sins, not merely political liberation. The name is gospel in miniature.

Classical Arminians gladly confess that Jesus truly saves, and they often hear “his people” in a way that keeps the door of sincere gospel invitation wide—God’s saving love in Christ is directed toward sinners who may receive Him by grace-enabled faith. The verse fuels mission and assurance for all who come.

Reformed

The angel defines both name and mission. Jesus saves “his people” from their sins—particular language that Reformed theology reads as the people given to the Son, effectively redeemed by Him.

This is not cold arithmetic; it is pastoral comfort. The Savior’s work is not a mere offer that might finally save no one; He accomplishes the salvation of those who are His.

Key contrast Arminians commonly stress that Christ’s saving purpose underwrites a genuine universal call and that “his people” are those who believe; Reformed theology commonly stresses that “his people” are the elect for whom Christ effectually saves, with faith as the appointed means.

Matthew 1:22

22All this took place to fulfill what the Lord had spoken by the prophet:

Arminian

Matthew’s first fulfillment formula roots the incarnation in Scripture. God keeps His word; the story is not improvisation.

Arminians rejoice that prophecy invites trust: what God promised, He performs. Fulfillment authenticates Jesus and calls hearers to respond to the God who speaks truly to all.

Reformed

“All this took place to fulfill” is Matthew’s doctrine of providence in a sentence. Events unfold because the Lord had spoken.

Reformed readers hear sovereignty and scriptural certainty together: God’s promise is not vulnerable to human confusion; it governs history until it stands complete in Christ.

Matthew 1:23

23“Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall call his name Immanuel” (which means, God with us).

Arminian

Isaiah’s sign becomes flesh: the virgin conceives, and the child’s identity is “God with us.” Presence is the heart of the gospel—God drawing near to save.

Classical Arminian piety stresses the wideness of that nearness: Immanuel is God’s approachable grace toward the world, summoning every hearer to welcome the God who comes close. Union with Christ by faith is how “God with us” becomes “God with me.”

Reformed

Immanuel confesses the incarnation’s wonder: God Himself is with His people in the person of the Son. Matthew’s translation (“God with us”) is theology, not a footnote.

Reformed comfort accents covenant presence—God with us as the fulfillment of promise to His own. The same Immanuel who is conceived by the Spirit will be with His church to the end of the age (Matt 28:20).

Key contrast Both adore Immanuel. Arminians often emphasize God’s nearness as gracious initiative toward all who may believe; Reformed readers often emphasize God’s effective covenant presence with the people He saves in Christ.

Matthew 1:24

24When Joseph woke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him: he took his wife,

Arminian

Joseph obeys promptly. Faith shows itself in action: he takes Mary as wife under God’s word, accepting cost and misunderstanding.

Arminian commentary treasures this as grace-enabled but real obedience—Joseph could have clung to fear; instead he yields. The pattern encourages believers: when God clarifies His will, respond without delay.

Reformed

Obedience follows revelation. Joseph’s waking act is the fruit of God’s intervening word; the command is effective in the life of this “just man.”

Reformed pastoral use: God not only plans redemption; He also works in His servants the willingness to serve the plan. Joseph’s yes is evidence of grace at work.

Matthew 1:25

25but knew her not until she had given birth to a son. And he called his name Jesus.

Arminian

Joseph’s restraint guards the sign of the virgin birth; his naming of Jesus publicly claims the child and confesses the angel’s gospel. The chapter ends on the saving name.

Across traditions this is shared ground: Jesus is truly born, truly named Savior, and truly the object of faith. Arminians press the invitation—receive this Jesus. The God who was with Mary and Joseph still grants enabling grace so that sinners may trust and follow Him.

Reformed

Matthew closes the birth account with purity of the sign and certainty of the name. Joseph’s naming enacts the angel’s charge: this child is Jesus, Savior of His people.

Reformed doxology: from genealogy to naming, God has kept every thread. The Son is born according to promise; His name announces accomplished mercy for those He came to save. Worship, not speculation, is the fitting end.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Matthew narrates Jesus’ birth: Mary’s virginal conception by the Holy Spirit—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Matthew 1:18–25.

Arminian Notable voices

“A just man — a strict observer of the law: therefore not thinking it right to keep her. It is the same name with Joshua (who was a type of him) which properly signifies, The Lord, Salvation. They shall call his name Emmanuel — to be called, only means, according to the Hebrews manner of speaking, that the person spoken of shall really and effectually be what he is called, and actually fulfil that title.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Matt 1:18–25)

Reformed Notable voices

“Now the birth of Jesus Christ Matthew does not as yet relate the place or manner of Christ's birth, but the way in which his heavenly generation was made known to Joseph. First, he says that Mary was found to be with child by the Holy Spirit Not that this secret work of God was generally known: but the historian mixes up, with the knowledge of men, the power of the Spirit, which was still unknown.”
John Calvin · Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists (on Matt 1:18–25)
