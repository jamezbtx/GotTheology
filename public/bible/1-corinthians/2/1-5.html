Passage

1 Corinthians 2

Verses 1–5 · WEB

Paul recalls how he first preached in Corinth: not with impressive oratory but resolved to know nothing among them except Jesus Christ crucified. He came in weakness and trembling, and his message was carried by the Spirit’s demonstration and power, so that their faith would rest on God’s power rather than human wisdom. Both traditions take this as a model for preaching. They differ slightly on how the Spirit’s power relates to the hearer’s response.

1 Corinthians 2:1

1When I came to you, brothers, I didn’t come with excellence of speech or of wisdom, proclaiming to you the testimony of God.

Arminian

Paul did not come with lofty speech or wisdom when he proclaimed God’s testimony. Arminian readers see a deliberate refusal to manipulate hearers, so that their decision would be a response to God’s message rather than to Paul’s skill. Faith should rest on truth, not on technique.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s restraint as an acknowledgment that only God can make the testimony effective. Rhetorical brilliance cannot produce faith, so the preacher’s task is faithful proclamation.

1 Corinthians 2:2

2For I determined not to know anything among you, except Jesus Christ, and him crucified.

Arminian

He decided to know nothing among them except Jesus Christ and him crucified. Arminian readers see the cross as the centre of the gospel offered to all, the place where God’s love for the world is displayed. Every other topic in the letter flows from this focus.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the cross as the heart of preaching and the substance of saving wisdom. Paul’s resolve shows that the content of the message, not the messenger, is what God uses.

1 Corinthians 2:3

3I was with you in weakness, in fear, and in much trembling.

Arminian

Paul was with them in weakness, fear, and much trembling. Arminian readers see an honest picture of a minister’s human frailty, which makes room for God’s strength. Weakness is not a barrier to God’s use of a servant.

Reformed

Reformed readers see weakness as the setting in which God displays his power, a theme Paul develops in 2 Corinthians 12. The preacher’s frailty ensures the glory goes to God.

1 Corinthians 2:4

4My speech and my preaching were not in persuasive words of human wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power,

Arminian

His message was not in persuasive words but in demonstration of the Spirit and power. Arminian readers see the Spirit’s convicting work accompanying the preached word, enabling hearers to respond freely. The Spirit persuades by truth, not by human manipulation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit’s demonstration as his effective work in the hearts of hearers, producing faith. The power lies not in argument but in the Spirit who opens blind eyes.

1 Corinthians 2:5

5that your faith wouldn’t stand in the wisdom of men, but in the power of God.

Arminian

So that their faith might not rest on human wisdom but on God’s power. Arminian readers see faith as the hearer’s trust, which must be placed on the right foundation. A faith built on rhetoric could be talked out of again.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faith as resting on God’s power both in its origin and its continuation. Faith produced by the Spirit stands secure because it is grounded in God, not in human persuasion.

Key contrast Both agree that faith must rest on God’s power rather than human wisdom. Arminian readers emphasize the hearer’s trust rightly placed through the Spirit’s enabling. Reformed readers emphasize the Spirit’s work creating faith as its true ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
