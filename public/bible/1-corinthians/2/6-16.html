Passage

1 Corinthians 2

Verses 6–16 · WEB

Paul clarifies that the gospel is wisdom, but a hidden wisdom of God, decreed before the ages for believers’ glory and unknown to the rulers who crucified the Lord of glory. God has revealed it by his Spirit, who searches the depths of God. The natural person does not accept the things of the Spirit, while the spiritual person discerns them, because believers have the mind of Christ. Verse 14 is a key text in debates about human inability, so Arminian and Reformed readers bring distinct emphases here.

1 Corinthians 2:6

6We speak wisdom, however, among those who are full grown; yet a wisdom not of this world, nor of the rulers of this world, who are coming to nothing.

Arminian

Among the mature Paul speaks a wisdom not of this age or its passing rulers. Arminian readers see maturity as growth in understanding that believers are called to pursue. The gospel has depth for those who go on in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the mature as those taught by the Spirit, who perceive the wisdom of God in the cross. The rulers of this age are passing away, while God’s purpose stands.

1 Corinthians 2:7

7But we speak God’s wisdom in a mystery, the wisdom that has been hidden, which God foreordained before the worlds for our glory,

Arminian

Paul speaks God’s hidden wisdom, decreed before the ages for our glory. Arminian readers see God’s eternal plan to save through Christ, a plan for all who would be in Christ. The decree concerns the way of salvation and its glorious goal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s eternal decree aimed at the glory of his people. Before the ages God purposed this wisdom, which supports the Reformed emphasis on God’s eternal counsel in salvation.

1 Corinthians 2:8

8which none of the rulers of this world has known. For had they known it, they wouldn’t have crucified the Lord of glory.

Arminian

None of the rulers of this age understood this, or they would not have crucified the Lord of glory. Arminian readers see human responsibility for the crucifixion and the blindness of worldly power. The title Lord of glory is a striking confession of Christ’s deity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s wisdom working even through the ignorance of the rulers, as Acts 2:23 also says. The Lord of glory crucified shows that the one who died is divine, one person in two natures.

1 Corinthians 2:9

9But as it is written, “Things which an eye didn’t see, and an ear didn’t hear, which didn’t enter into the heart of man, these God has prepared for those who love him.”

Arminian

What no eye has seen God prepared for those who love him. Arminian readers note that the blessings are prepared for those who love God, so loving God is the mark of those who receive them. God’s preparation invites wholehearted response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s gracious preparation of good things for his people, beyond human imagination. Those who love him are the ones in whom he has worked that love.

1 Corinthians 2:10

10But to us, God revealed them through the Spirit. For the Spirit searches all things, yes, the deep things of God.

Arminian

God revealed these things to us through the Spirit, who searches even the depths of God. Arminian readers see the Spirit as the revealer through whom apostles and believers know God’s purposes. Revelation is God’s gracious initiative toward people who could not discover it alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the necessity of the Spirit’s revelation, both in inspiring Scripture and in illuminating believers. The Spirit’s knowledge of God’s depths also points to his full deity.

1 Corinthians 2:11

11For who among men knows the things of a man, except the spirit of the man, which is in him? Even so, no one knows the things of God, except God’s Spirit.

Arminian

No one knows God’s thoughts except God’s Spirit. Arminian readers see an analogy showing why revelation is needed. Without God’s self-disclosure people can only guess about him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit’s personal and divine knowledge of God, which supports trinitarian doctrine. Only the Spirit can make God’s mind known.

1 Corinthians 2:12

12But we received, not the spirit of the world, but the Spirit which is from God, that we might know the things that were freely given to us by God.

Arminian

We received the Spirit from God that we might understand what God has freely given. Arminian readers see the Spirit given to believers so they may grasp and enjoy the gifts of grace. Understanding grows as believers walk in the Spirit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit’s illumination as part of salvation itself. Believers know the gifts of grace because the Spirit has been given to them.

1 Corinthians 2:13

13Which things also we speak, not in words which man’s wisdom teaches, but which the Holy Spirit teaches, comparing spiritual things with spiritual things.

Arminian

Paul speaks in words taught by the Spirit, interpreting spiritual things with spiritual words. Arminian readers see the Spirit guiding apostolic teaching so that it faithfully conveys God’s truths. The verse supports confidence in Scripture’s teaching.

Reformed

Reformed readers find support for verbal inspiration: the apostles speak in words the Spirit teaches. Spiritual truths are rightly communicated in Spirit-given language.

1 Corinthians 2:14

14Now the natural man doesn’t receive the things of God’s Spirit, for they are foolishness to him, and he can’t know them, because they are spiritually discerned.

Arminian

The natural person does not accept the things of God’s Spirit and cannot understand them. Arminian readers affirm human inability apart from grace but hold that God’s prevenient grace, working through the gospel, enables any hearer to respond. The natural person need not remain in that state, because the Spirit is at work drawing people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a key text for total inability: the unregenerate person cannot receive spiritual truth because it is spiritually discerned. Only the Spirit’s regenerating work changes the natural person into a spiritual one, which precedes and produces faith.

Key contrast Both agree that people cannot receive God’s truth apart from the Spirit. Arminian readers see prevenient grace enabling all hearers to respond, though they may resist. Reformed readers see regeneration as the necessary and effective change that brings the elect to faith.

1 Corinthians 2:15

15But he who is spiritual discerns all things, and he himself is judged by no one.

Arminian

The spiritual person judges all things but is judged by no one. Arminian readers see the Spirit-led believer able to discern truth, while warning that this is not a licence for arrogance, which the Corinthians showed. Discernment grows with spiritual maturity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer’s Spirit-given ability to evaluate everything by God’s word. The world’s verdicts cannot finally judge someone who belongs to God.

1 Corinthians 2:16

16“For who has known the mind of the Lord, that he should instruct him?” But we have Christ’s mind.

Arminian

Who has known the Lord’s mind to instruct him? But we have Christ’s mind. Arminian readers see believers granted understanding of Christ’s ways through the Spirit, calling for humility and growth. Having Christ’s mind is a gift to be lived out.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer’s share in Christ’s mind through the Spirit as a fruit of union with him. Human beings cannot counsel God, yet God graciously reveals his mind to his people.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
