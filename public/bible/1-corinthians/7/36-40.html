Passage

1 Corinthians 7

Verses 36–40 · WEB

Paul addresses a man and his virgin, likely a betrothed couple or possibly a father and daughter. If passions are strong, they should marry, which is no sin; if he has resolved to keep her as she is, he does well; marrying does well, and not marrying does better given the circumstances. A wife is bound while her husband lives, but a widow is free to marry anyone she wishes, only in the Lord, though Paul judges she is happier remaining single. Both traditions read this as practical wisdom rather than doctrinal dispute.

1 Corinthians 7:36

36But if any man thinks that he is behaving inappropriately toward his virgin, if she is past the flower of her age, and if need so requires, let him do what he desires. He doesn’t sin. Let them marry.

Arminian

If a man thinks he is acting improperly toward his virgin and passions are strong, let them marry; it is no sin. Arminian readers see freedom and honesty about desire. Marriage remains an honourable choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christian liberty in decisions about marriage. Interpreters differ on whether the man is a fiancé or a father, but marriage is no sin either way.

1 Corinthians 7:37

37But he who stands steadfast in his heart, having no urgency, but has power over his own will, and has determined in his own heart to keep his own virgin, does well.

Arminian

Whoever is firmly established in his heart, under no necessity, and decides to keep her as his virgin does well. Arminian readers see personal conviction and self-control honoured. The decision must be free and settled.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wise decision-making within God’s freedom. Neither path is forced.

1 Corinthians 7:38

38So then both he who gives his own virgin in marriage does well, and he who doesn’t give her in marriage does better.

Arminian

He who marries does well, and he who does not does better. Arminian readers see both choices as good, with singleness preferable in those circumstances. Paul is weighing advantages, not sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see relative counsel, not a ranking of holiness. Better is tied to the distress and devotion discussed above.

1 Corinthians 7:39

39A wife is bound by law for as long as her husband lives; but if the husband is dead, she is free to be married to whomever she desires, only in the Lord.

Arminian

A wife is bound while her husband lives; if he dies she may marry whom she wishes, only in the Lord. Arminian readers see freedom to remarry, with the guideline of marrying a believer. Choice operates within faithfulness to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see marriage’s lifelong bond and the requirement to marry in the Lord. Many Reformed readers apply only in the Lord as a call to marry a fellow believer.

Key contrast Both agree that widows may remarry and should marry in the Lord. Arminian readers stress the freedom of choice affirmed in the verse. Reformed readers stress the boundaries God’s law sets on that freedom.

1 Corinthians 7:40

40But she is happier if she stays as she is, in my judgment, and I think that I also have God’s Spirit.

Arminian

In Paul’s judgment she is happier remaining as she is, and he thinks he has God’s Spirit. Arminian readers see a modest yet authoritative counsel. Paul’s opinion carries the Spirit’s weight.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s mild irony toward those claiming spiritual superiority. His counsel is Spirit-guided and part of Scripture.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
