Passage

1 Corinthians 7

Verses 25–35 · WEB

Concerning virgins, Paul has no command from the Lord but gives his trustworthy judgment. Because of the present distress and the shortness of the time, he advises people to remain as they are, though marrying is not sin. The form of this world is passing away, so believers should hold earthly relationships and possessions loosely. The unmarried can devote themselves more fully to the Lord’s affairs, while the married are rightly concerned with pleasing their spouse. Both traditions share this pastoral wisdom. Their notes differ mainly in emphasis on providence and personal choice.

1 Corinthians 7:25

25Now concerning virgins, I have no commandment from the Lord, but I give my judgment as one who has obtained mercy from the Lord to be trustworthy.

Arminian

Concerning virgins Paul has no command from the Lord but gives his judgment as one who by mercy is trustworthy. Arminian readers see Paul’s counsel as wise and Spirit-led, but leaving room for choice. Mercy shaped his trustworthiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s trustworthiness as a gift of mercy. His considered judgment is part of inspired Scripture, even when he distinguishes it from a dominical command.

1 Corinthians 7:26

26Therefore I think that because of the distress that is on us, that it’s good for a man to remain as he is.

Arminian

Because of the present distress, it is good to remain as one is. Arminian readers see counsel suited to a particular crisis. Circumstances shape wise decisions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s advice tied to the pressures of the age, perhaps famine or persecution. God’s people must weigh their times.

1 Corinthians 7:27

27Are you bound to a wife? Don’t seek to be freed. Are you free from a wife? Don’t seek a wife.

Arminian

Bound to a wife? Do not seek release. Free? Do not seek a wife. Arminian readers see practical advice for stability under pressure. It does not forbid marriage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see contentment with God’s present providence. The counsel is not law but wisdom.

1 Corinthians 7:28

28But if you marry, you have not sinned. If a virgin marries, she has not sinned. Yet such will have oppression in the flesh, and I want to spare you.

Arminian

If you marry, you have not sinned, but such will have troubles. Arminian readers see freedom to marry, with honest warning about its burdens. Paul seeks to spare them hardship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see marriage affirmed as good and lawful. Paul’s concern is pastoral, not ascetic.

1 Corinthians 7:29

29But I say this, brothers: the time is short, that from now on, both those who have wives may be as though they had none;

Arminian

The time is short; from now on those with wives should live as though they had none. Arminian readers see an eschatological urgency reshaping priorities. Relationships must not eclipse devotion to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer’s life oriented to the coming age. As though not means holding earthly things with open hands, not neglecting duties.

Key contrast Both read verse 29 as a call to live in light of the passing age without neglecting real duties. Arminian readers stress the believer’s choices about priorities. Reformed readers stress a pilgrim posture shaped by God’s ordering of history.

1 Corinthians 7:30

30and those who weep, as though they didn’t weep; and those who rejoice, as though they didn’t rejoice; and those who buy, as though they didn’t possess;

Arminian

Those who weep, rejoice, or buy should do so as though not. Arminian readers see detachment from the world’s grip. Christians feel and own things but are not ruled by them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a pilgrim posture. Earthly experiences are real but not ultimate.

1 Corinthians 7:31

31and those who use the world, as not using it to the fullest. For the mode of this world passes away.

Arminian

Those who use the world should not be absorbed by it, for its present form is passing away. Arminian readers see a call to wise stewardship in light of eternity. The world’s scheme is temporary.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the transience of this age under God’s plan. Believers use the world as stewards, not as owners.

1 Corinthians 7:32

32But I desire to have you to be free from cares. He who is unmarried is concerned for the things of the Lord, how he may please the Lord;

Arminian

Paul wants them free from anxieties; the unmarried man cares for the Lord’s affairs. Arminian readers see singleness offering focused devotion. It is an opportunity to be embraced if gifted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see undistracted service as a blessing of singleness. Paul’s aim is the believer’s good.

1 Corinthians 7:33

33but he who is married is concerned about the things of the world, how he may please his wife.

Arminian

The married man is concerned with worldly matters, how to please his wife. Arminian readers see legitimate obligations, not sin. Marriage brings rightful responsibilities.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pleasing a spouse as a godly duty. Paul simply notes divided attention, not wrongdoing.

1 Corinthians 7:34

34There is also a difference between a wife and a virgin. The unmarried woman cares about the things of the Lord, that she may be holy both in body and in spirit. But she who is married cares about the things of the world—how she may please her husband.

Arminian

The unmarried woman cares for the Lord’s affairs; the married woman for how to please her husband. Arminian readers see the same principle applied to women, affirming their devotion to the Lord. Women’s service is valued equally.

Reformed

Reformed readers see holiness in body and spirit as a calling for all, with singleness offering particular freedom. Both states can be lived to God’s glory.

1 Corinthians 7:35

35This I say for your own profit; not that I may ensnare you, but for that which is appropriate, and that you may attend to the Lord without distraction.

Arminian

Paul says this for their benefit, not to restrain them, but for undivided devotion. Arminian readers see counsel that respects freedom. Paul does not lay a noose on anyone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pastoral wisdom serving devotion to the Lord. Christian liberty is preserved even as Paul gives advice.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
