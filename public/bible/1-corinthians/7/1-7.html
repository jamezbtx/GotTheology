Passage

1 Corinthians 7

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Paul turns to matters the Corinthians raised in their letter, beginning with a slogan: it is good for a man not to touch a woman. He affirms marriage as a guard against immorality and teaches mutual conjugal rights and obligations between husband and wife. Abstinence should be temporary, by agreement, and for prayer. Paul wishes all were single like him, yet recognizes that each has his own gift from God. Arminian and Reformed readers agree closely on this passage. Their notes differ mostly in how they describe gifts and providence.

1 Corinthians 7:1

1Now concerning the things about which you wrote to me: it is good for a man not to touch a woman.

Arminian

Paul responds to their letter and a slogan that it is good for a man not to touch a woman. Arminian readers see Paul engaging real questions with nuance, neither rejecting nor embracing the slogan outright. Celibacy has value but is not superior holiness for all.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul correcting an ascetic tendency while honouring singleness. The Reformers resisted any requirement of celibacy, appealing to texts like this.

1 Corinthians 7:2

2But, because of sexual immoralities, let each man have his own wife, and let each woman have her own husband.

Arminian

Because of temptation to immorality, each man should have his own wife and each woman her own husband. Arminian readers see marriage as God’s good provision and a protection against sin. It is a mutual, exclusive covenant.

Reformed

Reformed readers see marriage as a creation ordinance and a remedy against sin, as historic Reformed marriage liturgies put it. Monogamy is assumed.

1 Corinthians 7:3

3Let the husband give his wife the affection owed her, and likewise also the wife her husband.

Arminian

Husband and wife should give each other their conjugal rights. Arminian readers see striking mutuality in Paul’s teaching. Both spouses owe love and attention to the other.

Reformed

Reformed readers see marriage as a covenant of mutual self-giving. Both partners have real obligations before God.

1 Corinthians 7:4

4The wife doesn’t have authority over her own body, but the husband. Likewise also the husband doesn’t have authority over his own body, but the wife.

Arminian

The wife does not have authority over her own body but the husband does, and likewise the husband. Arminian readers see radical equality in the marriage bed for Paul’s day. Each spouse serves the other freely.

Reformed

Reformed readers see mutual belonging within the one-flesh union. The symmetry reflects equal dignity within the order of marriage.

1 Corinthians 7:5

5Don’t deprive one another, unless it is by consent for a season, that you may give yourselves to fasting and prayer, and may be together again, that Satan doesn’t tempt you because of your lack of self-control.

Arminian

Do not deprive one another except by agreement for a time, for prayer, then come together again. Arminian readers see wise guidance to avoid needless temptation. Spiritual discipline should not become an occasion for sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see realism about human weakness and Satan’s schemes. Abstinence is permitted, not commanded, and must be mutual.

1 Corinthians 7:6

6But this I say by way of concession, not of commandment.

Arminian

Paul says this as a concession, not a command. Arminian readers see room for Christian freedom and conscience in marriage. Not every pastoral counsel is binding law.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul distinguishing between his apostolic counsel and binding command. The concession likely concerns the temporary abstinence.

1 Corinthians 7:7

7Yet I wish that all men were like me. However each man has his own gift from God, one of this kind, and another of that kind.

Arminian

Paul wishes all were as he is, but each has his own gift from God. Arminian readers see diverse gifts that believers receive and steward. Neither singleness nor marriage is required of everyone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God sovereignly distributing gifts, including the gift of singleness or marriage. Contentment comes from accepting God’s assignment.

Key contrast Both see singleness and marriage as gifts from God. Arminian readers stress the believer’s stewardship and choice within those gifts. Reformed readers stress God’s sovereign distribution of each person’s gift.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
