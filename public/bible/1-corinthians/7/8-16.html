Passage

1 Corinthians 7

Verses 8–16 · WEB

Paul advises the unmarried and widows that remaining single is good, but marriage is better than burning with passion. He repeats the Lord’s command against divorce for married believers, then addresses mixed marriages: a believer should not leave a willing unbelieving spouse, since the unbeliever and children are made holy through the believing partner. If the unbeliever leaves, the believer is not bound. Verse 14 plays a role in the Reformed case for infant baptism, while verse 16 raises questions about whether a spouse’s salvation can be influenced. The traditions read these points differently while sharing the pastoral aim.

1 Corinthians 7:8

8But I say to the unmarried and to widows, it is good for them if they remain even as I am.

Arminian

To the unmarried and widows: it is good to remain as Paul is. Arminian readers see singleness honoured as a valuable calling. It offers freedom for service.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree, while denying that singleness earns special merit. Paul’s own example is commended, not imposed.

1 Corinthians 7:9

9But if they don’t have self-control, let them marry. For it’s better to marry than to burn.

Arminian

If they lack self-control, they should marry; better to marry than to burn with passion. Arminian readers see practical wisdom about human desire. Marriage is no failure.

Reformed

Reformed readers see marriage as God’s remedy for desire. Burning likely means consuming passion rather than judgment.

1 Corinthians 7:10

10But to the married I command—not I, but the Lord—that the wife not leave her husband

Arminian

To the married Paul gives the Lord’s command: a wife should not separate from her husband. Arminian readers see Paul relaying Jesus’ own teaching on divorce. Marriage is meant to be permanent.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the authority of Christ’s teaching carried forward by the apostle. The permanence of marriage reflects God’s design.

1 Corinthians 7:11

11(but if she departs, let her remain unmarried, or else be reconciled to her husband), and that the husband not leave his wife.

Arminian

If she does separate, she should remain unmarried or be reconciled; a husband should not divorce his wife. Arminian readers see reconciliation as the preferred path. Paul is realistic about broken marriages.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same priority on reconciliation. The Reformed confessions allow divorce on grounds such as adultery and desertion, drawing partly on this chapter.

1 Corinthians 7:12

12But to the rest I—not the Lord—say, if any brother has an unbelieving wife, and she is content to live with him, let him not leave her.

Arminian

To the rest Paul says, not the Lord: a brother with a willing unbelieving wife should not divorce her. Arminian readers see Paul applying principles to a situation Jesus did not address. His word remains authoritative.

Reformed

Reformed readers see apostolic authority extending to new cases. Not the Lord means no saying of Jesus covers it, not that Paul lacks inspiration.

1 Corinthians 7:13

13The woman who has an unbelieving husband, and he is content to live with her, let her not leave her husband.

Arminian

A woman with a willing unbelieving husband should not divorce him. Arminian readers see the believing spouse staying as a witness. Faith does not require abandoning a marriage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the marriage covenant remaining valid across religious differences. The believer’s faith sanctifies the household setting.

1 Corinthians 7:14

14For the unbelieving husband is sanctified in the wife, and the unbelieving wife is sanctified in the husband. Otherwise your children would be unclean, but now they are holy.

Arminian

The unbelieving spouse is made holy through the believer; otherwise the children would be unclean, but now they are holy. Arminian readers see a holiness of influence and setting, not the salvation of the unbeliever. Children of believers enjoy real privileges of exposure to the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see covenant holiness: the children of a believer belong to the covenant community. Many Reformed theologians cite this verse in support of infant baptism, since such children are set apart as holy.

Key contrast Both agree the holiness here does not mean personal salvation. Arminian readers see a sanctifying influence and privilege. Reformed readers see covenant membership that supports baptizing believers’ children.

1 Corinthians 7:15

15Yet if the unbeliever departs, let there be separation. The brother or the sister is not under bondage in such cases, but God has called us in peace.

Arminian

If the unbeliever leaves, let it be so; the believer is not bound; God has called you to peace. Arminian readers see freedom for the abandoned believer. Many Protestant traditions take this to permit remarriage after desertion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see desertion as grounds for dissolving the marriage, as the Westminster Confession says. God’s call to peace means the believer need not fight to hold a departing spouse.

1 Corinthians 7:16

16For how do you know, wife, whether you will save your husband? Or how do you know, husband, whether you will save your wife?

Arminian

How do you know whether you will save your spouse? Arminian readers often take this hopefully: the believer may be God’s instrument in the spouse’s salvation, which is a real possibility. It encourages patient witness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that it can also be read as caution: one cannot know, so holding on solely for the spouse’s salvation is not required. Salvation is God’s work, which may or may not use a spouse’s witness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
