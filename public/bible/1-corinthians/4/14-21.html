Passage

1 Corinthians 4

Verses 14–21 · WEB

Paul softens his tone as a father to his children. They may have countless guides, but he begot them in Christ through the gospel, so he urges them to imitate him and sends Timothy to remind them of his ways. Some arrogant members act as though he will not return, but he will come soon, if the Lord wills, to test their power, not their talk, because the kingdom of God is a matter of power. He asks whether he must come with a rod or in gentleness. Both traditions see here a model of firm and loving pastoral authority.

1 Corinthians 4:14

14I don’t write these things to shame you, but to admonish you as my beloved children.

Arminian

Paul writes not to shame them but to admonish them as beloved children. Arminian readers see loving correction aimed at repentance and restoration. Warnings come from a father’s heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers see admonition as a means of grace for God’s children. Pastoral rebuke flows from love, not contempt.

1 Corinthians 4:15

15For though you have ten thousand tutors in Christ, you don’t have many fathers. For in Christ Jesus, I became your father through the Good News.

Arminian

They have countless guides but not many fathers; Paul became their father in Christ through the gospel. Arminian readers see the preached gospel as the instrument by which the Corinthians came to faith. Paul’s relationship gives him a unique claim to be heard.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gospel as the means of new birth, as in James 1:18 and 1 Peter 1:23. Paul’s spiritual fatherhood is real, though God is the one who gives life.

Key contrast Both see the gospel as the instrument of new life. Arminian readers stress the Corinthians’ response to Paul’s preaching. Reformed readers stress God’s begetting through his word.

1 Corinthians 4:16

16I beg you therefore, be imitators of me.

Arminian

Paul urges them to imitate him. Arminian readers see a call to follow a concrete example of humble faith, as in 11:1, so far as he follows Christ. Discipleship is learned by imitation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s life as a model shaped by the gospel. Imitation is not of his person as such, but of his ways in Christ.

1 Corinthians 4:17

17Because of this I have sent Timothy to you, who is my beloved and faithful child in the Lord, who will remind you of my ways which are in Christ, even as I teach everywhere in every assembly.

Arminian

He sends Timothy to remind them of his ways in Christ, as he teaches everywhere. Arminian readers see consistency in apostolic teaching across churches. Timothy’s visit is a pastoral aid to their obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the unity of apostolic doctrine and practice. Paul taught the same truth in every church, which grounds a shared catholic faith.

1 Corinthians 4:18

18Now some are puffed up, as though I were not coming to you.

Arminian

Some are arrogant, as though Paul were not coming. Arminian readers see pride feeding on the absence of accountability. Leaders’ absence does not reduce believers’ responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sinful pride exposed. Paul treats it seriously because it threatens the church’s health.

1 Corinthians 4:19

19But I will come to you shortly, if the Lord is willing. And I will know, not the word of those who are puffed up, but the power.

Arminian

He will come soon, if the Lord wills, and test their power, not their talk. Arminian readers see Paul submitting his plans to God’s will while promising real confrontation. Words alone do not prove spiritual life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an example of submitting plans to divine providence. The test of authenticity is the power of God at work, not eloquence.

1 Corinthians 4:20

20For God’s Kingdom is not in word, but in power.

Arminian

The kingdom of God is not in talk but in power. Arminian readers see transformed lives as the evidence of God’s reign. Faith shows itself in fruit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the kingdom’s power as the Spirit’s effective work. Claims without power reveal the emptiness of mere profession.

1 Corinthians 4:21

21What do you want? Shall I come to you with a rod, or in love and a spirit of gentleness?

Arminian

Shall he come with a rod or with love and gentleness? Arminian readers see that the Corinthians’ choices will shape how Paul comes. They are genuinely responsible to respond to his appeal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pastoral discipline as an expression of love, adapted to the hearers’ response. Both rod and gentleness serve the church’s good.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
