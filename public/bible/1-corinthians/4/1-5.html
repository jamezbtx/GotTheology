Passage

1 Corinthians 4

Verses 1–5 · WEB

Paul asks the Corinthians to regard him and Apollos as servants of Christ and stewards of God’s mysteries, whose chief requirement is faithfulness. He is not troubled by their verdicts on him, nor does he trust his own clear conscience, because the Lord is his judge. They should not pronounce judgment before the time, when the Lord will expose hidden things and the purposes of hearts. Both traditions see here the accountability of ministers and the final judgment of all. Their differences appear chiefly in how they relate a clear conscience to justification.

1 Corinthians 4:1

1So let a man think of us as Christ’s servants, and stewards of God’s mysteries.

Arminian

Regard us as servants of Christ and stewards of God’s mysteries. Arminian readers see ministers entrusted with the gospel for the sake of all who hear it. The mysteries are truths once hidden and now revealed for proclamation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ministers as stewards of God’s revealed truth and, many add, of the sacraments. Their authority is derivative, exercised under Christ.

1 Corinthians 4:2

2Here, moreover, it is required of stewards, that they be found faithful.

Arminian

Stewards must be found faithful. Arminian readers stress that faithfulness, not results or popularity, is the measure of ministry. It is a responsibility each servant must fulfil.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that faithfulness is the standard, since results belong to God. This frees ministers from both pride and despair.

1 Corinthians 4:3

3But with me it is a very small thing that I should be judged by you, or by man’s judgment. Yes, I don’t judge my own self.

Arminian

It matters little to Paul whether he is judged by them or any human court; he does not even judge himself. Arminian readers see freedom from the tyranny of opinion. Only God’s verdict finally counts.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same freedom, rooted in standing before God. Even self-assessment cannot be final, since the heart is deceitful.

1 Corinthians 4:4

4For I know nothing against myself. Yet I am not justified by this, but he who judges me is the Lord.

Arminian

Paul knows nothing against himself, yet he is not thereby acquitted; the Lord judges him. Arminian readers see that a clear conscience is no guarantee of innocence before God. Paul keeps himself open to God’s searching judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that a clear conscience does not justify anyone, which agrees with justification resting on Christ alone. The verb here concerns acquittal, and Paul refuses to make his own conduct the ground of it.

Key contrast Both agree that a clear conscience is not the final verdict. Arminian readers stress ongoing accountability to the Lord’s judgment. Reformed readers stress that acquittal rests on Christ rather than on one’s own record.

1 Corinthians 4:5

5Therefore judge nothing before the time, until the Lord comes, who will both bring to light the hidden things of darkness, and reveal the counsels of the hearts. Then each man will get his praise from God.

Arminian

Do not judge before the time; the Lord will bring hidden things to light and then each will receive commendation from God. Arminian readers see final judgment evaluating motives as well as deeds. Believers should wait for God’s verdict rather than ranking each other.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s commendation as gracious, given to those whose work he himself produced. The exposure of hearts is a sobering and hopeful truth for faithful servants.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
