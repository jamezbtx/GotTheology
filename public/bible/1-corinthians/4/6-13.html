Passage

1 Corinthians 4

Verses 6–13 · WEB

Paul applies the lesson to the Corinthians’ pride. Everything they have was received, so boasting is absurd. With biting irony he contrasts their sense of being already full, rich, and reigning with the apostles’ life of hunger, beatings, toil, and contempt, treated as the refuse of the world. Verse 7 is a favourite text for the Reformed doctrine of grace, while Arminian readers also embrace it as excluding merit. The passage overall is a sharp call to humility that both traditions share.

1 Corinthians 4:6

6Now these things, brothers, I have in a figure transferred to myself and Apollos for your sakes, that in us you might learn not to think beyond the things which are written, that none of you be puffed up against one another.

Arminian

Paul applied these things to himself and Apollos so they would learn not to go beyond what is written. Arminian readers see a call to stay within Scripture’s teaching rather than inflating leaders. Pride grows when people move beyond God’s word.

Reformed

Reformed readers often cite this verse in support of the sufficiency of Scripture. What is written sets limits on human boasting and speculation.

1 Corinthians 4:7

7For who makes you different? And what do you have that you didn’t receive? But if you did receive it, why do you boast as if you had not received it?

Arminian

Who sees anything different in you? What do you have that you did not receive? Arminian readers affirm that all good in believers comes from God, including the enabling grace by which they believe. Yet they hold that receiving a gift freely is not itself a ground for boasting, so the verse does not require that faith be irresistibly given.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a central text for sovereign grace: if even faith was received, nothing distinguishes one person from another except God’s gift. Augustine famously appealed to this verse in his debates over grace.

Key contrast Both affirm that every good thing in believers is received from God. Arminian readers see enabling grace that may be freely received or resisted. Reformed readers see God’s gift as what finally makes one person differ from another.

1 Corinthians 4:8

8You are already filled. You have already become rich. You have come to reign without us. Yes, and I wish that you did reign, that we also might reign with you.

Arminian

Already you are filled, rich, reigning without us. Arminian readers see Paul’s irony exposing a premature sense of arrival. Believers still live in the time of the cross before the time of the crown.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an over-realized eschatology corrected. The reign of believers is real but awaits its fullness at Christ’s return.

1 Corinthians 4:9

9For, I think that God has displayed us, the apostles, last of all, like men sentenced to death. For we are made a spectacle to the world, both to angels and men.

Arminian

God has displayed the apostles last, like men condemned to death, a spectacle to the world, angels, and people. Arminian readers see the apostles’ suffering as a pattern of costly discipleship. Following Christ involves the way of the cross.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign placement of the apostles in suffering for the church’s good. Their weakness displays God’s power before the watching universe.

1 Corinthians 4:10

10We are fools for Christ’s sake, but you are wise in Christ. We are weak, but you are strong. You have honor, but we have dishonor.

Arminian

We are fools for Christ; you are wise in Christ. Arminian readers see sharp contrasts meant to shame the Corinthians into humility. The true marks of ministry are not honour but faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the paradox of the cross applied to Christian life. Strength in the world’s eyes is no proof of God’s favour.

1 Corinthians 4:11

11Even to this present hour we hunger, thirst, are naked, are beaten, and have no certain dwelling place.

Arminian

To this hour they hunger, thirst, are poorly clothed, beaten, and homeless. Arminian readers see the real hardships of apostolic life, freely embraced for the gospel. Discipleship can cost comfort and security.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s servants enduring hardship under his providence. Suffering is not a sign of failure but part of their calling.

1 Corinthians 4:12

12We toil, working with our own hands. When people curse us, we bless. Being persecuted, we endure.

Arminian

They toil with their own hands; reviled, they bless; persecuted, they endure. Arminian readers see Christ-like responses to abuse, which believers must choose to imitate. Paul’s work also kept the gospel free of charge.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace producing Christ-like character. Blessing those who curse reflects the Lord’s teaching and the Spirit’s work.

1 Corinthians 4:13

13Being defamed, we entreat. We are made as the filth of the world, the dirt wiped off by all, even until now.

Arminian

Slandered, they entreat; they have become the scum of the world. Arminian readers see humility and patience in the face of contempt. Paul’s ministry follows the humiliation of Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the apostles sharing the reproach of Christ. The world’s contempt does not alter their standing before God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
