Passage

1 Corinthians 14

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Paul applies the way of love to the use of gifts in worship. The Corinthians prized tongues, but Paul urges them to pursue love and to desire prophecy above all, because prophecy speaks to people for their building up, encouragement, and comfort, while uninterpreted tongues build up only the speaker. Like an instrument with no clear notes or a trumpet with an uncertain call, speech that cannot be understood helps no one. Since they are eager for spiritual gifts, they should seek to excel in those that build up the church. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that edification is the controlling principle. They differ more, though not along strict party lines, about whether prophecy and tongues continue today.

1 Corinthians 14:1

1Follow after love, and earnestly desire spiritual gifts, but especially that you may prophesy.

Arminian

Pursue love and earnestly desire spiritual gifts, especially prophecy. Arminian readers see believers encouraged to seek gifts actively. Because the gifts are given for others, desiring them is a form of love rather than ambition. Prophecy is singled out because it builds up the whole church in words all can understand.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priority of love and of gifts that build up through intelligible words. Desire for gifts must always be governed by love for the church.

1 Corinthians 14:2

2For he who speaks in another language speaks not to men, but to God; for no one understands; but in the Spirit he speaks mysteries.

Arminian

One who speaks in a tongue speaks to God, not people, for no one understands; he speaks mysteries in the Spirit. Arminian readers in Pentecostal traditions see tongues as genuine prayer to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul does not deny the value of tongues before God. His point is that without interpretation they do not serve the gathered church.

1 Corinthians 14:3

3But he who prophesies speaks to men for their edification, exhortation, and consolation.

Arminian

One who prophesies speaks to people for building up, encouragement, and comfort. Arminian readers see prophecy as Spirit-given speech for the church’s good. They note that building up, encouragement, and comfort describe the purpose of the gift, not a rank above others. Many Arminians, especially in Wesleyan and Pentecostal traditions, value this as the Spirit's ongoing ministry among ordinary believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers often connect New Testament prophecy closely to the ministry of the word. Calvin understood prophecy here largely in terms of interpreting and applying Scripture.

Key contrast Many Arminian and charismatic readers see prophecy as spontaneous Spirit-given words that continue today. Many Reformed readers, following Calvin, link it closely to preaching or treat revelatory prophecy as having ceased.

1 Corinthians 14:4

4He who speaks in another language edifies himself, but he who prophesies edifies the assembly.

Arminian

One who speaks in a tongue builds himself up, but one who prophesies builds up the church. Arminian readers see value in private edification, while public worship requires more.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s priority on the good of the body over private experience. Public worship exists for the edification of all. Speaking in a tongue is not condemned, but its benefit stays with the speaker unless it is interpreted. The measure of every gift is the good it does to others.

1 Corinthians 14:5

5Now I desire to have you all speak with other languages, but rather that you would prophesy. For he is greater who prophesies than he who speaks with other languages, unless he interprets, that the assembly may be built up.

Arminian

Paul wishes all spoke in tongues, but even more that they prophesied, unless someone interprets. Arminian readers see Paul’s positive view of tongues and his greater desire for prophecy.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul’s wish is not a command. The decisive value remains edification, which is why interpretation is required. Prophecy is greater here not because it is more spectacular but because it is understood. Many Reformed readers also link prophecy closely with the ministry of the word.

1 Corinthians 14:6

6But now, brothers, if I come to you speaking with other languages, what would I profit you, unless I speak to you either by way of revelation, or of knowledge, or of prophesying, or of teaching?

Arminian

If Paul came speaking in tongues, how would it benefit them unless he brought revelation, knowledge, prophecy, or teaching? Arminian readers see that intelligible words are essential in worship. Even an apostle’s tongue-speaking would not help them uninterpreted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the importance of the understood word in worship. This principle lay behind the Reformation’s insistence on worship in the common language. Revelation, knowledge, prophecy, and teaching all have content the hearer can receive. Without such content, even an apostle would bring no profit.

1 Corinthians 14:7

7Even things without life, giving a voice, whether pipe or harp, if they didn’t give a distinction in the sounds, how would it be known what is piped or harped?

Arminian

Lifeless instruments must give distinct sounds if anyone is to know what is played. Arminian readers see a simple illustration of the need for clarity.

Reformed

Reformed readers apply the illustration to clear preaching. A minister whose meaning is obscure leaves the congregation unable to follow. Music has meaning only when its notes are distinct. In the same way, worship needs clear content that the mind can grasp and the heart can embrace.

1 Corinthians 14:8

8For if the trumpet gave an uncertain sound, who would prepare himself for war?

Arminian

If the trumpet gives an uncertain sound, who will prepare for battle? Arminian readers see the importance of clear calls to action in the church. Hearers must understand in order to respond.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that unclear preaching leaves God’s people unready. The trumpet image fits the church’s spiritual warfare. A trumpet calls soldiers to act, and an uncertain sound leaves them confused. Clear teaching equips believers to respond in faith and obedience.

1 Corinthians 14:9

9So also you, unless you uttered by the tongue words easy to understand, how would it be known what is spoken? For you would be speaking into the air.

Arminian

Unless they speak intelligible words, they will be speaking into the air. Arminian readers see communication as essential to ministry. Speech that no one understands may be sincere, yet it does not reach anyone. Arminian readers see here a concern that God's word actually be heard and freely answered.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the principle that drove the Reformers to translate Scripture and liturgy into the people’s language. Words not understood are spoken into the air.

1 Corinthians 14:10

10There are, it may be, so many kinds of sounds in the world, and none of them is without meaning.

Arminian

There are many kinds of languages in the world, and none is without meaning. Arminian readers see that language has meaning only when understood. Paul's point is not to dismiss any language but to stress that speech exists to communicate. Gifts of speech serve the church only when meaning passes from speaker to hearer.

Reformed

Reformed readers apply the point to worship. Language has meaning only within a shared understanding, so worship must use words the church can grasp. Paul's illustration from human languages shows that sound alone builds no one up.

1 Corinthians 14:11

11If then I don’t know the meaning of the sound, I would be to him who speaks a foreigner, and he who speaks would be a foreigner to me.

Arminian

If Paul does not know the meaning of the sound, he will be a foreigner to the speaker. Arminian readers see that unintelligible speech divides rather than unites.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the danger of division in the Corinthian church. Unintelligible speech makes fellow believers into foreigners to one another. Paul's concern is not only order but fellowship, since worship should draw the body together.

1 Corinthians 14:12

12So also you, since you are zealous for spiritual gifts, seek that you may abound to the building up of the assembly.

Arminian

Since they are zealous for spiritual gifts, they should seek to abound in building up the church. Arminian readers see zeal redirected toward the good of others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see gifts measured by their usefulness to the body. Zeal is good when it seeks the church’s growth rather than personal display. Paul does not quench their desire for spiritual things but redirects it. The best gifts are those that strengthen others in Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
