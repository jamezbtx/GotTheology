Passage

1 Corinthians 14

Verses 13–25 · WEB

Paul continues: anyone who speaks in a tongue should pray for the power to interpret. He will pray and sing with the spirit and with the understanding also, so that others can say Amen. In the church he would rather speak five words with his understanding than ten thousand in a tongue. Citing Isaiah 28, he says tongues are a sign for unbelievers, while prophecy is for believers. If an outsider enters and hears all speaking in tongues, he will think them mad; but if all prophesy, he will be convicted, the secrets of his heart will be revealed, and he will fall down and worship God. Both traditions see verses 24–25 as a picture of conversion through the word. They describe the inner working of that conversion differently.

1 Corinthians 14:13

13Therefore let him who speaks in another language pray that he may interpret.

Arminian

Let one who speaks in a tongue pray that he may interpret. Arminian readers see believers seeking further gifts for the church’s sake. Prayer here is the means by which the gift becomes useful to others. Arminian readers see God's gifts working together with believers' earnest requests.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priority of understanding in worship. Prayer for interpretation turns a private gift into a public blessing. The gift is still from God, and so is its interpretation. Asking for it shows dependence on the Spirit for the church's good.

1 Corinthians 14:14

14For if I pray in another language, my spirit prays, but my understanding is unfruitful.

Arminian

If Paul prays in a tongue, his spirit prays but his understanding is unfruitful. Arminian readers see tongues as genuine spiritual prayer that bypasses the mind.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s concern that worship engage the mind as well as the spirit. God is to be worshiped with understanding. An unfruitful mind means the speaker does not grasp what is said. Paul does not want worship that bypasses understanding.

1 Corinthians 14:15

15What is it then? I will pray with the spirit, and I will pray with the understanding also. I will sing with the spirit, and I will sing with the understanding also.

Arminian

He will pray and sing with the spirit and with the understanding. Arminian readers see a balance of heartfelt and thoughtful worship. Paul refuses to choose between spirit and understanding. Both prayer and song should come from the heart and carry meaning that the mind can follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a balance of spirit and understanding. Some have cited this verse in support of congregational singing with clear words, such as metrical psalms.

1 Corinthians 14:16

16Otherwise if you bless with the spirit, how will he who fills the place of the unlearned say the “Amen” at your giving of thanks, seeing he doesn’t know what you say?

Arminian

Otherwise, how can the outsider say Amen to the thanksgiving? Arminian readers see worship as a shared act in which all can join. A prayer no one understands leaves others unable to agree with it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the congregational nature of worship. The Amen of the people requires understanding, a point the Reformers pressed against worship in an unknown tongue.

1 Corinthians 14:17

17For you most certainly give thanks well, but the other person is not built up.

Arminian

The speaker may give thanks well, but the other is not built up. Arminian readers see that sincerity is not enough; edification matters. The other person cannot say Amen to what he does not understand. Corporate worship is meant to be shared, so that all can join in it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same principle of edification. Genuine thanksgiving is not enough if it leaves the rest of the church unhelped. The Amen of the congregation requires words it can understand and affirm.

1 Corinthians 14:18

18I thank my God, I speak with other languages more than you all.

Arminian

Paul thanks God that he speaks in tongues more than all of them. Arminian readers see Paul’s own experience of this gift as evidence of its value.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul’s own gift does not lead him to promote it in public worship. His restraint is an example of love limiting the use of a gift.

1 Corinthians 14:19

19However in the assembly I would rather speak five words with my understanding, that I might instruct others also, than ten thousand words in another language.

Arminian

In church he would rather speak five words with understanding than ten thousand in a tongue. Arminian readers see edification as the priority of public worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priority of the intelligible word, a principle central to Reformed worship. Five understood words outweigh ten thousand that are not. Paul, who spoke in tongues more than all of them, still chose teaching in the assembly.

1 Corinthians 14:20

20Brothers, don’t be children in thoughts, yet in malice be babies, but in thoughts be mature.

Arminian

Do not be children in thinking, but in malice be babes, and in thinking be mature. Arminian readers see a call to mature discernment. Paul gently rebukes their fascination with the spectacular. Maturity means weighing gifts by their usefulness, while staying innocent of evil.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this call to growth in knowledge of God through the word. Innocence in evil and maturity in understanding belong together. Paul wants the Corinthians to judge spiritual things with discernment rather than enthusiasm.

1 Corinthians 14:21

21In the law it is written, “By men of strange languages and by the lips of strangers I will speak to this people. Not even thus will they hear me, says the Lord.”

Arminian

Paul quotes Isaiah 28:11–12: by strange tongues God will speak to this people, and yet they will not listen. Arminian readers see a people who resisted God’s word in judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see tongues as a sign of judgment on unbelief, as in Isaiah’s context. Foreign speech in Isaiah signaled that Israel had refused God’s plain word.

1 Corinthians 14:22

22Therefore other languages are for a sign, not to those who believe, but to the unbelieving; but prophesying is for a sign, not to the unbelieving, but to those who believe.

Arminian

Tongues are a sign for unbelievers, prophecy for believers. Arminian readers see tongues as a sign that may confirm unbelief when not understood. Paul quotes Isaiah 28:11-12, where foreign speech was a sign of judgment on Israel's unbelief. Prophecy, by contrast, serves those who believe and can lead outsiders to faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see tongues as a sign of judgment in Isaiah’s sense. Some Reformed interpreters have connected this to the transition from the old covenant to the new.

1 Corinthians 14:23

23If therefore the whole assembly is assembled together and all speak with other languages, and unlearned or unbelieving people come in, won’t they say that you are crazy?

Arminian

If the whole church speaks in tongues and outsiders enter, they will say you are mad. Arminian readers see concern for how worship affects seekers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see concern for the church’s witness. Disorderly worship gives outsiders a false picture of the God who is worshiped. If outsiders hear only unintelligible speech, they will think the church is mad. Paul cares about how the gospel is heard by those who do not yet believe.

1 Corinthians 14:24

24But if all prophesy, and someone unbelieving or unlearned comes in, he is reproved by all, and he is judged by all.

Arminian

If all prophesy and an unbeliever enters, he is convicted and judged by all. Arminian readers see the Spirit using the word to convict, and the hearer responding freely.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit’s convicting work through the word. Conviction is God’s work, not a product of human persuasion alone. Prophecy lays bare the hearer's heart before God. Reformed readers see the Spirit using the word to convict and draw sinners.

1 Corinthians 14:25

25And thus the secrets of his heart are revealed. So he will fall down on his face and worship God, declaring that God is among you indeed.

Arminian

The secrets of his heart are revealed, and he will fall down, worship God, and declare that God is among them. Arminian readers see a picture of conversion in which the convicted person freely responds to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a picture of effectual calling through the preached word. The Spirit reveals the heart, and the response of worship is the fruit of his work.

Key contrast Both traditions see verses 24–25 as conversion through the word. Arminian readers stress the hearer’s free response to grace that convicts. Reformed readers stress the Spirit’s effectual work producing the response of faith and worship.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
