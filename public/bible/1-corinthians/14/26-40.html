Passage

1 Corinthians 14

Verses 26–40 · WEB

Paul gives practical rules for worship. When they come together, each has a psalm, a teaching, a revelation, a tongue, or an interpretation, and all should be done for building up. Tongues should be limited to two or three, in turn, with interpretation; otherwise the speaker should be silent. Prophets should speak two or three, and others should weigh what is said. The spirits of prophets are subject to the prophets, for God is not a God of confusion but of peace. Verses 34–35, on women keeping silent, are among the most debated in Paul, and interpreters in both traditions disagree among themselves. Paul ends by saying they should not forbid tongues, but all things should be done decently and in order.

1 Corinthians 14:26

26What is it then, brothers? When you come together, each one of you has a psalm, has a teaching, has a revelation, has another language, has an interpretation. Let all things be done to build each other up.

Arminian

When they come together, each has something to contribute, and all should be done for edification. Arminian readers see participatory worship in which many share.

Reformed

Reformed readers see participation governed by edification. Every contribution must serve the building up of the body. Paul assumes many members contribute, yet each part must build up the whole. Gifts are given for service, not for display.

1 Corinthians 14:27

27If any man speaks in another language, let it be two, or at the most three, and in turn; and let one interpret.

Arminian

If any speak in a tongue, let it be two or at most three, in turn, and let one interpret. Arminian readers see order in the use of a genuine gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the principle of order applied to tongues. Limits and interpretation keep the gift serving the church. Two or three, one at a time, with interpretation: these limits show that the Spirit's gifts are compatible with order.

1 Corinthians 14:28

28But if there is no interpreter, let him keep silent in the assembly, and let him speak to himself, and to God.

Arminian

If there is no interpreter, let him be silent in the church and speak to himself and to God. Arminian readers see that self-control is possible in the use of gifts.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the gift is under the speaker’s control. True spiritual gifts do not require disorder. Without an interpreter, the speaker should keep silent in the church and speak to himself and to God. The gift serves the church, not the other way round.

1 Corinthians 14:29

29Let the prophets speak, two or three, and let the others discern.

Arminian

Let two or three prophets speak, and let the others weigh what is said. Arminian readers see prophecy tested by the church. Even genuine prophecy is to be tested by others, which suggests it does not carry the same authority as Scripture. Arminian readers see a church that listens to the Spirit while exercising discernment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that New Testament prophecy must be judged. Many use this to distinguish such prophecy from the authority of Scripture, which judges all.

1 Corinthians 14:30

30But if a revelation is made to another sitting by, let the first keep silent.

Arminian

If a revelation comes to another sitting by, let the first be silent. Arminian readers see the Spirit’s freedom in worship within an orderly framework.

Reformed

Reformed readers see order maintained even amid spontaneity. The Spirit does not cause speakers to compete with one another. If a revelation comes to another, the first speaker should yield. Humility and mutual deference mark true spiritual ministry.

1 Corinthians 14:31

31For you all can prophesy one by one, that all may learn, and all may be exhorted.

Arminian

All may prophesy one by one, so that all may learn and be encouraged. Arminian readers see broad participation for the good of all. Arminian readers note that the gift is not confined to a small class. Paul expects the Spirit to work through many members so that the whole church learns and is encouraged.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that learning is a key goal. Prophecy serves the instruction and comfort of the whole church. Speakers take turns so that the church can listen and receive what is said.

1 Corinthians 14:32

32The spirits of the prophets are subject to the prophets,

Arminian

The spirits of the prophets are subject to the prophets. Arminian readers see that the Spirit does not override human control, which fits their view of grace that works with human freedom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that true prophecy is not ecstatic frenzy. The Spirit works through the person’s faculties. Prophets remain responsible for how and when they speak. The Spirit does not override the person but works through his mind and will.

1 Corinthians 14:33

33for God is not a God of confusion, but of peace, as in all the assemblies of the saints.

Arminian

God is not a God of confusion but of peace, as in all the churches of the saints. Arminian readers see God’s character reflected in orderly worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s character reflected in orderly worship. This verse is often cited in support of ordered public worship in Reformed churches. God is not a God of confusion but of peace. The way the church worships should reflect who God is.

1 Corinthians 14:34

34Let the wives be quiet in the assemblies, for it has not been permitted for them to be talking except in submission, as the law also says,

Arminian

Let women be silent in the churches, for they are not permitted to speak, but to be in subjection, as the law also says. Arminian readers vary: some see a local situation of disruptive questions, some see a later addition to the text, as Gordon Fee argued, and others hold a restrictive view.

Reformed

Reformed readers vary too: many see a restriction on authoritative speaking or judging prophecy, since 11:5 assumes women prayed and prophesied. Others see a broader restriction.

Key contrast The debate here does not follow Arminian and Reformed lines closely. Many Wesleyan and Pentecostal Arminians affirm women in ministry and read verses 34–35 as limited to a particular disruption. Many Reformed readers see a restriction on authoritative speaking or judging prophecy.

1 Corinthians 14:35

35if they desire to learn anything. “Let them ask their own husbands at home, for it is shameful for a wife to be talking in the assembly.”

Arminian

If they want to learn, let them ask their husbands at home, for it is shameful for a woman to speak in the church. Arminian readers often see a concern for order in a setting where some women were interrupting worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see this as specifying the kind of speech Paul has in mind, such as questioning or judging prophecies. They read it within the larger order of worship described in the chapter.

1 Corinthians 14:36

36What!? Was it from you that the word of God went out? Or did it come to you alone?

Arminian

Did the word of God go out from them, or come to them only? Arminian readers see Paul challenging Corinthian independence. The church must submit to the shared practice of the churches.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul asserting apostolic authority over a church acting as if it were the source of the word. The church receives the word; it does not originate it.

1 Corinthians 14:37

37If any man thinks himself to be a prophet, or spiritual, let him recognize the things which I write to you, that they are the commandment of the Lord.

Arminian

If anyone thinks himself a prophet or spiritual, let him recognize that what Paul writes is the Lord’s command. Arminian readers see apostolic authority as the measure of true spirituality.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this verse as important for the authority of Scripture. Paul’s writings carry the Lord’s authority. Paul claims that what he writes is the Lord's command, and he expects anyone truly spiritual to recognize it.

1 Corinthians 14:38

38But if anyone is ignorant, let him be ignorant.

Arminian

If anyone does not recognize this, he is not recognized. Arminian readers see a serious warning to those who resist apostolic teaching. Arminian readers see a real call to respond to the word Paul has given. Those who refuse to recognize the Lord's command place themselves outside the recognition Paul gives to true prophets.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same warning. Rejecting the apostle’s word places a person outside the recognition of the church. Some take “he is not recognized” to mean God does not recognize such a person, which makes the warning more solemn still.

1 Corinthians 14:39

39Therefore, brothers, desire earnestly to prophesy, and don’t forbid speaking with other languages.

Arminian

Desire to prophesy and do not forbid speaking in tongues. Arminian readers in continuationist traditions see a clear instruction for the church today. Many Arminians outside those traditions also take it seriously, though they differ on how it applies. Either way, Paul wants eager pursuit of prophecy without suppressing other gifts.

Reformed

Reformed readers who are cessationist see this as applying to the apostolic church while gifts were active. Reformed continuationists read it as still in force.

1 Corinthians 14:40

40Let all things be done decently and in order.

Arminian

Let all things be done decently and in order. Arminian readers see order as the setting in which the Spirit’s gifts are best used. Order does not quench the Spirit but serves his work of building up the church. Arminian readers see freedom and order held together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this as a key principle of worship. Calvin and later Reformed churches cited it in regulating public worship. Some Reformed churches see this as a basis for structured worship. Others stress that order serves edification, not formality for its own sake.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
