Passage

1 Corinthians 1

Verses 18–25 · WEB

Paul contrasts the message of the cross with human wisdom. To those who are perishing it is folly, but to those being saved it is the power of God. God has chosen to save believers through what the world calls foolish preaching, and Christ crucified, a scandal to Jews and folly to Greeks, is God’s power and wisdom to those who are called. The language of the called in verse 24 is central to debates over effectual calling, so the two traditions read this paragraph with different emphases while sharing its cross-centred message.

1 Corinthians 1:18

18For the word of the cross is foolishness to those who are dying, but to us who are saved it is the power of God.

Arminian

The word of the cross is folly to the perishing but the power of God to us being saved. Arminian readers see two groups defined by their response to the message, with the present participles showing that people are on a path still open to change. The cross is God’s power for all who receive it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same message producing opposite effects, which raises the question of why some receive it. Paul answers in verse 24 with God’s calling, so the difference lies ultimately in God’s work, not in the hearer’s wisdom.

Key contrast Both agree the cross divides humanity into the perishing and the saved. Arminian readers stress the hearer’s response to a message that is power for all who believe. Reformed readers stress that God’s effectual call explains why some see the cross as power.

1 Corinthians 1:19

19For it is written, “I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, I will bring the discernment of the discerning to nothing.”

Arminian

Paul cites Isaiah: God will destroy the wisdom of the wise. Arminian readers see God overturning human pride so that all must come to him on the same humble terms. Human wisdom cannot reach God by its own efforts.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereignty over human thought. He deliberately frustrates the wisdom of the world so that salvation is clearly his own work.

1 Corinthians 1:20

20Where is the wise? Where is the scribe? Where is the lawyer of this world? Hasn’t God made foolish the wisdom of this world?

Arminian

Where is the wise man, the scribe, the debater? Arminian readers see God exposing the bankruptcy of human systems, both Jewish and Greek, as ways to know him. The question invites humility rather than despair.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God making foolish the wisdom of the world, a judgment that shows the need for revelation. Reason is not worthless, but fallen reason cannot find God on its own terms.

1 Corinthians 1:21

21For seeing that in the wisdom of God, the world through its wisdom didn’t know God, it was God’s good pleasure through the foolishness of the preaching to save those who believe.

Arminian

It pleased God through the folly of preaching to save those who believe. Arminian readers emphasize that the saved are described as those who believe, so faith is the condition God has appointed for salvation. The message is preached to all so that all may believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s good pleasure as the deciding factor and the preaching as his ordained means. Those who believe are saved, and Reformed readers see that belief as itself the fruit of God’s calling described in verse 24.

1 Corinthians 1:22

22For Jews ask for signs, Greeks seek after wisdom,

Arminian

Jews demand signs and Greeks seek wisdom. Arminian readers see the gospel challenging every culture’s preferred route to God. People must abandon their own conditions and accept God’s way.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in these demands the natural resistance of the human heart. Each group sets terms for God, and neither will accept the gospel apart from grace.

1 Corinthians 1:23

23but we preach Christ crucified; a stumbling block to Jews, and foolishness to Greeks,

Arminian

We preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles. Arminian readers see a universal proclamation that meets resistance but is meant for all. The offence of the cross must not be softened to win a hearing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same proclamation going to all, with the cross remaining offensive to the natural mind. The general call of the gospel is sincere even where it is rejected.

1 Corinthians 1:24

24but to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God.

Arminian

To those who are called, Christ is the power and wisdom of God. Arminian readers usually take the called as those who have heard and answered God’s invitation, so that calling here describes believers. God’s call goes out broadly, and those who respond in faith experience Christ as power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the effectual call, distinct from the general preaching of verse 23. The same Christ is preached to all, but only to the called is he power and wisdom, and that call is what overcomes the stumbling block and folly.

1 Corinthians 1:25

25Because the foolishness of God is wiser than men, and the weakness of God is stronger than men.

Arminian

The foolishness of God is wiser than men and his weakness stronger. Arminian readers see the paradox of the cross, where God’s love works through apparent defeat. God’s ways invite trust rather than demand comprehension.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s wisdom vindicated precisely where it seems weakest. The cross is the supreme display of God’s power and wisdom in redemption.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
