Passage

1 Corinthians 1

Verses 4–9 · WEB

Before confronting the Corinthians’ problems, Paul thanks God for the grace given them in Christ, for their rich speech and knowledge, and for their gifts as they wait for Christ’s return. He grounds his confidence that they will be kept blameless to the end in God’s faithfulness. This short paragraph is a key text in discussions of perseverance. Both traditions rejoice in God’s keeping power, but they explain its relation to human faithfulness differently.

1 Corinthians 1:4

4I always thank my God concerning you, for the grace of God which was given you in Christ Jesus;

Arminian

Paul thanks God for the grace given to the Corinthians in Christ Jesus. Arminian readers note that he gives thanks even for a deeply flawed church, recognizing that God’s grace is truly at work among them. The gifts they have come from God, not from their own cleverness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul thanking God, not the Corinthians, for their gifts, because grace is God’s work from first to last. This thanksgiving prepares for his later question in 4:7, what do you have that you did not receive?

1 Corinthians 1:5

5that in everything you were enriched in him, in all speech and all knowledge;

Arminian

They were enriched in every way, in speech and knowledge. Arminian readers see real gifts that the Corinthians were responsible to use well, gifts which they were in fact misusing through pride. Grace given does not guarantee grace rightly stewarded.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the abundance of God’s giving to his church. The same speech and knowledge the Corinthians prized will later be shown to be gifts, so they leave no room for boasting.

1 Corinthians 1:6

6even as the testimony of Christ was confirmed in you:

Arminian

The testimony about Christ was confirmed among them. Arminian readers see the gospel preached by Paul and received in faith, with its truth confirmed in their experience and gifts. The verse highlights the human reception of the message.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God himself establishing the testimony of Christ in them. The confirmation is God’s work, through the Spirit, making the gospel take root in the hearers.

1 Corinthians 1:7

7so that you come behind in no gift; waiting for the revelation of our Lord Jesus Christ;

Arminian

They lack no gift as they wait for the revelation of the Lord Jesus Christ. Arminian readers see the gifts as provision for a church living in hope and responsibility until Christ’s return. Waiting is an active posture of faith and watchfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gifts as tokens of the coming age, given to the church in the interval before Christ appears. The eschatological horizon will correct the Corinthians’ assumption that they have already arrived.

1 Corinthians 1:8

8who will also confirm you until the end, blameless in the day of our Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Christ will confirm them to the end, guiltless in the day of the Lord. Arminian readers see a strong promise of God’s sustaining grace for all who continue in faith. They read it alongside warnings in this same letter, such as 10:12, so that God’s keeping and the believer’s continuing trust go together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a clear promise of perseverance: the one who began the work will bring it to completion. Being kept blameless rests on Christ’s action, not on the Corinthians’ stability, which is why Paul can speak so confidently about a church with so many problems.

Key contrast Both affirm that Christ sustains believers to the end. Arminian readers hold this promise together with the need for continuing faith that can be abandoned. Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness guaranteeing that the truly called will persevere.

1 Corinthians 1:9

9God is faithful, through whom you were called into the fellowship of his Son, Jesus Christ, our Lord.

Arminian

God is faithful, who called them into the fellowship of his Son. Arminian readers see God’s faithfulness as utter reliability in keeping his promises to those in fellowship with Christ. Fellowship is a living relationship maintained through ongoing faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness as the ground of the believer’s preservation. Those whom God has effectually called he will not abandon, so the security of the called rests on his character rather than their performance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
