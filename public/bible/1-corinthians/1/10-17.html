Passage

1 Corinthians 1

Verses 10–17 · WEB

Paul turns to the first problem reported by Chloe’s household: rival groups rallying around Paul, Apollos, Cephas, or even Christ as a party slogan. He insists Christ is not divided and that no human teacher was crucified for them. He is even glad he baptized few of them, since his calling was to preach the gospel without clever rhetoric that would empty the cross of its power. Arminian and Reformed readers agree on the call to unity. Their notes differ mainly in emphasis on the role of baptism and preaching.

1 Corinthians 1:10

10Now I beg you, brothers, through the name of our Lord, Jesus Christ, that you all speak the same thing, and that there be no divisions among you, but that you be perfected together in the same mind and in the same judgment.

Arminian

Paul appeals to them through the name of Christ to agree and end their divisions. Arminian readers see a genuine appeal to the will of believers, who are able and responsible to pursue unity. Being united in mind and judgment is a goal the church must actively seek.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the unity of the church as grounded in its one Lord, whose name Paul invokes. The appeal is a means God uses to bring his people into the unity that their union with Christ already implies.

1 Corinthians 1:11

11For it has been reported to me concerning you, my brothers, by those who are from Chloe’s household, that there are contentions among you.

Arminian

Chloe’s people have reported quarrels among them. Arminian readers note that Paul names his sources openly and deals with real problems directly rather than through rumour. Quarrelling is a serious fault, not a minor matter of temperament.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note Paul’s candour. The report shows that even a richly gifted church remains in need of correction, since sanctification is real but incomplete in this life.

1 Corinthians 1:12

12Now I mean this, that each one of you says, “I follow Paul,” “I follow Apollos,” “I follow Cephas,” and, “I follow Christ.”

Arminian

Each says, I follow Paul, or Apollos, or Cephas, or Christ. Arminian readers see personality cults that distort the gospel by attaching loyalty to human leaders. Even the Christ party may be a slogan used against other believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the folly of ranking God’s servants as if they were the source of grace. Every minister is merely an instrument, so party spirit robs God of glory that belongs to him alone.

1 Corinthians 1:13

13Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized into the name of Paul?

Arminian

Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Arminian readers see Paul pointing to Christ’s death as the shared foundation of every believer’s salvation. No teacher can take Christ’s place as the one who died for them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the cross as the ground of the church’s unity, since Christ alone was crucified for his people. Baptism into Christ’s name marks belonging to him, not to any human leader.

Key contrast Both see the cross as the ground of church unity. Arminian readers stress loyalty to Christ freely chosen over loyalty to leaders. Reformed readers stress Christ’s saving work for his people as what binds them to him alone.

1 Corinthians 1:14

14I thank God that I baptized none of you, except Crispus and Gaius,

Arminian

Paul is thankful he baptized none of them except Crispus and Gaius. Arminian readers note that this does not belittle baptism but shows Paul’s concern that no one would form a faction around him. The rite points to Christ, not to the one who administers it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that the efficacy of baptism does not depend on the minister. The sign belongs to Christ, and its administrator is only a servant.

1 Corinthians 1:15

15so that no one should say that I had baptized you into my own name.

Arminian

He wants no one to say they were baptized in his name. Arminian readers see pastoral wisdom: leaders must avoid anything that draws loyalty to themselves. Humble ministry points people away from the minister.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same concern for God’s glory. A minister who gathers disciples to himself has misunderstood his role as a steward.

1 Corinthians 1:16

16(I also baptized the household of Stephanas; besides them, I don’t know whether I baptized any other.)

Arminian

Paul corrects himself: he also baptized the household of Stephanas. Arminian readers often take household language to include those who believed and were baptized, noting that 16:15 says this household devoted itself to serving the saints. Paul’s lapse of memory shows that he simply did not regard the tally as important.

Reformed

Many Reformed readers note household baptisms as part of the biblical pattern that supports infant baptism within the covenant community, though they acknowledge the text does not specify the ages of those baptized. Paul’s casual recollection also shows baptism’s administrator does not matter.

1 Corinthians 1:17

17For Christ sent me not to baptize, but to preach the Good News—not in wisdom of words, so that the cross of Christ wouldn’t be made void.

Arminian

Christ sent Paul to preach the gospel, not with eloquent wisdom, lest the cross be emptied. Arminian readers see preaching as the means by which God confronts hearers with the cross and calls them to believe. Rhetorical manipulation would put faith on the wrong foundation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the power of the gospel residing in the message of the cross itself, made effective by the Spirit. Human eloquence cannot add to it, and dependence on it would obscure God’s work.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
