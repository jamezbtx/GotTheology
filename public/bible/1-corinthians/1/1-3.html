Passage

1 Corinthians 1

Verses 1–3 · WEB

Paul opens his letter to the troubled church in Corinth by naming himself an apostle called by God’s will and greeting a congregation he calls sanctified and called to be saints. The greeting already sets the agenda: these believers belong to God and share a common Lord with all who call on Christ everywhere, which quietly rebukes the factions Paul will address. Arminian and Reformed readers share nearly all of this. They differ mainly in how they read the language of calling and sanctification.

1 Corinthians 1:1

1Paul, called to be an apostle of Jesus Christ through the will of God, and our brother Sosthenes,

Arminian

Paul introduces himself as called to be an apostle through the will of God, alongside Sosthenes. Arminian readers see a calling to a particular office and mission, which Paul embraced and faithfully carried out. His authority rests on God’s appointment, not on Corinthian approval.

Reformed

Paul grounds his apostleship in the will of God, not human choice or popularity. Reformed readers see God’s sovereign appointment of his servants, a theme that will undercut the Corinthians’ habit of ranking teachers by their own preferences.

1 Corinthians 1:2

2to the assembly of God which is at Corinth; those who are sanctified in Christ Jesus, called to be saints, with all who call on the name of our Lord Jesus Christ in every place, both theirs and ours:

Arminian

Paul addresses the church as sanctified in Christ Jesus and called to be saints, together with all who call on the Lord’s name everywhere. Arminian readers note that the same people called saints are about to be rebuked for serious sin, so holiness is both a status in Christ and a calling to be lived out. Calling on the name of the Lord is the believer’s response of faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see definitive sanctification: the church is already set apart in Christ by God’s act, even before its conduct matches that status. Called to be saints points to God’s effective summons that makes them his people, and this status becomes the basis for Paul’s appeals throughout the letter.

Key contrast Both see the Corinthians as truly set apart in Christ yet needing to live out that holiness. Arminian readers stress the calling to holiness that believers must embrace. Reformed readers stress the definitive status God has already given.

1 Corinthians 1:3

3Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Paul wishes them grace and peace from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. Arminian readers see grace as God’s undeserved favour freely offered and experienced in the life of the church. Placing Jesus beside the Father in the greeting assumes his divine status.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace as the source of every blessing Paul will mention and peace as its fruit in reconciliation with God. The joint naming of Father and Son reflects a high Christology that both traditions share.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
