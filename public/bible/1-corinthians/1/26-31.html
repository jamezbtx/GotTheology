Passage

1 Corinthians 1

Verses 26–31 · WEB

Paul points the Corinthians to their own calling: few of them were wise, powerful, or well born. God chose the foolish, weak, and despised things of the world to shame the strong, so that no one could boast before him. It is God’s doing that they are in Christ, who has become wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and redemption for them. This is one of the clearest election texts in Paul’s letters. Both traditions agree that salvation excludes human boasting, but they differ on what God’s choosing involves.

1 Corinthians 1:26

26For you see your calling, brothers, that not many are wise according to the flesh, not many mighty, and not many noble;

Arminian

Consider your calling: not many were wise, powerful, or of noble birth. Arminian readers see God’s call extended to ordinary people, showing that the gospel is not reserved for elites. The word not many leaves room for some of high status.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s calling falling on unlikely people according to his choice. The make-up of the church itself testifies that God’s grace does not follow human merit or status.

1 Corinthians 1:27

27but God chose the foolish things of the world that he might put to shame those who are wise. God chose the weak things of the world, that he might put to shame the things that are strong;

Arminian

God chose the foolish things of the world to shame the wise. Arminian readers often read this choosing as God’s deliberate preference for the lowly as a class, which reveals his character and humbles pride. Many also connect election to God’s foreknowledge of those who would believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign election of particular persons, chosen in a way that nullifies human claims. The choosing is God’s free act, which is why it leaves no ground for boasting.

Key contrast Arminian readers see God choosing a kind of people, the lowly who respond in faith, so that no one boasts. Reformed readers see God choosing particular persons by free grace, which is why all boasting is excluded.

1 Corinthians 1:28

28and God chose the lowly things of the world, and the things that are despised, and the things that are not, that he might bring to nothing the things that are:

Arminian

God chose the low and despised, even things that are not, to bring to nothing things that are. Arminian readers see God’s reversal of worldly values, opening salvation to the overlooked. The emphasis is on what kind of people God delights to save.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an echo of creation from nothing: God’s choosing makes something out of nothing. Election rests entirely in God, not in anything found in those chosen.

1 Corinthians 1:29

29that no flesh should boast before God.

Arminian

So that no human being might boast before God. Arminian readers stress that faith is not a meritorious work and so excludes boasting, since it simply receives what God gives. Salvation is entirely by grace even though it is received through a free response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the purpose of God’s choosing. If the decisive difference between believer and unbeliever lay in the human will, there would be some ground for boasting, so Reformed readers see God’s choice as decisive.

Key contrast Both traditions agree salvation leaves no room to boast. Arminian readers hold that faith, as receiving a gift, excludes boasting. Reformed readers hold that only God’s decisive choice fully excludes it.

1 Corinthians 1:30

30Because of him, you are in Christ Jesus, who was made to us wisdom from God, and righteousness and sanctification, and redemption:

Arminian

Because of him you are in Christ Jesus, who became wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and redemption. Arminian readers rejoice that everything needed for salvation is found in Christ and received by faith. Union with Christ is God’s gift to those who trust him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see union with Christ as the work of God, from him you are in Christ. Christ as our righteousness supports the doctrine of imputed righteousness, and the full list shows that every benefit of salvation is located in him.

1 Corinthians 1:31

31that, according as it is written, “He who boasts, let him boast in the Lord.”

Arminian

Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord, citing Jeremiah. Arminian readers see the fitting response of the redeemed: gratitude and praise directed entirely to God. Christian identity is grounded in the Lord, not in teachers or gifts.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the goal of the whole paragraph, God’s glory in salvation. Boasting in the Lord is the natural fruit of recognizing that everything comes from him.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
