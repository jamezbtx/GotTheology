Passage

1 Corinthians 9

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Chapter 9 can look like a detour from the question of food offered to idols, but it is Paul’s own example of the principle he set out in chapter 8: love gives up rights for the sake of others. He first establishes that he really has rights as an apostle, including the right to material support, and he grounds that right in common sense, in the law of Moses, in temple practice, and in a command of the Lord. Arminian and Reformed readers agree almost entirely on this passage. Their differences appear mainly in how they connect apostolic labor to the means God uses to bring people to faith.

1 Corinthians 9:1

1Am I not free? Am I not an apostle? Haven’t I seen Jesus Christ, our Lord? Aren’t you my work in the Lord?

Arminian

Paul asks four rhetorical questions: Is he not free, an apostle, a witness of the risen Jesus, and the founder of their church? Arminian readers see the Corinthians themselves as the evidence of a ministry God blessed when they responded to the gospel in faith. Paul’s freedom, which he will choose to limit, is the theme of the whole chapter.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul’s apostleship rests on a direct commission from the risen Christ. The church at Corinth is the work of the Lord through Paul, so its existence testifies to God’s effectual calling through the preached word.

1 Corinthians 9:2

2If to others I am not an apostle, yet at least I am to you; for you are the seal of my apostleship in the Lord.

Arminian

Even if others doubt him, the Corinthians are the seal of his apostleship. Arminian readers see the changed lives of those who believed as the visible confirmation of a genuine ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church as God’s own seal on Paul’s work. A seal authenticates, and the Corinthians’ conversion is God’s signature, not Paul’s achievement.

1 Corinthians 9:3

3My defense to those who examine me is this.

Arminian

Paul introduces his defense to those who are examining him. Arminian readers see a pastor willing to give an account of himself to a church that has questioned him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the defense that follows is not self-promotion but a model of Christian liberty limited by love. Paul answers his critics in order to press the point of chapter 8 by his own example.

1 Corinthians 9:4

4Have we no right to eat and to drink?

Arminian

Paul asks whether he does not have the right to eat and drink, likely at the church’s expense. Arminian readers see Paul establishing a genuine right that he can then freely choose to lay aside.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the right is real. Calvin observed that Paul’s argument only works if the liberty he gives up is truly his, since giving up nothing would teach nothing.

1 Corinthians 9:5

5Have we no right to take along a wife who is a believer, even as the rest of the apostles, and the brothers of the Lord, and Cephas?

Arminian

Paul notes that the other apostles, the Lord’s brothers, and Cephas take believing wives with them on their travels. Arminian readers see normal marriage and family life honored among the earliest leaders of the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers have long cited this verse against mandatory clerical celibacy. The apostles, including Peter, were married, and Paul treats this as a lawful right of ministers.

1 Corinthians 9:6

6Or have only Barnabas and I no right to not work?

Arminian

Paul asks whether only he and Barnabas must work for a living. Arminian readers see Paul’s tentmaking as a free, voluntary choice, not a sign that he lacked the right to support.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same voluntary choice. They also note that the dignity of ordinary labor is affirmed, since an apostle worked with his hands without loss of honor.

1 Corinthians 9:7

7What soldier ever serves at his own expense? Who plants a vineyard, and doesn’t eat of its fruit? Or who feeds a flock, and doesn’t drink from the flock’s milk?

Arminian

Soldiers, vineyard planters, and shepherds all live from their work. Arminian readers see Paul appealing to common sense and ordinary fairness that any listener would recognize.

Reformed

Reformed readers see natural law at work. What is fair in ordinary life reflects God’s created order, and Paul draws on it before he turns to Scripture.

1 Corinthians 9:8

8Do I speak these things according to the ways of men? Or doesn’t the law also say the same thing?

Arminian

Paul asks whether he speaks merely from human custom, or whether the law says the same. Arminian readers see Paul moving from human reasoning to the authority of Scripture.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul treats the law of Moses as still instructive for the church. The moral principle behind it carries over even where its specific form does not.

1 Corinthians 9:9

9For it is written in the law of Moses, “You shall not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain.” Is it for the oxen that God cares,

Arminian

Paul quotes Deuteronomy 25:4: do not muzzle the ox while it treads the grain. He then asks whether God is concerned only for oxen. Arminian readers see God’s care for the worker, human or animal, expressed in a law that has a wider purpose.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an example of reading the law by its underlying principle. The command about oxen expresses God’s justice toward all who labor, which is why Paul can apply it to ministers.

1 Corinthians 9:10

10or does he say it assuredly for our sake? Yes, it was written for our sake, because he who plows ought to plow in hope, and he who threshes in hope should partake of his hope.

Arminian

Paul says the text was written for our sake, so that the plowman and thresher work in hope of sharing the crop. Arminian readers see Scripture written for the instruction of every generation of believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same truth and connect it to the conviction that the whole Old Testament speaks to the church. Paul develops the same point in 10:11, where Israel’s history is written for our instruction.

1 Corinthians 9:11

11If we sowed to you spiritual things, is it a great thing if we reap your fleshly things?

Arminian

If Paul sowed spiritual things among them, is it too much to reap material things? Arminian readers see a fair exchange that the Corinthians should freely and gladly recognize. Gratitude for the gospel naturally expresses itself in support for those who bring it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the principle that those who receive the word owe support to those who teach it. Spiritual goods are greater than material ones, so the obligation is light by comparison.

1 Corinthians 9:12

12If others partake of this right over you, don’t we yet more? Nevertheless we did not use this right, but we bear all things, that we may cause no hindrance to the Good News of Christ.

Arminian

Others enjoy this right over them, yet Paul did not use it, but endured everything so as not to hinder the gospel. Arminian readers see Paul removing any obstacle that might keep people from freely responding to the message.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Paul removing obstacles. God’s purpose is sure, but he works through means, and Paul takes care that the means are not discredited by suspicion of greed.

Key contrast Both traditions read verse 12 as a ministry choice shaped by love. Arminian readers stress that hindrances can genuinely keep people from believing. Reformed readers stress that God uses faithful, unhindered means to call his elect.

1 Corinthians 9:13

13Don’t you know that those who serve around sacred things eat from the things of the temple, and those who wait on the altar have their portion with the altar?

Arminian

Those who serve in the temple eat from the temple, and those at the altar share in its offerings. Arminian readers see Paul drawing on a pattern God himself established in Israel’s worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same pattern. The priestly provision in Numbers 18 shows that God has always arranged for the support of those set apart to serve him.

1 Corinthians 9:14

14Even so the Lord ordained that those who proclaim the Good News should live from the Good News.

Arminian

The Lord ordained that those who proclaim the gospel should live by the gospel, likely recalling Jesus’ words in Luke 10:7. Arminian readers see a command that establishes a right without forcing Paul to use it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the basis for a supported ministry in the church. Calvin and later Reformed confessions treat the support of pastors as a duty of the congregation, rooted in Christ’s own command.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
