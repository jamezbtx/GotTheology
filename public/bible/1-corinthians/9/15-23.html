Passage

1 Corinthians 9

Verses 15–23 · WEB

Paul now explains why he refuses the support he has just proven he may claim. Preaching itself is a necessity laid on him, so his only reward is to offer the gospel free of charge. From there he states a broader rule of his ministry: though free from all, he has made himself a servant to all, becoming like Jews, like those under the law, like those outside the law, and like the weak, so that by all means he might save some. Arminian readers often find in verse 22 a strong statement of genuine human means in salvation. Reformed readers agree that the means are real but place them under God’s sovereign purpose to save his people.

1 Corinthians 9:15

15But I have used none of these things, and I don’t write these things that it may be done so in my case; for I would rather die, than that anyone should make my boasting void.

Arminian

Paul has used none of these rights and is not writing to get them now; he would rather die than lose his boast. Arminian readers see the freedom of a minister who serves out of love rather than obligation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul’s boast is not in himself but in a ministry kept free from any appearance of self-interest. His aim is that Christ alone, not the preacher, be honored.

1 Corinthians 9:16

16For if I preach the Good News, I have nothing to boast about; for necessity is laid on me; but woe is to me, if I don’t preach the Good News.

Arminian

Preaching is no grounds for boasting, for necessity is laid on him; woe to him if he does not preach. Arminian readers see a calling that Paul could resist, which is why he speaks of woe for disobedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the compulsion of a divine call. Paul was laid hold of by Christ, and the necessity he feels reflects God’s sovereign appointment of him to this work.

Key contrast Both traditions take Paul’s call as compelling. Arminian readers stress the real accountability implied by “woe.” Reformed readers stress the divine necessity laid on him by God’s appointment.

1 Corinthians 9:17

17For if I do this of my own will, I have a reward. But if not of my own will, I have a stewardship entrusted to me.

Arminian

If he preaches willingly he has a reward; if not, he is still entrusted with a stewardship. Arminian readers see a distinction between Paul’s free choices and his duty, which both matter to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul as a steward who must be faithful regardless of his feelings. The stewardship comes from God, and faithfulness is its measure, as Paul said in 4:2.

1 Corinthians 9:18

18What then is my reward? That, when I preach the Good News, I may present the Good News of Christ without charge, so as not to abuse my authority in the Good News.

Arminian

His reward is to make the gospel free of charge, so as not to use his full right. Arminian readers see Paul’s joy in offering grace as freely as he received it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a fitting match between the message and its delivery. A gospel of free grace is commended when it is preached without charge.

1 Corinthians 9:19

19For though I was free from all, I brought myself under bondage to all, that I might gain the more.

Arminian

Though free from all, Paul made himself a servant to all, so that he might gain the more. Arminian readers see Paul’s whole strategy aimed at persuading as many people as possible, which assumes that people can truly respond.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Paul labors to gain many. They see no tension between God’s election and this effort, since God has ordained the means of preaching as well as its result.

1 Corinthians 9:20

20To the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might gain Jews; to those who are under the law, as under the law, that I might gain those who are under the law;

Arminian

To the Jews he became as a Jew, and to those under the law as under the law, though not himself under the law. Arminian readers see flexible, culturally sensitive mission aimed at winning hearers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see liberty used in love. Paul could keep Jewish customs without making them a condition of salvation, which guards the gospel of grace while removing needless offense.

1 Corinthians 9:21

21to those who are without law, as without law (not being without law toward God, but under law toward Christ), that I might win those who are without law.

Arminian

To those without law he became as without law, yet not without law to God but under the law of Christ. Arminian readers see freedom that remains accountable to Christ’s commands.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in “the law of Christ” support for the moral law as a continuing rule of life for believers. Calvin called this the third and principal use of the law, guiding those already justified.

1 Corinthians 9:22

22To the weak I became as weak, that I might gain the weak. I have become all things to all men, that I may by all means save some.

Arminian

To the weak he became weak; he has become all things to all people, that by all means he might save some. Arminian readers see this as strong evidence that human means matter in salvation, and that the outcome of preaching is not fixed apart from them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul as God’s instrument. He says “save some,” not all, and Reformed interpreters see God working through Paul’s adaptable labors to save those he has chosen.

Key contrast Arminian readers read “by all means… save some” as showing that real human effort affects who is saved. Reformed readers read it as describing the God-ordained means by which the elect are surely brought to faith.

1 Corinthians 9:23

23Now I do this for the sake of the Good News, that I may be a joint partaker of it.

Arminian

Paul does all this for the gospel’s sake, that he may share in it with them. Arminian readers note that Paul seems to see his own participation in the gospel’s blessings as something he must pursue, which leads into the warning of verses 24–27.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s desire to be a fellow partaker as the natural longing of a believer. His labors flow from his share in the gospel rather than earning it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
