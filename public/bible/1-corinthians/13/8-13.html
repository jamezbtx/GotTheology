Passage

1 Corinthians 13

Verses 8–13 · WEB

Paul now contrasts the permanence of love with the temporary nature of gifts. Prophecies, tongues, and knowledge will pass away when the perfect comes, for now we know in part and see as in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Faith, hope, and love remain, and the greatest of these is love. The meaning of “the perfect” is a key point. Most interpreters in both traditions see it as Christ’s return and the final state. Some Reformed cessationists have read it as the completed canon, though many cessationists, including Reformed scholars, do not rely on that reading.

1 Corinthians 13:8

8Love never fails. But where there are prophecies, they will be done away with. Where there are various languages, they will cease. Where there is knowledge, it will be done away with.

Arminian

Love never fails, but prophecies, tongues, and knowledge will cease. Arminian readers see love as the permanent mark of the Christian life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see gifts serving the church in this age, while love belongs to the age to come as well. That is why love outranks every gift.

1 Corinthians 13:9

9For we know in part, and we prophesy in part;

Arminian

We know in part and prophesy in part. Arminian readers see the humility appropriate to present spiritual knowledge.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the limits of all human knowledge of God, even with true revelation. Theology now is real but partial.

1 Corinthians 13:10

10but when that which is complete has come, then that which is partial will be done away with.

Arminian

When the perfect comes, the partial will be done away. Arminian readers, especially continuationists, see “the perfect” as Christ’s return, when gifts will no longer be needed.

Reformed

Most Reformed readers also see Christ’s return here. Some cessationists have argued that “the perfect” means the completed canon or a mature church, though many cessationists base their view on other texts.

Key contrast Most Arminian and Reformed interpreters see “the perfect” as the return of Christ. A minority of Reformed cessationists have read it as the completed New Testament, though many cessationists ground their view elsewhere.

1 Corinthians 13:11

11When I was a child, I spoke as a child, I felt as a child, I thought as a child. Now that I have become a man, I have put away childish things.

Arminian

When Paul was a child he spoke, thought, and reasoned as a child, but as a man he put away childish things. Arminian readers see growth toward maturity in the Christian life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the contrast between the present age and the age to come. The church’s present knowledge, though true, is like a child’s compared with what it will be.

1 Corinthians 13:12

12For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part, but then I will know fully, even as I was also fully known.

Arminian

Now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face; now I know in part, but then fully, as I am fully known. Arminian readers see the hope of full knowledge and fellowship with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the beatific vision promised to believers. Being “fully known” points to God’s prior knowledge of his people, which some Reformed interpreters connect to election.

1 Corinthians 13:13

13But now faith, hope, and love remain—these three. The greatest of these is love.

Arminian

Faith, hope, and love remain, and the greatest is love. Arminian readers see love as the summit of Christian holiness, which Wesley described as perfect love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faith, hope, and love as abiding graces given by the Spirit. Love is greatest because it is the very nature of God and the goal of all his works.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
