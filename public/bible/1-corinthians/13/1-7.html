Passage

1 Corinthians 13

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Between two chapters on spiritual gifts, Paul sets the most famous passage on love in Scripture. Tongues of angels, prophetic powers, all knowledge, mountain-moving faith, giving everything to the poor, and even martyrdom are nothing without love. Love is then described by what it does and what it refuses to do: it is patient and kind, not envious or boastful, not rude or self-seeking, and it bears, believes, hopes, and endures all things. Arminian and Reformed readers share a deep admiration for this chapter. Wesleyan Arminians often connect it to Wesley’s teaching on Christian perfection as perfect love, while Reformed readers see it as describing the fruit of regeneration that grows through sanctification.

1 Corinthians 13:1

1If I speak with the languages of men and of angels, but don’t have love, I have become sounding brass, or a clanging cymbal.

Arminian

Without love, even speaking in tongues of men and angels is only a noisy gong or clanging cymbal. Arminian readers see love as the measure of all spiritual gifts and the heart of holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. Gifts may exist without grace, but love is the evidence of the Spirit’s saving work.

1 Corinthians 13:2

2If I have the gift of prophecy, and know all mysteries and all knowledge; and if I have all faith, so as to remove mountains, but don’t have love, I am nothing.

Arminian

Without love, prophecy, knowledge of all mysteries, and faith to move mountains are nothing. Arminian readers see that spiritual power without love is empty.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that gifts can be given even to those who are not saved, as Matthew 7:22–23 suggests. Love, not gifts, marks the true believer.

Key contrast Both traditions see love as the mark that gifts cannot replace. Arminian readers connect it to growth in holiness and perfect love. Reformed readers connect it to the evidence of regeneration that distinguishes true believers from gifted but unconverted people.

1 Corinthians 13:3

3If I give away all my goods to feed the poor, and if I give my body to be burned, but don’t have love, it profits me nothing.

Arminian

Giving away everything and even handing over the body to be burned profits nothing without love. Arminian readers see that outward sacrifice cannot replace a heart of love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a warning against trusting in works. Even the greatest acts are worthless before God unless they come from love worked by the Spirit.

1 Corinthians 13:4

4Love is patient and is kind; love doesn’t envy. Love doesn’t brag, is not proud,

Arminian

Love is patient and kind; love does not envy, boast, or become proud. Arminian readers see the character that God’s grace produces as believers cooperate with the Spirit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a portrait of Christ himself, whose love is reproduced in believers through sanctification. Each phrase describes what Christ is before it describes what we should be.

1 Corinthians 13:5

5doesn’t behave itself inappropriately, doesn’t seek its own way, is not provoked, takes no account of evil;

Arminian

Love does not behave rudely, seek its own, become provoked, or keep account of evil. Arminian readers see love directly opposing the Corinthians’ divisions and self-seeking.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the echo of God’s own character, who does not count sins against those in Christ. Love’s refusal to keep score reflects justification itself.

1 Corinthians 13:6

6doesn’t rejoice in unrighteousness, but rejoices with the truth;

Arminian

Love does not rejoice in unrighteousness but rejoices with the truth. Arminian readers see love tied to truth and holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see love and truth held together. Love is never indifferent to sin or to sound doctrine, and it delights when the truth prevails.

1 Corinthians 13:7

7bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things.

Arminian

Love bears, believes, hopes, and endures all things. Arminian readers see the endurance of love as part of persevering faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see love’s endurance as a reflection of God’s steadfast love, which keeps his people. Love bears all things because it rests on God’s faithfulness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
