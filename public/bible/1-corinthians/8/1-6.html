Passage

1 Corinthians 8

Verses 1–6 · WEB

Paul turns to food offered to idols. The Corinthians claim knowledge: idols are nothing, and there is only one God. Paul agrees with the theology but warns that knowledge puffs up while love builds up, and that being known by God matters more than knowing. In verse 6 he gives a striking confession: one God, the Father, from whom are all things, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things. Both traditions share this high Christology. They differ mainly in how they read being known by God.

1 Corinthians 8:1

1Now concerning things sacrificed to idols: We know that we all have knowledge. Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up.

Arminian

Concerning food sacrificed to idols, we all have knowledge, but knowledge puffs up and love builds up. Arminian readers see love as the governing principle of Christian freedom. Knowledge without love harms others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sound doctrine needing to be joined with love. Truth used proudly becomes a weapon rather than a blessing.

1 Corinthians 8:2

2But if anyone thinks that he knows anything, he doesn’t yet know as he ought to know.

Arminian

Anyone who thinks he knows something does not yet know as he ought. Arminian readers see humility as the mark of true understanding. Believers are always learners.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the limits of human knowledge before God. Right knowledge is relational and humble, not proud mastery.

1 Corinthians 8:3

3But if anyone loves God, the same is known by him.

Arminian

If anyone loves God, he is known by God. Arminian readers see a loving relationship with God as what matters most, with God’s knowing as his personal acknowledgment of those who love him. Love for God is the evidence of genuine relationship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see being known by God as his prior, electing love, as in Galatians 4:9 and Romans 8:29. God’s knowing precedes and grounds the believer’s love.

Key contrast Both see love for God and being known by him as more important than knowledge. Arminian readers read God’s knowing as his acknowledgment of those who love him. Reformed readers read it as God’s prior electing love that produces the believer’s love.

1 Corinthians 8:4

4Therefore concerning the eating of things sacrificed to idols, we know that no idol is anything in the world, and that there is no other God but one.

Arminian

An idol has no real existence, and there is no God but one. Arminian readers see the truth of monotheism affirmed. The Corinthians’ theology is right; their application is the problem.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the confession of the one true God, echoing the Shema. Idols are nothing, though 10:20 will show demons behind them.

1 Corinthians 8:5

5For though there are things that are called “gods”, whether in the heavens or on earth; as there are many “gods” and many “lords”;

Arminian

There may be so-called gods in heaven or on earth, many gods and lords. Arminian readers see Paul acknowledging the religious world of Corinth. These beings hold sway in people’s lives though not as true gods.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the reality of false worship and its spiritual dimension. So-called gods have no claim to divinity.

1 Corinthians 8:6

6yet to us there is one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we for him; and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things, and we live through him.

Arminian

For us there is one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things. Arminian readers see Christ included within the identity of the one God, sharing in creation. This confession grounds Christian worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a rich trinitarian and christological confession, with the Son as agent of creation. Many scholars note how Paul reworks the Shema to include Jesus within it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
