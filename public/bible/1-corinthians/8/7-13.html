Passage

1 Corinthians 8

Verses 7–13 · WEB

Not everyone has this knowledge. Some believers, used to idols, eat such food as idol food and their weak conscience is defiled. Food does not commend anyone to God, but the strong must not let their freedom become a stumbling block. If a weak believer is emboldened to eat in an idol’s temple, he may be destroyed, the brother for whom Christ died. Sinning against such a brother is sinning against Christ, so Paul would never eat meat rather than cause a brother to fall. Verse 11 is significant in debates about apostasy and the extent of the atonement.

1 Corinthians 8:7

7However, that knowledge isn’t in all men. But some, with consciousness of the idol until now, eat as of a thing sacrificed to an idol, and their conscience, being weak, is defiled.

Arminian

Not all possess this knowledge; some eat with a weak conscience and are defiled. Arminian readers see genuine believers who struggle with old associations. Their conscience must be respected.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the importance of conscience, even when ill-informed. Acting against conscience is sin for the one who does it.

1 Corinthians 8:8

8But food will not commend us to God. For neither, if we don’t eat, are we the worse; nor, if we eat, are we the better.

Arminian

Food will not bring us closer to God; we are no worse off if we do not eat. Arminian readers see food as morally neutral. Standing with God does not depend on diet.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christian liberty in matters indifferent. Neither eating nor abstaining earns favour with God.

1 Corinthians 8:9

9But be careful that by no means does this liberty of yours become a stumbling block to the weak.

Arminian

Take care that your right does not become a stumbling block to the weak. Arminian readers see love limiting liberty for others’ sake. Freedom must serve, not harm.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the principle that liberty is constrained by love for the neighbour. The strong bear responsibility toward the weak.

1 Corinthians 8:10

10For if a man sees you who have knowledge sitting in an idol’s temple, won’t his conscience, if he is weak, be emboldened to eat things sacrificed to idols?

Arminian

If someone sees you eating in an idol’s temple, will he not be emboldened to eat idol food? Arminian readers see how example can lead others into sin. Choices have social consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the influence of the strong over the weak. Encouraging someone to act against conscience leads him into sin.

1 Corinthians 8:11

11And through your knowledge, he who is weak perishes, the brother for whose sake Christ died.

Arminian

By your knowledge the weak brother is destroyed, the brother for whom Christ died. Arminian readers see a real possibility that a believer for whom Christ died can be destroyed, which supports both the possibility of apostasy and an atonement not limited to the finally saved. Paul’s warning is not hypothetical.

Reformed

Reformed readers often take destroyed to mean grievous spiritual harm rather than final loss, or read the warning as describing the tendency of the act. Some understand the brother as one judged by profession, while Christ’s death remains effective for all his elect.

Key contrast Both take the warning seriously as a call to love the weak. Arminian readers see evidence that a believer for whom Christ died can finally perish. Reformed readers see grievous harm or the tendency of the act, while holding that God preserves those Christ redeemed.

1 Corinthians 8:12

12Thus, sinning against the brothers, and wounding their conscience when it is weak, you sin against Christ.

Arminian

Sinning against brothers and wounding their weak conscience is sinning against Christ. Arminian readers see the union between Christ and his people making harm to them an offence against him. Love for Christ includes love for the weak.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s identification with his body, as in Acts 9:4. Harm to the least believer is felt by the Lord.

1 Corinthians 8:13

13Therefore if food causes my brother to stumble, I will eat no meat forever more, that I don’t cause my brother to stumble.

Arminian

If food makes my brother stumble, I will never eat meat. Arminian readers see Paul freely choosing to limit his rights out of love. His example invites believers to do the same.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-denial for the sake of others as the fruit of grace. Paul’s willingness anticipates the next chapter’s discussion of his rights.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
