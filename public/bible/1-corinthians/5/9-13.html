Passage

1 Corinthians 5

Verses 9–13 · WEB

Paul clarifies an earlier letter. He did not mean that believers must withdraw from contact with immoral people in the world, which would require leaving the world. He meant they should not associate with a so-called brother who lives in persistent immorality, greed, idolatry, slander, drunkenness, or swindling. God judges outsiders; the church must judge those inside and remove the evil person. Arminian and Reformed readers largely agree here. They differ mainly on how they describe the spiritual state of the disciplined person.

1 Corinthians 5:9

9I wrote to you in my letter to have no company with sexual sinners;

Arminian

Paul had written not to associate with sexually immoral people. Arminian readers note the reference to an earlier letter, now lost. Paul now corrects a misunderstanding of it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that not every apostolic letter was preserved, yet God has given what is needed. The issue is clarifying the scope of separation.

1 Corinthians 5:10

10yet not at all meaning with the sexual sinners of this world, or with the covetous and extortionists, or with idolaters; for then you would have to leave the world.

Arminian

He did not mean the immoral of this world, or they would have to leave the world. Arminian readers see believers called to engage the world as witnesses rather than retreat. Contact with sinners is part of mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same balance: separation from sin, not from society. The church lives in the world while not being of it.

1 Corinthians 5:11

11But as it is, I wrote to you not to associate with anyone who is called a brother who is a sexual sinner, or covetous, or an idolater, or a slanderer, or a drunkard, or an extortionist. Don’t even eat with such a person.

Arminian

Do not associate with anyone who bears the name of brother but is immoral, greedy, idolatrous, slanderous, a drunkard, or swindler. Arminian readers note that the person is called a brother, a professing believer, whose conduct now contradicts that profession. Discipline treats the profession seriously.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the phrase so-called brother and see a profession that the person’s life calls into question. Withholding table fellowship is a serious step meant to bring repentance.

Key contrast Both see the church responsible for those who profess faith. Arminian readers can see a true believer who has fallen into serious sin. Reformed readers see a professing believer whose life calls his profession into question.

1 Corinthians 5:12

12For what do I have to do with also judging those who are outside? Don’t you judge those who are within?

Arminian

What business is it of mine to judge outsiders? Do you not judge those inside? Arminian readers see the church’s responsibility limited to its own members. Holiness begins at home.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a clear boundary between church and world in matters of discipline. The church does not police society but must govern itself.

1 Corinthians 5:13

13But those who are outside, God judges. “Put away the wicked man from among yourselves.”

Arminian

God judges outsiders; remove the evil person from among you. Arminian readers see God’s justice over all and the church’s duty, echoing Deuteronomy, to purge evil from its fellowship. Judgment of the world belongs to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the continuity of God’s call to holiness from Israel to the church. Discipline in the church replaces the civil penalties of Israel’s law.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
