Passage

1 Corinthians 5

Verses 1–8 · WEB

Paul confronts a case of sexual immorality so shocking that even pagans would condemn it: a man living with his father’s wife. The church is proud rather than grieving. Paul has already judged the case and directs the assembly, gathered in the Lord’s name, to hand the man over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh so that his spirit may be saved on the day of the Lord. Using Passover imagery, he urges them to clear out the old leaven, since Christ our Passover has been sacrificed. Both traditions see here a mandate for church discipline aimed at restoration. They read the ultimate aim of verse 5 in somewhat different terms.

1 Corinthians 5:1

1It is actually reported that there is sexual immorality among you, and such sexual immorality as is not even named among the Gentiles, that one has his father’s wife.

Arminian

Sexual immorality is reported among them, of a kind not tolerated even among pagans. Arminian readers see that believers can fall into grievous sin and that the church is responsible to address it. Public scandal dishonours Christ before outsiders.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the seriousness of remaining sin in the visible church. The visible church can contain those whose lives contradict their profession, which is why discipline is needed.

1 Corinthians 5:2

2You are puffed up, and didn’t rather mourn, that he who had done this deed might be removed from among you.

Arminian

They are arrogant rather than mourning and removing the offender. Arminian readers see a failure of corporate responsibility, perhaps even a misplaced pride in tolerance. Grief over sin is the right response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church’s duty to guard its purity. Pride blinded the Corinthians to their obligation, and discipline is a mark of a true church in the Reformed confessions.

1 Corinthians 5:3

3For I most certainly, as being absent in body but present in spirit, have already, as though I were present, judged him who has done this thing.

Arminian

Though absent in body, Paul is present in spirit and has already judged the man. Arminian readers see apostolic authority exercised for the church’s good. The judgment concerns conduct that is beyond dispute.

Reformed

Reformed readers see apostolic authority guiding the church’s discipline. Paul’s judgment is not a substitute for the church’s action but its foundation.

1 Corinthians 5:4

4In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, you being gathered together, and my spirit, with the power of our Lord Jesus Christ,

Arminian

When they are assembled in the Lord’s name, with Paul’s spirit and the Lord’s power. Arminian readers see congregational action carried out with Christ’s authority. Discipline is a corporate act, not a private grudge.

Reformed

Reformed readers see discipline as the exercise of the keys of the kingdom by the gathered church. Christ’s power is present when the church acts in his name.

1 Corinthians 5:5

5are to deliver such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus.

Arminian

Hand this man over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord. Arminian readers see excommunication placing the man outside the church’s protection, with the hope that he will repent and return. Salvation is the goal, though it depends on his response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see discipline as God’s means of restoring a straying believer or exposing a false professor. The goal of salvation shows that discipline is remedial, not merely punitive.

Key contrast Both see church discipline as aimed at final salvation. Arminian readers stress the man’s need to repent in response to discipline. Reformed readers stress discipline as a means God uses to bring his own to repentance.

1 Corinthians 5:6

6Your boasting is not good. Don’t you know that a little yeast leavens the whole lump?

Arminian

Their boasting is not good; a little leaven leavens the whole lump. Arminian readers see sin’s spreading influence, which threatens the whole community. Tolerated sin shapes a church’s culture.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the corporate danger of unchecked sin. Discipline protects the body as well as the offender.

1 Corinthians 5:7

7Purge out the old yeast, that you may be a new lump, even as you are unleavened. For indeed Christ, our Passover, has been sacrificed in our place.

Arminian

Clean out the old leaven so you may be a new lump, as you really are unleavened; Christ our Passover has been sacrificed. Arminian readers see a call to live out the newness Christ’s sacrifice made possible. Believers must actively remove sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the indicative grounding the imperative: you are unleavened, so become what you are. Christ’s sacrifice is the basis of the church’s holiness.

1 Corinthians 5:8

8Therefore let us keep the feast, not with old yeast, neither with the yeast of malice and wickedness, but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth.

Arminian

Let us keep the feast with the bread of sincerity and truth. Arminian readers see the Christian life as an ongoing celebration of redemption marked by holiness. Malice and wickedness have no place at the feast.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the whole life of the church as a Passover feast grounded in Christ. Sincerity and truth are the fruits of redemption.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
