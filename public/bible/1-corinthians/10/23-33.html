Passage

1 Corinthians 10

Verses 23–33 · WEB

Paul ends the long discussion of food offered to idols with practical guidance. All things are lawful, but not all things build up, so each should seek the good of others. Believers may eat whatever is sold in the market and whatever is served at an unbeliever’s table without asking questions, since the earth is the Lord’s. But if someone points out that the food was offered in sacrifice, they should refrain for that person’s sake. Whether eating or drinking, they should do all to the glory of God, giving no offense and seeking the salvation of many. Arminian and Reformed readers largely agree here; both see verse 31 as a summary of the Christian life, and both read verse 33 as a call to mission, though they explain the saving of “many” in their own ways.

1 Corinthians 10:23

23“All things are lawful for me,” but not all things are profitable. “All things are lawful for me,” but not all things build up.

Arminian

All things are lawful, but not all are profitable or build up. Arminian readers see Christian liberty governed by love for others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same principle. The Reformed doctrine of Christian liberty, set out in the Westminster Confession chapter 20, includes the duty to use liberty for edification.

1 Corinthians 10:24

24Let no one seek his own, but each one his neighbor’s good.

Arminian

Let no one seek his own good, but each the good of the other. Arminian readers see love as the controlling principle of Christian freedom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-denial as the heart of Christian living, a theme Calvin developed at length in the Institutes. Seeking the good of others is the shape that love takes in the use of liberty.

1 Corinthians 10:25

25Whatever is sold in the butcher shop, eat, asking no question for the sake of conscience,

Arminian

Eat whatever is sold in the meat market, asking no question for conscience’s sake. Arminian readers see freedom from needless scruples for the believer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see liberty in things indifferent. Food has no power to defile, and the believer need not investigate its history.

1 Corinthians 10:26

26for “the earth is the Lord’s, and its fullness.”

Arminian

For the earth is the Lord’s, and its fullness, as Psalm 24:1 says. Arminian readers see God’s ownership of creation as the reason ordinary things can be enjoyed with thanks.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a foundation for a positive view of creation and ordinary life. All things belong to God and may be received with gratitude.

1 Corinthians 10:27

27But if one of those who don’t believe invites you to a meal, and you are inclined to go, eat whatever is set before you, asking no questions for the sake of conscience.

Arminian

If an unbeliever invites them and they want to go, eat whatever is set before them without asking. Arminian readers see freedom to build relationships with unbelievers, which serves the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same freedom. Believers are not to withdraw from ordinary social life with unbelievers, as Paul also said in 5:10.

1 Corinthians 10:28

28But if anyone says to you, “This was offered to idols,” don’t eat it for the sake of the one who told you, and for the sake of conscience. For “the earth is the Lord’s, and all its fullness.”

Arminian

But if someone says it was offered to idols, do not eat, for that person’s sake and for conscience. Arminian readers see love limiting liberty when another person’s faith or understanding is at stake.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same limit. Liberty is never surrendered in principle, but its exercise yields to love when it would be taken as approval of idolatry.

1 Corinthians 10:29

29Conscience, I say, not your own, but the other’s conscience. For why is my liberty judged by another conscience?

Arminian

Paul means the other person’s conscience, not one’s own, and asks why his freedom should be judged by another’s conscience. Arminian readers see Paul protecting both freedom and love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an important balance. The believer’s conscience is bound by God’s word, not by others, yet love may choose not to use a freedom it still has.

1 Corinthians 10:30

30If I partake with thankfulness, why am I denounced for something I give thanks for?

Arminian

If he partakes with thanks, why should he be denounced for that for which he gives thanks? Arminian readers see thanksgiving as what sanctifies ordinary food. Paul defends the freedom of the grateful believer even while limiting its use for love’s sake.

Reformed

Reformed readers see thanksgiving as the mark of lawful use, echoing 1 Timothy 4:4–5. Everything God made is good and is sanctified by the word of God and prayer.

1 Corinthians 10:31

31Whether therefore you eat, or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God.

Arminian

Whether you eat or drink or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God. Arminian readers see a call to direct all of life toward God in loving obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the chief end of human life. The Westminster Shorter Catechism’s first answer, that man’s chief end is to glorify God and enjoy him forever, cites this verse.

1 Corinthians 10:32

32Give no occasion for stumbling, either to Jews, or to Greeks, or to the assembly of God;

Arminian

Give no occasion for stumbling, whether to Jews, Greeks, or the church of God. Arminian readers see concern for every group of people that might be hindered from coming to or growing in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a concern for the honor of the gospel before all people. Conduct either adorns the gospel or obscures it.

1 Corinthians 10:33

33even as I also please all men in all things, not seeking my own profit, but the profit of the many, that they may be saved.

Arminian

Paul seeks to please everyone, not seeking his own profit but that of the many, that they may be saved. Arminian readers see Paul’s whole manner of life as aimed at the salvation of as many as possible, which assumes that God desires all to be saved.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul as God’s instrument for gathering the many whom God will save. Paul’s concern for all is the proper attitude of a minister who does not know who the elect are.

Key contrast Both traditions see Paul seeking the salvation of many. Arminian readers connect this to God’s desire that all be saved and to people’s genuine response. Reformed readers connect it to God’s purpose to save his people through faithful means.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
