Passage

1 Corinthians 10

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Paul turns to Israel in the wilderness as a warning for confident Corinthians. All the Israelites were under the cloud, passed through the sea, ate spiritual food, and drank from the spiritual rock, which was Christ; yet with most of them God was not pleased, and they fell in the desert. Their idolatry, sexual immorality, testing of the Lord, and grumbling were written down as examples for the church. The passage ends with a promise: God is faithful and will not allow believers to be tested beyond what they can bear. Arminian readers often see here a real danger of apostasy among the redeemed, while Reformed readers see warnings that God uses to keep his people and a promise of faithfulness that secures them.

1 Corinthians 10:1

1Now I would not have you ignorant, brothers, that our fathers were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea;

Arminian

Paul reminds them that all the fathers were under the cloud and all passed through the sea. Arminian readers note the repeated “all”: every Israelite shared in the redemption from Egypt, yet not all entered the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same “all” and see a picture of the visible covenant community. Outward participation in the people of God’s privileges does not by itself guarantee inward, saving faith.

1 Corinthians 10:2

2and were all baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea;

Arminian

All were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea. Arminian readers see a parallel to Christian baptism that warns against trusting the sacrament while drifting from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sea crossing as a type of baptism. Calvin used this verse to show that Old Testament believers had real sacraments, yet that the sign without faith did not save.

1 Corinthians 10:3

3and all ate the same spiritual food;

Arminian

All ate the same spiritual food, the manna. Arminian readers see real spiritual blessing given to all Israel, which makes their later fall more sobering.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the manna as a sacramental sign of Christ, given to the whole community. They add that it was received with benefit only by those who believed, since signs do not save apart from faith.

1 Corinthians 10:4

4and all drank the same spiritual drink. For they drank of a spiritual rock that followed them, and the rock was Christ.

Arminian

All drank the same spiritual drink from the spiritual rock that followed them, and the rock was Christ. Arminian readers see Christ present with Israel and offering himself to all of them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as the substance of Old Testament grace. This verse is central to the Reformed view of one covenant of grace administered differently before and after Christ’s coming.

1 Corinthians 10:5

5However with most of them, God was not well pleased, for they were overthrown in the wilderness.

Arminian

Yet with most of them God was not pleased, and they were overthrown in the wilderness. Arminian readers see people who genuinely shared in redemption and yet perished through unbelief, a pattern they believe can be repeated in the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the difference between the visible community and the true people of God. Many who shared the outward privileges did not have saving faith, as Hebrews 3–4 also teaches.

Key contrast Arminian readers see redeemed people who later perished through unbelief, as a warning that believers can fall away. Reformed readers see members of the visible covenant community who never had true, saving faith.

1 Corinthians 10:6

6Now these things were our examples, to the intent we should not lust after evil things, as they also lusted.

Arminian

These things became examples, so that we would not desire evil as they did. Arminian readers see Israel’s history as a direct warning to Christians who might be tempted to follow the same path.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree the warning is real and addressed to the church. God uses such examples as means to keep his people from the very sins that destroyed Israel.

1 Corinthians 10:7

7Don’t be idolaters, as some of them were. As it is written, “The people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play.”

Arminian

Do not be idolaters as some of them were, as Exodus 32 describes the people eating, drinking, and rising up to play. Arminian readers see a pointed warning to Corinthians who were eating in idol temples.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same application. Idolatry is the root sin, and the golden calf shows how quickly a redeemed people can turn to false worship when they think themselves secure.

1 Corinthians 10:8

8Let us not commit sexual immorality, as some of them committed, and in one day twenty-three thousand fell.

Arminian

Do not commit sexual immorality as some did, and twenty-three thousand fell in one day. Arminian readers see God’s judgment on covenant people who persisted in sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the seriousness of sin within the covenant community. Judgment on Israel shows that God’s holiness is not set aside by membership in his people.

1 Corinthians 10:9

9Let us not test Christ, as some of them tested, and perished by the serpents.

Arminian

Do not test the Lord, as some did and were destroyed by serpents. Some manuscripts read “Christ” here. Arminian readers see Christ as the one Israel provoked, which reinforces that Christians can provoke him too.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the high Christology if “Christ” is read, since it places Christ with Israel in the wilderness. Testing him is presuming on his grace.

1 Corinthians 10:10

10Don’t grumble, as some of them also grumbled, and perished by the destroyer.

Arminian

Do not grumble, as some did and were destroyed by the destroyer. Arminian readers see complaining against God as a sign of a heart turning away from faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grumbling as unbelief toward God’s providence. Trust in his care is the opposite of the murmuring that marked the wilderness generation.

1 Corinthians 10:11

11Now all these things happened to them by way of example, and they were written for our admonition, on whom the ends of the ages have come.

Arminian

These things happened as examples and were written for our admonition, on whom the ends of the ages have come. Arminian readers see the warnings as fully applicable to believers, not merely to outsiders.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church living in the last days, the fulfillment toward which Israel’s history pointed. The warnings are addressed to the church as genuine means of grace.

1 Corinthians 10:12

12Therefore let him who thinks he stands be careful that he doesn’t fall.

Arminian

Let him who thinks he stands take heed lest he fall. Arminian readers see this as a clear statement that a believer who now stands can fall, and they take the fall to include final apostasy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a warning against presumption. Those who rely on their own standing are in danger, while true believers heed the warning and are kept by God’s power.

Key contrast Arminian readers see a real possibility that a genuine believer may fall away. Reformed readers see a warning God uses to produce the humble perseverance by which the elect are kept.

1 Corinthians 10:13

13No temptation has taken you except what is common to man. God is faithful, who will not allow you to be tempted above what you are able, but will with the temptation also make the way of escape, that you may be able to endure it.

Arminian

No temptation has overtaken them except what is common, and God is faithful and will provide the way of escape. Arminian readers see God’s faithfulness in giving grace sufficient to resist, while believers remain responsible to take the way out.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness as the ground of perseverance. The God who warns in verse 12 is the same God who keeps his people in verse 13, which is why the warning and the promise belong together.

Key contrast Arminian readers see God’s faithfulness providing a real way of escape that believers must take, so falling remains possible if they refuse it. Reformed readers see the same faithfulness as God’s pledge that his people will be kept and will endure every trial.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
