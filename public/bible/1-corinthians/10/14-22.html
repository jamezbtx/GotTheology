Passage

1 Corinthians 10

Verses 14–22 · WEB

Paul now draws the conclusion: flee from idolatry. He appeals to the Lord’s Supper. The cup of blessing is a participation in Christ’s blood, and the bread is a participation in his body; because there is one bread, the many are one body. In the same way, Israel’s sacrifices meant fellowship with the altar, and pagan sacrifices are offered to demons. Believers cannot share both the Lord’s table and the table of demons. This passage is important for views of the Lord’s Supper. The Reformed tradition, following Calvin, sees a real spiritual communion with Christ, while many Arminian traditions, especially Wesleyan ones, also see the Supper as a means of grace, though some Arminians hold a more memorial view.

1 Corinthians 10:14

14Therefore, my beloved, flee from idolatry.

Arminian

Therefore, beloved, flee from idolatry. Arminian readers see a direct command that requires believers to act, building on the warning of verse 12.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command as the way God’s faithfulness in verse 13 works out. The way of escape often means fleeing.

1 Corinthians 10:15

15I speak as to wise men. Judge what I say.

Arminian

Paul speaks to them as wise people and asks them to judge what he says. Arminian readers see Paul appealing to their reasoned agreement rather than simply commanding.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an appeal to the renewed mind. Believers are expected to understand and weigh the teaching, not merely obey it.

1 Corinthians 10:16

16The cup of blessing which we bless, isn’t it a sharing of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, isn’t it a sharing of the body of Christ?

Arminian

The cup of blessing is a sharing in Christ’s blood, and the bread a sharing in his body. Arminian readers in the Wesleyan tradition see the Supper as a means of grace in which believers truly meet Christ, while others in the broader Arminian family stress remembrance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a real spiritual communion with Christ’s body and blood. Calvin taught that by the Spirit believers are truly nourished by Christ in the Supper, though not by a local presence in the elements.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm that the Supper is more than a bare ritual. Reformed readers emphasize a real spiritual feeding on Christ through the Spirit. Arminian views range from Wesley’s means of grace to a more memorial emphasis.

1 Corinthians 10:17

17Because there is one loaf of bread, we, who are many, are one body; for we all partake of the one loaf of bread.

Arminian

Because there is one bread, the many are one body. Arminian readers see the Supper expressing the unity of all who share in Christ by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Supper as a sign and seal of the church’s union with Christ and with one another. Communion with Christ creates communion among believers.

1 Corinthians 10:18

18Consider Israel according to the flesh. Don’t those who eat the sacrifices participate in the altar?

Arminian

Israel’s priests who ate the sacrifices were participants in the altar. Arminian readers see Paul reinforcing that eating in worship expresses real fellowship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same principle. Sacrificial meals were acts of communion, and the Corinthians cannot treat idol feasts as neutral social events.

1 Corinthians 10:19

19What am I saying then? That a thing sacrificed to idols is anything, or that an idol is anything?

Arminian

Paul asks whether he means that an idol or idol food is anything. Arminian readers see Paul guarding his earlier point from chapter 8 that idols have no real existence as gods.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the balance. Idols are nothing in themselves, yet the worship surrounding them is not harmless, as the next verse shows.

1 Corinthians 10:20

20But I say that the things which the Gentiles sacrifice, they sacrifice to demons, and not to God, and I don’t desire that you would have fellowship with demons.

Arminian

Pagan sacrifices are offered to demons, not to God, and Paul does not want them to have fellowship with demons. Arminian readers see real spiritual danger in idolatry that believers must avoid.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the spiritual reality behind idolatry. False worship is not merely mistaken; it is fellowship with evil powers, echoing Deuteronomy 32:17.

1 Corinthians 10:21

21You can’t both drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons. You can’t both partake of the table of the Lord, and of the table of demons.

Arminian

They cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons, or share both tables. Arminian readers see an exclusive loyalty that believers must choose to keep.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the exclusive claim of Christ on his people. Calvin applied this verse to the purity of worship, insisting that believers must not mix true and false worship.

1 Corinthians 10:22

22Or do we provoke the Lord to jealousy? Are we stronger than he?

Arminian

Do we provoke the Lord to jealousy? Are we stronger than he? Arminian readers see a warning that God’s covenant jealousy is real and that believers can provoke him. The question exposes the folly of thinking knowledge makes idol feasts safe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same warning. God’s jealousy for his people is the jealousy of love, and it should humble any who think their knowledge makes them safe.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
