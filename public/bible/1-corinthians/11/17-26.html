Passage

1 Corinthians 11

Verses 17–26 · WEB

Paul now has no praise for them. Their gatherings for the Lord’s Supper do more harm than good, because divisions along social lines mean that some go hungry while others get drunk. Paul reminds them of what he received from the Lord and delivered to them: on the night he was betrayed, Jesus took bread and the cup, saying that the bread is his body and the cup the new covenant in his blood, to be done in remembrance of him. Whenever they eat and drink, they proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes. Both traditions treasure this passage. Arminian readers, especially Wesleyans, often call the Supper a converting as well as confirming ordinance; Reformed readers stress the Supper as a sign and seal of the covenant for believers.

1 Corinthians 11:17

17But in giving you this command, I don’t praise you, that you come together not for the better but for the worse.

Arminian

Paul gives no praise, because their gatherings do more harm than good. Arminian readers see that the way believers treat one another affects whether worship pleases God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. Outward observance of the sacrament does not please God when love and unity are absent.

1 Corinthians 11:18

18For first of all, when you come together in the assembly, I hear that divisions exist among you, and I partly believe it.

Arminian

When they come together there are divisions, and Paul partly believes it. Arminian readers see divisions as a serious failure of love within the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divisions as contrary to the unity the Supper signifies, a point Paul made in 10:17. A divided table contradicts the one bread.

1 Corinthians 11:19

19For there also must be factions among you, that those who are approved may be revealed among you.

Arminian

There must be factions, so that the approved may be made known. Arminian readers see conflict as a test that reveals who is truly faithful.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence using even divisions to reveal genuine believers. The divisions remain sinful, yet God overrules them for the good of his church.

1 Corinthians 11:20

20When therefore you assemble yourselves together, it is not the Lord’s supper that you eat.

Arminian

When they come together, it is not the Lord’s Supper they eat. Arminian readers see Paul’s strong judgment that their conduct empties the meal of its meaning.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that the Supper requires the right use as well as the right words. Without love it is not truly the Lord’s table.

1 Corinthians 11:21

21For in your eating each one takes his own supper first. One is hungry, and another is drunken.

Arminian

Each goes ahead with his own supper, one hungry and another drunk. Arminian readers see social and economic divisions dishonoring the poor among them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. Calvin noted that the Lord’s Supper should express a shared fellowship, not a private meal.

1 Corinthians 11:22

22What, don’t you have houses to eat and to drink in? Or do you despise God’s assembly, and put them to shame who don’t have enough? What shall I tell you? Shall I praise you? In this I don’t praise you.

Arminian

Do they not have houses to eat in? Do they despise the church and shame those who have nothing? Arminian readers see special care for the poor as essential to the church’s life. The wealthy were treating the Lord’s table as their private banquet.

Reformed

Reformed readers see contempt for the poor as contempt for the church of God. The church belongs to God, and how it is treated reflects on him.

1 Corinthians 11:23

23For I received from the Lord that which also I delivered to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night in which he was betrayed took bread.

Arminian

Paul received from the Lord what he delivered: on the night of his betrayal, Jesus took bread. Arminian readers see the Supper’s authority resting on Christ’s own institution.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the words of institution as the foundation for the sacrament. The Reformed churches insist that the Supper be administered according to Christ’s institution and no additions.

1 Corinthians 11:24

24When he had given thanks, he broke it, and said, “Take, eat. This is my body, which is broken for you. Do this in memory of me.”

Arminian

Jesus gave thanks, broke the bread, and said, This is my body, which is broken for you; do this in remembrance of me. Arminian readers see remembrance as a living, faith-filled recalling of Christ’s death, not mere memory, and Wesley taught that it was a means of grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in “for you” the personal application of Christ’s death to believers. Remembrance is joined to real communion with Christ by the Spirit, as Calvin taught.

Key contrast Both traditions reject a bare memorial reading in their mainstream forms. Wesleyan Arminians see a means of grace that can even bring sinners to faith. Reformed readers see a sign and seal that nourishes those already united to Christ.

1 Corinthians 11:25

25In the same way he also took the cup, after supper, saying, “This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink, in memory of me.”

Arminian

The cup is the new covenant in his blood. Arminian readers see the new covenant offered to all who come in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the new covenant of Jeremiah 31, which God will surely fulfill in his people. Reformed theology often links this to a definite atonement that secures the covenant blessings.

1 Corinthians 11:26

26For as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes.

Arminian

As often as they eat and drink, they proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes. Arminian readers see the Supper as a proclamation of the gospel to all present.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Supper as a visible word that proclaims Christ’s death. It looks back to the cross and forward to his return.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
