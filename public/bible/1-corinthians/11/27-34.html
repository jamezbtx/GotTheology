Passage

1 Corinthians 11

Verses 27–34 · WEB

Paul warns that whoever eats and drinks in an unworthy manner will be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. Each person should examine himself, because eating without discerning the body brings judgment, which in Corinth has already shown itself in sickness and even death. Yet this judgment is discipline so that believers will not be condemned with the world. Paul ends by telling them to wait for one another and to eat at home if hungry. This passage raises questions about assurance and judgment. Arminian readers often see a real danger to believers who persist in sin; Reformed readers see the Lord’s fatherly discipline keeping his people from condemnation.

1 Corinthians 11:27

27Therefore whoever eats this bread or drinks the Lord’s cup in a way unworthy of the Lord will be guilty of the body and the blood of the Lord.

Arminian

Whoever eats or drinks unworthily will be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. Arminian readers see a real danger in treating holy things lightly.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the word describes the manner of eating, not the worthiness of the person. No one is worthy in themselves, but all must come in faith and repentance.

1 Corinthians 11:28

28But let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of the bread, and drink of the cup.

Arminian

Let a person examine himself, and so let him eat. Arminian readers see self-examination as an ongoing responsibility of believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-examination as preparation for the Supper, a practice reflected in the Heidelberg Catechism and Westminster Larger Catechism. The goal is to come, not to stay away.

1 Corinthians 11:29

29For he who eats and drinks in an unworthy way eats and drinks judgment to himself, if he doesn’t discern the Lord’s body.

Arminian

Whoever eats without discerning the body eats and drinks judgment. Arminian readers often take “the body” as the church, whose unity they were despising.

Reformed

Reformed readers see both Christ’s body given for them and the church as his body. Failing to recognize either is a failure to discern what the Supper means.

1 Corinthians 11:30

30For this cause many among you are weak and sickly, and not a few sleep.

Arminian

For this reason many are weak and sick, and some sleep. Arminian readers see God’s real judgment on sin within the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see temporal discipline. “Sleep” is Paul’s word for the death of believers, which suggests that even those disciplined to death were not lost.

1 Corinthians 11:31

31For if we discerned ourselves, we wouldn’t be judged.

Arminian

If we judged ourselves, we would not be judged. Arminian readers see that believers can avoid judgment by honest repentance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-judgment as the fruit of a tender conscience, which God works in his people. Honest self-examination is how believers anticipate and avoid the Lord’s discipline.

1 Corinthians 11:32

32But when we are judged, we are punished by the Lord, that we may not be condemned with the world.

Arminian

When judged, we are disciplined by the Lord, so that we will not be condemned with the world. Arminian readers see discipline as God’s gracious call back from a path that could otherwise end in condemnation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a clear statement of the purpose of discipline. God chastens his children precisely so that they will not be condemned, which supports the perseverance of the saints.

Key contrast Arminian readers see discipline as God’s effort to prevent believers from falling under condemnation, an outcome that is still possible. Reformed readers see discipline as the sure means by which God keeps his children from condemnation.

1 Corinthians 11:33

33Therefore, my brothers, when you come together to eat, wait for one another.

Arminian

When they come together to eat, they should wait for one another. Arminian readers see the practical expression of love in the gathered church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see order and charity as marks of true worship. Waiting for one another is a small act that expresses the unity of the body.

1 Corinthians 11:34

34But if anyone is hungry, let him eat at home, lest your coming together be for judgment. The rest I will set in order whenever I come.

Arminian

If anyone is hungry, let him eat at home, so their gathering will not bring judgment; the rest Paul will set in order when he comes. Arminian readers see Paul’s pastoral concern to keep worship from becoming a cause of judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Supper distinguished from ordinary meals. They also see apostolic authority to set the church’s worship in order, which Paul promises to exercise when he comes.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
