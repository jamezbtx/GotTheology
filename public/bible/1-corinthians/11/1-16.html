Passage

1 Corinthians 11

Verses 1–16 · WEB

Paul begins this section by asking the Corinthians to imitate him as he imitates Christ, and then turns to worship. He addresses how men and women should pray and prophesy, grounding his instruction about head coverings in the order of headship (Christ, man, woman, with God as the head of Christ), in creation, and in nature and church practice. This is one of the most debated passages in Paul, but the debate runs mostly along lines other than Arminian and Reformed. Interpreters in both traditions differ among themselves over headship, coverings, and cultural application. Where the two traditions differ, it is mainly in how they relate the order of creation to God’s providential design.

1 Corinthians 11:1

1Be imitators of me, even as I also am of Christ.

Arminian

Be imitators of me, as I also am of Christ. Arminian readers see this as the conclusion of chapter 10: Paul’s self-giving love is a pattern believers are called to follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same, and note that imitation is always derivative. Paul is a model only insofar as he follows Christ.

1 Corinthians 11:2

2Now I praise you, brothers, that you remember me in all things, and hold firm the traditions, even as I delivered them to you.

Arminian

Paul praises them for remembering him and holding to the traditions he delivered. Arminian readers see faithfulness to apostolic teaching as essential to a healthy church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the traditions as apostolic teaching now preserved in Scripture. This verse does not support an unwritten tradition alongside Scripture but points to what the apostles delivered.

1 Corinthians 11:3

3But I would have you know that the head of every man is Christ, and the head of the woman is man, and the head of Christ is God.

Arminian

The head of every man is Christ, the head of the woman is man, and the head of Christ is God. Arminian interpreters are divided: some read “head” as authority, others as source, and many Wesleyan and Pentecostal traditions emphasize equality in ministry.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters are also divided, but many read “head” as authority within an order of loving relationship. The headship of God over Christ shows that order does not imply inferiority.

1 Corinthians 11:4

4Every man praying or prophesying, having his head covered, dishonors his head.

Arminian

A man who prays or prophesies with his head covered dishonors his head. Arminian readers note that Paul assumes public prayer and prophecy as normal in the gathered church.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the man’s “head” may mean both his own head and Christ. Worship is to reflect the order God has established, and dishonoring that order dishonors Christ.

1 Corinthians 11:5

5But every woman praying or prophesying with her head uncovered dishonors her head. For it is one and the same thing as if she were shaved.

Arminian

A woman who prays or prophesies with her head uncovered dishonors her head. Arminian readers, especially in Wesleyan and Holiness traditions, stress that women did pray and prophesy publicly, which they see as support for women in ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers acknowledge that women prayed and prophesied. Many relate this to 14:34–35 by distinguishing prophecy from authoritative teaching or the judging of prophecy.

1 Corinthians 11:6

6For if a woman is not covered, let her hair also be cut off. But if it is shameful for a woman to have her hair cut off or be shaved, let her be covered.

Arminian

If a woman will not be covered, let her hair be cut off; but if it is shameful, let her be covered. Arminian readers see Paul appealing to the cultural sense of honor and shame in Corinth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same cultural appeal. Many hold that the principle of honoring God’s order endures even if the specific sign may differ by culture.

1 Corinthians 11:7

7For a man indeed ought not to have his head covered, because he is the image and glory of God, but the woman is the glory of the man.

Arminian

A man ought not cover his head, being the image and glory of God, while woman is the glory of man. Arminian readers note that Genesis 1:27 says both male and female bear God’s image, so Paul’s point concerns glory and honor in worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers also affirm that both bear the image. They see Paul speaking of the order of creation in Genesis 2, in which the man was formed first.

1 Corinthians 11:8

8For man is not from woman, but woman from man;

Arminian

Man is not from woman, but woman from man. Arminian readers see a reference to Genesis 2 that Paul will balance in verses 11–12.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an appeal to creation order in Genesis 2. They take this as evidence that Paul’s instruction rests on more than local custom, even if its outward expression may vary.

1 Corinthians 11:9

9for man wasn’t created for the woman, but woman for the man.

Arminian

Man was not created for the woman, but woman for the man. Arminian readers see Genesis 2:18 as describing a partner and helper suited to him, not a lesser being.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same Genesis text. The woman’s role as helper is honorable, the same word used of God as Israel’s helper.

1 Corinthians 11:10

10For this cause the woman ought to have authority over her own head, because of the angels.

Arminian

For this reason the woman ought to have authority on her head, because of the angels. Arminian readers note that the Greek says “authority,” which some take as her own authority to pray and prophesy.

Reformed

Reformed readers often take it as a sign of authority, that is, of the order under which she ministers. Both traditions admit “because of the angels” is difficult, often reading it as angels observing the church’s worship.

1 Corinthians 11:11

11Nevertheless, neither is the woman independent of the man, nor the man independent of the woman, in the Lord.

Arminian

Nevertheless, in the Lord neither is woman independent of man nor man of woman. Arminian readers see Paul stressing mutual dependence and equality in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see mutuality. Order and equality are held together, and neither may be used to cancel the other.

1 Corinthians 11:12

12For as woman came from man, so a man also comes through a woman; but all things are from God.

Arminian

As woman came from man, so man comes through woman, and all things are from God. Arminian readers see the balancing truth that each sex depends on the other.

Reformed

Reformed readers see all things grounded in God’s creative purpose. The final phrase places the whole discussion under God’s sovereign design.

1 Corinthians 11:13

13Judge for yourselves. Is it appropriate that a woman pray to God unveiled?

Arminian

Judge for yourselves whether it is fitting for a woman to pray to God uncovered. Arminian readers see Paul appealing to their own judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an appeal to propriety and good order in worship, a theme Paul repeats in 14:40. Paul expects believers to recognize what is fitting by sanctified judgment.

1 Corinthians 11:14

14Doesn’t even nature itself teach you that if a man has long hair, it is a dishonor to him?

Arminian

Does not nature itself teach that long hair is a dishonor to a man? Arminian readers often see “nature” as the settled sense of what is natural in that culture. Paul appeals to what his readers would already feel to be fitting.

Reformed

Reformed readers vary, some reading “nature” as created order and others as settled custom. Calvin took it largely as what was accepted by common custom.

1 Corinthians 11:15

15But if a woman has long hair, it is a glory to her, for her hair is given to her for a covering.

Arminian

Long hair is a woman’s glory, given to her for a covering. Arminian readers see Paul’s appeal to ordinary observations about honor and beauty.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this verse suggests the covering may be related to hair itself. Some Reformed interpreters take this as the main point, while others see hair as a natural pointer to the covering Paul commends.

1 Corinthians 11:16

16But if any man seems to be contentious, we have no such custom, neither do God’s assemblies.

Arminian

If anyone seems contentious, we have no such custom, nor do the churches of God. Arminian readers see Paul appealing to the common practice of the churches to settle disputes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an appeal to the shared practice of the churches. Some take this as a reason to see the specific practice as a matter of custom rather than of binding principle.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
