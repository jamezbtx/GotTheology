Passage

1 Corinthians 16

Verses 13–24 · WEB

Paul’s final exhortations are brief and pointed: watch, stand firm in the faith, be courageous, be strong, and let all be done in love. He commends the household of Stephanas, the first converts in Achaia, who devoted themselves to serving the saints, and asks the church to submit to such people. He sends greetings from the churches of Asia, from Aquila and Prisca, and from all the brothers, and adds a greeting in his own hand. He closes with a solemn warning, Maranatha, a prayer for grace, and a word of love for them all in Christ Jesus. Arminian and Reformed readers both read these lines as a summary of the letter. They differ mainly on verse 22, which speaks of those who do not love the Lord.

1 Corinthians 16:13

13Watch! Stand firm in the faith! Be courageous! Be strong!

Arminian

Watch, stand firm in the faith, be courageous, be strong. Arminian readers see continued vigilance as necessary, since believers must keep standing in faith. The call echoes 10:12.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same call. God keeps his people through their watchfulness and steadfastness, not apart from it.

1 Corinthians 16:14

14Let all that you do be done in love.

Arminian

Let all you do be done in love. Arminian readers see love as the summary of the whole letter, echoing chapter 13.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same summary of the letter. Knowledge, gifts, and liberty all must be governed by love.

1 Corinthians 16:15

15Now I beg you, brothers (you know the house of Stephanas, that it is the first fruits of Achaia, and that they have set themselves to serve the saints),

Arminian

The household of Stephanas was the first fruits of Achaia and devoted themselves to serving the saints. Arminian readers see a family that responded early to the gospel and then gave themselves to service.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s grace in the first converts and the fruit of that grace in service. Some Reformed interpreters also connect household baptism in 1:16 to infant baptism.

1 Corinthians 16:16

16that you also be in subjection to such, and to everyone who helps in the work and labors.

Arminian

Be subject to such people and to everyone who works and labors with them. Arminian readers see respect for proven servants of the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see support for honoring recognized leaders. Their authority is tied to faithful service rather than to status.

1 Corinthians 16:17

17I rejoice at the coming of Stephanas, Fortunatus, and Achaicus; for that which was lacking on your part, they supplied.

Arminian

Paul rejoices at the coming of Stephanas, Fortunatus, and Achaicus, who supplied what was lacking. Arminian readers see the encouragement of fellowship and partnership.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the communion of saints expressed through personal presence and mutual help. These men brought the church’s love to Paul.

1 Corinthians 16:18

18For they refreshed my spirit and yours. Therefore acknowledge those who are like that.

Arminian

They refreshed Paul’s spirit and theirs; acknowledge such people. Arminian readers see the ministry of encouragement honored.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the value of ordinary members who strengthen the church. Refreshing others’ spirits is a real ministry worthy of recognition.

1 Corinthians 16:19

19The assemblies of Asia greet you. Aquila and Priscilla greet you much in the Lord, together with the assembly that is in their house.

Arminian

The churches of Asia greet them, and Aquila and Prisca with the church in their house. Arminian readers see the wide network of early churches and the leadership of a married couple in ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the house church as the setting of early worship. Aquila and Priscilla appear as faithful co-workers, as in Acts 18:26.

1 Corinthians 16:20

20All the brothers greet you. Greet one another with a holy kiss.

Arminian

All the brothers greet them; greet one another with a holy kiss. Arminian readers see warm affection among believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the holy kiss expressed reconciliation in a divided church. Greetings carry the unity Paul has urged throughout.

1 Corinthians 16:21

21This greeting is by me, Paul, with my own hand.

Arminian

Paul writes this greeting with his own hand. Arminian readers see a personal touch that also authenticates the letter.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the authentication of the letter. This bears on the apostolic authority that Paul claimed in 14:37.

1 Corinthians 16:22

22If any man doesn’t love the Lord Jesus Christ, let him be accursed. Come, Lord!

Arminian

If anyone does not love the Lord, let him be accursed; Maranatha, Come, Lord. Arminian readers see a solemn warning that love for Christ is essential and can be absent even among those in the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same warning. Love for Christ is the fruit of regeneration, and its absence reveals an unconverted heart.

Key contrast Both traditions see love for Christ as essential. Arminian readers see a warning that love can grow cold and be lost. Reformed readers see absence of love as evidence that a person was never truly regenerated.

1 Corinthians 16:23

23The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with you.

Arminian

The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with you. Arminian readers see grace as the beginning and end of the letter.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the letter that began with grace in 1:3 ends with grace. Everything between rests on God’s unmerited favor.

1 Corinthians 16:24

24My love to all of you in Christ Jesus. Amen.

Arminian

Paul’s love is with them all in Christ Jesus. Amen. Arminian readers see Paul’s affection for even a troubled church.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul’s love is rooted in their common union with Christ. Even after hard words, he ends with affection.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
