Passage

1 Corinthians 16

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Paul closes the letter with practical matters. He gives instructions for the collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem: on the first day of every week each should set something aside as he has prospered, so that no collections need be made when Paul comes. He shares his travel plans, to stay in Ephesus until Pentecost because a wide door for effective work has opened, though there are many adversaries. He commends Timothy and explains that Apollos is not willing to come now. Arminian and Reformed readers read this section in much the same way. Both see in the open door and the travel plans a picture of how God’s providence and human planning work together, though they describe that relationship differently.

1 Corinthians 16:1

1Now concerning the collection for the saints, as I commanded the assemblies of Galatia, you do likewise.

Arminian

Concerning the collection for the saints, Paul gives the same direction he gave the churches of Galatia. Arminian readers see generosity toward fellow believers as a natural fruit of faith. They also note that the collection expressed the unity of Jewish and Gentile churches.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same unity, and they see in the collection the communion of saints made practical. Reformed churches have often cited this passage in the ordering of the diaconate and care for the poor.

1 Corinthians 16:2

2On the first day of the week, let each one of you save, as he may prosper, that no collections be made when I come.

Arminian

On the first day of the week each should set aside something as he may prosper. Arminian readers see regular, proportionate, and willing giving. They also see early evidence that believers gathered on the first day, the day of the resurrection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same evidence for Lord’s Day worship, which the Westminster Confession calls the Christian Sabbath. Giving in proportion to prosperity reflects stewardship of what God has provided.

1 Corinthians 16:3

3When I arrive, I will send whoever you approve with letters to carry your gracious gift to Jerusalem.

Arminian

When Paul arrives, he will send those they approve with letters to carry their gift to Jerusalem. Arminian readers see accountability and transparency in handling the church’s money.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same care. Calvin noted Paul’s concern to avoid any suspicion in money matters, which fits his refusal of support in chapter 9.

1 Corinthians 16:4

4If it is appropriate for me to go also, they will go with me.

Arminian

If it is fitting for Paul to go also, they will go with him. Arminian readers see Paul’s flexibility and willingness to serve.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wise and humble leadership. Paul plans, but he holds his plans with an open hand.

1 Corinthians 16:5

5But I will come to you when I have passed through Macedonia, for I am passing through Macedonia.

Arminian

Paul will come after passing through Macedonia. Arminian readers see ordinary missionary planning. Paul makes real decisions about where to go and when.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ordinary planning that is fully consistent with trust in God’s providence. God’s sovereignty does not make careful plans unnecessary.

1 Corinthians 16:6

6But with you it may be that I will stay, or even winter, that you may send me on my journey wherever I go.

Arminian

He may stay or even winter with them, so they can send him on his way. Arminian readers see Paul’s desire for real fellowship with the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s desire for fellowship with the church. They also note the church’s role in sending and supporting the mission of its ministers.

1 Corinthians 16:7

7For I do not wish to see you now in passing, but I hope to stay a while with you, if the Lord permits.

Arminian

Paul does not want to see them only in passing, but hopes to stay a while, if the Lord permits. Arminian readers see Paul’s plans held under God’s will, in line with James 4:15.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the phrase “if the Lord permits” as an expression of God’s sovereign providence over every plan. Paul’s plans are made under God’s will.

Key contrast Both traditions take “if the Lord permits” as humble dependence on God. Arminian readers see God’s will responding to and guiding real human choices. Reformed readers see God’s decree ordering every detail of Paul’s journeys.

1 Corinthians 16:8

8But I will stay at Ephesus until Pentecost,

Arminian

Paul will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost. Arminian readers see Paul responding to opportunities as they arise.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s wise use of the time God gives. He discerns where the Lord is at work and stays there.

1 Corinthians 16:9

9for a great and effective door has opened to me, and there are many adversaries.

Arminian

A great and effective door has opened to him, and there are many adversaries. Arminian readers see opportunity and opposition together, with people free to respond to the gospel or resist it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God opening the door, as in Acts 16:14, where the Lord opened Lydia’s heart. Opposition does not close a door God has opened.

1 Corinthians 16:10

10Now if Timothy comes, see that he is with you without fear, for he does the work of the Lord, as I also do.

Arminian

If Timothy comes, they should see that he is with them without fear, for he does the Lord’s work as Paul does. Arminian readers see care for a young minister who may be intimidated.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the respect owed to faithful ministers. Their authority rests on doing the Lord’s work rather than on age or personality.

1 Corinthians 16:11

11Therefore let no one despise him. But set him forward on his journey in peace, that he may come to me; for I expect him with the brothers.

Arminian

Let no one despise him, but send him on in peace. Arminian readers see Paul protecting Timothy from the Corinthians’ tendency to rank leaders.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. The church must honor those whom God sends, as Paul also urges in 1 Timothy 4:12.

1 Corinthians 16:12

12Now concerning Apollos, the brother, I strongly urged him to come to you with the brothers; and it was not at all his desire to come now; but he will come when he has an opportunity.

Arminian

Paul strongly urged Apollos to visit, but he was not willing at present; he will come when he has opportunity. Arminian readers see Apollos as free to make his own decision, even contrary to Paul’s urging.

Reformed

Reformed readers see freedom among ministers and note the absence of rivalry between Paul and Apollos. The Corinthian factions of chapter 1 had no basis in the leaders themselves.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
