Passage

1 Corinthians 3

Verses 18–23 · WEB

Paul closes his argument against boasting in human leaders. Anyone who thinks himself wise should become a fool to become truly wise, because God catches the wise in their craftiness. Rather than boasting in men, the Corinthians should realize that all things are theirs, Paul, Apollos, Cephas, the world, life, death, present and future, because they belong to Christ and Christ belongs to God. Both traditions find great comfort here. Their differences are mostly in emphasis.

1 Corinthians 3:18

18Let no one deceive himself. If anyone thinks that he is wise among you in this world, let him become a fool, that he may become wise.

Arminian

Let no one deceive himself; whoever thinks himself wise should become a fool to become wise. Arminian readers see a call to humble repentance from self-reliance. The Corinthians must choose to abandon worldly wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same call, recognizing that true wisdom begins with acknowledging dependence on God. Self-deception is a constant danger for gifted believers.

1 Corinthians 3:19

19For the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God. For it is written, “He has taken the wise in their craftiness.”

Arminian

The wisdom of this world is folly with God; he catches the wise in their craftiness. Arminian readers see God exposing the futility of proud human schemes, citing Job. Wisdom that excludes God undermines itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereignty over even the schemes of the clever. He uses their own craftiness to trap them.

1 Corinthians 3:20

20And again, “The Lord knows the reasoning of the wise, that it is worthless.”

Arminian

The Lord knows the thoughts of the wise are futile. Arminian readers see the Psalm reminding readers that God sees through all human pretension. Pride in wisdom is empty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s complete knowledge of human thought. Nothing in human reasoning is hidden from him or able to stand apart from him.

1 Corinthians 3:21

21Therefore let no one boast in men. For all things are yours,

Arminian

Let no one boast in men, for all things are yours. Arminian readers see a liberating perspective: believers do not belong to any leader but share in all God’s gifts. Party spirit makes them poorer than they really are.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer’s inheritance in Christ, which makes boasting in ministers absurd. Every servant exists for the church’s good.

1 Corinthians 3:22

22whether Paul, or Apollos, or Cephas, or the world, or life, or death, or things present, or things to come. All are yours,

Arminian

Paul, Apollos, Cephas, the world, life, death, present, future, all are yours. Arminian readers see the sweeping scope of the believer’s riches in Christ, received through faith. Even death serves those who belong to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence ordering all things for the good of his people, in harmony with Romans 8:28. Nothing in life or death lies outside his purpose for them.

Key contrast Both rejoice that all things serve the believer in Christ. Arminian readers emphasize the riches enjoyed by all who are in Christ by faith. Reformed readers emphasize God’s sovereign ordering of all things for his elect.

1 Corinthians 3:23

23and you are Christ’s, and Christ is God’s.

Arminian

You are Christ’s and Christ is God’s. Arminian readers see belonging to Christ as the believer’s true identity, which ends rival loyalties. Christ’s relation to the Father frames the whole order of salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer’s secure belonging to Christ, and Christ’s mediatorial relation to the Father. The church is owned by Christ, who is himself sent by and devoted to God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
