Passage

1 Corinthians 3

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Paul explains why he could not give the Corinthians solid food: their jealousy and strife show they are still fleshly, infants in Christ. Their slogans about Paul and Apollos prove the point. Ministers are only servants through whom they believed; Paul planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth. Arminian and Reformed readers agree on the dignity and limits of ministry. They differ in how they describe the relation between God’s growth and human cooperation.

1 Corinthians 3:1

1Brothers, I couldn’t speak to you as to spiritual, but as to fleshly, as to babies in Christ.

Arminian

Paul could not address them as spiritual but as fleshly, as infants in Christ. Arminian readers see genuine believers who have failed to grow, showing that spiritual maturity requires responsive cooperation with grace. Being in Christ is no excuse for staying immature.

Reformed

Reformed readers see true believers, in Christ, who still behave in fleshly ways. Sanctification is progressive and uneven, and remaining sin can be very visible even in the regenerate.

1 Corinthians 3:2

2I fed you with milk, not with meat; for you weren’t yet ready. Indeed, not even now are you ready,

Arminian

He fed them milk, not solid food, because they were not ready and still are not. Arminian readers see a pastoral rebuke aimed at stirring the Corinthians to growth they are capable of. Stagnation is a choice with consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul adapting teaching to the hearers’ condition, a model for pastoral wisdom. The rebuke is itself a means God uses to bring growth.

1 Corinthians 3:3

3for you are still fleshly. For insofar as there is jealousy, strife, and factions among you, aren’t you fleshly, and don’t you walk in the ways of men?

Arminian

Jealousy and strife show they are fleshly and walking in merely human ways. Arminian readers stress that behaviour reveals spiritual condition and that believers can drift into living like unbelievers. Paul’s diagnosis is a warning to be heeded.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the conflict between flesh and Spirit in the believer. Factionalism is evidence of remaining sin, which must be mortified.

1 Corinthians 3:4

4For when one says, “I follow Paul,” and another, “I follow Apollos,” aren’t you fleshly?

Arminian

Saying I follow Paul or I follow Apollos shows they are merely human. Arminian readers see party loyalty as a failure to grasp the gospel’s focus on Christ. Paul uses himself as the example to avoid offending others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same point: exalting ministers misunderstands the source of grace. Paul’s naming of himself shows he will not tolerate a party in his own name.

1 Corinthians 3:5

5Who then is Apollos, and who is Paul, but servants through whom you believed; and each as the Lord gave to him?

Arminian

Paul and Apollos are servants through whom they believed, as the Lord assigned to each. Arminian readers note that the Corinthians believed through ministers, so preaching is a real means calling for a real response. Each servant has an assigned task.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God assigning to each minister his role and giving the results. Servants are instruments through whom faith came, and God is the one who gives faith.

1 Corinthians 3:6

6I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the increase.

Arminian

I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth. Arminian readers see a partnership in which God works through human labour, with growth depending on God’s blessing. Ministry matters, but it never replaces God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the sole giver of spiritual life and growth. Planting and watering are necessary means, yet only God makes the seed live.

Key contrast Both agree that ministers are instruments and God gives the growth. Arminian readers stress God’s cooperation with human ministry and human response. Reformed readers stress God’s sovereign giving of life through the means he appoints.

1 Corinthians 3:7

7So then neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase.

Arminian

Neither planter nor waterer is anything, but only God who gives growth. Arminian readers see a strong rebuke to celebrity culture in the church. Ministers are valued only as God uses them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sovereignty of God over spiritual fruit, which humbles ministers and congregations alike. Credit for conversion belongs to God.

1 Corinthians 3:8

8Now he who plants and he who waters are the same, but each will receive his own reward according to his own labor.

Arminian

Planter and waterer are one, and each will receive wages according to his labour. Arminian readers see real accountability and reward tied to faithful work. God honours human effort that he enables.

Reformed

Reformed readers see rewards as gracious, God crowning his own gifts in his servants. Ministers are united in purpose, so rivalry between them is absurd.

1 Corinthians 3:9

9For we are God’s fellow workers. You are God’s farming, God’s building.

Arminian

We are God’s fellow workers; you are God’s field, God’s building. Arminian readers often read fellow workers as working together with God, a dignified cooperation in his mission. The church belongs to God, not to its ministers.

Reformed

Reformed readers often read it as fellow workers belonging to God, working alongside each other under him. Either way, the field and building are God’s, and he directs the work.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
