Passage

1 Corinthians 12

Verses 12–26 · WEB

Paul now uses the image of the body. The body is one but has many members, and so is Christ. By one Spirit all were baptized into one body, Jews or Greeks, slaves or free, and all were given one Spirit to drink. A foot cannot leave the body because it is not a hand, and the eye cannot tell the hand it has no need of it. God has arranged the members as he chose, and he has given greater honor to the parts that lack it, so that the members care for one another. Both traditions read this passage in largely the same way, as a call to unity and mutual care. Where they differ, it is in their accounts of Spirit baptism in verse 13 and how God arranges the members in verse 18.

1 Corinthians 12:12

12For as the body is one, and has many members, and all the members of the body, being many, are one body; so also is Christ.

Arminian

As the body is one with many members, so also is Christ. Arminian readers see the church united by its shared faith in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church’s union with Christ as so close that Paul can call it “Christ.” This union, worked by the Spirit, is the basis of every saving benefit. Many members form one body, and Christ is its head. Unity and diversity both come from the same Lord.

1 Corinthians 12:13

13For in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body, whether Jews or Greeks, whether bond or free; and were all given to drink into one Spirit.

Arminian

By one Spirit all were baptized into one body and all were given one Spirit to drink. Arminian readers see every believer receiving the Spirit at conversion, though Pentecostal and Holiness Arminians may also speak of a later filling or baptism.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Spirit baptism as the work that unites every believer to Christ at conversion. It is not a second blessing for some but the common possession of all believers.

Key contrast Reformed readers and many Arminians read verse 13 as conversion-initiation shared by all believers. Some Pentecostal and Holiness Arminians distinguish this from a later Spirit baptism or entire sanctification.

1 Corinthians 12:14

14For the body is not one member, but many.

Arminian

The body is not one member but many. Arminian readers see diversity as God’s good design. Each believer’s distinct gift is needed for the church to function.

Reformed

Reformed readers see diversity in the church as a gift to be honored rather than a problem to be solved. God made the body many-membered on purpose.

1 Corinthians 12:15

15If the foot would say, “Because I’m not the hand, I’m not part of the body,” it is not therefore not part of the body.

Arminian

If the foot says it is not part of the body because it is not a hand, it is still part of the body. Arminian readers see encouragement for those who feel their gifts are less important.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same encouragement. A believer’s place in the body is given by God, not by comparison with others.

1 Corinthians 12:16

16If the ear would say, “Because I’m not the eye, I’m not part of the body,” it’s not therefore not part of the body.

Arminian

The same is true if the ear says it is not part of the body because it is not an eye. Arminian readers see Paul pressing the point against discouragement.

Reformed

Reformed readers see envy and self-pity both answered by God’s appointment. The ear belongs to the body because God placed it there, not because it compares well with the eye.

1 Corinthians 12:17

17If the whole body were an eye, where would the hearing be? If the whole were hearing, where would the smelling be?

Arminian

If the whole body were an eye, where would hearing be? Arminian readers see the need for every kind of gift. A church of identical members could not do the work God gives it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom in God’s design, which makes uniformity both impossible and undesirable. The variety of gifts serves the fullness of the body.

1 Corinthians 12:18

18But now God has set the members, each one of them, in the body, just as he desired.

Arminian

God has set the members, each one, in the body as he desired. Arminian readers see God’s wise arrangement of gifts, received by believers who respond and serve.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign appointment of each member’s place. This is an example of the providence that orders the details of the church’s life.

1 Corinthians 12:19

19If they were all one member, where would the body be?

Arminian

If all were one member, where would the body be? Arminian readers see diversity as essential to the church. Paul presses the absurdity to break the Corinthians’ fixation on one gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the necessity of every gift that God gives. A body made of one member would not be a body at all.

1 Corinthians 12:20

20But now they are many members, but one body.

Arminian

There are many members but one body. Arminian readers see unity and diversity held together. Neither may be sacrificed to the other.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same balance and connect it to the unity of the church confessed in the Nicene Creed. The church is one because Christ is one.

1 Corinthians 12:21

21The eye can’t tell the hand, “I have no need for you,” or again the head to the feet, “I have no need for you.”

Arminian

The eye cannot tell the hand, I have no need of you. Arminian readers see a warning against pride among the gifted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a warning against spiritual pride. No member is self-sufficient, and no one may despise another’s gift.

1 Corinthians 12:22

22No, much rather, those members of the body which seem to be weaker are necessary.

Arminian

The members that seem weaker are necessary. Arminian readers see God valuing those whom others overlook. The church’s estimate of importance is not God’s.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s pattern of choosing the weak, as in 1:27–28. The same grace that chose the lowly in salvation honors them in the body.

1 Corinthians 12:23

23Those parts of the body which we think to be less honorable, on those we bestow more abundant honor; and our unpresentable parts have more abundant propriety;

Arminian

We give greater honor to the parts we think less honorable. Arminian readers see love giving special care to those who seem less important.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the principle of honoring the humble members of the body. Love gives special care to what seems least presentable.

1 Corinthians 12:24

24whereas our presentable parts have no such need. But God composed the body together, giving more abundant honor to the inferior part,

Arminian

God has composed the body, giving more honor to the part that lacked. Arminian readers see God’s care for the lowly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign wisdom in arranging the body so that no member is neglected. God himself has composed it and assigned honor.

1 Corinthians 12:25

25that there should be no division in the body, but that the members should have the same care for one another.

Arminian

So that there may be no division, but the members care for one another. Arminian readers see mutual love as the goal of God’s design.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s arrangement of the body directly opposes the divisions Paul addressed in chapter 1. Mutual care is the purpose built into the design.

1 Corinthians 12:26

26When one member suffers, all the members suffer with it. Or when one member is honored, all the members rejoice with it.

Arminian

If one member suffers, all suffer; if one is honored, all rejoice. Arminian readers see genuine fellowship in which each shares the life of the others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the communion of saints confessed in the Apostles’ Creed. Believers share in one another’s sorrows and joys because they share in Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
