Passage

1 Corinthians 12

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Paul turns to spiritual gifts, a subject the Corinthians had asked about. He first sets the test: no one speaking by the Spirit says “Jesus is accursed,” and no one can say “Jesus is Lord” except by the Holy Spirit. Then he stresses that there are varieties of gifts, service, and workings, but the same Spirit, Lord, and God. Each is given a manifestation of the Spirit for the common good, and the one Spirit distributes to each as he wills. Both traditions see a strong Trinitarian pattern here. Their differences arise mainly over verse 3 and the Spirit’s role in bringing people to confess Christ, and in how far the Spirit’s distribution of gifts reflects sovereign choice.

1 Corinthians 12:1

1Now concerning spiritual things, brothers, I don’t want you to be ignorant.

Arminian

Paul does not want them ignorant about spiritual gifts. Arminian readers see a concern that believers understand and use the Spirit’s gifts rightly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same concern. Understanding the Spirit’s work guards the church from both neglect and abuse of his gifts.

1 Corinthians 12:2

2You know that when you were heathen, you were led away to those mute idols, however you might be led.

Arminian

When they were Gentiles, they were led astray to mute idols. Arminian readers see Paul contrasting their former bondage with the freedom of life in the Spirit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a reminder of their former helplessness. They were carried away, which underlines that their turning to Christ was God’s work.

1 Corinthians 12:3

3Therefore I make known to you that no man speaking by God’s Spirit says, “Jesus is accursed.” No one can say, “Jesus is Lord,” but by the Holy Spirit.

Arminian

No one speaking by the Spirit says Jesus is accursed, and no one can say Jesus is Lord except by the Holy Spirit. Arminian readers see the necessity of the Spirit’s grace for true confession, which they understand as prevenient grace enabling a free response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a clear statement that genuine faith is the Spirit’s work. Sinners cannot confess Christ from their own resources, which supports the doctrine of effectual calling.

Key contrast Both traditions agree that no one confesses Christ as Lord without the Spirit’s work. Arminian readers see grace that enables and may be resisted. Reformed readers see grace that effectually produces faith in the elect.

1 Corinthians 12:4

4Now there are various kinds of gifts, but the same Spirit.

Arminian

There are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit. Arminian readers see unity in diversity within the church. No single gift defines spirituality, because one Spirit gives them all.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same, noting the Trinitarian structure of verses 4–6: Spirit, Lord, and God. The unity of the church reflects the unity of the triune God.

1 Corinthians 12:5

5There are various kinds of service, and the same Lord.

Arminian

There are varieties of service, but the same Lord. Arminian readers see every ministry as service rendered to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as the head who orders the various ministries of his church. Every service is rendered to him and under his authority.

1 Corinthians 12:6

6There are various kinds of workings, but the same God, who works all things in all.

Arminian

There are varieties of workings, but the same God who works all things in all. Arminian readers see God’s energy at work in every believer’s service.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign activity behind every gift and act of service. The phrase “works all things in all” emphasizes divine initiative.

1 Corinthians 12:7

7But to each one is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the profit of all.

Arminian

To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the common good. Arminian readers see every believer gifted and called to serve.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. Gifts are given, not earned, and they are for the body, not for personal standing.

1 Corinthians 12:8

8For to one is given through the Spirit the word of wisdom, and to another the word of knowledge, according to the same Spirit;

Arminian

To one is given a word of wisdom, to another a word of knowledge, by the same Spirit. Arminian readers see gifts of insight that serve the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see gifts of understanding and teaching that build up the church through the word. Calvin connected wisdom and knowledge here closely to grasping and applying divine truth.

1 Corinthians 12:9

9to another faith, by the same Spirit; and to another gifts of healings, by the same Spirit;

Arminian

To another faith, to another gifts of healing, by the same Spirit. Arminian readers note that “faith” here is a special gift for particular tasks, not saving faith, which all believers share.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that this is wonder-working faith, not saving faith. Many Reformed interpreters hold that the miraculous gifts ceased with the apostolic age, though some do not.

1 Corinthians 12:10

10and to another workings of miracles; and to another prophecy; and to another discerning of spirits; to another different kinds of languages; and to another the interpretation of languages.

Arminian

To another miracles, prophecy, discerning of spirits, tongues, and interpretation. Arminian readers, especially in Pentecostal and Holiness traditions, often see these gifts as continuing today.

Reformed

Reformed readers have historically been cessationist about some of these gifts, though there are continuationist Reformed voices. Both sides agree the gifts serve the building of the church.

Key contrast Many in the Arminian, Pentecostal, and charismatic traditions see all these gifts as continuing. Many Reformed readers hold that revelatory and sign gifts ceased with the apostles, though Reformed continuationists also exist.

1 Corinthians 12:11

11But the one and the same Spirit produces all of these, distributing to each one separately as he desires.

Arminian

The one Spirit works all these, distributing to each as he wills. Arminian readers see the Spirit’s free giving, while believers remain responsible to seek and use the gifts.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit’s sovereign will in distributing gifts. Gifts are not merited or self-chosen but given by the Spirit as he determines.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
