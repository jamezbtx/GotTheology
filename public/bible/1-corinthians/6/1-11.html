Passage

1 Corinthians 6

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Paul is appalled that believers are taking one another to pagan courts. Since the saints will judge the world and even angels, surely they can settle everyday disputes. It would be better to suffer wrong than to wrong and defraud fellow believers. He then warns that the unrighteous will not inherit God’s kingdom, listing sexual sins, idolatry, theft, greed, drunkenness, slander, and swindling, before reminding them that some of them were once like this, but they were washed, sanctified, and justified in the name of the Lord Jesus and by the Spirit. Verses 9 to 11 are important for both traditions’ teaching on assurance and holiness.

1 Corinthians 6:1

1Dare any of you, having a matter against his neighbor, go to law before the unrighteous, and not before the saints?

Arminian

Does anyone dare take a grievance before the unrighteous instead of the saints? Arminian readers see a failure to live as the distinct community God has made. Believers are responsible to resolve conflicts within the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church as a community under Christ’s rule, capable of wise judgment. Taking disputes to pagan courts dishonours that identity.

1 Corinthians 6:2

2Don’t you know that the saints will judge the world? And if the world is judged by you, are you unworthy to judge the smallest matters?

Arminian

Do you not know the saints will judge the world? Arminian readers see the future role of believers in Christ’s reign as a reason to act wisely now. Dignity brings responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the saints sharing in Christ’s judgment at the last day. This future glory makes petty disputes absurd.

1 Corinthians 6:3

3Don’t you know that we will judge angels? How much more, things that pertain to this life?

Arminian

Do you not know we will judge angels? Arminian readers see the astonishing destiny of redeemed humanity. If so, everyday matters are well within their capacity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see believers united with Christ in his exaltation over all creation. Judging angels likely refers to fallen angels at the final judgment.

1 Corinthians 6:4

4If then, you have to judge things pertaining to this life, do you set them to judge who are of no account in the assembly?

Arminian

If you have such cases, why appoint those with no standing in the church? Arminian readers see a rebuke for seeking verdicts from outsiders. The church should value its own wise members.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an implied call for the church to provide wise arbitration. Some translations read this as an ironic command, but the point is the same.

1 Corinthians 6:5

5I say this to move you to shame. Isn’t there even one wise man among you who would be able to decide between his brothers?

Arminian

Paul says this to their shame: is there no wise person among them? Arminian readers see irony aimed at a church proud of its wisdom. Their pride has not produced practical wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a call for church officers or members to exercise godly judgment. Wisdom is a gift meant for service.

1 Corinthians 6:6

6But brother goes to law with brother, and that before unbelievers!

Arminian

Brother goes to law against brother, and before unbelievers. Arminian readers see the damage done to the church’s witness. Family quarrels are aired before outsiders.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an offence against the unity of the body of Christ. The world sees a divided church.

1 Corinthians 6:7

7Therefore it is already altogether a defect in you, that you have lawsuits one with another. Why not rather be wronged? Why not rather be defrauded?

Arminian

Lawsuits are already a defeat; why not rather suffer wrong or be defrauded? Arminian readers see Jesus’ teaching on non-retaliation applied to the church. Love can choose to absorb wrong.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same ethic grounded in Christ, who suffered wrong for his people. Victory in court can be spiritual defeat.

1 Corinthians 6:8

8No, but you yourselves do wrong, and defraud, and that against your brothers.

Arminian

Instead they themselves wrong and defraud their brothers. Arminian readers see believers capable of committing real injustice. Paul calls them to account for it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see remaining sin in the church. Defrauding fellow believers violates the love that union with Christ requires.

1 Corinthians 6:9

9Or don’t you know that the unrighteous will not inherit God’s Kingdom? Don’t be deceived. Neither the sexually immoral, nor idolaters, nor adulterers, nor male prostitutes, nor homosexuals,

Arminian

The unrighteous will not inherit the kingdom; do not be deceived. Arminian readers take this warning to apply to believers who return to such sins and persist in them without repentance. Do not be deceived signals that Christians could deceive themselves into thinking such a life is safe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a description of those who are outside the kingdom, given to warn against false assurance. True believers may stumble, but a settled life of unrepentant sin shows that one has never been changed.

Key contrast Both agree that those who live in unrepentant sin will not inherit the kingdom. Arminian readers see a warning that a believer could fall away into such a life. Reformed readers see a warning that such a life reveals that someone was never truly changed.

1 Corinthians 6:10

10nor thieves, nor covetous, nor drunkards, nor slanderers, nor extortionists, will inherit God’s Kingdom.

Arminian

Thieves, the greedy, drunkards, slanderers, and swindlers will not inherit the kingdom. Arminian readers note that the list includes everyday sins, so no one should feel secure in any persistent sin. The call is to repentance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the comprehensive reach of sin and the need for grace. The list levels all people before God.

1 Corinthians 6:11

11Such were some of you, but you were washed. But you were sanctified. But you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus, and in the Spirit of our God.

Arminian

Such were some of you, but you were washed, sanctified, justified in the name of the Lord Jesus and by the Spirit. Arminian readers rejoice in the real transformation God works in those who believe. The past tense describes a decisive change that believers must now live out.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the work of the triune God in salvation: washing, sanctifying, and justifying, accomplished through Christ and applied by the Spirit. The order shows that justification is not the result of moral change but part of God’s complete rescue.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
