Passage

1 Corinthians 15

Verses 35–49 · WEB

Paul answers the question, How are the dead raised, and with what body? He uses the image of a seed: what is sown does not come to life unless it dies, and God gives it a body as he chooses. There are different kinds of flesh and different kinds of glory. So it is with the resurrection: sown perishable, raised imperishable; sown in dishonor, raised in glory; sown in weakness, raised in power; sown a natural body, raised a spiritual body. The first Adam became a living soul, the last Adam a life-giving spirit. As we have borne the image of the earthly man, we shall also bear the image of the heavenly. Both traditions agree on bodily resurrection and the transformation of believers.

1 Corinthians 15:35

35But someone will say, “How are the dead raised?” and, “With what kind of body do they come?”

Arminian

Someone will ask how the dead are raised and with what body. Arminian readers see Paul answering honest questions. Paul takes the question seriously even while calling it foolish. Arminian readers see the gospel inviting thoughtful response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul answering a mocking objection as well as an honest question. The resurrection is hard to imagine, but not hard for God.

1 Corinthians 15:36

36You foolish one, that which you yourself sow is not made alive unless it dies.

Arminian

Foolish one, what you sow does not come to life unless it dies. Arminian readers see death as the path to new life. A seed must be sown and die before the plant appears. Paul uses this image to show that death does not end the believer's bodily existence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the continuity and transformation of the body. The same body that is sown is raised, yet changed beyond recognition. Paul's analogy answers the objection that resurrection is impossible.

1 Corinthians 15:37

37That which you sow, you don’t sow the body that will be, but a bare grain, maybe of wheat, or of some other kind.

Arminian

You do not sow the body that will be, but a bare seed. Arminian readers see continuity and difference between present and future bodies. The seed and the plant are connected, yet the plant is far more glorious. Arminian readers see the resurrection body as truly ours, yet transformed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the seed image guarding both identity and change. The plant is not the seed, yet it truly grows from it. Reformed readers stress that this identity rests on God's power, not on the body's natural capacities.

1 Corinthians 15:38

38But God gives it a body even as it pleased him, and to each seed a body of its own.

Arminian

God gives it a body as he pleases. Arminian readers see God’s creative power in the resurrection. The one who makes each seed’s body can make the resurrection body.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign will in the form of the resurrection body. The phrase “as he pleases” places the outcome in God’s good pleasure.

1 Corinthians 15:39

39All flesh is not the same flesh, but there is one flesh of men, another flesh of animals, another of fish, and another of birds.

Arminian

All flesh is not the same. Arminian readers see diversity in God’s creation. If God made many kinds of bodies, he can make a new kind for the resurrection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the variety of creation as evidence of God’s power. The objection that a resurrection body is unimaginable underestimates the Creator. Not all flesh is the same, and God can give a body suited to each state.

1 Corinthians 15:40

40There are also celestial bodies, and terrestrial bodies; but the glory of the celestial differs from that of the terrestrial.

Arminian

There are heavenly and earthly bodies with different glories. Arminian readers see variety in God’s creation. Glory comes in many forms. Paul's point is that God can give different kinds of bodies. The resurrection body will be fitted for the age to come.

Reformed

Reformed readers see creation’s range of glories preparing for the glory of the resurrection body. What God has already made hints at what he will make.

1 Corinthians 15:41

41There is one glory of the sun, another glory of the moon, and another glory of the stars; for one star differs from another star in glory.

Arminian

Sun, moon, and stars differ in glory. Arminian readers see diversity in glory, which some apply to differing rewards. Others think the point is simply that God gives varied glory, so a resurrection body need not be like the present one.

Reformed

Reformed readers see differences of glory among the heavenly bodies. Some, including Calvin, connected this to degrees of glory among the saints. Others read the verse simply as illustrating the variety of God's creation.

1 Corinthians 15:42

42So also is the resurrection of the dead. The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable.

Arminian

So is the resurrection: sown perishable, raised imperishable. Arminian readers see the transformation of believers’ bodies. What is sown perishable is raised imperishable. Arminian readers see this as God's promise to those who remain in Christ by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the transformation of believers’ bodies as part of their glorification. What is sown in corruption is raised beyond corruption. The body sown in weakness belongs to the same person who is raised.

1 Corinthians 15:43

43It is sown in dishonor; it is raised in glory. It is sown in weakness; it is raised in power.

Arminian

Sown in dishonor, raised in glory; sown in weakness, raised in power. Arminian readers see hope for those who suffer. Arminian readers see the bodies of believers, weakened by sickness, age, and persecution, raised in power. Their present suffering is not the final word.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the reversal of the effects of sin. Dishonor and weakness give way to glory and power by God’s work. Reformed readers see the change as the completion of what God began in regeneration.

1 Corinthians 15:44

44It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body. There is a natural body and there is also a spiritual body.

Arminian

Sown a natural body, raised a spiritual body. Arminian readers see a body animated by the Spirit, not an immaterial body. “Spiritual” describes a body fully governed by the Holy Spirit. The resurrection body is real and physical, yet suited to the new creation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that “spiritual” means governed by the Spirit, not non-physical. The resurrection body is a real body made fully alive by the Spirit.

1 Corinthians 15:45

45So also it is written, “The first man, Adam, became a living soul.” The last Adam became a life-giving spirit.

Arminian

The first Adam became a living soul, the last Adam a life-giving spirit. Arminian readers see Christ as the head of a new humanity. Paul quotes Genesis 2:7 and sets Christ as the last Adam. Arminian readers see Christ giving life to all who are joined to him by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the federal theology of two Adams, central to covenant theology. The last Adam gives the life the first Adam lost. Through his resurrection, Christ is the source of the Spirit's life.

1 Corinthians 15:46

46However that which is spiritual isn’t first, but that which is natural, then that which is spiritual.

Arminian

The natural comes first, then the spiritual. Arminian readers see God’s order in salvation history. Present life prepares for the life to come. The natural comes first, then the spiritual.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the movement from creation to consummation. Even before the fall, creation was headed toward a higher, Spirit-filled state. The resurrection brings creation to its intended goal.

1 Corinthians 15:47

47The first man is of the earth, made of dust. The second man is the Lord from heaven.

Arminian

The first man is from the earth, the second from heaven. Arminian readers see Christ’s heavenly origin. His life is not merely improved human life but life from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the contrast of two heads and two humanities. Christ, the man from heaven, begins a new creation. The first man is from the earth, the second from heaven.

1 Corinthians 15:48

48As is the one made of dust, such are those who are also made of dust; and as is the heavenly, such are they also that are heavenly.

Arminian

As is the earthly, so are those of earth; as is the heavenly, so are those of heaven. Arminian readers see believers sharing Christ’s likeness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer’s future shaped by union with Christ. Those joined to the heavenly man share his likeness. As we have borne the image of the earthly man, we will bear the image of the heavenly.

1 Corinthians 15:49

49As we have borne the image of those made of dust, let’s also bear the image of the heavenly.

Arminian

As we have borne the image of the earthly, we shall also bear the image of the heavenly. Arminian readers see the hope of transformation into Christ’s likeness, with some manuscripts reading “let us bear” as an exhortation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the certainty of glorification, the final link in the chain of Romans 8:29–30. God will finish conforming his people to the image of his Son.

Key contrast Arminian readers often note the variant “let us bear” and see a call to pursue Christ’s image. Reformed readers see the promise that those predestined to be conformed to Christ will surely bear his image.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
