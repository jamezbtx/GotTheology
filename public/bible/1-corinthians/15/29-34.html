Passage

1 Corinthians 15

Verses 29–34 · WEB

Paul adds further arguments. Why are people baptized for the dead if the dead are not raised? Why do the apostles face danger every hour, and why did Paul fight with beasts at Ephesus? If the dead are not raised, let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die. He warns them not to be deceived, for bad company corrupts good morals, and calls them to wake up and stop sinning, for some have no knowledge of God. Verse 29 is one of the hardest verses in the New Testament, with many interpretations, and neither Arminian nor Reformed readers have a single view. Both traditions reject any practice of proxy baptism for the dead.

1 Corinthians 15:29

29Or else what will they do who are baptized for the dead? If the dead aren’t raised at all, why then are they baptized for the dead?

Arminian

Why are people baptized for the dead if the dead are not raised? Arminian readers offer various interpretations. Many see a reference to a practice Paul mentions in argument without endorsing it.

Reformed

Reformed readers offer similar interpretations. Calvin suggested it referred to those baptized when near death, while others see it as baptism in hope of reunion with believers who had died.

Key contrast This verse does not divide Arminian and Reformed readers. Both traditions offer a range of views and both reject proxy baptism as a Christian practice.

1 Corinthians 15:30

30Why do we also stand in jeopardy every hour?

Arminian

Why do we face danger every hour? Arminian readers see apostolic suffering as meaningful only in light of the resurrection. Paul’s own risks are an argument for what he believes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same argument from Paul’s life. No one would endure constant danger for a hope that ends at death. Paul's daily danger would be foolish if death were the end.

1 Corinthians 15:31

31I affirm, by the boasting in you which I have in Christ Jesus our Lord, I die daily.

Arminian

Paul dies daily, as surely as he boasts of them in Christ. Arminian readers see daily danger and self-denial in Paul’s ministry. His life is a living testimony to resurrection hope.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s daily exposure to death and connect it to the daily mortification of the believer. His boast in the Corinthians is a boast in God’s work among them.

1 Corinthians 15:32

32If I fought with animals at Ephesus for human purposes, what does it profit me? If the dead are not raised, then “let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die.”

Arminian

If he fought beasts at Ephesus for human reasons, what profit is it? If the dead are not raised, let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die. Arminian readers see that without the resurrection, self-indulgence would make sense.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul quotes Isaiah 22:13. Without the resurrection, the outlook of those who live for pleasure would be the logical one. Paul's fighting with “wild beasts” at Ephesus may be literal or a figure for fierce opposition.

1 Corinthians 15:33

33Don’t be deceived! “Evil companionships corrupt good morals.”

Arminian

Do not be deceived: bad company corrupts good morals. Arminian readers see a warning about the influence of false teachers. The quotation is from a Greek proverb, used by the poet Menander. Arminian readers see that believers are responsible for the company they keep and the teaching they accept.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul quotes a Greek proverb associated with the poet Menander. Bad teaching about the resurrection leads to bad living. False belief and moral decline go together.

1 Corinthians 15:34

34Wake up righteously, and don’t sin, for some have no knowledge of God. I say this to your shame.

Arminian

Wake up righteously and do not sin, for some have no knowledge of God. Arminian readers see a call to repentance. Paul calls them to sober up and stop sinning. Ignorance of God among some of them is a matter of shame, not of neutral opinion.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that ignorance of God is the root of moral corruption. The call to wake up is a call back to true knowledge of God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
