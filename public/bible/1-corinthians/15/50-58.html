Passage

1 Corinthians 15

Verses 50–58 · WEB

Paul concludes the chapter with a mystery: flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God, but we will not all sleep; we will all be changed in a moment, at the last trumpet. The dead will be raised imperishable, and the perishable must put on the imperishable. Then the saying will be fulfilled, Death is swallowed up in victory. Death’s sting is sin, and the power of sin is the law, but thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ. Therefore, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord. Both traditions find great comfort here. Arminian readers often stress the exhortation in verse 58; Reformed readers stress the gift of victory in verse 57.

1 Corinthians 15:50

50Now I say this, brothers, that flesh and blood can’t inherit God’s Kingdom; neither does the perishable inherit imperishable.

Arminian

Flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God. Arminian readers see that transformation is necessary for the kingdom. Present bodies, mortal and perishable, are not fitted for the age to come. Arminian readers see God's promise that believers will be changed, not left behind.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the body must be changed, not discarded. Flesh and blood in their present mortal state cannot enter the kingdom. Paul speaks of perishable bodies, not of physical existence as such.

1 Corinthians 15:51

51Behold, I tell you a mystery. We will not all sleep, but we will all be changed,

Arminian

We will not all sleep, but we will all be changed. Arminian readers see the hope of living believers at Christ’s return. Not all believers will die before Christ returns, but all will be transformed. Arminian readers see a mystery revealed for the comfort of the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a mystery revealed: both living and dead believers will be transformed. No believer will miss the change. The change is God's act, sure for all who are his.

1 Corinthians 15:52

52in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet. For the trumpet will sound, and the dead will be raised incorruptible, and we will be changed.

Arminian

In a moment, at the last trumpet, the dead will be raised and we will be changed. Arminian readers see the sudden return of Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the suddenness of Christ’s return and the power of God that changes his people in a moment. The trumpet sounds, the dead are raised, and the living are changed. What God promised will happen in a moment.

1 Corinthians 15:53

53For this perishable body must become imperishable, and this mortal must put on immortality.

Arminian

The perishable must put on the imperishable, and the mortal immortality. Arminian readers see the final transformation of believers. Paul says what must happen: the perishable must put on the imperishable. Arminian readers see this as a promised future for all who belong to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that immortality is God’s gift, not a natural possession of the soul. The perishable must be clothed by him. The image of clothing implies the same person receives a new condition.

1 Corinthians 15:54

54But when this perishable body will have become imperishable, and this mortal will have put on immortality, then what is written will happen: “Death is swallowed up in victory.”

Arminian

Then death will be swallowed up in victory. Arminian readers see Isaiah 25:8 fulfilled. The promise of the prophets finds its completion in the resurrection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fulfillment of God’s covenant promises in Isaiah 25:8. Death, which entered through Adam, is finally swallowed up. Paul quotes Isaiah 25:8 as fulfilled at the resurrection.

1 Corinthians 15:55

55“Death, where is your sting? Hades, where is your victory?”

Arminian

Death, where is your sting? Hades, where is your victory? Arminian readers see triumph over death, echoing Hosea 13:14. The taunt belongs to all who share Christ’s victory.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same triumph, echoing Hosea 13:14. Death has lost its sting for believers because Christ bore it in their place. Paul's taunt shows confidence that death is defeated.

1 Corinthians 15:56

56The sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law.

Arminian

The sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law. Arminian readers see sin and the law as the forces that give death its power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law’s role in exposing sin and condemning sinners, a theme developed in Romans 7. Christ removes death’s sting by dealing with sin and the law’s curse.

1 Corinthians 15:57

57But thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ. Arminian readers see victory offered and received through faith. Victory is God's gift, not human achievement. Arminian readers see believers sharing in that victory by continuing in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see victory as God’s gift, accomplished by Christ and applied to his people. The thanks belongs to God because the victory is his work.

1 Corinthians 15:58

58Therefore, my beloved brothers, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the Lord’s work, because you know that your labor is not in vain in the Lord.

Arminian

Therefore be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing your labor is not in vain. Arminian readers see a call to persevere in faithful labor.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the call to steadfastness grounded in the certainty of the victory just described. Labor is not in vain because the outcome is secure in the Lord.

Key contrast Both traditions see a call to steadfast labor. Arminian readers stress perseverance as the believer’s responsibility. Reformed readers stress that God’s gift of victory guarantees the labor is not in vain.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
