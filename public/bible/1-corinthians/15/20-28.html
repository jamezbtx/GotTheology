Passage

1 Corinthians 15

Verses 20–28 · WEB

Paul now states the positive truth: Christ has been raised as the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep. As death came through one man, so resurrection comes through one man; as in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive, each in his own order. Christ reigns until he has put all enemies under his feet, and the last enemy to be destroyed is death. Then the Son himself will be subjected to the Father, so that God may be all in all. Verse 22 is often discussed in debates over the scope of salvation, and verse 28 has a long history in discussions of the Trinity. Arminian and Reformed readers differ over the scope of “all” in verse 22, though both reject universalism.

1 Corinthians 15:20

20But now Christ has been raised from the dead. He became the first fruits of those who are asleep.

Arminian

Christ has been raised as the firstfruits of those who sleep. Arminian readers see the guarantee of the full harvest of believers. Firstfruits were the first part of the harvest offered to God, a pledge that the rest would follow. Christ's resurrection guarantees the future resurrection of those who belong to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the firstfruits belong to and guarantee the harvest. Christ’s resurrection is the pledge that his people will be raised. Firstfruits also recalls the Old Testament feast, linking Christ's resurrection to God's covenant calendar.

1 Corinthians 15:21

21For since death came by man, the resurrection of the dead also came by man.

Arminian

Since death came by a man, the resurrection also came by a man. Arminian readers see Christ’s humanity as essential to his saving work. Death entered through Adam, and resurrection comes through Christ. Arminian readers see both men affecting all humanity, with the benefits of Christ received by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the federal headship of Adam and Christ, a key theme in covenant theology. Each man acts for those he represents. Through one man came death, and through one man comes resurrection.

1 Corinthians 15:22

22For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive.

Arminian

As in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive. Arminian readers see Christ’s work as broad in scope, offered to all, while only those in Christ by faith share the resurrection to life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see “all in Christ” as those united to him, just as “all in Adam” are those united to Adam. The parallel shows Christ’s headship effectively securing life for his people.

Key contrast Both traditions reject universalism here. Arminian readers stress a provision that matches Adam’s reach, received by faith. Reformed readers stress two heads, each securing the destiny of those united to him.

1 Corinthians 15:23

23But each in his own order: Christ the first fruits, then those who are Christ’s, at his coming.

Arminian

Each in his own order: Christ the firstfruits, then those who belong to Christ at his coming. Arminian readers see the resurrection to life for those who are Christ’s.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that “those who are Christ’s” recalls the people given to the Son by the Father in John 6:37–39. The order of resurrection is secure because they belong to him.

1 Corinthians 15:24

24Then the end comes, when he will deliver up the Kingdom to God, even the Father; when he will have abolished all rule and all authority and power.

Arminian

Then comes the end, when he hands over the kingdom to the Father, having abolished all rule and power. Arminian readers see the final victory of Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the consummation of Christ’s mediatorial kingdom, a theme in Reformed eschatology. The Son’s work as mediator reaches its goal when he presents the kingdom to the Father.

1 Corinthians 15:25

25For he must reign until he has put all his enemies under his feet.

Arminian

He must reign until he has put all enemies under his feet. Arminian readers see Christ’s present reign moving toward final victory. Arminian readers see the church sharing in that victory as the gospel goes out to all nations.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Psalm 110:1 fulfilled. Christ reigns now, and his reign is certain to prevail over every enemy. Christ's reign is not merely future but already underway at God's right hand.

1 Corinthians 15:26

26The last enemy that will be abolished is death.

Arminian

The last enemy to be destroyed is death. Arminian readers see the ultimate defeat of death in Christ’s victory. Death is the final consequence of sin to be undone.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that death is an enemy, not a friend. Its destruction completes the reversal of the curse that began with Adam. Death is the last enemy to be destroyed. Its defeat will be complete only at the resurrection.

1 Corinthians 15:27

27For, “He put all things in subjection under his feet.” But when he says, “All things are put in subjection”, it is evident that he is excepted who subjected all things to him.

Arminian

He has put all things under his feet, except the one who put them under him. Arminian readers see Psalm 8 fulfilled in Christ. Psalm 8 speaks of humanity's dominion, which Christ fulfills as the true man. The Father who subjects all things is himself excepted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Father’s sovereign appointment of all things to the Son. Psalm 8’s vision of human dominion is fulfilled in the last Adam.

1 Corinthians 15:28

28When all things have been subjected to him, then the Son will also himself be subjected to him who subjected all things to him, that God may be all in all.

Arminian

Then the Son will be subjected to the Father, that God may be all in all. Arminian readers see the Son’s subjection as his role as mediator, not a denial of his deity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. Calvin read the Son’s subjection in terms of his mediatorial office, not his divine nature, which is equal with the Father’s.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
