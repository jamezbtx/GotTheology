Passage

Ephesians 2

Verses 1–10 · ESV · Voices

Paul reminds Gentile believers they were dead in trespasses, following the world and the prince of the air, by nature children of wrath—until God, rich in mercy, made them alive with Christ, saved them by grace through faith, and created them in Christ for prepared good works.

Ephesians 2:1

1And you were dead in the trespasses and sins

Arminian

Paul’s first word to the saved is a hard one: “you were dead.” Trespasses and sins were not a surface illness; they were a spiritual grave. Classical Arminians refuse to soften the diagnosis.

Yet deadness is not the last word. Enabling grace can reach those who cannot raise themselves. The God who names the tomb also makes a genuine faith-response possible—so the dead are summoned, not flattered, into life.

Reformed

Calvin’s accent is Paul’s: this is no mere danger of dying, but a real and present death. Spiritual death is alienation from God. We are born dead and live dead until we share Christ’s life.

Reformed reading therefore hears inability, not exaggeration. Until God makes alive, there is no native power to believe. The verse humbles every boast and prepares the “But God” of mercy.

Key contrast Both take “dead” as real spiritual ruin, not a figure of speech. Arminians typically hold that enabling grace can still draw a genuine response from the dead; Reformed readers typically hold that only effectual quickening actually raises those dead in trespasses.

Ephesians 2:2

2in which you once walked, following the course of this world, following the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that is now at work in the sons of disobedience—

Arminian

The former walk had a path, a prince, and a spirit. “Course of this world” and “prince of the power of the air” name real bondage under this present age. Disobedience was not a private quirk; it was a realm.

Classical Arminians still insist the walk was ours: “you once walked… following.” Saving grace interrupts that following. The gospel sincerely calls people to leave the prince’s course.

Reformed

Paul stacks powers: world, prince, and the spirit now at work in the sons of disobedience. Unregenerate life is not morally neutral wandering; it is captivity under a hostile ruler.

Reformed comfort and warning: this spirit “is now at work.” Only a greater power can break that work. Deliverance will be God’s making-alive, not a self-directed change of lifestyle.

Ephesians 2:3

3among whom we all once lived in the passions of our flesh, carrying out the desires of the flesh and the mind, and were by nature children of wrath, like the rest of mankind.

Arminian

“We all” shuts every ethnic boast. Passions of flesh and mind, desires carried out, children of wrath by nature—like the rest of mankind. Original corruption and deserved wrath are confessed, not denied.

Arminian pastoral use: if all stood under wrath, all need mercy. Nature does not make repentance impossible once grace enables; it does make self-rescue impossible. Humility, not despair, is the aim.

Reformed

Nature, not merely nurture, placed us under wrath. Flesh and mind together were bent. “Like the rest of mankind” levels Jew and Gentile before the same holy God.

Reformed reading: this is why salvation must be resurrection, not self-improvement. Children of wrath do not become children of God by trying harder. Mercy will have to create life where wrath was due.

Ephesians 2:4

4But God, being rich in mercy, because of the great love with which he loved us,

Arminian

The hinge is mercy, and the motive is love. “But God” does not cancel the death of vv. 1–3; it overrules it. He is rich in mercy because His love is great, not because we were likely.

Classical Arminians hear a Father whose love is truly kind. The same richness that reached “us” is the character from which the gospel is preached. Mercy is praised as the source of every saving turn.

Reformed

“But God” is the gospel’s interruption. Rich mercy and great love explain why the dead live. God loved us—this church, these former children of wrath—and that love is not a mood but the cause of what follows.

Reformed comfort: salvation begins in God’s affection, not in our appeal. The love is as particular as the making-alive it produces. Doxology, not self-credit, is the right response.

Ephesians 2:5

5even when we were dead in our trespasses, made us alive together with Christ—by grace you have been saved—

Arminian

God made us alive together with Christ “even when we were dead.” Grace is the cause; union with Christ is the place. “By grace you have been saved” is already true of believers—a completed kindness, not a wage.

Classical Arminians add: this life is received. Faith is the empty hand that takes what grace confers. Being made alive does not erase a genuine response; it is the kindness that makes response possible and real.

Reformed

The subject is God; the condition is death; the act is making alive with Christ. This is resurrection language for conversion. “By grace you have been saved” names a gift already given, not a cooperative project.

Reformed accent: the dead do not assist the first breath. Effectual quickening is God’s work in union with Christ. Faith will appear, but it appears as the living act of those God has raised.

Key contrast Both glory in grace that saves the dead with Christ. Arminians typically describe making-alive as grace that enables a real faith-response; Reformed readers typically describe it as effectual resurrection that precedes and produces faith.

Ephesians 2:6

6and raised us up with him and seated us with him in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus,

Arminian

Raised and seated with Him in the heavenly places—already, in Christ Jesus. Salvation is not only pardon; it is participation in the risen Lord’s exaltation. The location is “with him” and “in Christ Jesus.”

Arminian pastoral note: this dignity belongs to those united to Christ by faith. Live as people already welcomed above, not as slaves still negotiating a seat. Grace that raised you also calls you to occupy the mercy given.

Reformed

The same power that raised Jesus has raised His people and seated them with Him. The heavenly places of 1:3, 20 now include the church in the Son. Status is as sure as Christ’s session.

Reformed comfort: you are not climbing toward a chair God might pull away. In Christ the seating is done. Assurance rests on His exaltation, not on the mood of our prayers. What God has seated, He will not unseat.

Ephesians 2:7

7so that in the coming ages he might show the immeasurable riches of his grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus.

Arminian

The purpose stretches into “coming ages”: God intends to show the immeasurable riches of His grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus. Salvation is a display of kindness, not a grudging rescue.

Arminian preaching loves this verse’s wideness of heart. If the ages will exhibit grace, the church should exhibit it now—kindness that makes the gospel believable and invites sinners to the same Christ.

Reformed

God’s aim is the public showing of grace. Kindness in Christ Jesus will be the exhibit of the ages. What He has done in making the dead alive will not be a footnote; it will be the forever demonstration of His wealth.

Reformed worship: if the point is to show grace, boasting is absurd and despair is answered. The coming ages belong to kindness already pledged in Christ.

Ephesians 2:8

8For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God,

Arminian

“By grace… through faith.” Wesley’s line is the classical Arminian grammar: grace confers the gift without respect to worthiness; faith, with an empty hand and no pretence of desert, receives it. Faith is the instrument, not the price.

“This is not your own doing; it is the gift of God.” Being saved through faith is God’s gift. No one may treat faith as a meritorious work. The hand that receives is empty because grace filled it with Christ.

Reformed

Grace is the fountain; faith is the channel; “this” is not of yourselves. Reformed readers typically take the gift to cover the entire salvation, faith included. Even the believing is under grace, not beside it.

Pastoral aim is the same humility Paul wants: no self-made Christians. If it is the gift of God, the dead were raised by mercy. Faith is real, and faith itself is God’s gift in the saved.

Key contrast Both deny that salvation is self-produced and both honor faith as the empty-handed way of receiving Christ. Arminians typically treat faith as a grace-enabled response that is not itself a work; Reformed readers typically include faith within the gift, so that even believing is God’s doing.

Ephesians 2:9

9not a result of works, so that no one may boast.

Arminian

“Not a result of works, so that no one may boast.” Works neither begin nor buy salvation. If anyone could contribute merit, the cross would share credit and pride would live.

Classical Arminians agree with Paul against every works-gospel. Faith is not a substitute work; it is the refusal of boasting. Gratitude, not self-congratulation, is the only fitting noise.

Reformed

The purpose clause is pastoral and theological: no one may boast. Works are excluded from the ground of being saved. Even religious achievement cannot be smuggled in as the reason God made the dead alive.

Reformed reading presses the same knife to the heart: if grace is gift, boasting is a lie. The church is a company of the unboastful, or it has not yet understood this verse.

Ephesians 2:10

10For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them.

Arminian

“His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works.” Salvation’s purpose includes a walk, not a sofa. Good works follow the new creation; they do not cause it. We are made for them.

“Which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them.” God has laid a path of obedience. Walking is real human life under grace—enabled, responsible, and expected. Faith’s empty hand is followed by busy feet.

Reformed

We are God’s making. Created in Christ Jesus, the good works are not an optional extra but the end for which the new creation exists. Even the works were “prepared beforehand.”

Reformed accent: sanctification is as much God’s prior work as justification. We walk in a path He marked out. Holiness is fruit of workmanship, not the wage that purchases life. The walk is real because God prepared it.

Key contrast Both insist that good works follow salvation and that they are God’s purpose for the new creation. Arminians typically stress the believer’s enabled walk along a prepared path; Reformed readers typically stress that the works themselves were ordained beforehand as part of God’s making.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on spiritual death, being made alive with Christ, and grace through faith—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Ephesians 2:1–10.

Arminian Notable voices

“By grace ye are saved through faith — Grace, without any respect to human worthiness, confers the glorious gift. Faith, with an empty hand, and without any pretence to personal desert, receives the heavenly blessing. And this is not of yourselves — This refers to the whole preceding clause, That ye are saved through faith, is the gift of God.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Eph 2:8)

Reformed Notable voices

“He does not mean simply that they were in danger of death; but he declares that it was a real and present death under which they labored. As spiritual death is nothing else than the alienation of the soul from God, we are all born as dead men, and we live as dead men, until we are made partakers of the life of Christ.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Ephesians (on Eph 2:1)
