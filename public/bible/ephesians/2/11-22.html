Passage

Ephesians 2

Verses 11–22 · ESV · Voices

Remembering their Gentile far-off past, Paul announces they have been brought near by Christ’s blood: He is our peace, making one new humanity, reconciling both to God through the cross, giving access in one Spirit, and building them as God’s household on the apostolic/prophetic foundation with Christ the cornerstone.

Ephesians 2:11

11Therefore remember that at one time you Gentiles in the flesh, called “the uncircumcision” by what is called the circumcision, which is made in the flesh by hands—

Arminian

“Therefore remember.” Gentile believers must not forget the flesh-labels: “the uncircumcision,” named by those whose mark was made by hands. Memory of exclusion is meant to humble and to bind them to mercy.

Arminian pastoral use: remember grace so you do not despise others still outside. The God who brought you near still calls the far-off. Circumcision in the flesh never was the saving thing; Christ is.

Reformed

Paul commands memory of a real former state. Ethnic and covenantal lines were not imaginary. Hand-made circumcision could name a people, but it could not give the life of vv. 4–10.

Reformed reading: remember, because forgetting the pit breeds pride. The church’s unity later in the chapter rests on a shared confession that we did not invent our nearness. God made the far into household.

Ephesians 2:12

12remember that you were at that time separated from Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world.

Arminian

Five absences stack: separated from Christ, alienated from Israel’s commonwealth, strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope, without God in the world. Gentile lostness was not a mild disadvantage.

Classical Arminians preach this as the world’s real condition apart from Christ—and as the reason the gospel must be offered. “No hope” is honest diagnosis; the blood of v. 13 is honest hope.

Reformed

Without Christ, without commonwealth, without covenants, without hope, without God. The verse is a map of ruin. “In the world” yet “without God” is the most devastating pairing.

Reformed humility: this is what we were. Nearness will have to be created by God in Christ, not negotiated by Gentile effort. The emptiness of v. 12 makes the “But now” of v. 13 astonishing.

Ephesians 2:13

13But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ.

Arminian

“But now in Christ Jesus” turns the story. Those once far off “have been brought near by the blood of Christ.” Distance is closed by sacrifice, not by becoming Jews first. Nearness is located in Him.

Arminian accent: the blood truly brings near, and that nearness is received by faith. The far-off are invited; the brought-near should never bar the door they themselves walked through.

Reformed

“But now” is as decisive as “But God” in v. 4. In Christ, by His blood, the far-off have been brought near—accomplished fact, not a possibility waiting on ritual.

Reformed comfort: blood does what labels and covenants-in-the-flesh could not. Those in Christ need not wonder if they are still far. The nearness is as real as the cross that brought them in.

Key contrast Both locate nearness in Christ’s blood, not in Gentile self-improvement. Arminians typically emphasize that this nearness is offered to the far-off to be received by faith; Reformed readers typically emphasize that those in Christ have actually been brought near by an accomplished reconciling work.

Ephesians 2:14

14For he himself is our peace, who has made us both one and has broken down in his flesh the dividing wall of hostility

Arminian

“He himself is our peace.” Wesley heard not only a purchased peace but Christ as the bond and centre of union. He made both—Jew and Gentile—one church, and broke down the dividing wall of hostility in His flesh.

Classical Arminians preach a Person, not a program. Peace is not first a feeling; it is Jesus. Those who trust Him are joined to one another because they are joined to Him. The wall falls where He is received.

Reformed

Calvin’s question follows: if Christ is our peace, those outside Him remain at variance with God. “Our peace” is His beautiful title—the peace between God and men. In His flesh the wall of hostility is broken.

Reformed pastoral word: let no one who dwells in Christ doubt that he is reconciled to God. Peace is not a mood we maintain; it is the Son we are given. Hostility’s wall is down because He died.

Key contrast Both confess that Christ Himself is our peace and that He made Jew and Gentile one. Arminians typically stress the bond received as people come to Him in faith; Reformed readers typically stress the effectual reconciling accomplished in His flesh for those who dwell in Him.

Ephesians 2:15

15by abolishing the law of commandments expressed in ordinances, that he might create in himself one new man in place of the two, so making peace,

Arminian

In His flesh He abolished “the law of commandments expressed in ordinances”—the barrier-system that kept nations apart—so that He might create in Himself one new man in place of the two, so making peace.

Arminian hope: the new humanity is in Christ, and peace is made there. The church must not rebuild ordinances as a second wall. All who come to Him are welcome into the one new man.

Reformed

Abolition and creation sit together. Christ does not merely cancel hostility; He creates one new man in Himself. Peace is the product of that new creation, not a truce between unchanged rivals.

Reformed accent: “in himself” is the location of the new humanity. God is the creator; the cross is the means. The church’s unity is Christ’s workmanship, as sure as His flesh.

Ephesians 2:16

16and might reconcile us both to God in one body through the cross, thereby killing the hostility.

Arminian

The aim reaches further: to reconcile both to God in one body through the cross, thereby killing the hostility. Vertical peace with God and horizontal peace with one another share one wood.

Classical Arminians hear a finished cross and a living call. Hostility is killed there; we must not revive it here. The same blood that brings the far near also makes enemies into one body as they receive the peace He made.

Reformed

Double reconciliation: both to God, in one body, through the cross. Hostility is not managed; it is killed. The body is one because the sacrifice is one.

Reformed comfort: what the cross killed stays dead in God’s accounting. Unity with God and with one another is not a human peace-plan. It is the fruit of Christ’s reconciling work—already accomplished, then lived out in the body.

Ephesians 2:17

17And he came and preached peace to you who were far off and peace to those who were near.

Arminian

He came and preached peace to the far off and peace to the near. The same announcement reaches Gentile and Jew. Peace is news before it is a feeling—proclaimed so that it may be heard and trusted.

Arminian pastoral note: preaching peace is Christ’s ongoing work through the gospel. Those still far off need the same word you received. Do not privatize what He published.

Reformed

The Preacher is Christ; the message is peace; the audiences are both far and near. What He made in the flesh He announces in the word. The two peaces are one peace, because the two peoples are being made one.

Reformed reading: the sermon is as divine as the sacrifice. God does not leave reconciliation silent. Those who hear and believe are hearing the voice of the Peacemaker Himself.

Ephesians 2:18

18For through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father.

Arminian

“Through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father.” Access is Trinitarian and shared: the Son is the way, the Spirit the one atmosphere, the Father the welcome. Jew and Gentile approach on the same terms.

Classical Arminians treasure access as a real open door. Grace brings us to the Father, and faith uses the access Christ provides. No second-class children wait in the courtyard; both have the Spirit’s entrance.

Reformed

Through the Son, in one Spirit, to the Father—this is the Christian’s approach. Access is a gift of the reconciling work, not a ladder we raise. “We both” means the former far and near now share one Spirit.

Reformed assurance: if you are in Christ, the Father is not distant. The Spirit is the living proof of access. What God has opened in His Son He does not close to those He has brought near.

Key contrast Both confess Trinitarian access for Jew and Gentile alike. Arminians typically emphasize that this access is truly open to be used by faith; Reformed readers typically emphasize that access is the effectual fruit of Christ’s reconciling work for those in Him.

Ephesians 2:19

19So then you are no longer strangers and aliens, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God,

Arminian

“So then” draws the civic and family conclusion. No longer strangers and aliens, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of God’s household. Identity has changed; belonging is the new fact.

Arminian application: live like family. Household manners are holiness and welcome. Those God has made citizens should not treat one another as guests on probation.

Reformed

Status language is gospel language. Strangers become citizens; aliens become household members. God names the new address before He commands the new walk.

Reformed comfort: you are not crashing a family gathering. Membership is granted in Christ. The household of God is as sure as the blood that brought you near. Belonging is gift before it is duty.

Ephesians 2:20

20built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone,

Arminian

The household is “built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the cornerstone.” The church does not invent its base. Apostolic and prophetic witness, with Christ as cornerstone, holds the structure true.

Arminian trust: rest your faith on this foundation, not on private visions or ethnic pride. The cornerstone aligns every stone. To receive the apostolic gospel is to be set on Christ.

Reformed

Foundation and cornerstone are given, not voted. Apostles and prophets—the Christ-witness of the church’s beginning—lie under us; Jesus Himself squares the whole.

Reformed reading: the church’s security is architectural and Christological. If the cornerstone is Christ, the building will not finally skew. Stay on what God has laid, and do not replace the apostolic foundation He gave.

Ephesians 2:21

21in whom the whole structure, being joined together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord.

Arminian

“In whom the whole structure, being joined together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord.” Union with Christ is the joining; holiness is the temple’s character; growth is living, not static.

Classical Arminians hear a call to remain in the joining. The temple grows as people are fitted to Christ and to one another. Holiness is communal: a dwelling, not a pile of private bricks.

Reformed

The structure grows “in whom”—in Christ. Joining-together is His work in the Lord. The temple is holy because the Lord is its location and life.

Reformed accent: growth is organic and given. God is building a sanctuary, not a club. What He joins, He grows. The church’s holiness is the holiness of being in the Lord, not a private project of loose stones.

Ephesians 2:22

22In him you also are being built together into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit.

Arminian

“In him you also are being built together into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit.” “You also” pulls Gentile believers into the same construction. The tense is present: building continues.

Arminian pastoral hope: God still dwells where the Spirit joins people to Christ and to one another. Cooperate with the building—peace, holiness, welcome—because the dwelling is for God, not for our brand.

Reformed

In Him, by the Spirit, you also are being built together. The dwelling place is God’s; the agent is the Spirit; the location is Christ. Gentile inclusion is not an annex; it is the same house.

Reformed comfort: the present tense does not mean uncertainty. It means God is not finished displaying what v. 7 promised. The Spirit will have a temple. Those in Christ are already stones in that dwelling.

Historic words on Christ as our peace, one new humanity, and God’s household—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Ephesians 2:11–22.

Arminian Notable voices

“For he is our peace — Not only as he purchased it, but as he is the very bond and centre of union. He who hath made both — Jews and gentiles, one church.
