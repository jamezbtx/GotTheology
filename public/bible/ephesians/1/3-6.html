Passage

Ephesians 1

Verses 3–6 · ESV · Voices

Paul’s opening blessing to the Ephesian church—praise that unfolds election, adoption, and grace.

Ephesians 1:3

3Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places,

Arminian

Paul opens with doxology: God is worthy of blessing because He has already blessed “us” in Christ. Every spiritual gift is located in union with the Son—those in Him by faith share heaven’s grace. Classical Arminian reading stresses that this union is entered through a genuine response to enabling grace that God freely offers and people may receive. Whatever follows about choosing and predestining must still sound like praise for a generous Father, not a cold decree.

Reformed

The blessing is Trinitarian in shape and Christ-centered in substance. Every spiritual gift flows from the Father through the Son; the “heavenly places” name the realm where Christ already reigns. “Us… in Christ” points to the elect united to the Mediator—blessings secured in the One the Father has given to His people. Assurance follows: election, adoption, and redemption rest on God’s prior generosity, not on our initiative.

Ephesians 1:4

4even as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless before him. In love

Arminian

“Chose us in him” is often read corporately: God eternally elects the people found in Christ—the Messiah is the elect One, and those joined to Him by faith share His chosen status. Classical Arminians also speak of conditional election: God, foreknowing who would freely believe, chooses them in Christ. Either way, faith is a real human response—enabled by grace, yet not coerced. The purpose clause (“holy and blameless”) aims at moral transformation; “in love” frames the whole as paternal affection.

Reformed

The choosing is personal, prior, and unconditional. Before creation God set His love on a people in Christ—not on the ground of foreseen faith or future merit, but God’s own good pleasure. Faith is the fruit of election, not its cause. “In him” names the sphere of salvation: they were chosen in the Son who would redeem them. Holiness and blamelessness are the appointed end; “in love” insists that sovereign choice is warm, not mechanical.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically locate the decisive human factor in faith (foreseen or as entry into the elect body in Christ); Reformed readings locate the decisive factor in God’s unconditional choice, with faith as the effectual result.

Ephesians 1:5

5he predestined us for adoption to himself as sons through Jesus Christ, according to the purpose of his will,

Arminian

Predestination here names the destiny God appointed for those in Christ: adoption into God’s household. It need not mean God picks some and passes over others irrespective of faith; it can mark out beforehand the glorious end of the believing people. Adoption is Christ-shaped—life in the Son. Grace enables and the Spirit draws, yet that grace remains resistible. “According to the purpose of his will” affirms God’s initiative without canceling sincere universal love and genuine human response.

Reformed

Predestination unto adoption is the Father’s sovereign appointment of the elect to sonship—particular and certain. “Through Jesus Christ” ties adoption to the Son’s person and work; effectual calling and justifying faith are historical means, not the creators of the purpose. Grace here is effectual for those the Father has given to the Son. Grounding in God’s will excludes foreseen human virtue and opens assurance: adoption cannot finally be undone by frailty.

Key contrast For Arminians, predestination often describes the foreordained destiny of believers (corporate or conditional); for the Reformed, it is God’s unconditional determination of who will be adopted—grace that prevails, not only enables.

Ephesians 1:6

6to the praise of his glorious grace, with which he has blessed us in the Beloved.

Arminian

The goal of election and adoption is doxology—the praise of glorious grace lavished “in the Beloved.” Grace is glorious because it is sincere toward all and sufficient for all who believe. The invitation remains wide; every conversion magnifies the kindness that made response possible. The pastoral note is wonder and thanksgiving, not scoring points for a decree.

Reformed

Election and adoption terminate in worship: God predestines “to the praise of his glorious grace.” “In the Beloved” concentrates everything in Christ—no blessing outside Him, and no failure of grace inside Him. The pastoral note is humility and comfort: if the aim is the praise of grace, self-reliance is muted and assurance is strengthened.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Paul’s opening blessing to the Ephesian church—praise that unfolds election, adoption, and grace—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Ephesians 1:3–6.

Arminian Notable voices

“Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who hath blessed us - God's blessing us is his bestowing all spiritual and heavenly blessings upon us. Our blessing God is the paying him our solemn and grateful acknowledgments, both on account of his own essential blessedness, and of the blessings which he bestows upon us.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Eph 1:3–6)

Reformed Notable voices

“The lofty terms in which he extolls the grace of God toward the Ephesians, are intended to rouse their hearts to gratitude, to set them all on flame, to fill them even to overflowing with this thought. They who perceive in themselves discoveries of the Divine goodness, so full and absolutely perfect, and who make them the subject of earnest meditation, will never embrace new doctrines, by which the very grace which they feel so powerfully in themselves is thrown into the shade.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Ephesians (on Eph 1:3–6)
