Passage

Ephesians 1

Verses 7–14 · ESV · Voices

After the blessing of election (vv. 3–6, already live), Paul names redemption by Christ’s blood, the revealed mystery to unite all things in Him, the inheritance predestined by God’s counsel, and the Spirit’s seal as guarantee.

Ephesians 1:7

7In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses, according to the riches of his grace,

Arminian

“In him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses.” Wesley’s accent is pastoral and sequential: we who believe have, from the moment we believe, redemption from guilt and power, through what Christ has done and suffered.

Classical Arminians locate this “have” in living union entered by faith. The riches of grace are abundant overflowings of free mercy—sincerely offered, sufficient, received when sinners trust the blood. Forgiveness is not a distant idea; it is present pardon in Christ.

Reformed

Redemption is “in him,” through His blood, according to the riches of His grace. Calvin directs the mind to the blood as the means of restored favor: redemption is immediately styled the forgiveness of sins.

Reformed comfort: the cross actually obtains what it intends. Grace here is effectual for those united to Christ; forgiveness is blood-bought standing in the Son. The riches are the Father’s, already at work where He has placed His people.

Ephesians 1:8

8which he lavished upon us, in all wisdom and insight

Arminian

The grace of v. 7 is not stingy. God lavished it—poured it out—in all wisdom and insight. The manner of giving matches the Giver: wise, insightful, abundant.

Arminian note: lavish grace is enabling and sufficient. Wisdom and insight are given so that people may truly understand and respond. Overflowing mercy does not cancel the call to receive; it makes receiving possible and joyful.

Reformed

Lavished grace in all wisdom and insight names both quantity and quality. God does not drip mercy; He pours it with perfect understanding of His purpose.

Reformed accent: the wisdom is God’s, not ours as the source. What He lavishes He has planned; insight belongs first to the Counselor who redeems, then to those He enlightens in Christ.

Ephesians 1:9

9making known to us the mystery of his will, according to his purpose, which he set forth in Christ

Arminian

God makes known the mystery of His will—revelation, not guesswork—according to His purpose set forth in Christ. The secret is now gospel: Christ is the place where God’s intention is displayed.

Classical Arminians hear a purpose that is Christ-shaped and sincerely published. Making known implies hearers who can understand and believe. The mystery unveiled is an invitation into the purpose, not a locked list withheld from the church.

Reformed

The mystery of His will is made known according to His purpose, set forth in Christ. Revelation serves a prior purpose; Christ is where that purpose was always going.

Reformed reading: “His will” and “His purpose” are not tentative. God discloses what He has determined to do in the Son. The church’s knowledge is gift, and the purpose stands in Christ whether or not every hearer welcomes it.

Ephesians 1:10

10as a plan for the fullness of time, to unite all things in Christ, things in heaven and things on earth in him.

Arminian

The plan aims at the fullness of time: to unite all things in Christ, heaven and earth. Cosmic reconciliation is Christ-centered; nothing is finally left outside His summing-up.

Arminian pastoral weight: “all things” displays the wide scope of Christ’s work and the sincere reach of the gospel. Uniting is not automatic universalism; it is God’s purpose in the Son, received by faith, awaiting the day’s completion.

Reformed

The plan for the fullness of time is to sum up all things in Christ—heaven and earth in Him. History has a Head; the end is not accident.

Reformed accent: this is God’s plan, set forth in Christ, certain of fulfillment. Cosmic unity under the Son comforts the church: powers in heaven and earth are not rivals to the purpose already in motion.

Ephesians 1:11

11In him we have obtained an inheritance, having been predestined according to the purpose of him who works all things according to the counsel of his will,

Arminian

“In him we have obtained an inheritance, having been predestined according to the purpose of him who works all things according to the counsel of his will.” Inheritance belongs to those in Christ; predestination marks the destiny of the believing people.

Classical Arminians confess that God works all things and that His counsel is wise. They typically read the predestining here as the foreordained inheritance of those who are in the Son by grace-enabled faith—not an unconditional selection that would render the gospel offer insincere. Counsel does not cancel resistible grace.

Reformed

In Christ the people have obtained an inheritance because they were predestined according to the purpose of Him who works all things according to the counsel of His will. The counsel is comprehensive; the predestination is the ground of the inheritance.

Reformed reading: “all things” and “counsel of his will” name effectual sovereignty, not mere foresight. Those in Him were appointed to this inheritance by God’s purpose; faith is the fruit of that counsel, not its condition. Assurance rests here: the will that works all things will not drop the inheritance.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take “predestined according to the purpose of him who works all things according to the counsel of his will” as God’s appointed destiny for those in Christ by faith—counsel that enables and does not coerce. Reformed readings take the same counsel as God’s effectual purpose that works all things, including who obtains the inheritance; faith follows the counsel rather than founding it.

Ephesians 1:12

12so that we who were the first to hope in Christ might be to the praise of his glory.

Arminian

The aim is doxology: “we who were the first to hope in Christ” exist for the praise of His glory. Jewish believers (or the first generation) hoped first; hope is real trust in the Messiah.

Arminian note: hoping in Christ is a living act grace makes possible. Praise rises from people who have actually hoped—not from a purpose that bypasses response. Glory is magnified when hope is sincere and shared.

Reformed

The first to hope in Christ are appointed to the praise of His glory. Doxology is the end of predestination, not an afterthought.

Reformed comfort: hope itself is located in God’s purpose (v. 11). Those who hoped first were not the authors of their privilege; they exist for His praise. Glory is safe because it rests on His counsel.

Ephesians 1:13

13In him you also, when you heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation, and believed in him, were sealed with the promised Holy Spirit,

Arminian

“In him you also, when you heard the word of truth, the gospel of your salvation, and believed in him, were sealed with the promised Holy Spirit.” The order on the page is pastoral: hearing the gospel, believing in Him, then sealing.

Classical Arminians hear a real sequence. The word of truth is sincerely preached; faith is a genuine response to enabling grace; the Spirit then seals those who have believed. Sealing confirms; it does not replace the call to trust the gospel when it is heard.

Reformed

Gentile readers (“you also”) are in Him: they heard the gospel, believed, and were sealed with the promised Spirit. The historical order of hearing and believing is the way God brings His people into possession of what He purposed.

Reformed reading: sealing is God’s mark and gift, not a human achievement. Hearing and believing are the appointed means; the Spirit’s seal is the Father’s own stamp on those united to Christ. Faith is real, and it is the fruit of effectual grace, not the cause of the union.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically stress the sequence—heard, believed, then sealed—as evidence that faith is a real, grace-enabled condition before the Spirit’s seal. Reformed readings typically treat hearing and believing as God’s appointed means, with the seal confirming those whom He has united to Christ by effectual grace; faith is the result, not the cause, of that union.

Ephesians 1:14

14who is the guarantee of our inheritance until we acquire possession of it, to the praise of his glory.

Arminian

The Spirit is the guarantee—the down payment—of our inheritance until we acquire possession of it, again to the praise of His glory. Present seal pledges future fullness.

Arminian pastoral note: the down payment is real and generous. It assures those who remain in Christ by the faith the gospel first evoked. Glory will be praised when the inheritance is fully possessed; until then the seal both comforts and calls.

Reformed

The Spirit is God’s own pledge until the inheritance is fully possessed. The same doxology of v. 12 closes the unit: praise of His glory.

Reformed comfort: a divine guarantee cannot finally fail. The Spirit who seals will see the people through to possession. Assurance is not arrogance; it is trust in the God whose counsel (v. 11) and pledge (v. 14) agree.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on redemption by Christ’s blood, the mystery that unites all things in Him, the inheritance predestined by God’s counsel, and the Spirit’s seal—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Ephesians 1:7–14.

Arminian Notable voices

“By whom we — Who believe. Have — From the moment we believe. Redemption — From the guilt and power of sin. Through his blood — Through what he hath done and suffered for us. According to the riches of his grace — According to the abundant overflowings of his free mercy and favour.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Eph 1:7)

Reformed Notable voices

“By his death he has restored us to favor with the Father; and therefore we ought always to direct our minds to the blood of Christ, as the means by which we obtain divine grace. After mentioning that, through the blood of Christ, we obtain redemption, he immediately styles it the forgiveness of sins.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Ephesians (on Eph 1:7)
