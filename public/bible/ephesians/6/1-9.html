Passage

Ephesians 6

Verses 1–9 · ESV · Voices

Paul’s household code for children and parents, then bondservants and masters—honor and obedience in the Lord, fathers warned not to provoke, masters told to stop threatening, because heaven’s Master shows no partiality.

Ephesians 6:1

1Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right.

Arminian

Children are called to obey “in the Lord, for this is right.” The qualifier is pastoral, not ornamental: obedience is for Christ’s sake and within what is lawful. Classical Arminians hear a real moral summons—grace enables honor, yet the command is never a blank check for parental sin. A child is not required to follow a parent into evil. The tone is formation, not fear: what God asks is fitting, a sincere response to those who gave them life.

Reformed

Obedience is the chief evidence of the honor God commands. The human heart recoils from subjection, so Paul presses the harder part. “In the Lord” locates the duty under God’s own authority: parents are to be obeyed only so far as is consistent with piety to God, which comes first. The appointment is divine, not merely cultural; yet that same appointment forbids treating a parent’s will as absolute. Rightness is grounded in God’s command, whose will is the rule of goodness.

Key contrast Both bound obedience by loyalty to God, not by unchecked parental power. Arminians typically stress a grace-enabled, responsible honor that remains “in all things lawful”; Reformed readers typically stress God’s appointed order, with “in the Lord” as the piety that both grounds and limits the child’s duty.

Ephesians 6:2

2"Honor your father and mother" (this is the first commandment with a promise),

Arminian

Honor means love, reverence, obedience, and assistance—not mere ceremony. The mother is named because she is more easily slighted. The fifth commandment’s attached promise seasons the duty with hope. Grace trains children, and adult children, to honor both parents as a sincere act toward God; the command still asks for real love, not a grudging show.

Reformed

Paul cites the Decalogue to show that household honor is worship-shaped. The unique attached promise is meant to make obedience cheerful. Honor includes the whole of sincere affection and support; ceremony without obedience is empty before God. The commandment stands inside Christ’s household: God Himself has sealed this duty with a remarkable promise.

Ephesians 6:3

3"that it may go well with you and that you may live long in the land."

Arminian

The promise of well-being and long life is usually fulfilled to the dutiful, and under the gospel is to be read chiefly in a more exalted, spiritual sense. Grace that enables honor also invites hope: God is not stingy with those who take His household seriously. The land-word is not a bargain that buys salvation, but a Father’s encouragement to walk in what is right.

Reformed

Long life belongs among God’s gifts. Paul leaves out Canaan’s special mention because the same divine blessing now extends to the whole world. The reward is fitting: those who honor the parents from whom they received life are assured that in this life it will be well with them. Providence, not superstition, stands behind the promise.

Ephesians 6:4

4Fathers, do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord.

Arminian

Fathers are named as more apt to be stern; mothers are not excused. “Do not provoke” forbids needless exasperation. Bringing children up is to be with tenderness and mildness, in the Lord’s discipline and instruction—Christian knowledge and practice together. Enabling grace makes gentle authority possible; parents remain accountable not to crush the little ones entrusted to them. This verse will not baptize harshness as “discipline.”

Reformed

Unreasonable severity excites hatred and makes children throw off the yoke; writing to the Colossians, Paul adds “lest they be discouraged.” Bring-up carries gentleness, yet “the instruction and reproof of the Lord” guards against spoiling. God orders the household so that parental power is real and bounded: mild, considerate, aimed at the fear of the Lord—not tyranny, and not neglect.

Key contrast Both refuse abusive or embittering fatherhood. Arminians typically stress grace-enabled tenderness and the parent’s real responsibility not to provoke; Reformed readers typically stress God-ordered headship that includes both restraint of severity and faithful discipline in the Lord.

Ephesians 6:5

5Bondservants, obey your earthly masters with fear and trembling, with a sincere heart, as you would Christ,

Arminian

Paul addresses bondservants inside a first-century structure he does not baptize as God’s ideal. “Earthly masters” are “according to the flesh”—afterward the servant is free from his master. Fear and trembling with a sincere heart are offered as to Christ: integrity before God, not a theology of human ownership. This verse must never be used to excuse cruelty, trafficking, or abuse. Grace enables honest work unto Christ even in constrained callings; it does not make oppression righteous.

Reformed

The servants in view were slaves, not hired hands, and some imagined the gospel had already purchased earthly freedom. Paul relocates service under Christ so that sincerity, not mere dread of punishment, marks Christian servants. “According to the flesh” softens the harsh aspect by reminding them that spiritual freedom remains untouched. The text regulates a then-present institution under Christ’s claim; it does not declare that institution the gospel’s goal, and verse 9 will hang impartial judgment over every master.

Key contrast Both refuse to turn this verse into a charter for abuse. Arminians typically stress sincere, grace-enabled service to Christ within a fallen structure that is not itself the gospel’s aim; Reformed readers typically stress the Lord’s sovereign claim on the worker’s heart and the relative, non-ultimate status of earthly masters under providence—while still insisting those masters will answer to God.

Ephesians 6:6

6not by the way of eye-service, as people-pleasers, but as bondservants of Christ, doing the will of God from the heart,

Arminian

Eye-service and people-pleasing are named as the counterfeit. Bondservants of Christ do God’s will from the heart, with their might, whether watched or unseen. Enabling grace makes inward honesty possible; the command still asks for a real, undivided heart. God is not impressed by performance staged for human eyes.

Reformed

Flattery while the master watches, and contempt when his back is turned, is the old disease of forced service. God requires truth and sincerity of heart: when they serve faithfully they obey God, who sees the hidden life. Eye-service is a species of unbelief—living as if only man were watching.

Ephesians 6:7

7rendering service with a good will as to the Lord and not to man,

Arminian

Good will “as to the Lord and not to man” turns common labor into a sacrifice. Have an eye to Him in all things, even as if there were no other master. Grace invites a glad offering of ordinary work; believers remain responsible not to let resentment become the real master of the task.

Reformed

Good will is contrasted with the suppressed indignation that swells when authority is only endured. The Lord is the true audience, so service need not wait on a worthy earthly master to become worship. Providence does not make cruelty good; it does mean the believer’s labor can still be rendered unto Christ.

Ephesians 6:8

8knowing that whatever good anyone does, this he will receive back from the Lord, whether he is a bondservant or is free.

Arminian

Whatever good anyone does, the Lord will repay—whether bondservant or free. Status does not lock anyone out of God’s recompense. Classical Arminians hear a real reward for real obedience, not a purchase of salvation: grace saves, and the Lord still notices faithful good. The line comforts the unseen laborer and warns against despair that effort is wasted on ungrateful people.

Reformed

However unworthy, ungrateful, or cruel the master, God accepts the service as rendered to Himself. No distinction is made between slave and free; the world sets little value on a slave’s labor, but God esteems it as highly as a king’s duties. Outward station is thrown aside; each is judged according to the uprightness of the heart. Reward is the Lord’s to give.

Key contrast Both confess impartial recompense from the Lord. Arminians typically hold the reward together with grace-enabled, responsible faithfulness; Reformed readers typically stress God’s certain, sovereign estimate that ignores worldly rank and will not let hidden good be lost.

Ephesians 6:9

9Masters, do the same to them, and stop your threatening, knowing that he who is both their Master and yours is in heaven, and that there is no partiality with him.

Arminian

Masters must “do the same”—act from the same Christ-ward principle—and stop threatening: gentleness and humanity, not a harsh or domineering way. Heaven’s Master is theirs too, and there is no partiality with Him. This verse is a warning to the powerful, not a tool for the powerful. Grace that saves the master also summons him to put away intimidation; abuse of household or workplace authority is sin under an impartial Judge.

Reformed

Civil codes once granted masters vast power; many treated cruelty as lawful because the law allowed it. Even without royal restraints, God allows masters no power beyond the law of love. Threatenings and barbarity arise when servants are treated as cattle. They have the same Master in heaven, to whom they must render account; God is no respecter of persons, and the cause of the meanest will not be less regarded than that of the loftiest monarch. Rank will not shield the abuser at Christ’s tribunal.

Key contrast Both make verse 9 a bridle on power. Arminians typically stress a sincere, grace-enabled duty of gentleness that masters may obey or refuse—and will answer for; Reformed readers typically stress God’s impartial, sovereign judgment that already relativizes earthly rank and will repay without respect of persons.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Paul’s household code for children and parents, then bondservants and masters—honor and obedience in the Lord, with masters warned and no partiality in heaven—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Ephesians 6:1–9.

Arminian Notable voices

“Children, obey your parents - In all things lawful. The will of the parent is a law to the child. In the Lord - For his sake. For this is right - Manifestly just and reasonable. And, ye fathers - Mothers are included; but fathers are named, as being more apt to be stern and severe. Provoke not your children to wrath - Do not needlessly fret or exasperate them. But bring them up - With all tenderness and mildness. In the instruction and discipline of the Lord - Both in Christian knowledge and practice. Do the same things to them - That is, act toward them from the same principle. Forbearing threatening - Behaving with gentleness and humanity, not in a harsh or domineering way.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Eph 6:1–9)

Reformed Notable voices

“Hence it follows, that parents are to be obeyed, so far only as is consistent with piety to God, which comes first in order. Parents, on the other hand, are exhorted not to irritate their children by unreasonable severity. This would excite hatred, and would lead them to throw off the yoke altogether. But though no royal edicts had ever been issued for the protection of slaves, God allows to masters no power over them beyond what is consistent with the law of love. Paul here reminds them, that, while masters possess authority over their servants, they have themselves the same Master in heaven, to whom they must render an account. God is no respecter of persons, and the cause of the meanest man will not be a whit less regarded by him than that of the loftiest monarch.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Ephesians (on Eph 6:1–9)
