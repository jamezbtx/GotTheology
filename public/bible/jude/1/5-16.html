Passage

Jude 1

Verses 5–16 · WEB

Jude recalls judgments on unbelief and rebellion, then exposes the destructive character of the intruders. Israel’s deliverance, rebellious angels, and Sodom show that privilege cannot shelter persistent wickedness. His vivid images warn the church to discern fruit rather than trust impressive claims. Both traditions take these warnings seriously while differing over their relation to final apostasy.

Jude 1:5

5Now I desire to remind you, though you already know this, that the Lord, having saved a people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed those who didn’t believe.

Arminian

The people were delivered from Egypt, yet unbelief brought destruction. Arminian readers see a sobering pattern for Christians: prior experience of God’s rescue must not become an excuse to abandon faith. The corporate deliverance is clear; the verse does not describe every individual’s inward spiritual history.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish Israel’s corporate rescue from the saving faith of every person within it. Outward covenant privilege does not guarantee final salvation. The warning is addressed seriously to the church and serves to awaken faith rather than permit presumption.

Key contrast Arminian readers see the pattern as warning believers against a genuinely possible fall from faith. Reformed readers emphasize that shared outward deliverance does not establish that those destroyed were regenerate.

Jude 1:6

6Angels who didn’t keep their first domain, but deserted their own dwelling place, he has kept in everlasting bonds under darkness for the judgment of the great day.

Arminian

Angels who abandoned their appointed place are now kept for judgment. Arminian readers see rebellion bringing real accountability even among exalted creatures. Jude’s point is the danger of rejecting God’s order, not a full account of angelic freedom or the details of their original offense.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see that creaturely greatness offers no immunity from God’s judgment. The contrast between their failure to keep their place and God’s keeping them in bonds exposes the folly of rebellion. Speculation about angelic history should not displace Jude’s warning.

Jude 1:7

7Even as Sodom and Gomorrah and the cities around them, having in the same way as these given themselves over to sexual immorality and gone after strange flesh, are shown as an example, suffering the punishment of eternal fire.

Arminian

Sodom and its neighboring cities illustrate judgment on sexual immorality and transgressive desire. Arminian readers see God’s holiness confronting conduct that the intruders excuse. The warning calls for repentance rather than treating grace as protection from the consequences of persistent evil.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that divine grace does not suspend God’s moral judgment. Sodom provides a public example of the seriousness of sexual rebellion and coming punishment. Jude’s comparison condemns the teachers’ conduct rather than inviting the church to imagine itself beyond correction.

Jude 1:8

8Yet in the same way, these also in their dreaming defile the flesh, despise authority, and slander celestial beings.

Arminian

The intruders repeat earlier patterns through bodily defilement, contempt for authority, and reckless slander. Arminian readers see claimed spiritual experience failing the test of obedient conduct. Dreams or private impressions cannot justify behavior that contradicts devotion to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers similarly judge spiritual claims by their fruit rather than their impressiveness. The exact identity of the celestial beings is debated, but Jude’s charge is clear: these people speak arrogantly about realities they do not rightly understand.

Jude 1:9

9But Michael, the archangel, when contending with the devil and arguing about the body of Moses, dared not bring against him an abusive condemnation, but said, “May the Lord rebuke you!”

Arminian

Michael’s restraint contrasts with the intruders’ abusive speech. Arminian readers see an example of entrusting judgment to the Lord rather than claiming authority to pronounce it independently. Jude does not explain all the circumstances of the dispute, so the emphasis should remain on humility.

Reformed

Reformed readers also stress Michael’s dependence on the Lord’s rebuke. Even an archangel does not treat authority as permission for reckless condemnation. The account restrains spiritual arrogance; its unexplained background should not be expanded into a confident reconstruction of events.

Jude 1:10

10But these speak evil of whatever things they don’t know. They are destroyed in these things that they understand naturally, like the creatures without reason.

Arminian

The teachers insult what they do not understand and are ruined through the appetites they follow. Arminian readers see responsibility for refusing reverent understanding and yielding to desire. Jude contrasts their spiritual pretensions with conduct governed by instinct rather than faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ignorance joined to moral corruption, not merely lack of information. Their claims to insight conceal bondage to appetite. Both traditions recognize that genuine spiritual understanding produces humility and holiness, not contempt for whatever challenges personal desire.

Jude 1:11

11Woe to them! For they went in the way of Cain, and ran riotously in the error of Balaam for hire, and perished in Korah’s rebellion.

Arminian

Cain, Balaam, and Korah supply patterns of wickedness, greed, and rebellion. Arminian readers see choices with destructive consequences, warning the church not to follow corrupt examples. The pursuit of gain can make religious leadership a path away from God rather than service to others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the intruders repeating familiar forms of opposition to God. Jude speaks of their end with certainty to expose the seriousness of their present conduct. Religious association and apparent leadership cannot make greed or rebellion safe.

Jude 1:12

12These are hidden rocky reefs in your love feasts when they feast with you, shepherds who without fear feed themselves; clouds without water, carried along by winds; autumn trees without fruit, twice dead, plucked up by the roots;

Arminian

The intruders share the community’s meals but endanger it rather than nourish it. Arminian readers see the need to test participation by faithful fruit. The image of dead, uprooted trees warns of ruin; it should not be pressed into a complete sequence of salvation, loss, and restoration.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish presence at the church’s table from living faith. Self-feeding shepherds and fruitless trees reveal the emptiness of the intruders’ ministry. The images expose spiritual barrenness without requiring every detail to function as a technical description of regeneration.

Jude 1:13

13wild waves of the sea, foaming out their own shame; wandering stars, for whom the blackness of darkness has been reserved forever.

Arminian

Restless waves and wandering stars portray shame, disorder, and a fearful destination. Arminian readers see persistent ungodliness moving toward judgment, not freedom. Jude’s severe language serves the pastoral purpose of preventing the community from being drawn into the same destructive course.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a stark contrast between the intruders’ claims to leadership and their inability to guide anyone safely. Reserved darkness assures the church that evil will not finally escape God’s judgment. Their impressive activity cannot replace the light of faithful obedience.

Jude 1:14

14About these also Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied, saying, “Behold, the Lord came with ten thousands of his holy ones,

Arminian

Jude presents Enoch’s prophecy as a witness to the Lord’s coming judgment. Arminian readers see the present teachers included within this warning against ungodliness. The focus is God’s public reckoning, not an invitation to construct a chronology from the brief prophetic scene.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Lord arriving with his holy ones to expose and judge evil. Jude’s appeal reinforces the continuity of God’s opposition to wickedness. The verse supplies a warning for the church rather than grounds for speculation about Enoch’s experiences.

Jude 1:15

15to execute judgment on all, and to convict all the ungodly of all their works of ungodliness which they have done in an ungodly way, and of all the hard things which ungodly sinners have spoken against him.”

Arminian

Judgment reaches both ungodly deeds and defiant speech against the Lord. Arminian readers note that accountability concerns actual rebellion, not an arbitrary accusation. The repeated emphasis on ungodliness exposes how badly the intruders have misrepresented grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the final judgment publicly reveals the justice of God’s verdict. Both actions and words display hostility toward him. Grace forgives and transforms sinners; it never makes contempt for the Lord morally harmless.

Jude 1:16

16These are murmurers and complainers, walking after their lusts—and their mouth speaks proud things—showing respect of persons to gain advantage.

Arminian

Grumbling, self-indulgence, boasting, and flattery reveal the intruders’ practical character. Arminian readers see habits that undermine love and faithfulness within the church. Discerning error therefore requires attention not only to statements of belief but also to the use of other people.

Reformed

Reformed readers similarly see proud speech serving personal appetite and advantage. Flattery becomes manipulation rather than love of neighbor. Both traditions recognize that faithful ministry seeks the good of others instead of exploiting relationships for status or gain.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
