Passage

Jude 1

Verses 17–25 · WEB

After describing the intruders, Jude turns to the beloved community: remember the apostles, build one another up, pray, remain in God’s love, and rescue those in danger. The letter closes by praising God’s power to preserve and present his people faultless. Human vigilance and divine keeping belong together, though the traditions explain their relationship differently.

Jude 1:17

17But you, beloved, remember the words which have been spoken before by the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Jude turns from the intruders to the beloved community and calls them to remember apostolic teaching. Arminian readers see remembrance as an active means of remaining faithful. The church need not be destabilized by behavior about which it has already been warned.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the apostles’ teaching as the community’s stable guide amid deceptive voices. Remembering is part of the ordinary care through which God preserves his people. The answer to novelty and division is renewed attention to Christ’s authorized witnesses.

Jude 1:18

18They said to you, “In the last time there will be mockers, walking after their own ungodly lusts.”

Arminian

The apostles warned that mockers would follow ungodly desires in the last time. Arminian readers see this prediction as a call to vigilance, not fatalism. Knowing that mockery will occur does not require anyone to join it or excuse those who do.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the warning as preparing the church for opposition rather than making opposition harmless. Mockery expresses desire-driven resistance to God. Both traditions understand the prediction to strengthen discernment and perseverance, not curiosity about dates.

Jude 1:19

19These are those who cause divisions and are sensual, not having the Spirit.

Arminian

The divisive teachers do not have the Spirit despite their claims. Arminian readers recognize that the present description excludes them from Spirit-led life; Jude does not explicitly narrate their earlier spiritual condition. The church must not mistake divisive self-assertion for spiritual maturity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a contrast between outward association with believers and possession of the Spirit. Division and merely natural impulses expose the teachers’ claims as empty. Their present lack of the Spirit does not require reconstructing an otherwise unstated conversion history.

Jude 1:20

20But you, beloved, keep building up yourselves on your most holy faith, praying in the Holy Spirit.

Arminian

Believers are to build one another up in their shared holy faith and pray in the Spirit. Arminian readers see grace-enabled practices through which faith is nourished. Spiritual perseverance is communal and prayerful, not a solitary achievement or a demand to sustain oneself without God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see mutual edification and Spirit-directed prayer as means of God’s preserving grace. Divine keeping does not make these practices unnecessary; it gives them their foundation. Both traditions encourage prayer shaped by the gospel rather than spiritual display.

Jude 1:21

21Keep yourselves in God’s love, looking for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ to eternal life.

Arminian

Keeping themselves in God’s love means continuing in the life of trust and obedience nourished by faith and prayer. Arminian readers see a genuine responsibility that can be neglected. Eternal life remains awaited mercy from Christ, never a wage earned by sufficient vigilance.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand the command as remaining in the enjoyment and expression of God’s love, not keeping his eternal purpose from failing. Believers really act, and God sustains that action through faith, prayer, and hope. Christ’s mercy, not their performance, brings eternal life.

Key contrast Both affirm active perseverance sustained by grace. Arminian readers see remaining as a condition believers may abandon; Reformed readers see the command as a means by which God ensures his people continue.

Jude 1:22

22On some have compassion, making a distinction,

Arminian

The community must respond to people in danger with discriminating compassion. Arminian readers see mercy rather than quick dismissal as the proper response to those affected by error. In the WEB wording, making a distinction calls for attention to differing needs and situations.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see pastoral discernment accompanying compassion. Contending for truth does not eliminate patient care for people unsettled by false teaching. The church should distinguish the vulnerable from the corrupting influences without abandoning mercy toward either.

Jude 1:23

23and some save, snatching them out of the fire with fear, hating even the clothing stained by the flesh.

Arminian

Some need urgent rescue, but helpers must not become entangled in the sin they oppose. Arminian readers see compassionate intervention as one way God works through believers to turn others from ruin. Hatred belongs to defilement, not contempt for the person being helped.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God using the church’s warnings and care as instruments of rescue. Fear acknowledges the seriousness of sin and the helper’s own vulnerability. Both traditions hold together mercy toward endangered people and refusal to accommodate the evil harming them.

Jude 1:24

24Now to him who is able to keep them from stumbling, and to present you faultless before the presence of his glory in great joy,

Arminian

Jude ends by praising God’s ability to prevent stumbling and bring his people joyfully into his presence. Arminian readers find strong assurance in this sufficient preserving grace while reading it alongside the command to remain in God’s love. Preservation is God’s gift, not independent human endurance.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the doxology as assurance that the God who calls also preserves and finally presents his people faultless. The exhortations are not competitors to his keeping but instruments of it. The focus shifts from the intruders’ danger to God’s power and promised glory.

Key contrast Arminian readers affirm God’s full ability to preserve while allowing that believers can reject sustaining grace. Reformed readers understand this praise within the letter’s assurance that God will bring his true people safely into glory.

Jude 1:25

25to God our Savior, who alone is wise, be glory and majesty, dominion and power, both now and forever. Amen.

Arminian

The final word is worship: salvation, wisdom, glory, and authority belong to God. Arminian readers see all boasting excluded, since even faithful endurance depends on the Savior’s mercy. Jude closes in confidence and praise rather than leaving his readers preoccupied with threatening teachers.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s saving majesty as the proper ground of the church’s confidence. His dominion extends beyond the present conflict into eternity. Both traditions end where Jude ends, honoring the Savior rather than celebrating a theological party’s superiority.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
