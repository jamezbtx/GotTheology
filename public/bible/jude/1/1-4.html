Passage

Jude 1

Verses 1–4 · WEB

Jude addresses a community kept for Jesus Christ but threatened by teachers who turn grace into permission for immorality. His planned celebration of their shared salvation becomes an urgent appeal to defend the faith entrusted to God’s people. Both traditions affirm that grace leads to holiness, not denial of Christ’s authority.

Jude 1:1

1Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James, to those who are called, sanctified by God the Father, and kept for Jesus Christ:

Arminian

Jude identifies himself as Christ’s servant and describes his readers through God’s gracious action. Arminian readers see calling and sanctification as gifts received through grace-enabled faith, with divine keeping providing real security. The later command to remain in God’s love keeps this assurance connected to continuing trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the assurance of God’s effective calling and preserving care. The community belongs to the Father and is kept for Christ, not secured by its own strength. The later exhortations describe the means through which God sustains the people he has called.

Key contrast Both ground security in God’s grace. Arminian readers understand keeping in relation to continuing faith; Reformed readers see God’s saving call as ensuring the perseverance of those truly called.

Jude 1:2

2May mercy, peace, and love be multiplied to you.

Arminian

Mercy, peace, and love are blessings Jude desires to increase among his readers. Arminian readers see believers as continually dependent on God’s generosity, not merely on a past experience. A church confronting error needs this gracious provision as much as it needs courage.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see ongoing dependence on God rather than self-sufficiency. Multiplying mercy, peace, and love equips the church to face conflict without becoming harsh or fearful. Jude’s warning grows out of pastoral concern for the people God loves.

Jude 1:3

3Beloved, while I was very eager to write to you about our common salvation, I was constrained to write to you exhorting you to contend earnestly for the faith which was once for all delivered to the saints.

Arminian

The faith is a received message shared by all the saints, not a private spiritual invention. Arminian readers understand contending as faithful witness and holy living sustained by grace. Defending the gospel includes resisting teaching that separates salvation from allegiance to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the church receives rather than creates the apostolic faith. Its once-for-all delivery calls for steadfast teaching and obedience, not constant reinvention. Contending earnestly does not authorize personal hostility; it protects the common salvation Jude wanted to celebrate.

Jude 1:4

4For there are certain men who crept in secretly, even those who were long ago written about for this condemnation: ungodly men, turning the grace of our God into indecency, and denying our only Master, God, and Lord, Jesus Christ.

Arminian

The intruders misuse grace and deny Christ’s mastery. Arminian readers commonly understand their condemnation as foretold in earlier warnings against ungodliness, rather than as an unconditional decree causing their unbelief. Their conduct is culpable precisely because grace never licenses the evil they practice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the intruders’ judgment as no surprise to God: Scripture has already marked out such rebellion for condemnation. Some relate this language to God’s decree, while others emphasize prior prophetic warning. Neither reading makes God the author of their sin or removes their responsibility.

Key contrast Arminian readers emphasize a previously announced judgment on ungodliness. Reformed readings may also relate that certainty to divine ordination, though the phrase itself is read with differing emphases within the tradition.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
