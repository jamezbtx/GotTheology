Passage

Luke 21

Verses 1–19 · WEB

A widow’s costly gift follows the warning about those who devour widows’ houses, before Jesus predicts the temple’s ruin and prepares his followers for deception and persecution. Both traditions value sincere devotion, Christ’s provision for witness, and enduring faith rather than institutional security. Readings focused on Jerusalem’s fall in AD 70 and readings emphasizing future fulfillment must be distinguished from the Arminian/Reformed comparison; neither tradition has one required prophetic timetable.

Luke 21:1

1He looked up and saw the rich people who were putting their gifts into the treasury.

Arminian

Jesus notices gifts that others might measure only by size. Arminian readers emphasize that faithful stewardship concerns the heart’s response to God, not impressive resources.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ discerning the giver beyond outward appearances. Wealth and visible generosity cannot establish anyone’s righteousness before God.

Luke 21:2

2He saw a certain poor widow casting in two small brass coins.

Arminian

The poor widow is visible to Jesus even when her gift appears insignificant. Arminian readers see encouragement for humble trust without romanticizing deprivation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize Christ’s attention to the vulnerable. The small offering challenges assessments of spiritual worth based on social standing or financial power.

Luke 21:3

3He said, “Truly I tell you, this poor widow put in more than all of them,

Arminian

Jesus reverses an assessment based simply on monetary totals. Arminian readers see wholehearted devotion valued above religious display, not a formula for purchasing divine favor.

Reformed

Reformed readers share the distinction between outward amount and inward devotion. Christ’s judgment exposes the inadequacy of treating religious generosity as a measure of merit.

Luke 21:4

4for all these put in gifts for God from their abundance, but she, out of her poverty, put in all that she had to live on.”

Arminian

Her gift involves her whole livelihood, unlike surplus giving. Arminian readers recognize costly devotion without turning this observation into a command to leave vulnerable people destitute.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the cost seriously, especially after the warning about exploiting widows. The passage should not be used to pressure poor believers into financial insecurity.

Luke 21:5

5As some were talking about the temple and how it was decorated with beautiful stones and gifts, he said,

Arminian

Admiration for the temple’s beauty prepares for Jesus’ unsettling warning. Arminian readers distinguish reverence for God from misplaced confidence in impressive religious structures.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise resist identifying architectural grandeur with spiritual security. Sacred surroundings cannot shield a community from the searching judgment of Christ.

Luke 21:6

6“As for these things which you see, the days will come in which there will not be left here one stone on another that will not be thrown down.”

Arminian

Jesus foretells the temple’s devastation rather than guaranteeing its permanence. Arminian readers hear a warning against resting spiritual confidence in institutions instead of God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ’s authority over the temple’s future. This prediction concerns judgment on a structure and does not itself define a doctrine of individual election.

Luke 21:7

7They asked him, “Teacher, so when will these things be? What is the sign that these things are about to happen?”

Arminian

The question asks about timing and signs of the predicted events. Arminian readers should let Jesus’ answer govern their expectations rather than impose a preferred timetable.

Reformed

Reformed readers share the need for patient attention to the question’s setting. Chronological interpretations vary within both traditions and are not equivalent to their soteriological differences.

Luke 21:8

8He said, “Watch out that you don’t get led astray, for many will come in my name, saying, ‘I am he,’ and, ‘The time is at hand.’ Therefore don’t follow them.

Arminian

Jesus commands discernment against false messiahs and urgent claims about the time. Arminian readers see a real responsibility to resist deception through fidelity to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise treat this warning as protective instruction for disciples. Confidence in God’s care never requires following sensational claims made under Jesus’ name.

Luke 21:9

9When you hear of wars and disturbances, don’t be terrified, for these things must happen first, but the end won’t come immediately.”

Arminian

War and unrest do not justify panic or immediate declarations of the end. Arminian readers find a call to steady trust amid frightening events.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm God’s governance while refusing to make every conflict a prophetic deadline. Both traditions can obey this restraint without sharing one eschatological scheme.

Luke 21:10

10Then he said to them, “Nation will rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom.

Arminian

Jesus prepares disciples for conflict among peoples and kingdoms. Arminian readers see realistic pastoral preparation, not permission to celebrate war or abandon peacemaking.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize that Christ’s disciples live amid upheaval without being ruled by fear. The prediction does not assign divine approval to either side of every conflict.

Luke 21:11

11There will be great earthquakes, famines, and plagues in various places. There will be terrors and great signs from heaven.

Arminian

Disasters and frightening signs expose the fragility of ordinary life. Arminian readers seek faithful readiness rather than assuming every tragedy reveals its victims’ personal guilt.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see reason for sober dependence on God. Neither tradition needs to turn this description into speculative dates or condemnations of particular sufferers.

Luke 21:12

12But before all these things, they will lay their hands on you and will persecute you, delivering you up to synagogues and prisons, bringing you before kings and governors for my name’s sake.

Arminian

Disciples will suffer official and religious opposition because of Jesus’ name. Arminian readers understand costly witness as a setting for grace-enabled fidelity, not proof of divine abandonment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize persecution as compatible with God’s preserving care. The coming trials are occasions for allegiance to Christ rather than evidence that his promises have failed.

Luke 21:13

13It will turn out as a testimony for you.

Arminian

Hostility becomes an opportunity for testimony. Arminian readers see grace enabling meaningful witness within circumstances they would not have chosen for themselves.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God turning opposition into an occasion for the gospel. Suffering need not be sought or called good in itself to become a setting for testimony.

Luke 21:14

14Settle it therefore in your hearts not to meditate beforehand how to answer,

Arminian

Jesus forbids anxious rehearsal in the persecution setting he describes. Arminian readers understand dependence on his help, not a general prohibition on study or thoughtful preparation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish this particular assurance from an excuse for careless teaching. Christ calls threatened witnesses away from self-protective anxiety toward trust in his provision.

Luke 21:15

15for I will give you a mouth and wisdom which all your adversaries will not be able to withstand or to contradict.

Arminian

Christ promises wisdom opponents cannot successfully refute. Arminian readers depend on his gracious help while recognizing that compelling testimony does not guarantee an audience’s conversion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise ground faithful witness in Christ’s provision. The promise concerns the adequacy of his wisdom, not immunity from imprisonment or the death mentioned next.

Luke 21:16

16You will be handed over even by parents, brothers, relatives, and friends. They will cause some of you to be put to death.

Arminian

Even intimate relationships can become sources of betrayal and death. Arminian readers see the painful cost of discipleship without assuming that persecution means grace has withdrawn.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hold this warning together with divine preservation. Christ does not promise bodily survival to every believer, so assurance must reach beyond present safety.

Luke 21:17

17You will be hated by all men for my name’s sake.

Arminian

Hatred arises because of allegiance to Jesus. Arminian readers distinguish suffering for his name from hostility provoked by a disciple’s own cruelty or misconduct.

Reformed

Reformed readers share this distinction and encourage steadfastness under genuine persecution. Being disliked is not automatically evidence of faithfulness; the stated cause is Christ’s name.

Luke 21:18

18And not a hair of your head will perish.

Arminian

Read beside the prediction of deaths, this assurance cannot mean universal bodily immunity. Arminian readers find confidence in God’s ultimate care for those who remain in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand comprehensive divine keeping beyond present suffering. The promise affirms final security in God’s care, not the absence of martyrdom or loss.

Luke 21:19

19“By your endurance you will win your lives.

Arminian

Jesus joins the gaining of life with endurance. Arminian readers emphasize continuing faith sustained by grace, not suffering as payment for salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise insist on perseverance, understanding endurance as the fruit and means of God’s preservation rather than a work that earns life.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
