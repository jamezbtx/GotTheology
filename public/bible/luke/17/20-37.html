Passage

Luke 17

Verses 20–37 · WEB

Jesus answers a question about God’s Kingdom and warns the disciples about longing, deception, sudden judgment, and costly readiness. Noah and Lot illustrate how ordinary life can continue until judgment arrives. Interpretations of the timing and imagery vary within both traditions; millennial positions do not follow simply from the Arminian/Reformed distinction. Verse 36 is not printed in the selected WEB edition, which reports the addition found in some manuscripts only in a footnote, so it is omitted here as in other passages on the site.

Luke 17:20

20Being asked by the Pharisees when God’s Kingdom would come, he answered them, “God’s Kingdom doesn’t come with observation;

Arminian

Jesus challenges the expectation that the Kingdom can be identified through the kind of observation his questioners seek. Arminian readers see a call to attend to God’s present action. The answer does not cancel the later warning about the Son of Man’s unmistakable revelation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish recognition of the Kingdom in Jesus’ presence from attempts to calculate its arrival by visible clues. The present answer and later future warning belong together. The verse does not itself provide a complete scheme of eschatological events.

Luke 17:21

21neither will they say, ‘Look, here!’ or, ‘Look, there!’ for behold, God’s Kingdom is within you.”

Arminian

The WEB renders Jesus’ statement as the Kingdom being within his hearers. Arminian interpreters differ over inward and communal senses, especially because Pharisees are addressed. The verse should not be used to assume that every hearer already possessed saving faith.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters likewise discuss an inward sense and the Kingdom’s presence among the hearers in Jesus’ ministry. The wording does not establish that every individual is regenerate. Both traditions must interpret it with the identity of the questioners and the surrounding dialogue in view.

Luke 17:22

22He said to the disciples, “The days will come when you will desire to see one of the days of the Son of Man, and you will not see it.

Arminian

Jesus prepares disciples for a time of longing when they cannot see what they desire. Arminian readers see encouragement to patient faith rather than pursuit of sensational claims. His absence from sight will not mean that longing itself supplies a reliable guide.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see disciples needing steadiness during a period of waiting. Hope must remain tied to Jesus’ teaching rather than to the force of their desire. This warning concerns faithful expectation, not identification of one tradition with a particular millennial timeline.

Luke 17:23

23They will tell you, ‘Look, here!’ or ‘Look, there!’ Don’t go away or follow after them,

Arminian

Disciples must not chase competing reports about the Son of Man’s location. Arminian readers see responsibility to test claims by Jesus’ instruction. Eagerness for deliverance can become vulnerability to deception when it replaces patient trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ protecting his people through a direct warning against false guidance. Confidence in his return should produce discernment, not credulity. Both traditions reject the idea that every urgent religious announcement deserves obedience.

Luke 17:24

24for as the lightning, when it flashes out of one part under the sky, shines to another part under the sky, so will the Son of Man be in his day.

Arminian

Lightning illustrates the unmistakable character of the Son of Man’s day. Arminian readers see a contrast with secretive reports that require disciples to chase them. The image establishes recognizable manifestation without furnishing a date or resolving every question of prophetic chronology.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the force of the contrast between rumor and open revelation. How this relates to other judgment passages is discussed differently within the tradition. The warning’s practical force does not depend on selecting a millennial position.

Luke 17:25

25But first, he must suffer many things and be rejected by this generation.

Arminian

Suffering and rejection must precede the revelation Jesus describes. Arminian readers see the road to glory passing through the cross rather than avoiding it. The necessity belongs to Jesus’ mission and does not make those who reject him morally innocent.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the cross within the divine purpose while maintaining human responsibility for rejection. The disciples must not expect triumph detached from suffering. Both traditions recognize that Jesus’ coming glory cannot be interpreted apart from his humiliation.

Luke 17:26

26As it was in the days of Noah, even so it will also be in the days of the Son of Man.

Arminian

The days of Noah supply a pattern for the Son of Man’s days. Arminian readers see a warning against complacency before judgment. The comparison asks for attentive trust rather than a detailed attempt to match every feature of the two periods.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see remembered judgment challenging the assumption that ordinary life will continue indefinitely. Noah’s example directs attention to readiness. It does not by itself establish the sequence of a millennium, tribulation, or other disputed events.

Luke 17:27

27They ate, they drank, they married, and they were given in marriage until the day that Noah entered into the ship, and the flood came and destroyed them all.

Arminian

Ordinary activities continued until the flood brought sudden destruction. Arminian readers see the danger of living without heed to God’s warning. Eating and marriage are not condemned in themselves; the issue is unpreparedness when judgment interrupts familiar routines.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see normal life offering no protection against God’s decisive judgment. The comparison exposes complacency rather than forbidding ordinary responsibilities. Both traditions call for faithfulness within daily life instead of treating its apparent stability as ultimate security.

Luke 17:28

28Likewise, even as it was in the days of Lot: they ate, they drank, they bought, they sold, they planted, they built;

Arminian

The example of Lot again lists familiar patterns of daily life. Arminian readers see commerce and cultivation unable to guarantee safety. Jesus is not condemning every ordinary task but warning against a life whose horizon excludes accountability before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the ordinary character of the activities described. Social busyness can conceal spiritual unreadiness. The example directs disciples to discern their dependence on God without assuming that leaving every daily responsibility is itself the essence of faith.

Luke 17:29

29but in the day that Lot went out from Sodom, it rained fire and sulfur from the sky and destroyed them all.

Arminian

Judgment fell on the day Lot departed from Sodom. Arminian readers see the seriousness of responding to God’s warning before the decisive moment. The account should awaken repentance rather than fascination with catastrophe detached from its moral significance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see both rescue and judgment in the same remembered event. God’s intervention exposes the limits of apparent security. Both traditions understand the comparison as a warning to trust divine instruction rather than the permanence of present circumstances.

Luke 17:30

30It will be the same way in the day that the Son of Man is revealed.

Arminian

Jesus applies the examples to the day of the Son of Man’s revelation. Arminian readers see sudden disclosure bringing hidden priorities into the open. The emphasis is readiness; the sentence does not identify a timetable that separates one salvation tradition from the other.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see judgment and revelation interrupting complacency. Interpretations of the passage’s historical and final horizons vary, but the warning remains direct. Disciples should not use disputes over chronology to evade the demand for present faithfulness.

Luke 17:31

31In that day, he who will be on the housetop and his goods in the house, let him not go down to take them away. Let him who is in the field likewise not turn back.

Arminian

The warning not to return for possessions stresses urgent response over attachment to goods. Arminian readers see the need to act on Jesus’ word rather than hesitate for security in property. The scene should be read as a warning, not a universal ban on owning things.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see possessions becoming dangerous when loyalty to them overrides Christ’s instruction. The paired house and field examples underline urgency. Both traditions recognize the need for a heart ready to relinquish what would obstruct obedient trust.

Luke 17:32

32Remember Lot’s wife!

Arminian

The short command recalls the danger of turning back toward what God has judged. Arminian readers see a summons to sustained obedience rather than a momentary beginning. The warning should be heard without constructing an otherwise unstated account of Lot’s wife’s eternal condition.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a concrete warning against divided allegiance. Remembering Lot’s wife is one means of stirring vigilance rather than complacency. The example does not require a complete reconstruction of her inward spiritual history to carry its force.

Luke 17:33

33Whoever seeks to save his life loses it, but whoever loses his life preserves it.

Arminian

Self-protective attachment can destroy the life it tries to secure, while surrender preserves it. Arminian readers see faithfulness requiring trust beyond immediate survival. The warning calls for continuing allegiance, not a calculated payment of suffering in exchange for salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see self-denial as the fruit of trusting Christ above present security. Losing life is not an independent merit that purchases eternal life. Both traditions reject both self-preservation as the highest good and reckless danger-seeking as an end in itself.

Luke 17:34

34I tell you, in that night there will be two people in one bed. One will be taken and the other will be left.

Arminian

People sharing the closest ordinary setting can meet different outcomes. Arminian readers see personal readiness as something proximity cannot supply. Whether being taken denotes rescue or judgment is debated; the verse should not be forced into a confident scenario beyond its stated contrast.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see outward association unable to guarantee the same outcome for every person. Interpretations of taken and left differ and must be considered with the surrounding judgment imagery. This is not an Arminian/Reformed division or a complete rapture chronology.

Luke 17:35

35There will be two grinding grain together. One will be taken and the other will be left.”

Arminian

The shared work of grinding grain does not erase the coming distinction. Arminian readers see the warning reaching everyday labor as well as rest. The point is readiness before God, not a license to identify another person’s final destiny from present circumstances.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see ordinary companionship unable to substitute for a right relation to God. The image continues the warning about differing outcomes. Both traditions should resist making its few details settle every dispute over the order of end-time events.

Luke 17:37

37They, answering, asked him, “Where, Lord?” He said to them, “Where the body is, there the vultures will also be gathered together.”

Arminian

Jesus answers the question about location with an image of a body and gathered vultures. Arminian readers see judgment’s seriousness rather than a map for speculation. Interpretations of the image differ, so its force should not become certainty about geographical details.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise treat the image as a sobering conclusion rather than a simple timetable key. The relation between the saying and being taken is discussed within both traditions. Shared urgency and accountability are clearer than a fully specified account of where every event occurs.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
