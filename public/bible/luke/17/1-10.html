Passage

Luke 17

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Jesus teaches his disciples to avoid causing others to stumble, confront sin, and forgive repeated repentance. Their request for faith leads to instruction about dependence on God and humble service. Both traditions reject the idea that obedience puts God in our debt: faithful service remains a response to grace, not a claim to merit.

Luke 17:1

1He said to the disciples, “It is impossible that no occasions of stumbling should come, but woe to him through whom they come!

Arminian

Jesus warns that stumbling blocks will come without excusing those who cause them. Arminian readers see real accountability for harming another person’s faith. The inevitability of temptation does not cancel the duty to resist it or protect vulnerable disciples.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the certainty that offenses occur from moral permission to commit them. God’s providence never makes harmful conduct innocent. The warning calls the church to protect others rather than hide responsibility behind a general doctrine of divine control.

Luke 17:2

2It would be better for him if a millstone were hung around his neck, and he were thrown into the sea, rather than that he should cause one of these little ones to stumble.

Arminian

The severe image shows how seriously Jesus regards harm to the little ones. Arminian readers see vulnerable disciples as people whose spiritual good demands active care. The warning condemns exploitation; it does not authorize violence or prescribe a human punishment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the gravity of leading vulnerable people into sin. Jesus overturns any tendency to measure wrongdoing only by the offender’s prominence. Those entrusted with influence must use it for protection, not self-serving damage to Christ’s people.

Luke 17:3

3Be careful. If your brother sins against you, rebuke him. If he repents, forgive him.

Arminian

Careful discipleship includes both confronting wrong and forgiving repentance. Arminian readers see love refusing either indifference to sin or permanent hostility toward the repentant. Rebuke aims at restoration rather than humiliation, and forgiveness is not a denial that harm occurred.

Reformed

Reformed readers see truthful correction and generous pardon as complementary duties. Sin must not be ignored, but repentance must not meet a closed door. Both traditions recognize that forgiveness does not require concealing abuse or removing appropriate measures of protection.

Luke 17:4

4If he sins against you seven times in the day, and seven times returns, saying, ‘I repent,’ you shall forgive him.”

Arminian

Repeated repentance must meet repeated forgiveness rather than an exhausted quota of mercy. Arminian readers see a demanding pattern of grace-enabled love. The instruction challenges resentment without requiring a victim to pretend that repeated wrongdoing is harmless.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see mercy reflecting the patience God shows his people. The repeated number emphasizes generosity rather than a limit after which retaliation becomes acceptable. Forgiving a repentant person does not eliminate the need for wisdom and accountability.

Luke 17:5

5The apostles said to the Lord, “Increase our faith.”

Arminian

The apostles ask Jesus for greater faith instead of claiming self-sufficiency. Arminian readers see faith nourished by divine help and actively sought in prayer. Their response to difficult commands acknowledges that obedient love needs more than unaided determination.

Reformed

Reformed readers see dependence on Christ even in the exercise and growth of faith. The request itself models prayer for what God must supply. Both traditions reject the idea that the disciples can fulfill Jesus’ demanding instruction through autonomous moral strength.

Luke 17:6

6The Lord said, “If you had faith like a grain of mustard seed, you would tell this sycamore tree, ‘Be uprooted and be planted in the sea,’ and it would obey you.

Arminian

The tiny seed directs attention away from impressive quantities of faith toward the God trusted. Arminian readers see grace enabling what seems impossible. Jesus is not offering a technique for controlling circumstances or guaranteeing every desired miracle.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the effectiveness of faith resting on God’s power rather than its apparent size. The striking tree image challenges self-reliance. Faith receives and depends; it does not become an independent force with which disciples command God.

Luke 17:7

7But who is there among you, having a servant plowing or keeping sheep, that will say when he comes in from the field, ‘Come immediately and sit down at the table’?

Arminian

Jesus introduces an ordinary master-servant situation to challenge entitlement. Arminian readers see service as an obligation rather than a favor that places the master in debt. The illustration’s immediate purpose is humility, not a comprehensive endorsement of ancient labor arrangements.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to the illustration’s point rather than treating every social detail as a command. Work already performed does not establish a right to special honor. Disciples must not turn obedience into a bargaining position before God.

Luke 17:8

8Wouldn’t he rather tell him, ‘Prepare my supper, clothe yourself properly, and serve me while I eat and drink. Afterward you shall eat and drink’?

Arminian

The servant’s duties continue beyond the day’s fieldwork in the story. Arminian readers see the challenge to self-congratulation after limited obedience. The comparison concerns the disciple’s posture toward God, not permission for human employers to disregard others’ dignity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see comprehensive service exposing the weakness of claims to independent merit. The point is not that God lacks kindness, but that duty cannot be used to demand his favor. Other biblical pictures of generous welcome need not be denied.

Luke 17:9

9Does he thank that servant because he did the things that were commanded? I think not.

Arminian

Completing an assigned duty does not make the master indebted to the servant. Arminian readers see obedience as fitting allegiance rather than a purchase of grace. They affirm that any promised reward rests on God’s generosity, not an achievement beyond dependence on him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise reject the idea that a creature’s obedience puts God under an obligation created by merit. The verse humbles self-righteous calculation. It does not mean that God never commends his servants or graciously rewards faithful service.

Luke 17:10

10Even so you also, when you have done all the things that are commanded you, say, ‘We are unworthy servants. We have done our duty.’ ”

Arminian

Even complete obedience leaves disciples without a claim to self-earned favor. Arminian readers see grace-enabled faithfulness as duty, not surplus merit. Being unworthy servants describes the absence of a claim on God, not a denial that God loves or values his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a clear rejection of boasting in works as a basis for divine acceptance. All service is already owed, and any reward is gracious. Both traditions distinguish humble dependence from the destructive belief that a person has no worth before God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
