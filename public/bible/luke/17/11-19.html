Passage

Luke 17

Verses 11–19 · WEB

On the journey to Jerusalem, Jesus cleanses ten lepers, but only one returns to give thanks. The grateful man is a Samaritan, whose response challenges assumptions about religious privilege. Both traditions see mercy preceding any claim to reward and faith responding to Jesus with gratitude rather than entitlement.

Luke 17:11

11As he was on his way to Jerusalem, he was passing along the borders of Samaria and Galilee.

Arminian

Luke places the encounter on Jesus’ journey toward Jerusalem near Samaria and Galilee. Arminian readers see the setting preparing for the surprising prominence of a Samaritan. The geographical notice serves the narrative without requiring a speculative reconstruction of every stage of the route.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the journey framing mercy shown along the way to Jesus’ suffering. The border setting prepares readers to notice who receives and acknowledges grace. Luke’s emphasis is the encounter, not a detailed map of the travel itinerary.

Luke 17:12

12As he entered into a certain village, ten men who were lepers met him, who stood at a distance.

Arminian

Ten lepers approach while remaining at a distance. Arminian readers see shared need preceding the later distinction in response. Luke does not give each man’s personal history, so their condition should not be turned into a judgment about individual moral guilt.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a group united by suffering and need for mercy rather than by any stated merit. Their distance underscores the separation in the scene. Both traditions should avoid inferring that illness itself reveals a particular person’s spiritual condition.

Luke 17:13

13They lifted up their voices, saying, “Jesus, Master, have mercy on us!”

Arminian

Their appeal addresses Jesus as Master and asks for mercy. Arminian readers see needy trust reaching toward the one able to help. The request is not a claim of deserving treatment, and grace is not a debt created by their suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see petition resting on Jesus’ compassionate authority. All ten ask for help, so the later thanksgiving cannot be assumed from the appeal alone. Luke invites attention to what receiving mercy should awaken in those who receive it.

Luke 17:14

14When he saw them, he said to them, “Go and show yourselves to the priests.” As they went, they were cleansed.

Arminian

Jesus sends them to the priests, and cleansing occurs as they go. Arminian readers see an obedient response to his word, enabled and answered by mercy. Their action does not earn healing; Jesus’ authority, not the journey itself, brings cleansing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ’s powerful word producing healing while calling forth a concrete response. The men act before the result is described. Both traditions hold together dependence on Jesus and obedience without treating obedience as payment for grace.

Luke 17:15

15One of them, when he saw that he was healed, turned back, glorifying God with a loud voice.

Arminian

One healed man returns and loudly glorifies God. Arminian readers see a response that goes beyond receiving benefit to acknowledging its giver. Gratitude is fitting faithfulness, not an additional price required to make the earlier healing real.

Reformed

Reformed readers see thanksgiving as the appropriate fruit of mercy received. The narrative distinguishes bodily cleansing from the fuller response Luke highlights in this man. It does not provide enough information to pronounce a final spiritual verdict on every other recipient.

Luke 17:16

16He fell on his face at Jesus’ feet, giving him thanks; and he was a Samaritan.

Arminian

The man gives thanks at Jesus’ feet, and Luke identifies him as a Samaritan. Arminian readers see humble gratitude from someone whose background might make his prominence surprising. Religious status cannot substitute for responding personally to mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see grace exposing the limits of assumed privilege. The Samaritan’s posture joins glorifying God with honoring Jesus. Both traditions recognize that an outsider’s response can reveal the ingratitude of those who presume themselves closer to God.

Luke 17:17

17Jesus answered, “Weren’t the ten cleansed? But where are the nine?

Arminian

Jesus’ question confirms that all ten were cleansed while exposing the absence of nine. Arminian readers see a real distinction between receiving a gift and responding thankfully. The text does not say that their ingratitude reversed the physical healing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the gift was real for all ten, though only one is shown returning. Jesus calls attention to gratitude, not to a defective miracle. The question should prompt self-examination rather than imagined accounts of the nine men’s later lives.

Luke 17:18

18Were there none found who returned to give glory to God, except this foreigner?”

Arminian

The foreigner alone is identified as returning to glorify God. Arminian readers see the invitation to gratitude extending beyond expected boundaries. Jesus challenges complacency among insiders without making ethnic background either a saving qualification or a moral defect.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the unexpected recipient honoring God while others fail to return. The scene undermines confidence in outward privilege. Both traditions affirm that grace should yield praise and that no community can treat acknowledgment of God’s mercy as unnecessary.

Luke 17:19

19Then he said to him, “Get up, and go your way. Your faith has healed you.”

Arminian

Jesus connects the man’s healing with his faith. Arminian readers understand faith as receptive trust made possible by grace, not a meritorious achievement. The grateful return gives the encounter spiritual depth, but the WEB’s healing language should not be made to describe every detail of his future.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see faith as the means of receiving Christ’s benefit, never the power that independently produces it. The man’s gratitude honors the giver. Both traditions can recognize a saving resonance without replacing Luke’s concrete account of healing with an abstract formula.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
