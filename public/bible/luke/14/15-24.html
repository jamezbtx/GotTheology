Passage

Luke 14

Verses 15–24 · WEB

A guest’s blessing on the kingdom’s banquet prompts a parable about an ample feast whose original invitees make excuses. The host brings in overlooked neighbours and sends the servant farther out, exposing presumption while displaying generous welcome. The final warning and urgent invitation raise questions about refusal and effective grace without authorising religious coercion.

Luke 14:15

15When one of those who sat at the table with him heard these things, he said to him, “Blessed is he who will feast in God’s Kingdom!”

Arminian

A guest praises the blessing of the kingdom’s banquet, and Jesus responds with a warning about receiving its invitation. Arminian readers see that admiration for future salvation must become a present response. Religious enthusiasm alone is not the same as accepting the host’s call.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish speaking warmly of the kingdom from truly receiving its grace. The coming story challenges presumed entitlement to a place. Both traditions should hear the blessing and warning together rather than assume the speaker’s words prove either salvation or hypocrisy.

Luke 14:16

16But he said to him, “A certain man made a great supper, and he invited many people.

Arminian

The banquet begins with the host’s generous initiative and an invitation to many. Arminian readers see a genuine welcome that calls for willing acceptance. The story should first be heard as a parable about invitation and refusal, not an exact inventory of the final number saved.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that the feast is provided by the host rather than created by the guests. Invitation precedes the response it seeks. Both traditions can acknowledge the generosity of the call before asking how it relates to broader accounts of election.

Luke 14:17

17He sent out his servant at supper time to tell those who were invited, ‘Come, for everything is ready now.’

Arminian

The servant announces that the feast is ready; the guests are not asked to supply what the host lacks. Arminian readers see grace offering a provision to be received through responsive trust. The call is urgent because an actual invitation awaits an answer.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a completed provision and the summons to receive it. The gospel invitation is not a request that sinners make themselves worthy first. Both traditions should preserve the host’s initiative without making the guests’ response unimportant.

Luke 14:18

18They all as one began to make excuses. “The first said to him, ‘I have bought a field, and I must go and see it. Please have me excused.’

Arminian

A field becomes the first reason for declining the summons. Arminian readers see an ordinary concern used to refuse a greater invitation. The parable does not condemn agriculture or property as such; it exposes what happens when possessions govern allegiance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the excuse revealing disordered priorities rather than a lack of information about the feast. The invited guest is responsible for refusing. Both traditions can recognise that outwardly respectable concerns may conceal unwillingness to receive grace.

Luke 14:19

19“Another said, ‘I have bought five yoke of oxen, and I must go try them out. Please have me excused.’

Arminian

Another guest gives attention to newly bought oxen instead of attending the meal. Arminian readers see business concerns crowding out a genuine invitation. Responsible work is not condemned, but it cannot justify treating God’s call as dispensable.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise observe that rejection can take the form of ordinary priorities rather than open hostility. The excuse does not make the host’s invitation insincere. Both traditions should examine respectable forms of refusal without despising everyday labour.

Luke 14:20

20“Another said, ‘I have married a wife, and therefore I can’t come.’

Arminian

Marriage supplies a further excuse for absence. Arminian readers see even a good relationship wrongly used to displace the host’s summons. The story does not portray marriage itself as sinful or require abandoning responsibilities toward a spouse.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the goodness of marriage from its use as a reason to refuse. Legitimate gifts can become governing loyalties. Both traditions should read the verse as an issue of priority rather than a blanket suspicion of family life.

Luke 14:21

21“That servant came, and told his lord these things. Then the master of the house, being angry, said to his servant, ‘Go out quickly into the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in the poor, maimed, blind, and lame.’

Arminian

The host responds to refusal by bringing in people normally overlooked at such a feast. Arminian readers see judgment on presumption joined to widening grace. The reversal does not equate every original guest with all Jewish people or every poor guest with an automatically saved class.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the host’s freedom and generosity overturn expected boundaries. Those lacking social leverage receive an invitation they could not secure through status. Both traditions must resist making a narrative of mercy into collective contempt for any people.

Luke 14:22

22“The servant said, ‘Lord, it is done as you commanded, and there is still room.’

Arminian

The servant reports obedience and the continuing availability of room. Arminian readers hear encouragement to extend the invitation rather than regard grace as a limited stock already exhausted. The story’s abundance is not a declaration that everyone will respond.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the host’s generosity reaching beyond the first group brought in. Mission continues because the master desires guests rather than an empty celebration. Both traditions should avoid reading the available room as a numerical chart of election.

Luke 14:23

23“The lord said to the servant, ‘Go out into the highways and hedges, and compel them to come in, that my house may be filled.

Arminian

Arminian readers commonly understand compelling as urgent persuasion and reassurance that outsiders are genuinely welcome, not force that manufactures faith. Grace enables a willing response without making refusal impossible. The historical misuse of this verse to defend coercion must not determine its meaning; the servant extends the host’s welcome rather than enforcing religious conformity.

Reformed

Reformed readers may see an analogy to the effectual call by which God makes people willing, while also reading the servant’s task as earnest invitation. This is not permission for human coercion or a claim that force creates faith. Interpretations of the parable’s details vary; God’s effective grace must be distinguished from compelling outward conformity.

Key contrast Both reject coercion as a means of creating faith and affirm earnest invitation. Arminian readers commonly stress persuasive welcome through resistible enabling grace; Reformed readers may also see the host’s purpose fulfilled through the Spirit’s effectual call. Interpretations vary within each tradition, and the servant’s human task must not be equated with God’s inward work.

Luke 14:24

24For I tell you that none of those men who were invited will taste of my supper. For many are called, but few are chosen. ’ ”

Arminian

Those who refuse exclude themselves from the supper despite their prior invitation. Arminian readers stress responsible rejection of offered grace and understand the final chosen language through faith and belonging to Christ. The WEB footnotes that final sentence as absent in several listed editions, so it should not carry the argument alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish the outward invitation from saving election when reading the final sentence, while locating the exclusion in the guests’ culpable refusal. The WEB prints the sentence but notes editions that omit it. The parable’s warning against presumption is clear without treating a textually footnoted clause as a complete account of election.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
