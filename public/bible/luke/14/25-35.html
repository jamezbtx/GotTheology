Passage

Luke 14

Verses 25–35 · WEB

Jesus turns to the crowds and makes clear that following him requires priority over competing loyalties, willingness to bear the cross, and surrender of possessions. Two illustrations urge sober consideration of the commitment, and the salt saying warns against a discipleship that loses its purpose. These are demands of grace-shaped allegiance, not a way to earn salvation or permission to mistreat family members.

Luke 14:25

25Now great multitudes were going with him. He turned and said to them,

Arminian

Jesus addresses the great crowds following him with the cost of discipleship. Arminian readers see the difference between attraction to Jesus and a continuing, grace-enabled allegiance. Large numbers do not remove the need to understand what following him involves.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish public association from genuine discipleship. Christ does not secure a following by concealing its demands. Both traditions should hear this instruction as clarification of allegiance rather than the introduction of a second, superior class of Christians.

Luke 14:26

26“If anyone comes to me, and doesn’t disregard his own father, mother, wife, children, brothers, and sisters, yes, and his own life also, he can’t be my disciple.

Arminian

The WEB renders the demand as disregarding competing claims and footnotes hate as an alternative. Arminian readers understand this strong Semitic contrast as priority for Christ over family and even self-preservation, not permission for malice or neglect. The priority is costly and may bring conflict, but it cannot justify cruelty toward dependants.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand family and life themselves as subordinate to Christ’s claim. The hate wording noted by the WEB is read as an emphatic comparison of loyalties, not a command to cultivate hostility. Neither tradition should soften the demand into convenience or use it to excuse abuse, abandonment, or contempt.

Luke 14:27

27Whoever doesn’t bear his own cross and come after me, can’t be my disciple.

Arminian

Bearing the cross means following Jesus despite shame, loss, and possible death. Arminian readers stress a grace-enabled commitment that goes beyond initial enthusiasm. Suffering does not earn acceptance, and not every self-imposed hardship is obedience to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see costly discipleship as the fruit of belonging to the crucified Lord. The command does not add the believer’s suffering to Christ’s atonement. Both traditions distinguish faithful endurance from seeking pain or requiring victims to remain exposed to abuse.

Luke 14:28

28For which of you, desiring to build a tower, doesn’t first sit down and count the cost, to see if he has enough to complete it?

Arminian

The builder’s calculation illustrates the seriousness of beginning a costly undertaking. Arminian readers see thoughtful commitment rather than an impulsive response that ignores dependence on grace. Counting the cost is not calculating enough merit to purchase salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see sober recognition of discipleship’s demands rather than bargaining over the terms. Dependence on divine provision does not make reflection unnecessary. Both traditions should avoid using the illustration to imply that people must first prove self-sufficient.

Luke 14:29

29Or perhaps, when he has laid a foundation and isn’t able to finish, everyone who sees begins to mock him,

Arminian

An unfinished foundation exposes the failure to reckon with completion. Arminian readers see the danger of beginning without continuing in faithful dependence. The illustration warns against superficial commitment rather than pronouncing a final verdict on every struggling believer.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see an inadequate profession exposed when endurance is absent. The image calls hearers to serious discipleship rather than confidence in a beginning alone. Both traditions should distinguish a warning about failure from permission to mock people who need help.

Luke 14:30

30saying, ‘This man began to build and wasn’t able to finish.’

Arminian

The spectators’ verdict highlights a beginning that never reached its intended end. Arminian readers connect the picture with the need to continue following Christ through grace. The point is perseverance, not gaining salvation by finishing a sufficient quantity of works.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see completion as essential to the kind of discipleship Jesus describes. Enduring faith depends on God’s preserving work rather than the strength of an initial declaration. Both traditions can hear the illustration without reducing grace to a reward for human stamina.

Luke 14:31

31Or what king, as he goes to encounter another king in war, will not sit down first and consider whether he is able with ten thousand to meet him who comes against him with twenty thousand?

Arminian

A king weighs the reality of unequal forces before committing to battle. Arminian readers see a second appeal to sober judgment about the demands of following Jesus. The example is not a command for the church to expand the kingdom by military force.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see deliberate assessment rather than reckless confidence. Readers differ over how closely to map each king onto a theological referent. Both traditions should retain the comparison’s call to seriousness without inventing an allegory in which every number carries a hidden meaning.

Luke 14:32

32Or else, while the other is yet a great way off, he sends an envoy and asks for conditions of peace.

Arminian

Recognising weakness leads the king to seek terms while there is time. Arminian readers may see practical foresight and an image of yielding claims to God rather than trusting one’s resources. The illustration should not turn salvation into a negotiation that changes Christ’s demands.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise can understand the king’s action as realistic recognition of inability, with surrender forming a possible further application. The parable does not explicitly identify every actor. Both traditions should avoid making the details prove a complete doctrine of free will or grace.

Luke 14:33

33So therefore, whoever of you who doesn’t renounce all that he has, he can’t be my disciple.

Arminian

Jesus applies the illustrations by requiring the renunciation of all one has. Arminian readers stress actual surrender of possessions and control, expressed in concrete generosity rather than a merely inward claim of detachment. Applications differ, including radical dispossession, but property cannot be exempted from allegiance to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ claiming authority over every resource, not merely asking for a religious portion. Stewardship must include readiness to part with possessions, with different applications across callings and communities. Both traditions reject turning renunciation into a purchase of salvation or empty rhetoric that changes nothing.

Luke 14:34

34“Salt is good, but if the salt becomes flat and tasteless, with what do you season it?

Arminian

Tasteless salt illustrates a discipleship that has lost its distinctive purpose. Arminian readers hear a serious warning against allowing allegiance to Christ to fade. The image’s force should not depend on an invented account of ancient salt production or a chemical explanation Luke does not provide.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the need for a profession to retain the character appropriate to following Christ. The image warns against complacency rather than offering a formula for measuring spiritual value. Both traditions should read it with the summons to hear in the next verse.

Luke 14:35

35It is fit neither for the soil nor for the manure pile. It is thrown out. He who has ears to hear, let him hear.”

Arminian

Discarded salt sharpens the warning, and Jesus calls for attentive hearing. Arminian readers commonly connect the image with the danger of abandoning faith and losing a previously lived discipleship, though applications vary. The response sought is renewed faithfulness through grace, not a declaration that anyone who has failed is beyond mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly understand the image as exposing a profession without enduring reality and the warning as a means of preserving genuine disciples. It does not mean that every lapse proves someone never belonged to Christ. Both traditions should let the call to hear lead toward repentance rather than confident diagnoses of another person’s final state.

Key contrast Both take the warning seriously and affirm the need for enduring, grace-enabled discipleship. Arminian readers commonly allow a real abandonment of saving faith; Reformed readers understand final desertion as exposing a false profession while God preserves his people through warnings. The salt image does not by itself describe every stage of conversion or restoration.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
