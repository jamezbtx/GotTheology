Passage

Luke 14

Verses 1–14 · WEB

At a Sabbath meal in a Pharisaic ruler’s house, Jesus heals a man and challenges the inconsistency of withholding mercy. He then addresses the guests’ pursuit of honour and the host’s expectation of repayment. The meal becomes a setting for teaching humility, generous inclusion, and hope in God’s resurrection reward.

Luke 14:1

1When he went into the house of one of the rulers of the Pharisees on a Sabbath to eat bread, they were watching him.

Arminian

Jesus shares a Sabbath meal in a Pharisaic ruler’s house while being closely observed. Arminian readers see an encounter within ongoing table fellowship rather than a refusal to engage religious opponents. The scrutiny sets up a question about mercy and the purpose of Sabbath.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to the shared meal as the setting for instruction and dispute. Jesus’ participation resists a simple picture of complete social separation from all Pharisees. Both traditions should read the particular encounter rather than assume identical motives in every member of the group.

Luke 14:2

2Behold, a certain man who had dropsy was in front of him.

Arminian

A man with dropsy stands before Jesus, making the question of healing concrete. Arminian readers see a person in need, not merely a prop in an argument. Luke does not state how he came to be there or whether others arranged a test.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise should not infer an unreported conspiracy from the man’s presence. His suffering calls for attention in its own right. Both traditions can recognise that theological disputes must not erase the dignity of those whose needs bring them into focus.

Luke 14:3

3Jesus, answering, spoke to the lawyers and Pharisees, saying, “Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath?”

Arminian

Jesus asks whether healing is lawful on the Sabbath, inviting the legal experts to consider mercy within God’s instruction. Arminian readers see a challenge to interpretation rather than a declaration that holiness no longer matters. Compassion must shape how religious rules are applied.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Jesus directing the question toward the proper use of God’s law. The alternatives are not divine authority or human well-being as though they were necessarily opposed. Both traditions can examine whether their practice reflects the good God commands.

Luke 14:4

4But they were silent. He took him, and healed him, and let him go.

Arminian

The experts remain silent, but Jesus heals the man and sends him on his way. Arminian readers see mercy enacted without waiting for unanimous institutional approval. The man receives a gift rather than earning care through a debate or display of worthiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ’s initiative and authority in the healing. Silence from the observers does not make the act less fitting. Both traditions should avoid treating this immediate physical restoration as a promise that every present illness will end in the same way.

Luke 14:5

5He answered them, “Which of you, if your son or an ox fell into a well, wouldn’t immediately pull him out on a Sabbath day?”

Arminian

Jesus compares healing with the immediate rescue of a son or ox from a well. Arminian readers see the inconsistency of accepting urgent care in one’s own affairs while resisting mercy for another. The main WEB reading son is followed; its footnote records donkey as an alternative.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see accepted rescue used to expose selective application of Sabbath rules. The ethical force concerns prompt care rather than delay in the name of observance. The WEB’s footnoted son or donkey variation does not remove the comparison’s challenge to withholding mercy.

Luke 14:6

6They couldn’t answer him regarding these things.

Arminian

The observers cannot answer Jesus’ comparison. Arminian readers see their objection exposed without assuming that silence itself proves repentance. The invitation is to recognise the fittingness of mercy rather than merely lose a public argument.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish an unanswered challenge from a complete account of the hearers’ inward response. Jesus’ reasoning carries moral force, yet Luke does not narrate their conversion here. Both traditions should resist inventing either reconciliation or permanent rejection beyond what is said.

Luke 14:7

7He spoke a parable to those who were invited, when he noticed how they chose the best seats, and said to them,

Arminian

The guests’ choice of prominent seats prompts Jesus to teach about honour. Arminian readers see ordinary social conduct revealing the pull of pride. Table fellowship becomes a setting for repentance rather than an arena where status should be secured.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the desire for precedence exposed through a familiar practice. Renewed character must reach social habits, not remain an abstract belief. Both traditions should hear the teaching as more than etiquette for obtaining recognition.

Luke 14:8

8“When you are invited by anyone to a wedding feast, don’t sit in the best seat, since perhaps someone more honorable than you might be invited by him,

Arminian

Taking the best seat assumes an honour that may not be one’s own. Arminian readers see a call to relinquish entitlement and attend to others. The illustration challenges pride rather than teaching anxiety about whether one is socially important enough.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see self-promotion placed under judgment. The invitation is received, not a right to control everyone else’s place. Both traditions can recognise humility without affirming that ordinary social rank measures a person’s worth before God.

Luke 14:9

9and he who invited both of you would come and tell you, ‘Make room for this person.’ Then you would begin, with shame, to take the lowest place.

Arminian

The attempt to claim priority can end in public humiliation. Arminian readers see the instability of honour secured by self-assertion. Jesus’ point is not to encourage enjoyment of another person’s shame but to expose the false security of pride.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the reversal as an illustration of self-exaltation’s failure. Social positioning cannot guarantee lasting honour. Both traditions should let the warning correct their own ambition rather than become a strategy for embarrassing rivals.

Luke 14:10

10But when you are invited, go and sit in the lowest place, so that when he who invited you comes, he may tell you, ‘Friend, move up higher.’ Then you will be honored in the presence of all who sit at the table with you.

Arminian

Choosing the lower place leaves recognition with the host rather than seizing it. Arminian readers see trust expressed in humility, not a performance designed to force promotion. The promised honour should not turn modest behaviour into another form of calculation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see humility receiving rather than manufacturing a place. Its deepest force is not a technique for advancing status but a relinquishment of self-importance. Both traditions should connect this illustration with the broader principle that follows.

Luke 14:11

11For everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted.”

Arminian

God’s reversal of self-exaltation gives the social illustration its deeper force. Arminian readers see humble reception of grace opposed to pride, not humility earning salvation. The warning includes those who use religion to establish superiority.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God, not the ambitious self, as the giver of true honour. Humility is the fitting fruit of receiving mercy. Both traditions reject making this principle a guarantee that every modest person will gain public status in the present.

Luke 14:12

12He also said to the one who had invited him, “When you make a dinner or a supper, don’t call your friends, nor your brothers, nor your kinsmen, nor rich neighbors, or perhaps they might also return the favor, and pay you back.

Arminian

Jesus challenges hospitality organised around the expectation of repayment. Arminian readers stress generosity that reaches beyond reciprocal advantage. The contrast does not require refusing all meals with family or friends; it confronts a closed circle of self-benefiting exchange.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s generosity challenging the host’s social calculations. Ordinary relationships are not forbidden, but they must not exhaust the reach of hospitality. Both traditions should ask whether their welcome depends on what guests can offer in return.

Luke 14:13

13But when you make a feast, ask the poor, the maimed, the lame, or the blind;

Arminian

The invitation is to include people excluded by poverty or disability. Arminian readers see concrete welcome, not merely kind thoughts about the vulnerable. These guests must be treated as persons worthy of fellowship rather than instruments for displaying the host’s virtue.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a gracious pattern that does not rank guests by their capacity to repay. Material sharing and social inclusion belong together. Both traditions should avoid treating disability as spiritual inferiority or turning hospitality into a means of self-congratulation.

Luke 14:14

14and you will be blessed, because they don’t have the resources to repay you. For you will be repaid in the resurrection of the righteous.”

Arminian

The absence of repayment places hope in God’s future rather than immediate social advantage. Arminian readers see reward as gracious recognition of faithful love, not a purchase of resurrection. The promise encourages generosity when no profitable return can be expected.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand reward within grace rather than as independent merit. God remembers service that a status-conscious world overlooks. Both traditions connect future hope with present care without making the poor a means of buying one’s own blessing.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
