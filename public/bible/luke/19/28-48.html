Passage

Luke 19

Verses 28–48 · WEB

Jesus completes his approach to Jerusalem amid a royal welcome from his disciples. Their praise gives way to his tears over the city and his warning about its failure to recognize God’s visitation. He then enters the temple, challenges its misuse, and teaches while opposition gathers against him. Both traditions affirm his kingship, sincere compassion, and authority; they differ mainly over how the lament relates God’s saving desire to human refusal and his sovereign purpose.

Luke 19:28

28Having said these things, he went on ahead, going up to Jerusalem.

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus moving ahead toward Jerusalem after correcting expectations of immediate glory. His purposeful journey holds together the coming suffering and his call for faithful discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the narrative returning from the parable to Jesus’ determined approach to Jerusalem. Both traditions recognize that the king’s road leads through rejection rather than around it.

Luke 19:29

29When he came near to Bethsphage and Bethany, at the mountain that is called Olivet, he sent two of his disciples,

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus involving two disciples in the practical preparations for his arrival. Ordinary obedience becomes part of the larger journey, without turning every place name or detail into a hidden spiritual lesson.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus directing the approach to Jerusalem through specific instructions. The disciples’ task is modest but meaningful, reminding believers that service often takes a practical and unremarkable form.

Luke 19:30

30saying, “Go your way into the village on the other side, in which, as you enter, you will find a colt tied, which no man has ever sat upon. Untie it and bring it.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the disciples called to trust Jesus’ instructions before seeing their outcome. The unused colt prepares for his public arrival, and their role is faithful obedience rather than control of the event.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus exercising knowledge and authority as he prepares his entry. His direction gives the disciples a clear task, joining his purposeful rule with their ordinary action.

Luke 19:31

31If anyone asks you, ‘Why are you untying it?’ say to him: ‘The Lord needs it.’ ”

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus anticipating a reasonable question and supplying an answer. Obedience here is accountable and specific; the verse is not permission to take another person’s property by claiming a private message from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Lord’s authority expressed through instructions that include how to answer others. Both traditions should resist using this unique narrative event to excuse disregard for neighbors or their possessions.

Luke 19:32

32Those who were sent went away and found things just as he had told them.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the disciples finding that Jesus’ word can be trusted in the task before them. Their obedience does not manufacture the outcome, but it brings them into what he has prepared.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fulfillment of Jesus’ instructions as confirming his knowledge and authority. The disciples act within his direction, offering a simple picture of trust expressed through doing what he says.

Luke 19:33

33As they were untying the colt, its owners said to them, “Why are you untying the colt?”

Arminian

Arminian readers see the owners asking exactly the kind of question Jesus anticipated. Their concern is not condemned, and faithful action need not avoid reasonable questions from people affected by it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ordinary human interaction unfolding within Jesus’ prior instructions. Confidence in his care does not require treating the owners’ question as hostility or as a lack of faith.

Luke 19:34

34They said, “The Lord needs it.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see the disciples giving the answer Jesus supplied rather than inventing their own explanation. Trust is expressed in a plain response, and the account does not invite embellishment about what the owners privately believed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the disciples relying on Jesus’ word in the situation he described. Both traditions can value this quiet obedience without claiming more about the owners’ response than Luke records.

Luke 19:35

35Then they brought it to Jesus. They threw their cloaks on the colt and sat Jesus on them.

Arminian

Arminian readers see disciples making their own cloaks available as Jesus is seated on the colt. Their service supports his arrival and gives a practical expression to the honor they offer him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the king entering with the help of his disciples rather than through an armed display. The cloaks and colt fit the narrative’s royal welcome, while the coming tears keep triumph from being confused with easy victory.

Luke 19:36

36As he went, they spread their cloaks on the road.

Arminian

Arminian readers see public honor taking the form of something people lay down before Jesus. Such gestures can express devotion, but the wider narrative calls for a faithfulness deeper than the excitement of a procession.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the spreading of cloaks as part of the royal welcome Jesus receives. Both traditions distinguish an outward act of honor from a full account of the spiritual condition of everyone present.

Luke 19:37

37As he was now getting near, at the descent of the Mount of Olives, the whole multitude of the disciples began to rejoice and praise God with a loud voice for all the mighty works which they had seen,

Arminian

Arminian readers see praise rising from the mighty works the disciples have witnessed. Remembering what God has done can nourish joyful trust, even though gratitude still needs to grow into enduring discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see praise directed to God for the works accomplished through Jesus. Shared worship responds to divine mercy rather than generating it, and loud celebration is not itself proof that every expectation is rightly formed.

Luke 19:38

38saying, “Blessed is the King who comes in the name of the Lord! Peace in heaven, and glory in the highest!”

Arminian

Arminian readers see the disciples acclaiming Jesus as king and giving glory to God. Peace is bound to his coming, yet the lament that follows shows that such peace must not be confused with automatic public acceptance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the royal acclamation revealing the significance of Jesus’ arrival. Both traditions affirm his kingship and the peace associated with him, while recognizing that the crowd’s understanding need not be complete.

Luke 19:39

39Some of the Pharisees from the multitude said to him, “Teacher, rebuke your disciples!”

Arminian

Arminian readers see some Pharisees seeking to restrain the disciples’ praise. Luke identifies particular voices in the crowd, so their objection must not be generalized to every Pharisee or to Jewish people as a whole.

Reformed

Reformed readers see opposition emerging within the welcome itself. The request challenges Jesus to silence his followers, but the text gives no basis for assigning one motive or response to an entire religious community.

Luke 19:40

40He answered them, “I tell you that if these were silent, the stones would cry out.”

Arminian

Arminian readers hear Jesus affirming that this moment calls for testimony that cannot simply be suppressed. The vivid image honors his identity without requiring a literal account of stones beginning to speak.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the certainty that the king’s significance will be acknowledged. Both traditions can receive the force of the image without using it to dismiss the real responsibility of human witnesses.

Luke 19:41

41When he came near, he saw the city and wept over it,

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus’ tears as genuine grief for the city, not a performance hiding indifference. His compassion gives the approaching judgment a deeply pastoral setting and warns believers against rejoicing in others’ ruin.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise insist that Jesus truly mourns the city he approaches. Confidence in God’s sovereign purpose does not cancel his compassion, and his tears teach believers to grieve over unbelief and suffering.

Luke 19:42

42saying, “If you, even you, had known today the things which belong to your peace! But now, they are hidden from your eyes.

Arminian

Arminian readers often hear a gracious desire for Jerusalem’s peace that has met human refusal. The present hiddenness warns that resistance has consequences, without implying that Jesus’ sorrow or the offered peace was insincere.

Reformed

Reformed readers often distinguish God’s revealed call to peace from his sovereign purpose, affirming both Jesus’ sincere lament and the reality of judicial hiddenness. This verse does not disclose the spiritual destiny of every individual in the city.

Key contrast Arminian readers commonly emphasize a gracious saving desire resisted by the city; Reformed readers commonly distinguish God’s revealed call from his sovereign purpose. Both affirm Jesus’ genuine grief and the seriousness of the city’s refusal.

Luke 19:43

43For the days will come on you when your enemies will throw up a barricade against you, surround you, hem you in on every side,

Arminian

Arminian readers see the loss of peace described in the concrete terror of a coming siege. The warning makes refusal serious, while Jesus’ tears forbid treating the suffering of a city as a reason for religious pride.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an announced judgment that will involve real human devastation. Divine judgment is not an abstract doctrine here, and its gravity calls for humility and compassion rather than detached speculation.

Luke 19:44

44and will dash you and your children within you to the ground. They will not leave in you one stone on another, because you didn’t know the time of your visitation.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see failure to recognize God’s visitation named as the reason for the city’s coming ruin. This is a warning about a corporate rejection and its consequences, not a verdict on the eternal destiny of every child mentioned.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the seriousness of missing God’s gracious visitation in Jesus. The corporate judgment must not be turned into collective blame across generations or a claim that every person who suffers is individually condemned.

Luke 19:45

45He entered into the temple and began to drive out those who bought and sold in it,

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus acting against practices that compromise the temple’s purpose. Compassion for the lost does not mean indifference to wrongdoing, especially where worship is being distorted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus asserting authority in the temple as his arrival becomes confrontation. Both traditions understand that grace and holy correction belong together, without importing details about the sellers that Luke does not supply here.

Luke 19:46

46saying to them, “It is written, ‘My house is a house of prayer,’ but you have made it a ‘den of robbers’!”

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus measuring temple practice by its God-given purpose as a house of prayer. Religious activity cannot excuse conduct that shelters wrongdoing or displaces genuine worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus invoking Scripture to expose the difference between sacred appearance and faithfulness to God. Both traditions hear a warning that a holy place cannot make corrupt practice acceptable.

Luke 19:47

47He was teaching daily in the temple, but the chief priests, the scribes, and the leading men among the people sought to destroy him.

Arminian

Arminian readers see patient public teaching continuing while leaders seek Jesus’ destruction. Their hostile intent remains their responsibility, and the passage calls readers to hear the message rather than protect a threatened status.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus continuing his ministry in the face of organized opposition. His purposeful mission does not excuse the leaders’ hostility, and the narrative carefully identifies those involved rather than blaming every listener.

Luke 19:48

48They couldn’t find what they might do, for all the people hung on to every word that he said.

Arminian

Arminian readers see eager attention to Jesus temporarily frustrating the plans against him. Listening closely is a welcome beginning, but the story invites a response that goes beyond fascination with his words.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the people’s attention functioning as a restraint on the leaders’ plans. Both traditions distinguish this public interest from a claim that every hearer possesses saving faith.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
