Passage

Luke 19

Verses 1–10 · WEB

As Jesus passes through Jericho on his way to Jerusalem, Luke brings a wealthy tax collector into the center of the story. Zacchaeus wants to see Jesus, but Jesus takes the initiative in calling him and entering his home. The crowd objects, while generosity and restitution accompany the announcement that salvation has come. Both traditions affirm that grace seeks the lost and bears practical fruit; they differ mainly over how God’s seeking grace brings a person to faith and whether that grace can be resisted.

Luke 19:1

1He entered and was passing through Jericho.

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus continuing his journey toward Jerusalem through an ordinary place where someone unexpected will receive him. The encounter that follows shows that no person’s reputation puts them beyond his gracious attention.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the saving mission of Jesus unfolding as he passes through Jericho. Both traditions recognize that the story turns on his personal encounter with a man the crowd would dismiss.

Luke 19:2

2There was a man named Zacchaeus. He was a chief tax collector, and he was rich.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that Zacchaeus has both wealth and a position that draws public suspicion. Neither status nor money can supply the salvation he needs, and neither makes him unreachable by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Zacchaeus is not introduced as an obvious candidate for religious approval. His riches and office make the welcome Jesus gives him a striking display of grace rather than a reward for social respectability.

Luke 19:3

3He was trying to see who Jesus was, and couldn’t because of the crowd, because he was short.

Arminian

Arminian readers see a real desire to encounter Jesus, despite an immediate obstacle. They may connect such awakening interest with grace that draws people toward Christ, without treating curiosity alone as saving faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers notice Zacchaeus’s interest while looking ahead to Jesus taking the initiative in addressing him. The verse describes his wish to see Jesus, not the hidden workings of regeneration, so it should not be made to settle their order.

Luke 19:4

4He ran on ahead and climbed up into a sycamore tree to see him, for he was going to pass that way.

Arminian

Arminian readers see Zacchaeus acting on his desire instead of letting the crowd decide whether he can see Jesus. His effort illustrates an attentive response, not a work that earns the Lord’s welcome.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the small, concrete circumstances through which this encounter takes place. Climbing a tree does not merit salvation; it places Zacchaeus in the scene where Christ will graciously call him.

Luke 19:5

5When Jesus came to the place, he looked up and saw him, and said to him, “Zacchaeus, hurry and come down, for today I must stay at your house.”

Arminian

Arminian readers emphasize that Jesus approaches Zacchaeus personally before any public pledge of restitution. Grace takes the initiative and invites a welcome that the next verse describes as joyful.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see Jesus’ personal summons and declared purpose as a picture of effective saving grace. He does not merely notice Zacchaeus at a distance but brings his saving presence into the man’s home.

Luke 19:6

6He hurried, came down, and received him joyfully.

Arminian

Arminian readers see Zacchaeus freely and gladly receiving the welcome Jesus has offered. His prompt response matters, yet it answers grace rather than creating a claim upon it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the joyful reception as the fitting fruit of Christ’s gracious initiative. Both traditions affirm that saving grace does not make people indifferent to Jesus but draws forth an actual welcome.

Luke 19:7

7When they saw it, they all murmured, saying, “He has gone in to lodge with a man who is a sinner.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see how easily concern for reputation can harden into resistance to mercy. The crowd’s complaint challenges believers to welcome the people Jesus seeks rather than treating past sin as a permanent barrier.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the offense caused by grace when it reaches someone regarded as undeserving. Sharing a home with Zacchaeus does not mean approving wrongdoing; the following verses connect salvation with a changed relationship to money.

Luke 19:8

8Zacchaeus stood and said to the Lord, “Behold, Lord, half of my goods I give to the poor. If I have wrongfully exacted anything of anyone, I restore four times as much.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see generosity and restitution as concrete expressions of repentance, not payment for salvation. Some understand these words as a new pledge, while others hear a statement of existing practice; the present wording invites care before deciding.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the concern for the poor and those wronged as consistent with the fruit of grace. Whether Zacchaeus announces new commitments or describes his practice is debated across traditions, but his money is now discussed under Jesus’ lordship.

Luke 19:9

9Jesus said to him, “Today, salvation has come to this house, because he also is a son of Abraham.

Arminian

Arminian readers hear Jesus publicly welcoming Zacchaeus into the story of God’s saving mercy. Salvation is present in this encounter, and neither his generosity nor his ancestry should be treated as an automatic guarantee for every member of the household.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Jesus’ authoritative announcement that salvation has come today. Calling Zacchaeus a son of Abraham answers the crowd’s exclusion, while the verse does not describe the spiritual condition of each person in his house.

Luke 19:10

10For the Son of Man came to seek and to save that which was lost.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see the heart of Jesus’ mission in his initiative toward the lost. They commonly understand this seeking grace as enabling a genuine response that can be resisted; Zacchaeus illustrates its joyful reception.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus not merely offering help but accomplishing salvation for the lost he seeks. They commonly connect this mission with effective calling, while agreeing that those saved truly welcome him and show the fruit of repentance.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm that Christ initiates salvation and that faith truly responds. Arminian readers commonly understand this seeking grace as resistible, while Reformed readers commonly see it as effectively bringing the lost to faith.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
