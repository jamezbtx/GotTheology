Passage

Luke 15

Verses 11–32 · WEB

The third parable tells of a father and two sons. The younger son demands his inheritance, wastes it, and, starving, comes to his senses and returns home, hoping to be a servant. His father runs to meet him, embraces him, and throws a feast. The elder son, angry at the celebration, refuses to go in, and the father comes out to plead with him too. The story ends open: will the elder brother join the feast? Both traditions read it as a portrait of the Father’s grace toward both the openly rebellious and the self-righteous. Their main difference is how they read the son “coming to himself.”

Luke 15:11

11He said, “A certain man had two sons.

Arminian

“A certain man had two sons.” From the start, both sons matter to the story. Arminian readers note that the parable is aimed at the grumblers of verse 2 as much as at sinners.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Both traditions see the elder son as an essential part of the parable, not an afterthought.

Luke 15:12

12The younger of them said to his father, ‘Father, give me my share of your property.’ So he divided his livelihood between them.

Arminian

The younger son demands his share early, which in that culture would have been deeply shameful. The father grants it. Arminian readers stress that the father lets the son go, respecting his freedom even when it is misused.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a picture of sin’s rebellion against the Father. Both traditions agree the father’s patience is remarkable.

Luke 15:13

13Not many days after, the younger son gathered all of this together and traveled into a far country. There he wasted his property with riotous living.

Arminian

The son travels far away and wastes everything in reckless living. Arminian readers note that sin brings real loss.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point. Both traditions see the “far country” as life away from God.

Luke 15:14

14When he had spent all of it, there arose a severe famine in that country, and he began to be in need.

Arminian

A famine strikes, and he starts to go hungry. Arminian readers see consequences that God can use to awaken a sinner.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence at work in the hard circumstances. Both traditions note that suffering often prepares the way for repentance.

Luke 15:15

15He went and joined himself to one of the citizens of that country, and he sent him into his fields to feed pigs.

Arminian

He hires himself out to feed pigs, unclean animals for a Jew. Arminian readers note how low he has fallen.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared ground.

Luke 15:16

16He wanted to fill his belly with the pods that the pigs ate, but no one gave him any.

Arminian

He longs to eat the pigs’ pods, but no one gives him anything. Arminian readers note the emptiness of what the world offers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same, reading it as a picture of the sinner’s helpless state. Both traditions find the depth of his need painfully clear.

Luke 15:17

17But when he came to himself, he said, ‘How many hired servants of my father’s have bread enough to spare, and I’m dying with hunger!

Arminian

“When he came to himself” marks the turning point. He remembers his father’s generosity. Arminian readers often see this as a genuine response to grace: prevenient grace awakening the sinner, who then truly chooses to turn.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the parable is not a full account of how conversion works, and that Scripture elsewhere teaches that God gives the new heart that makes such a turn possible. Both traditions agree the turn is real, and both caution against building a complete doctrine on one parable.

Key contrast Both see real repentance here. Arminians commonly see the son responding freely to prevenient grace; Reformed readers commonly hold that such coming to oneself flows from God’s prior, effectual work, though the parable does not describe it.

Luke 15:18

18I will get up and go to my father, and will tell him, “Father, I have sinned against heaven and in your sight.

Arminian

He plans to go back and confess, “I have sinned against heaven and in your sight.” Arminian readers note that true repentance includes honest confession.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both traditions treat his confession as a model of repentance.

Luke 15:19

19I am no more worthy to be called your son. Make me as one of your hired servants.” ’

Arminian

He feels unworthy to be a son and plans to ask to be a hired servant. Arminian readers note that he still thinks he must earn his way back.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point, contrasting his plan with the father’s grace. Both traditions see that grace will exceed his hopes.

Luke 15:20

20“He arose and came to his father. But while he was still far off, his father saw him and was moved with compassion, and ran, fell on his neck, and kissed him.

Arminian

While he is still far off, the father sees him, is moved with compassion, runs, embraces, and kisses him. An older man running was undignified in that culture. Arminian readers stress the father’s eagerness to receive a returning child.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the father acts first in the meeting, welcoming before the speech is finished. Both traditions see this verse as one of Scripture’s clearest pictures of the Father’s love.

Luke 15:21

21The son said to him, ‘Father, I have sinned against heaven and in your sight. I am no longer worthy to be called your son.’

Arminian

The son begins his confession, but he does not get to the request to be a servant. Arminian readers note that the father’s welcome interrupts the plan to earn a place.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point: grace ends the bargaining. Both traditions find the omission meaningful.

Luke 15:22

22“But the father said to his servants, ‘Bring out the best robe and put it on him. Put a ring on his hand and sandals on his feet.

Arminian

The father calls for the best robe, a ring, and sandals, signs of full restoration as a son, not a servant. Arminian readers stress complete reinstatement.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see a picture of justification and adoption, the son clothed in what he did not earn. Both traditions read these gifts as signs of sonship restored.

Luke 15:23

23Bring the fattened calf, kill it, and let’s eat and celebrate;

Arminian

He calls for the fattened calf and a feast. Arminian readers note that the celebration is public and generous.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both traditions see the feast as an echo of heaven’s joy (vv. 7, 10).

Luke 15:24

24for this, my son, was dead and is alive again. He was lost and is found.’ Then they began to celebrate.

Arminian

“This, my son, was dead and is alive again. He was lost and is found.” Arminian readers often note that the son was alive before leaving, so the language describes a real departure and real restoration, a theme some apply to backsliding.

Reformed

Reformed readers often hear the language of spiritual death and new life (Ephesians 2:1–5). Both traditions agree the main point is the father’s joy at recovery, and they caution against pressing every detail.

Luke 15:25

25“Now his elder son was in the field. As he came near to the house, he heard music and dancing.

Arminian

The elder son, coming in from the field, hears the music. Arminian readers note the shift in the story to the second son.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared narrative ground.

Luke 15:26

26He called one of the servants to him and asked what was going on.

Arminian

He asks a servant what is happening. Arminian readers note that he does not go straight in to his father.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same distance. This verse is shared ground.

Luke 15:27

27He said to him, ‘Your brother has come, and your father has killed the fattened calf, because he has received him back safe and healthy.’

Arminian

The servant explains that the brother is home and the father has killed the fattened calf. Arminian readers note the simple, joyful report.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared ground.

Luke 15:28

28But he was angry and would not go in. Therefore his father came out and begged him.

Arminian

He is angry and refuses to go in, so the father comes out and pleads with him. Arminian readers stress that the father’s love seeks both sons, and that grace can be refused.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same pleading love, and see in the elder son the danger of self-righteousness. Both traditions note that the father goes out to each son.

Luke 15:29

29But he answered his father, ‘Behold, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed a commandment of yours, but you never gave me a goat, that I might celebrate with my friends.

Arminian

He complains of years of service and obedience without a feast. Arminian readers note that he relates to his father like a servant, not a son.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-righteousness that misses grace. Both traditions see the Pharisees and scribes of verse 2 reflected in him.

Luke 15:30

30But when this your son came, who has devoured your living with prostitutes, you killed the fattened calf for him.’

Arminian

He calls his brother “this your son” and accuses him of wasting money on prostitutes. Arminian readers note his refusal to claim his brother.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same bitterness. Both traditions see a warning against resenting grace given to others.

Luke 15:31

31“He said to him, ‘Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours.

Arminian

The father answers tenderly: “Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours.” Arminian readers stress that the elder son has always had access to the father’s love.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point: he lived near grace without enjoying it. Both traditions find this gentle reply striking.

Luke 15:32

32But it was appropriate to celebrate and be glad, for this, your brother, was dead, and is alive again. He was lost, and is found.’ ”

Arminian

It was right to celebrate, because “this, your brother” was dead and is alive. The parable ends without telling us if the elder son went in. Arminian readers see an open invitation to the hearers to respond.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the open ending as a challenge to the self-righteous. Both traditions agree that the story invites the listener to join the Father’s joy.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
