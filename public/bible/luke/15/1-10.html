Passage

Luke 15

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Tax collectors and sinners crowd around Jesus to hear him, and the Pharisees and scribes grumble that he welcomes sinners and eats with them. Jesus answers with two parables, a lost sheep and a lost coin, and the lost son follows. In each one, something lost is sought, found, and celebrated, and Jesus says heaven rejoices over one sinner who repents. Arminian and Reformed readers both treat this chapter as a vivid picture of God’s seeking grace and joy. They differ mainly on how God’s seeking relates to the sinner’s repentance.

Luke 15:1

1Now all the tax collectors and sinners were coming close to him to hear him.

Arminian

Tax collectors, seen as collaborators and cheats, and “sinners,” people known for breaking the law, draw near to hear Jesus. Arminian readers note their eagerness to listen, a response grace makes possible and invites.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in their coming the drawing work of God (John 6:44). Both traditions note that hearing Jesus is where repentance begins.

Luke 15:2

2The Pharisees and the scribes murmured, saying, “This man welcomes sinners, and eats with them.”

Arminian

The religious leaders complain: “This man welcomes sinners, and eats with them.” Sharing a table meant fellowship. Arminian readers note that the complaint unknowingly describes the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point. Both traditions see the three parables as Jesus’ answer to this grumbling.

Luke 15:3

3He told them this parable:

Arminian

“He told them this parable” introduces a single parable in three parts. Arminian readers note that the parables are addressed to the critics as much as to sinners.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. This verse is shared ground.

Luke 15:4

4“Which of you men, if you had one hundred sheep and lost one of them, wouldn’t leave the ninety-nine in the wilderness and go after the one that was lost, until he found it?

Arminian

The shepherd leaves the ninety-nine to search for the one lost sheep until he finds it. Arminian readers stress God’s earnest seeking of every lost person, reflecting his desire that none perish.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the shepherd searches “until he found it”: God’s seeking succeeds. Both traditions find here a picture of God taking the initiative toward the lost.

Key contrast Both see God seeking the lost. Arminians tend to emphasize God’s seeking of every sinner, which may be refused; Reformed readers tend to emphasize the shepherd’s search that does not fail.

Luke 15:5

5When he has found it, he carries it on his shoulders, rejoicing.

Arminian

He carries it home on his shoulders, rejoicing. Arminian readers note the tenderness of the restoration and the absence of any scolding.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sheep carried, not walking home on its own, as a picture of grace. Both traditions find great comfort in this image.

Luke 15:6

6When he comes home, he calls together his friends and his neighbors, saying to them, ‘Rejoice with me, for I have found my sheep which was lost!’

Arminian

The shepherd calls friends and neighbors to rejoice with him. Arminian readers note that joy over the found should be shared.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point. Both traditions see an invitation, even to the grumblers, to join the celebration.

Luke 15:7

7I tell you that even so there will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents, than over ninety-nine righteous people who need no repentance.

Arminian

Heaven rejoices more over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine “righteous” who need no repentance. The phrase is likely ironic, aimed at those who think they need no repentance. Arminian readers stress that the parable explicitly names repentance, a real human turning that grace enables.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that repentance is real and necessary, and in light of verse 5, they see it as the result of being found. Both traditions read the “righteous” as a gentle challenge to the critics.

Key contrast Both affirm that repentance is real and required. Arminians typically see it as a grace-enabled response the sinner gives; Reformed readers typically see the sinner’s repentance as the fruit of God finding and changing them.

Luke 15:8

8“Or what woman, if she had ten drachma coins, if she lost one drachma coin, wouldn’t light a lamp, sweep the house, and seek diligently until she found it?

Arminian

A woman searches her house with lamp and broom for one lost coin, worth about a day’s wages. Arminian readers note the diligent search for something of value.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same, and the fact that a coin cannot help find itself. Both traditions note that Jesus uses a woman’s work as a picture of God’s seeking.

Luke 15:9

9When she has found it, she calls together her friends and neighbors, saying, ‘Rejoice with me, for I have found the drachma which I had lost!’

Arminian

She finds it and calls her friends to rejoice. Arminian readers note the repetition of joy from the first parable.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared ground.

Luke 15:10

10Even so, I tell you, there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner repenting.”

Arminian

There is joy before God’s angels over one sinner who repents. Arminian readers stress that God personally delights in each sinner’s return.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same joy of God over his found people. Both traditions agree that the point is God’s joy, and the critics should share it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
