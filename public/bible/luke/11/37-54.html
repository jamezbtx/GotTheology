Passage

Luke 11

Verses 37–54 · WEB

At a Pharisee’s table, a dispute over washing becomes a searching exposure of inward corruption beneath religious appearances. Jesus condemns neglected justice and love, status seeking, oppressive teaching, and resistance to God’s messengers. The lawyers’ possession of the key of knowledge makes their obstruction especially serious. Both traditions apply these warnings to religious leadership today without treating every Pharisee, or Jewish people generally, as the target of collective blame.

Luke 11:37

37Now as he spoke, a certain Pharisee asked him to dine with him. He went in and sat at the table.

Arminian

Jesus accepts a Pharisee’s invitation to dinner. Both traditions note that the ensuing rebuke occurs within personal encounter, not refusal to associate with religious opponents.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus willing to enter a setting where his conduct will be scrutinized. The invitation becomes an occasion to expose deeper needs than table custom.

Luke 11:38

38When the Pharisee saw it, he marveled that he had not first washed himself before dinner.

Arminian

The host is surprised that Jesus has not washed before eating. Both traditions understand the issue as ritual expectation rather than a lesson against ordinary hygiene.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the contrast between concern for ceremonial appearance and neglect of inward character. Jesus’ response addresses this reversal of priorities.

Luke 11:39

39The Lord said to him, “Now you Pharisees cleanse the outside of the cup and of the platter, but your inward part is full of extortion and wickedness.

Arminian

Clean utensils contrast with inward extortion and wickedness. Arminian readers stress that outward practice cannot excuse a heart and life that exploit others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a searching exposure of corruption beneath religious discipline. Both traditions insist that genuine holiness reaches motives and treatment of neighbors.

Luke 11:40

40You foolish ones, didn’t he who made the outside make the inside also?

Arminian

The Creator made both outward and inward life. Both traditions reject dividing religion into public appearances that matter and hidden motives that escape God’s concern.

Reformed

Reformed readers ground the claim in God’s authority over the whole person. External conformity cannot hide the inner life from its maker.

Luke 11:41

41But give for gifts to the needy those things which are within, and behold, all things will be clean to you.

Arminian

Giving to the needy challenges the greed just exposed. Interpreters differ over the precise sense of things within; Arminian readers do not treat almsgiving as a purchase of pardon.

Reformed

Reformed readers see generous action as expressing the inward change Jesus demands. Both traditions reject a mechanical reading in which a donation makes an otherwise unrepentant life clean.

Luke 11:42

42But woe to you Pharisees! For you tithe mint and rue and every herb, but you bypass justice and God’s love. You ought to have done these, and not to have left the other undone.

Arminian

Careful tithing of herbs cannot compensate for neglecting justice and God’s love. Both traditions hear Jesus requiring rightly ordered obedience, not contempt for every smaller duty.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Jesus condemns selective obedience rather than diligence itself. The weightier moral demands must shape the entire practice of religion.

Luke 11:43

43Woe to you Pharisees! For you love the best seats in the synagogues and the greetings in the marketplaces.

Arminian

The desire for prominent seats and greetings exposes status seeking. Arminian readers apply the warning to the temptation to use religious service for personal recognition.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the honor of office must not become an idol. Both traditions recognize a warning for Christian leaders, not merely an accusation safely confined to the past.

Luke 11:44

44Woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! For you are like hidden graves, and the men who walk over them don’t know it.”

Arminian

Hidden graves picture harm that others do not recognize. Both traditions see religious hypocrisy spreading defilement beneath an apparently respectable surface.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the danger of influence that looks holy but misleads. The image calls leaders to self-examination rather than reassuring them that visible esteem proves inward soundness.

Luke 11:45

45One of the lawyers answered him, “Teacher, in saying this you insult us also.”

Arminian

A lawyer recognizes that Jesus’ rebuke touches his own group. Arminian readers note the opportunity to receive correction rather than treating discomfort as proof that the speaker is wrong.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the objection drawing legal teachers into the direct warning. Both traditions distinguish the offense of truthful correction from a justification for careless insult.

Luke 11:46

46He said, “Woe to you lawyers also! For you load men with burdens that are difficult to carry, and you yourselves won’t even lift one finger to help carry those burdens.

Arminian

The lawyers impose heavy burdens without offering help. Both traditions condemn teaching that increases others’ distress while excusing the teacher from compassionate service.

Reformed

Reformed readers see authority distorted when it demands without shepherding. The warning calls those who teach to share burdens rather than shelter behind their position.

Luke 11:47

47Woe to you! For you build the tombs of the prophets, and your fathers killed them.

Arminian

Building prophets’ tombs can honor their memory while resisting their message. Arminian readers see Jesus exposing a contradiction between inherited reverence and present conduct.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that monuments cannot substitute for obedience. The charge must be read with the continuing rejection of God’s messengers, not as a condemnation of remembering the dead itself.

Luke 11:48

48So you testify and consent to the works of your fathers. For they killed them, and you build their tombs.

Arminian

The leaders’ conduct aligns them with the rejection practiced by their ancestors. Both traditions locate the guilt in continuing that resistance, not in biological descent by itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers see professed honor contradicted by present hostility. Identification with past persecutors arises from shared opposition to God’s word.

Luke 11:49

49Therefore also the wisdom of God said, ‘I will send to them prophets and apostles; and some of them they will kill and persecute,

Arminian

God’s wisdom announces the sending and rejection of prophets and apostles. Both traditions recognize God’s persistent witness and human responsibility for persecution; the saying is not explicitly identified as a quotation from a particular Old Testament verse.

Reformed

Reformed readers see opposition to God’s messengers occurring within the announced course of his purposes. Interpreters differ over the precise identification of the wisdom of God here.

Luke 11:50

50that the blood of all the prophets, which was shed from the foundation of the world, may be required of this generation,

Arminian

The accumulated shedding of prophetic blood culminates in judgment on this generation. Arminian readers hear the seriousness of joining an established pattern of resisting God’s messengers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see corporate historical accountability without making every descendant personally guilty of every earlier act. Both traditions avoid extending this charge into collective blame against Jewish people in later generations.

Luke 11:51

51from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zachariah, who perished between the altar and the sanctuary.’ Yes, I tell you, it will be required of this generation.

Arminian

Abel and Zachariah frame a history of righteous bloodshed. Both traditions recognize the allusion to Abel’s murder and the temple setting of Zachariah’s death; the latter’s precise identification is debated.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the solemn certainty of the announced reckoning. The warning concerns God’s vindication of his witnesses, not permission for disciples to seek vengeance themselves.

Luke 11:52

52Woe to you lawyers! For you took away the key of knowledge. You didn’t enter in yourselves, and those who were entering in, you hindered.”

Arminian

The teachers hold the key of knowledge yet obstruct entry. Arminian readers see a grave misuse of responsibility: refusing God’s truth can also hinder those seeking it.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that entrusted knowledge increases accountability. Neither theological learning nor office guarantees that a teacher personally receives the truth he is called to make clear.

Luke 11:53

53As he said these things to them, the scribes and the Pharisees began to be terribly angry, and to draw many things out of him,

Arminian

The rebukes provoke intense hostility and attempts to press Jesus with questions. Both traditions distinguish this reaction from humble engagement that is willing to be corrected.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the preceding warnings confirmed in the leaders’ response. Exposure of hypocrisy can become an occasion for deeper resistance when the heart refuses repentance.

Luke 11:54

54lying in wait for him, and seeking to catch him in something he might say, that they might accuse him.

Arminian

The opponents seek words they can use to accuse Jesus. Arminian readers note the moral difference between listening for truth and listening for a trap.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the conflict moving toward the rejection of Christ. Both traditions warn against weaponizing another person’s speech rather than addressing what is true.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
