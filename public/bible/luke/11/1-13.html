Passage

Luke 11

Verses 1–13 · WEB

A disciple asks Jesus to teach them to pray after watching him pray. Jesus gives a pattern centered on the Father’s name and kingdom, daily provision, forgiveness, and protection from temptation. The midnight visitor and the examples of earthly fathers encourage persistent, confident asking, culminating in the Father’s gift of the Holy Spirit. Both traditions affirm that prayer depends on God’s generosity, not a technique for overcoming divine reluctance.

Luke 11:1

1When he finished praying in a certain place, one of his disciples said to him, “Lord, teach us to pray, just as John also taught his disciples.”

Arminian

Jesus’ own prayer prompts the request to learn. Both traditions see prayer as a practiced dependence on God, learned from Christ rather than sustained by spiritual self-sufficiency.

Reformed

The disciples ask their Lord to shape their praying. Reformed readers share the conviction that Christ teaches not only what to believe but how to approach the Father.

Luke 11:2

2He said to them, “When you pray, say, ‘Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy. May your Kingdom come. May your will be done on earth, as it is in heaven.

Arminian

Prayer begins with the Father’s holiness, kingdom, and will before personal needs. Arminian readers stress willing submission to God’s gracious rule; the printed WEB wording is retained in full.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize God’s fatherly welcome and the priority of his reign. Both traditions pray for his will to govern earthly life, rather than treating prayer as a way to advance private agendas.

Luke 11:3

3Give us day by day our daily bread.

Arminian

Daily bread teaches dependence for ordinary necessities. Arminian readers see a repeated invitation to trust God today and to remember the needs of others in the plural request.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ordinary provision as the Father’s gift through his providence. Both affirm that such dependence is compatible with work, planning, and generous care for neighbors.

Luke 11:4

4Forgive us our sins, for we ourselves also forgive everyone who is indebted to us. Bring us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one.’ ”

Arminian

Disciples ask forgiveness while forgiving those indebted to them. Arminian readers stress that a forgiving life belongs to continuing fellowship with God; neither forgiveness nor protection from temptation is earned by moral effort.

Reformed

Reformed readers see forgiving others as necessary fruit of received mercy, not payment for pardon. Both traditions acknowledge continuing need for cleansing and ask God to preserve them from the evil one.

Luke 11:5

5He said to them, “Which of you, if you go to a friend at midnight and tell him, ‘Friend, lend me three loaves of bread,

Arminian

The midnight request pictures a bold appeal in genuine need. Arminian readers find encouragement to bring inconvenient and concrete concerns to God instead of withholding them.

Reformed

Reformed readers take the story as encouragement to approach God confidently. The comparison does not make the Father an unwilling neighbor who must be manipulated into kindness.

Luke 11:6

6for a friend of mine has come to me from a journey, and I have nothing to set before him,’

Arminian

The bread is sought for a traveler, not simply for the petitioner’s comfort. Both traditions can see here a pattern of prayer that serves another person’s need.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that hospitality exposes the host’s lack of resources. Need becomes an occasion for seeking help rather than pretending to possess everything required for service.

Luke 11:7

7and he from within will answer and say, ‘Don’t bother me. The door is now shut, and my children are with me in bed. I can’t get up and give it to you’?

Arminian

The sleeping household explains why the request is inconvenient. Arminian readers distinguish this reluctant human response from the greater generosity Jesus will attribute to the Father.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the obstacle heightening the story’s encouragement. Both traditions reject the idea that prayer must wake God or make him attentive to needs he has overlooked.

Luke 11:8

8I tell you, although he will not rise and give it to him because he is his friend, yet because of his persistence, he will get up and give him as many as he needs.

Arminian

The WEB’s persistence encourages bold, continued asking. Interpreters differ over the precise force of the expression, but Arminian readers need not turn persistence into a work that merits an answer.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise encourage persevering prayer while recognizing debate over boldness, persistence, and the story’s social setting. Both rest the final assurance on God’s character, not the petitioner’s endurance alone.

Luke 11:9

9“I tell you, keep asking, and it will be given you. Keep seeking, and you will find. Keep knocking, and it will be opened to you.

Arminian

Asking, seeking, and knocking summon active reliance on God. Arminian readers emphasize responding to the invitation repeatedly, without assuming that prayer guarantees every desired outcome.

Reformed

Reformed readers see prayer as a means God appoints for receiving his gifts. Both traditions encourage expectant dependence rather than treating divine sovereignty as a reason not to ask.

Luke 11:10

10For everyone who asks receives. He who seeks finds. To him who knocks it will be opened.

Arminian

The promise warmly includes everyone who asks. In context, Arminian readers understand this through the Father’s good gifts, not as permission to demand anything contrary to his will.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the promise’s generous invitation and reliability. The Father answers as a wise Father, so confident prayer is not a contractual claim to every imagined benefit.

Luke 11:11

11“Which of you fathers, if your son asks for bread, will give him a stone? Or if he asks for a fish, he won’t give him a snake instead of a fish, will he?

Arminian

The father-and-child comparison appeals to ordinary parental care. Both traditions hear Jesus directing attention from anxiety about harmful answers to the trustworthiness of the heavenly Father.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the printed WEB includes both the bread-and-stone and fish-and-snake examples. The point is God’s goodness, not a denial that earthly parents sometimes fail.

Luke 11:12

12Or if he asks for an egg, he won’t give him a scorpion, will he?

Arminian

A scorpion instead of an egg would betray the child’s need. Arminian readers see assurance that God does not mock sincere dependence with deliberately destructive gifts.

Reformed

Reformed readers join Arminians in recognizing the contrast between nourishing provision and harm. God’s wisdom, not merely the intensity of desire, defines what a good gift is.

Luke 11:13

13If you then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him?”

Arminian

The Spirit is the Father’s supreme gift to those who ask. Arminian readers often connect this with grace-enabled prayer and receptive faith, while affirming that the Spirit already works before any saving response.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish the Spirit’s work enabling faith from his ongoing gifts and presence sought in prayer. Both welcome the promise; this verse does not by itself settle the order of regeneration and faith.

Key contrast Both affirm the Father’s generosity and the Spirit’s prior gracious work. Arminian readers emphasize grace-enabled asking and reception; Reformed readers distinguish the Spirit’s regenerating initiative from the ongoing gifts believers ask for. The promise alone does not settle the order of salvation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
