Passage

Luke 11

Verses 14–28 · WEB

Jesus frees a man from a demon, but some attribute his work to Beelzebul while others demand a heavenly sign. Jesus exposes the accusation’s inconsistency and identifies his deliverance as evidence that God’s kingdom has arrived. The stronger man, the returning spirit, and the blessing on those who hear and keep God’s word all press the need for allegiance to Jesus. Both traditions distinguish outward improvement from a life truly turned toward God.

Luke 11:14

14He was casting out a demon, and it was mute. When the demon had gone out, the mute man spoke; and the multitudes marveled.

Arminian

The delivered man speaks, and the crowds marvel. Both traditions recognize a concrete act of liberation, not merely an occasion for abstract argument about evil.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s authority demonstrated in restoring a suffering person. The following controversy exposes how the same merciful work can be met with either wonder or hostility.

Luke 11:15

15But some of them said, “He casts out demons by Beelzebul, the prince of the demons.”

Arminian

Some interpret deliverance as demonic cooperation. Arminian readers note the culpable refusal to acknowledge mercy, without making every honest question equivalent to such an accusation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see hostility distorting the interpretation of an evident good. Both traditions warn that religious confidence can coexist with grave misjudgment about Christ.

Luke 11:16

16Others, testing him, sought from him a sign from heaven.

Arminian

The demand for a heavenly sign tests Jesus despite the deliverance just witnessed. Both traditions distinguish sincere searching from demands that evade the significance of available evidence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that additional wonders do not automatically produce faith. Jesus will answer this demand by directing the hearers toward the sign of Jonah.

Luke 11:17

17But he, knowing their thoughts, said to them, “Every kingdom divided against itself is brought to desolation. A house divided against itself falls.

Arminian

Jesus exposes the accusation by the image of a divided kingdom and household. Arminian readers see him reasoning with opponents and calling them to recognize their contradiction.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Jesus’ knowledge of their thoughts and the coherence of his answer. Both traditions affirm that faith need not abandon careful reasoning about his works.

Luke 11:18

18If Satan also is divided against himself, how will his kingdom stand? For you say that I cast out demons by Beelzebul.

Arminian

If exorcism were Satan’s attack on his own dominion, the accusation would undermine itself. Both traditions see Jesus showing that his liberation opposes Satan rather than serves him.

Reformed

Reformed readers recognize an actual conflict between Christ and the powers of evil. Satan’s kingdom is not an equal rival to God, and its defeat is already being displayed.

Luke 11:19

19But if I cast out demons by Beelzebul, by whom do your children cast them out? Therefore they will be your judges.

Arminian

Jesus appeals to exorcists associated with his accusers, exposing a double standard. Arminian readers understand their children here as their own people or followers, not necessarily biological children.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an argument that turns the opponents’ accepted practices against their accusation. Both traditions avoid treating the verse as an unrestricted endorsement of every claim to exorcism.

Luke 11:20

20But if I by God’s finger cast out demons, then God’s Kingdom has come to you.

Arminian

God’s finger identifies divine power at work, recalling Exodus 8:19. Both traditions see the deliverance as evidence of the kingdom’s arrival in Jesus, calling for acknowledgment rather than further evasion.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the initiative and power of God’s reign. The kingdom has confronted these hearers in Christ; the statement does not imply that everyone present has already entered it in saving faith.

Luke 11:21

21“When the strong man, fully armed, guards his own dwelling, his goods are safe.

Arminian

The armed strong man represents evil’s entrenched hold in the illustration. Both traditions acknowledge that freedom requires more than unaided human resolve.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the image preparing for Christ’s superior strength. It should not be isolated into a claim that Satan possesses authority independent of God’s sovereignty.

Luke 11:22

22But when someone stronger attacks him and overcomes him, he takes from him his whole armor in which he trusted, and divides his plunder.

Arminian

Jesus is the stronger deliverer who defeats the enemy and frees what he held. Arminian readers ground salvation in Christ’s victory, not in the sinner’s independent power to escape.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize Christ’s decisive conquest. Both traditions rejoice in his supremacy; the picture itself does not specify every detail of how redemption is applied to individuals.

Luke 11:23

23“He who is not with me is against me. He who doesn’t gather with me scatters.

Arminian

There is no neutral stance toward Jesus in this conflict. Arminian readers emphasize the summons to allegiance and participation in his gathering work.

Reformed

Reformed readers see true loyalty to Christ expressed in joining his mission. Both traditions distinguish this searching claim from a license to label every disagreement among Christians opposition to Christ.

Luke 11:24

24The unclean spirit, when he has gone out of the man, passes through dry places, seeking rest; and finding none, he says, ‘I will turn back to my house from which I came out.’

Arminian

The returning spirit introduces a warning about relief without enduring transformation. Both traditions avoid assuming that the spirit’s departure, by itself, proves the man had been regenerated.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the story in the setting of Jesus’ call for allegiance. Freedom from one form of bondage must not be confused with a heart established in devotion to God.

Luke 11:25

25When he returns, he finds it swept and put in order.

Arminian

The house is orderly, but order alone offers no lasting security. Arminian readers apply the picture to outward reform that lacks continuing commitment to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish moral tidiness from renewal by grace. Both traditions recognize that respectable appearances cannot substitute for a life turned toward Christ.

Luke 11:26

26Then he goes and takes seven other spirits more evil than himself, and they enter in and dwell there. The last state of that man becomes worse than the first.”

Arminian

The worse return warns against treating initial relief as the whole of discipleship. Arminian readers hear a serious warning, but the story does not explicitly identify the man as a former believer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the danger of superficial reform without saving renewal. Both traditions resist using this illustration alone to settle debates about apostasy or the experience of genuine believers.

Luke 11:27

27It came to pass, as he said these things, a certain woman out of the multitude lifted up her voice and said to him, “Blessed is the womb that bore you, and the breasts which nursed you!”

Arminian

The woman blesses Jesus’ mother because of her relation to him. Both traditions recognize her admiration while attending to Jesus’ redirection toward hearing and obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that admiration for Jesus’ family is not the passage’s final measure of blessing. The answer opens the call to all who receive God’s word.

Luke 11:28

28But he said, “On the contrary, blessed are those who hear the word of God, and keep it.”

Arminian

Blessing belongs to those who hear God’s word and keep it. Arminian readers emphasize a living, grace-enabled response, not merely exposure to religious teaching.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience as the fruit of genuine reception of the word. Both traditions deny that biological closeness or religious enthusiasm can replace faithful discipleship.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
