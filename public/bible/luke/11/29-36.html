Passage

Luke 11

Verses 29–36 · WEB

Responding to the demand for a sign, Jesus points to Jonah and contrasts his generation with Gentiles who responded to lesser messengers. The Queen of the South sought Solomon’s wisdom, and Nineveh repented at Jonah’s preaching; someone greater is now present. The lamp and eye sayings expose the danger of refusing available light. Both traditions hear a call to repentance and receptive attention, rather than endless demands for further evidence.

Luke 11:29

29When the multitudes were gathering together to him, he began to say, “This is an evil generation. It seeks after a sign. No sign will be given to it but the sign of Jonah the prophet.

Arminian

Jesus rejects a demand for signs that masks resistance to him. Both traditions understand the sign of Jonah as directing attention to God’s appointed witness rather than satisfying every demand for spectacle.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Jesus himself defines what sign will be given. The crowd cannot set the terms on which God must establish his messenger’s authority.

Luke 11:30

30For even as Jonah became a sign to the Ninevites, so the Son of Man will also be to this generation.

Arminian

Jonah’s role as a sign anticipates the Son of Man’s witness to this generation. Arminian readers note that Luke foregrounds the messenger and the response to his preaching.

Reformed

Reformed readers may also connect the saying with Christ’s resurrection in light of Matthew 12:40. Both distinguish Luke’s wording from Matthew’s explicit reference to three days and nights.

Luke 11:31

31The Queen of the South will rise up in the judgment with the men of this generation and will condemn them, for she came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon; and behold, one greater than Solomon is here.

Arminian

The Queen of the South’s pursuit of wisdom exposes the failure to hear someone greater than Solomon. Both traditions see privilege bringing responsibility rather than guaranteeing a receptive heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s superior dignity and wisdom. The comparison challenges hearers who possess greater light but will not welcome it.

Luke 11:32

32The men of Nineveh will stand up in the judgment with this generation, and will condemn it, for they repented at the preaching of Jonah; and behold, one greater than Jonah is here.

Arminian

Nineveh repented at Jonah’s preaching, exposing the present generation’s refusal. Arminian readers see a summons to respond to the greater messenger now before them.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm both the reality of Nineveh’s repentance and the seriousness of rejecting Christ. Both traditions understand repentance as more than curiosity about signs.

Luke 11:33

33“No one, when he has lit a lamp, puts it in a cellar or under a basket, but on a stand, that those who come in may see the light.

Arminian

A lamp is placed where its light can be seen. In this setting, both traditions read the image as challenging resistance to light already being offered through Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers see no defect in the light Christ provides. The surrounding sayings shift attention from demands for more evidence to the hearer’s receptivity.

Luke 11:34

34The lamp of the body is the eye. Therefore when your eye is good, your whole body is also full of light; but when it is evil, your body also is full of darkness.

Arminian

The eye pictures the way a person receives light. Arminian readers stress an undivided, receptive disposition, while acknowledging that spiritual sight itself requires grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the contrast exposing the heart’s effect on perception. Both recognize that this is moral and spiritual imagery, not a claim that physical blindness indicates spiritual evil.

Luke 11:35

35Therefore see whether the light that is in you isn’t darkness.

Arminian

Jesus calls hearers to examine whether supposed illumination is really darkness. Both traditions find a warning against confident self-deception, especially when rejecting God’s messenger.

Reformed

Reformed readers apply the warning to religious presumption as well as open unbelief. Genuine discernment welcomes correction from Christ instead of making personal certainty its own authority.

Luke 11:36

36If therefore your whole body is full of light, having no part dark, it will be wholly full of light, as when the lamp with its bright shining gives you light.”

Arminian

The whole person illuminated by light pictures the good of receiving Jesus’ teaching. Arminian readers see an invitation to wholehearted openness under God’s gracious work.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the transforming reach of divine illumination. Both avoid treating the image as a technical claim that faithful disciples have already become incapable of sin or error.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
