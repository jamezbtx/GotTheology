Passage

Luke 10

Verses 38–42 · WEB

Following the command to show mercy, Jesus enters a household where Martha welcomes him and Mary listens at his feet. Martha’s extensive preparations become a source of distraction and resentment, prompting her to ask Jesus to intervene. He responds with personal concern for her anxiety and defends Mary’s attention to his word. The scene does not cancel the preceding call to active love, but places service beneath the priority of receiving the Lord himself.

Luke 10:38

38As they went on their way, he entered into a certain village, and a certain woman named Martha received him into her house.

Arminian

Martha’s reception of Jesus establishes her hospitality as a genuine welcome before the narrative describes her distraction. Arminian readers see active service as a good response that can nonetheless require reordering. The village is unnamed in this verse, so the interpretation need not depend on importing a location from another Gospel’s related household accounts.

Reformed

Reformed readers recognize that ordinary household hospitality forms the setting for Christ’s teaching. Martha is not introduced as an enemy or a representative of unbelief. The coming correction addresses the ordering of her service, reminding readers that even sincerely welcoming Jesus does not eliminate the need to hear his word about their priorities.

Luke 10:39

39She had a sister called Mary, who also sat at Jesus’ feet and heard his word.

Arminian

Mary sits at Jesus’ feet and listens, taking the posture of a learner rather than allowing household expectations to exclude her from his instruction. Arminian readers emphasize receptive attention as a genuine act of discipleship. Her response values the gift of Jesus’ presence and teaching rather than measuring faithfulness only through visible activity.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the priority of receiving Christ’s word before trying to serve him through one’s own agenda. Mary’s place as a hearer is defended by Jesus, not dismissed as idleness. The scene honors her discipleship without suggesting that attentive listening removes the continuing claims of love and practical responsibility.

Luke 10:40

40But Martha was distracted with much serving, and she came up to him, and said, “Lord, don’t you care that my sister left me to serve alone? Ask her therefore to help me.”

Arminian

Martha’s service becomes distracting, and she interprets her sister’s absence from the work as something Jesus should correct. Arminian readers see how a good task can become entangled with anxiety and comparison. Her complaint invites honest self-examination about whether service flows from attention to Christ or becomes a demand that he endorse one’s frustration.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish useful labor from the troubled inward posture now governing it. Martha addresses Jesus as though his care should be demonstrated by directing Mary back to her preferred arrangement. The problem is not work itself, but distraction that lets the worker’s expectations displace the authority and welcome of the guest.

Luke 10:41

41Jesus answered her, “Martha, Martha, you are anxious and troubled about many things,

Arminian

Jesus answers Martha personally and names the anxiety beneath the complaint rather than simply redistributing the work. Arminian readers see gracious correction directed toward restoration, not humiliation. The repeated name conveys attentive address, while the diagnosis invites her to release the many concerns that have come to govern her reception of him.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Christ knows and addresses the heart beneath outward service. He does not measure the household solely by the amount of activity being performed. His response exposes anxious preoccupation while keeping Martha within the conversation, making correction an expression of care rather than dismissal of her hospitality.

Luke 10:42

42but one thing is needed. Mary has chosen the good part, which will not be taken away from her.”

Arminian

Mary’s choice is commended because she receives what Jesus is presently giving, and he refuses to take that good portion from her. Arminian readers see meaningful, grace-enabled responsiveness rather than merit achieved by inactivity. The assurance concerns this defended place of hearing; it should not alone be pressed into settling every question about final perseverance.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear Christ protect the priority of his word against the pressure of competing expectations. Mary’s good portion is secured by his judgment rather than the household’s estimate of productivity. The scene grounds service in receptive communion with him, not a rivalry in which contemplation is holy and all practical labor is spiritually inferior.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
