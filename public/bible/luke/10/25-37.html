Passage

Luke 10

Verses 25–37 · WEB

A legal expert tests Jesus with a question about inheriting eternal life, and Jesus directs him to the law’s commands to love God and neighbor. The man’s desire to justify himself leads to a narrower question about the identity of his neighbor. Jesus answers with a story in which a Samaritan, rather than a priest or Levite, shows costly mercy to a wounded stranger. The conclusion turns attention from limiting the category of neighbor to becoming a neighbor through merciful action.

Luke 10:25

25Behold, a certain lawyer stood up and tested him, saying, “Teacher, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?”

Arminian

The legal expert’s question concerns eternal life, but Luke identifies his motive as testing Jesus. Arminian readers distinguish a serious theological question from the resistant posture in which it may be asked. Inheriting life is not presented here as a casual reward for politeness; the exchange will confront the depth of love God requires.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the tension between the language of inheritance and a question framed around personal action. Luke’s description of testing prepares for the later exposure of self-justification. Jesus will not dismiss God’s moral demands, but will uncover the inadequacy of the questioner’s attempt to control or limit what those demands mean.

Luke 10:26

26He said to him, “What is written in the law? How do you read it?”

Arminian

Jesus sends the questioner to the law and asks how he reads it, engaging both the written command and its interpretation. Arminian readers see Scripture as authoritative for moral response, not merely material for debate. Responsible hearing requires allowing its claims to challenge the reader’s motives and conduct rather than confirm a preferred answer.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Jesus receives the law as God’s authoritative instruction while questioning the expert’s handling of it. Correct knowledge will not by itself establish a righteous standing. The conversation moves from what is written to whether the man will face its meaning without using interpretation as a shield against its searching demand.

Luke 10:27

27He answered, “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your strength, and with all your mind; and your neighbor as yourself.”

Arminian

The answer joins wholehearted love for God with love for the neighbor, drawing on Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18, as the WEB footnotes identify. Arminian readers emphasize the comprehensive response grace calls forth: affection, thought, strength, and action belong together. Neighbor-love cannot be detached from devotion to God or reduced to selective generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers recognize the law’s unified demand for love, reaching the inner person as well as outward conduct. The scope of the command exposes the inadequacy of merely external conformity. It also gives positive direction to renewed life, so its searching function does not erase the enduring goodness of loving God and neighbor.

Luke 10:28

28He said to him, “You have answered correctly. Do this, and you will live.”

Arminian

Jesus affirms the answer and requires its actual practice, not merely its correct recitation. Arminian readers take the summons to love seriously while denying that fallen people earn eternal life by unaided achievement. The exchange can expose the need for grace as well as describe the life grace calls forth, so it should not be made a simple law-versus-grace divide.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the law’s true demand: wholehearted love, not a manageable standard reduced for self-justification. The promise does not mean sinners can establish righteousness by imperfect compliance. Read with the man’s next attempt to justify himself, it exposes need for mercy while preserving the genuine moral summons to love in practice.

Luke 10:29

29But he, desiring to justify himself, asked Jesus, “Who is my neighbor?”

Arminian

The man’s wish to justify himself turns attention toward defining the neighbor rather than examining his own love. Arminian readers see how an apparent request for precision can become resistance to obedience. The coming story will confront the desire to set safe limits around responsibility without requiring us to invent an unreported private motive beyond Luke’s explanation.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the self-justifying movement explicitly named by Luke. The expert seeks a boundary within which he may regard himself as having met the command. Jesus answers by displacing that project, showing what mercy does for another person rather than supplying a narrow list of people whom the law requires him to love.

Luke 10:30

30Jesus answered, “A certain man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho, and he fell among robbers, who both stripped him and beat him, and departed, leaving him half dead.

Arminian

The story begins with a traveler stripped, beaten, and left half dead, making urgent need the first fact to confront. Arminian readers see compassion called forth by vulnerability rather than a qualification the victim must establish. The man’s identity is not specified, so interpretations should not depend on invented details about his ethnicity, virtue, or religious status.

Reformed

Reformed readers attend to the wounded man’s helplessness before asking which passerby will act. Jesus creates a test of neighbor-love whose force does not depend on the victim’s deservingness. Allegorical readings may serve later reflection, but the immediate exchange concerns costly mercy toward a person whom others could choose to leave unaided.

Luke 10:31

31By chance a certain priest was going down that way. When he saw him, he passed by on the other side.

Arminian

The priest sees the wounded traveler but passes on the other side, separating awareness from compassionate response. Arminian readers see that religious position does not substitute for active love. Jesus supplies no explanation of the priest’s motive, so fear, ritual concerns, or scheduling pressures should not be asserted as the reason for his refusal.

Reformed

Reformed readers recognize that a respected religious role offers no guarantee of obedience from the heart. The priest’s sight of the victim makes his passing significant within the story. The narrative exposes failed mercy without allowing the reader to excuse or condemn him through details Jesus has not provided.

Luke 10:32

32In the same way a Levite also, when he came to the place and saw him, passed by on the other side.

Arminian

The Levite repeats the priest’s pattern, strengthening the contrast between religious standing and practical neighbor-love. Arminian readers hear a challenge to let knowledge become action rather than imitation of another person’s neglect. The repetition keeps the wounded man’s need in view; neither passerby’s position has supplied the care he requires.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the second refusal closing off the assumption that membership among religious servants ensures righteousness. The law’s demand cannot be satisfied by identity or office. Jesus does not analyze the Levite’s inward reasoning, but his visible failure prepares the hearer to receive a surprising example from outside the expected group.

Luke 10:33

33But a certain Samaritan, as he traveled, came where he was. When he saw him, he was moved with compassion,

Arminian

The Samaritan sees the same need but responds with compassion, overturning expectations shaped by the preceding refusals. Arminian readers emphasize mercy crossing a communal boundary rather than waiting for social permission. The contrast with the refused hospitality in Luke 9 also cautions against treating every member of a people as a single moral type.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the exemplary response comes from a person the questioner may not have expected to honor. Religious privilege does not monopolize compassionate conduct. The Samaritan’s inward compassion becomes the source of concrete care in the following verses, exposing the failure of merely knowing or discussing the command to love.

Luke 10:34

34came to him, and bound up his wounds, pouring on oil and wine. He set him on his own animal, brought him to an inn, and took care of him.

Arminian

Compassion becomes practical attention to wounds, transportation, lodging, and continued care. Arminian readers see neighbor-love expressed through available resources rather than an abstract favorable attitude. The account gives a pattern of personal involvement without turning oil, wine, the animal, or the inn into a guaranteed symbolic code for other doctrines.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that mercy incurs effort and inconvenience rather than remaining an inward feeling. The Samaritan makes the wounded man’s need his own concern and uses ordinary means to help. This is the kind of embodied love the law requires, not a detached religious performance designed to secure recognition.

Luke 10:35

35On the next day, when he departed, he took out two denarii, gave them to the host, and said to him, ‘Take care of him. Whatever you spend beyond that, I will repay you when I return.’

Arminian

The Samaritan provides money and promises further payment, extending care beyond the first moment of rescue. Arminian readers see responsibility continuing when immediate emotion might have faded. The two denarii are a concrete expense in the story; the text does not require calculating an exact modern price or a precise length of recovery.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the open-ended character of the commitment: the traveler’s need, rather than the rescuer’s convenience, governs the promised help. Mercy includes provision through another person as well as direct personal care. The innkeeper’s involvement shows practical cooperation without diminishing the Samaritan’s responsibility for what he has undertaken.

Luke 10:36

36Now which of these three do you think seemed to be a neighbor to him who fell among the robbers?”

Arminian

Jesus changes the focus from identifying a permissible object of love to recognizing the person who acted as a neighbor. Arminian readers hear a summons to become responsive to need rather than defend restricted obligations. The question asks the expert to judge the conduct in the story and then face its implications for himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus undoing the project of self-justification by redirecting the man’s interpretive question. The issue is no longer whether the victim qualifies within a controlled category. It is which traveler fulfilled the law’s demand through mercy, exposing the inadequacy of boundaries designed to preserve a comfortable verdict on oneself.

Luke 10:37

37He said, “He who showed mercy on him.” Then Jesus said to him, “Go and do likewise.”

Arminian

The expert identifies the merciful person, and Jesus commands corresponding action. Arminian readers emphasize grace-enabled obedience that crosses inherited boundaries, not a doctrine that earns life through isolated good deeds. The imperative should retain its practical force: recognizing the right answer is incomplete if the hearer continues to leave the wounded without help.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm that the command calls for real mercy, not merely admission of moral inability. The story exposes self-righteousness while directing the life of those who receive grace. Costly love is the fitting fruit of faith rather than the basis of justification, so doctrinal reflection must not neutralize the summons to act.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
