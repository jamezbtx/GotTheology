Passage

Luke 10

Verses 1–24 · WEB

As Jesus continues toward Jerusalem, he appoints a larger company to prepare the places he intends to visit. His instructions combine dependence on hospitality, proclamation of the Kingdom, healing, and solemn warnings about rejection. The returning messengers celebrate authority over demons, but Jesus redirects their joy toward belonging to heaven. He then praises the Father for gracious revelation and declares the Son’s unique authority to make the Father known.

Luke 10:1

1Now after these things, the Lord also appointed seventy others, and sent them two by two ahead of him into every city and place where he was about to come.

Arminian

Jesus appoints others beyond the twelve and sends them in pairs to places he intends to visit. Arminian readers see a widening opportunity for responsive service under his direction. This WEB edition reads “seventy” here and in verse 17; its chapter footnotes do not flag the seventy/seventy-two variation.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the Lord selects and sends the workers, determining their destination before they undertake the mission. Their role is preparatory, directing attention toward his arrival rather than themselves. The WEB footnote explains the wording about going ahead of him as literally “before his face,” preserving the personal orientation of their service.

Luke 10:2

2Then he said to them, “The harvest is indeed plentiful, but the laborers are few. Pray therefore to the Lord of the harvest, that he may send out laborers into his harvest.

Arminian

The plentiful harvest creates urgency, while the shortage of laborers becomes an occasion for prayer rather than despair. Arminian readers emphasize that God invites his people to participate in his gracious purpose through asking and going. The mission remains his harvest, so responsive human action never makes its workers the owners of the field.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the Lord of the harvest supplies workers through the very prayers he commands. Divine rule does not make prayer or labor unnecessary; it establishes their place within his purpose. Those being sent must themselves ask for more laborers, acknowledging that the task exceeds their present resources.

Luke 10:3

3Go your ways. Behold, I send you out as lambs among wolves.

Arminian

Jesus sends his messengers into danger under an image of vulnerability, not superior force. Arminian readers see a call to trust and faithful witness without coercion. The warning prepares them for hostility rather than promising universal welcome, while the sender’s authority gives courage to undertake the mission despite that risk.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Christ knowingly sends servants into a setting they cannot master through their own strength. Their vulnerability does not imply that the mission has escaped his care. The lamb-and-wolf contrast excludes treating gospel proclamation as domination and directs confidence toward the one who sends them.

Luke 10:4

4Carry no purse, nor wallet, nor sandals. Greet no one on the way.

Arminian

The restrictions on supplies and greetings underline the urgency and dependence of this particular mission. Arminian readers understand the instruction as focused obedience rather than permission for habitual rudeness. The messengers are to avoid distractions and trust the provision Jesus directs them to receive through the hospitality encountered along the way.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Christ orders both the message and the manner of travel. The lack of stored resources makes room for provision through receptive households, while the prohibition on greetings keeps the journey purposeful. These situational directions should not become a universal ban on preparation, ordinary courtesy, or material support for ministry.

Luke 10:5

5Into whatever house you enter, first say, ‘Peace be to this house.’

Arminian

The first word offered to a household is peace, not suspicion or demand. Arminian readers see the generous character of the approach: welcome is sought through blessing rather than pressure. This greeting fits the Kingdom’s announcement and prepares for a real response from the people who may receive the messengers.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand the greeting as a blessing spoken under Christ’s commission, not an independent power the workers can manipulate. The offer of peace precedes any calculation of the household’s usefulness. The next verse will distinguish reception from refusal while preserving the integrity of the message offered to either household.

Luke 10:6

6If a son of peace is there, your peace will rest on him; but if not, it will return to you.

Arminian

A receptive household receives the blessing, while refusal does not make the messengers’ offer of peace meaningless. Arminian readers emphasize that response matters within the mission Jesus authorizes. A “son of peace” describes a person suited to receive the greeting here; the expression does not itself explain the entire process of conversion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ distinguishing receptive hearers without leaving his servants responsible for manufacturing receptivity. Peace is not lost or defeated when a household refuses it. The workers may offer the blessing faithfully and entrust its reception to God, rather than attempting to force a result or reading every refusal as their personal failure.

Luke 10:7

7Remain in that same house, eating and drinking the things they give, for the laborer is worthy of his wages. Don’t go from house to house.

Arminian

Remaining with one household restrains self-interest, while receiving food recognizes that service may rightly be supported. Arminian readers see mutual responsibility rather than exploitation: hosts share resources, and messengers avoid shopping for more comfortable arrangements. Dependence on grace therefore has an ordinary social form in faithful hospitality and contentment.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Christ authorizes support for his workers while placing limits on their ambitions. The laborer’s wages are legitimate, not evidence that ministry has become mercenary. Staying in one house protects the mission from status-seeking and helps keep material provision subordinate to the task for which the workers were sent.

Luke 10:8

8Into whatever city you enter and they receive you, eat the things that are set before you.

Arminian

Acceptance of a city’s hospitality calls for grateful participation in what is provided. Arminian readers emphasize the flexibility required to receive others without demanding preferred arrangements or food. The instruction concerns this mission’s table fellowship, so it should not be made into a careless rule overriding every medical, moral, or practical consideration.

Reformed

Reformed readers see contentment as part of the workers’ witness to dependence on their Lord. Their calling is not to make local hosts reproduce a lifestyle of the messengers’ choosing. Receiving what is set before them allows hospitality to serve the Kingdom’s advance rather than become another occasion for asserting privilege.

Luke 10:9

9Heal the sick who are there and tell them, ‘God’s Kingdom has come near to you.’

Arminian

Healing and proclamation together tell the city that God’s reign has drawn near through Jesus’ mission. Arminian readers see grace addressing real human distress while summoning hearers to respond to the message. The healing sign is not a replacement for the announcement, nor does receiving bodily help automatically amount to saving faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the nearness of the Kingdom rests on the King’s initiative, not the city’s achievement. The messengers announce a reality that authorizes their service. Healing accompanies that announcement as a sign of Christ’s authority, rather than as a general promise that every illness will immediately disappear wherever Christians preach.

Luke 10:10

10But into whatever city you enter and they don’t receive you, go out into its streets and say,

Arminian

Jesus prepares his servants for refusal as well as welcome, giving instruction rather than leaving them to improvise resentment. Arminian readers see that a gracious mission may meet accountable resistance. Going into the streets makes the response public, but the following words prescribe testimony rather than retaliation against the refusing city.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish the certainty of Christ’s authority from a guarantee that each city will accept his representatives. Rejection does not invalidate the commission or free the workers to invent another method of coercion. They remain under Jesus’ direction in disappointment just as much as they do when hospitality is offered.

Luke 10:11

11‘Even the dust from your city that clings to us, we wipe off against you. Nevertheless know this, that God’s Kingdom has come near to you.’

Arminian

Wiping off the dust testifies against refusal, yet the messengers still announce that the Kingdom has come near. Arminian readers emphasize the seriousness of resisting a real gracious approach. The action marks accountability without authorizing force, and the repeated announcement prevents the city’s refusal from redefining the mission as something insignificant.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a solemn distinction between rejecting the message and canceling the reality it proclaims. Christ’s reign remains authoritative even where his servants are unwelcome. The symbolic testimony leaves judgment with God and releases the workers from measuring faithfulness solely by immediate acceptance or their ability to change hostile hearers.

Luke 10:12

12I tell you, it will be more tolerable in that day for Sodom than for that city.

Arminian

Jesus warns that refusing the Kingdom’s messengers carries grave responsibility, even in comparison with Sodom. Arminian readers emphasize accountability proportionate to the light received. The saying is a call to take gracious visitation seriously, not permission for the workers to pronounce themselves superior or delight in the coming punishment of their hearers.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand the comparison as affirming the justice and differing severity of divine judgment. Greater privilege does not guarantee safety when Christ is refused. Jesus, rather than the messengers’ frustration, establishes the warning’s authority, so it must be delivered with sobriety rather than used to sanctify personal hostility.

Luke 10:13

13“Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida! For if the mighty works had been done in Tyre and Sidon which were done in you, they would have repented long ago, sitting in sackcloth and ashes.

Arminian

Jesus’ lament over Chorazin and Bethsaida makes their response to witnessed mighty works morally serious. Arminian readers emphasize grace resisted and the urgency of repentance while hearing his comparison with Tyre and Sidon. The counterfactual shows Jesus’ knowledge beyond actual events, but does not by itself supply a complete philosophical account of divine foreknowledge.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the truth of Jesus’ statement about what Tyre and Sidon would have done under different circumstances. They distinguish God’s freedom in distributing historical privileges from any injustice in judging their rejection. The text intensifies responsibility for received revelation without explaining every question about why different communities encounter different opportunities.

Luke 10:14

14But it will be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon in the judgment than for you.

Arminian

The comparison with Tyre and Sidon makes clear that privileged exposure to Jesus increases rather than removes responsibility. Arminian readers hear a warning against postponing repentance because one belongs to a religiously favored community. God judges actual responses, and receiving more light is a summons to greater attentiveness rather than a ground for presumption.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize God’s equitable judgment across communities whose opportunities have differed. The saying rules out treating historical privilege as automatic protection from condemnation. Its point is not that notorious wrongdoing elsewhere becomes harmless, but that rejecting Christ amid extraordinary revelation brings a correspondingly weighty reckoning.

Luke 10:15

15You, Capernaum, who are exalted to heaven, will be brought down to Hades.

Arminian

Capernaum’s apparent elevation cannot shield it from the consequences of refusing Jesus. Arminian readers see a warning against resting in proximity to grace while withholding faithful response. The WEB footnote explains Hades as the lower realm of the dead, or Hell; the contrast here emphasizes devastating reversal rather than supplying a complete map of the afterlife.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that no town’s prominence or religious opportunity can overturn Christ’s judgment. Exaltation in earthly estimation becomes humiliation when the King’s visitation is despised. The saying addresses corporate pride and accountability, not a warrant to identify the eternal destiny of each individual who once lived in the city.

Luke 10:16

16Whoever listens to you listens to me, and whoever rejects you rejects me. Whoever rejects me rejects him who sent me.”

Arminian

Receiving or rejecting these commissioned messengers reaches beyond them to Jesus and the Father who sent him. Arminian readers emphasize the seriousness of hearing the gospel through ordinary human agents. This delegated authority belongs to their faithful mission, not to every personal opinion or demand a religious worker might attach to it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a chain of representation grounded in the Father’s sending of the Son. The authority of the proclamation comes from its source rather than the messengers’ prestige. This gives courage to servants without making them infallible in everything they say or authorizing them to claim Christ’s authority for self-serving instructions.

Luke 10:17

17The seventy returned with joy, saying, “Lord, even the demons are subject to us in your name!”

Arminian

The returning seventy rejoice that demons submit in Jesus’ name, recognizing the source of their authority. Arminian readers see the joy of responsive service and the need to bring its results back to the sender. The WEB again reads “seventy”; the chapter footnotes provide no alternate number at this verse.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the powers submit to Christ’s authority rather than to the workers’ independent strength. Their joyful report confirms that the mission extends his victory through appointed agents. Jesus will affirm that triumph while redirecting their deepest confidence away from successful ministry toward the grace of belonging to heaven.

Luke 10:18

18He said to them, “I saw Satan having fallen like lightning from heaven.

Arminian

Jesus interprets the report within a larger picture of Satan’s defeat, using the vivid comparison of lightning falling from heaven. Arminian readers see the mission participating in Christ’s confrontation with evil. The verse encourages confidence in his authority without requiring the reader to reconstruct an exact chronology of every stage of Satan’s fall.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Jesus sees the enemy’s downfall beyond the limited perspective of the returning workers. Their victories belong within his royal triumph, not an accumulation of isolated human achievements. Interpretations differ over the precise temporal reference of the vision, but the context unmistakably connects it with the defeat of hostile spiritual power.

Luke 10:19

19Behold, I give you authority to tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy. Nothing will in any way hurt you.

Arminian

Jesus assures these messengers of authority and protection against the enemy’s power. Arminian readers see encouragement for the mission he has just entrusted to them, not an invitation to reckless tests of faith. The promise should not be used to blame injured believers or to guarantee that disciples will never suffer bodily harm.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the superiority of Christ’s delegated authority over the enemy his servants confront. The protection belongs to his commission and care, rather than to a technique for controlling danger. Reading the promise alongside his teaching about suffering guards against treating it as a universal assurance of physical invulnerability.

Luke 10:20

20Nevertheless, don’t rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice that your names are written in heaven.”

Arminian

Jesus makes belonging to heaven a firmer ground of joy than visible spiritual achievement. Arminian readers connect this assurance with God’s gracious knowledge of those who belong to Christ through faith. Their confidence rests in him rather than their performance, while conditional-perseverance readings understand its enjoyment within continuing trust, not as permission to abandon him.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear assurance grounded in God’s saving election rather than the fluctuating success of ministry. Names written in heaven direct attention to a gift that precedes and outlasts the workers’ accomplishments. The verse does not describe the whole order of salvation, but coheres with confidence that God preserves those he has made his own.

Key contrast Arminian readers ground assurance in God’s gracious election in Christ received through continuing faith; Reformed readers ground it in unconditional election that ensures the perseverance of those truly belonging to Christ.

Luke 10:21

21In that same hour, Jesus rejoiced in the Holy Spirit, and said, “I thank you, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that you have hidden these things from the wise and understanding, and revealed them to little children. Yes, Father, for so it was well-pleasing in your sight.”

Arminian

Jesus praises the Father’s active hiding and revealing, so understanding is a gift rather than a prize for intellectual achievement. Arminian readers affirm the need for prior illuminating grace and commonly relate the contrast to receptive humility and resistant pride. This approach must still reckon with God’s initiative, not reduce the verse to people discovering truth unaided.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the Father’s good pleasure as the explicit basis for revelation to the childlike. Saving knowledge is not distributed according to intellectual distinction or earned receptivity. The contrast supports sovereign grace that humbles human boasting, without making intelligence itself sinful or suggesting that lack of education automatically constitutes faith.

Key contrast Arminian readings commonly relate gracious revelation and judicial hiding to humility and resistance under enabling grace; Reformed readings emphasize the Father’s sovereign good pleasure as decisive in granting saving understanding.

Luke 10:22

22Turning to the disciples, he said, “All things have been delivered to me by my Father. No one knows who the Son is, except the Father, and who the Father is, except the Son, and he to whomever the Son desires to reveal him.”

Arminian

The Father and Son uniquely know one another, and knowledge of the Father comes through the Son’s chosen revelation. Arminian readers affirm that no one reaches God by unaided ability and understand Christ’s revealing grace as genuinely enabling faith without making the response irresistible. The verse asserts the Son’s freedom, while the wider doctrinal account requires more than this sentence.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize both the Son’s unique knowledge of the Father and his effective freedom to reveal him. Saving understanding therefore rests on sovereign grace rather than autonomous human discovery. The statement supports particular, effectual revelation without reducing the Son to a mere messenger who lacks the Father’s divine fullness or authority.

Key contrast Arminian readers affirm necessary revelation through grace that enables but does not irresistibly determine faith; Reformed readers understand the Son’s saving revelation as effectually bringing its recipients to know the Father.

Luke 10:23

23Turning to the disciples, he said privately, “Blessed are the eyes which see the things that you see,

Arminian

Jesus privately calls the disciples blessed because they see what his ministry is bringing to fulfillment. Arminian readers emphasize gratitude for received revelation rather than superiority over others. Their privilege calls for a faithful response, especially because the chapter has already warned that exposure to great works must not become an excuse for presumption.

Reformed

Reformed readers locate this blessedness in God’s gracious placement of the disciples within the time of fulfillment. What they see is a gift of redemptive history rather than evidence of greater natural insight. Their joy should therefore be humble, centered on the person revealed rather than their distinction as witnesses.

Luke 10:24

24for I tell you that many prophets and kings desired to see the things which you see, and didn’t see them, and to hear the things which you hear, and didn’t hear them.”

Arminian

The disciples receive what prophets and kings longed to see and hear, placing their experience within a history of expectation. Arminian readers see the privilege of living at this point in God’s saving purpose. The contrast concerns the arrival of fulfillment, not a claim that earlier faithful people lacked every genuine knowledge of God.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the unity and unfolding character of God’s revelation across generations. Earlier desire and present sight belong to one saving story that reaches a decisive stage in Jesus. The disciples’ greater historical privilege rests on God’s timing and generosity, so it should produce gratitude rather than boasting over those who came before.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
