Passage

Luke 7

Verses 1–17 · WEB

After teaching about faithful hearing and obedience, Jesus heals a centurion’s servant and raises a widow’s only son at Nain. A Gentile’s remarkable faith and the Lord’s unrequested compassion reveal authority that crosses distance, social boundaries, and death itself.

Luke 7:1

1After he had finished speaking in the hearing of the people, he entered into Capernaum.

Arminian

The sermon gives way to an encounter in Capernaum. Arminian readers see Jesus’ teaching embodied in mercy toward a household outside Israel’s usual boundaries.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect the preceding teaching with Christ’s acts of mercy. His arrival prepares a demonstration of the authority on which disciples may rely.

Luke 7:2

2A certain centurion’s servant, who was dear to him, was sick and at the point of death.

Arminian

The centurion values his dying servant, whose need he cannot remedy by command. Arminian readers see intercession beginning in concern for someone vulnerable.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise notice the servant’s value to his master. Neither military power nor social position can secure life; the household needs Christ’s mercy.

Luke 7:3

3When he heard about Jesus, he sent to him elders of the Jews, asking him to come and save his servant.

Arminian

Hearing about Jesus leads the centurion to seek help through Jewish elders. Arminian readers see a response to testimony that turns toward Christ in dependent petition.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the importance of hearing and intercession as means of seeking Christ. The elders carry a request for rescue, not an explanation of how grace originates.

Luke 7:4

4When they came to Jesus, they begged him earnestly, saying, “He is worthy for you to do this for him,

Arminian

The elders appeal to the centurion’s worthiness. Arminian readers distinguish their commendation of his generosity from the humble dependence he will express himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish a favorable reputation from a claim on divine mercy. The elders’ assessment is not Jesus’ declaration that healing can be earned.

Luke 7:5

5for he loves our nation, and he built our synagogue for us.”

Arminian

The centurion’s love for the nation and synagogue support explain the elders’ affection. Arminian readers see generosity across an ethnic boundary without making benefaction the price of healing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize these concrete acts of kindness. They commend his conduct while reserving the narrative’s emphasis for his faith in Christ’s authority.

Luke 7:6

6Jesus went with them. When he was now not far from the house, the centurion sent friends to him, saying to him, “Lord, don’t trouble yourself, for I am not worthy for you to come under my roof.

Arminian

Jesus approaches, but the centurion disclaims worthiness to receive him. Arminian readers see humility that welcomes mercy without insisting on entitlement.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the contrast with the elders’ appeal to merit. The centurion depends on the Lord’s willingness and authority rather than his own standing.

Luke 7:7

7Therefore I didn’t even think myself worthy to come to you; but say the word, and my servant will be healed.

Arminian

The centurion trusts that Jesus’ word is sufficient even at a distance. Arminian readers see genuine faith receiving a gift it cannot accomplish or deserve.

Reformed

Reformed readers share this account of dependent faith. Confidence rests in the person who speaks, not in the strength of the centurion’s feelings or a ritual.

Luke 7:8

8For I also am a man placed under authority, having under myself soldiers. I tell this one, ‘Go!’ and he goes; and to another, ‘Come!’ and he comes; and to my servant, ‘Do this,’ and he does it.”

Arminian

His experience of authority helps him recognize the effectiveness of Jesus’ command. Arminian readers see trust expressed through an everyday analogy, not a technique for controlling Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the authority that makes the analogy work. Commands are obeyed within the centurion’s sphere, while Jesus’ greater authority extends even to disease.

Luke 7:9

9When Jesus heard these things, he marveled at him, and turned and said to the multitude who followed him, “I tell you, I have not found such great faith, no, not in Israel.”

Arminian

Jesus commends exceptional faith in a Gentile, challenging assumptions that privilege guarantees trust. Arminian readers see an anticipation of Gentile inclusion through faith rather than ethnic entitlement.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see grace reaching beyond Israel’s boundaries. The comparison rebukes presumption without rejecting Israel as a people or claiming that all Israelites lack faith.

Luke 7:10

10Those who were sent, returning to the house, found that the servant who had been sick was well.

Arminian

The messengers find the servant well, confirming Jesus’ word. Arminian readers see mercy answering intercession without physical contact between Jesus and the sick man.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the effective result of Christ’s authority. Both traditions locate the healing power in Jesus, not in the centurion’s rank or the messengers’ efforts.

Luke 7:11

11Soon afterwards, he went to a city called Nain. Many of his disciples, along with a great multitude, went with him.

Arminian

Jesus approaches Nain with disciples and a large crowd. Arminian readers see the scene widening from threatened death to a funeral already underway.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the movement toward a public encounter with death. The coming miracle discloses Christ’s compassion without a recorded request for it.

Luke 7:12

12Now when he came near to the gate of the city, behold, one who was dead was carried out, the only born son of his mother, and she was a widow. Many people of the city were with her.

Arminian

The dead man is his widowed mother’s only son, making her loss especially grievous. Arminian readers see a particular bereavement, not merely an occasion for displaying power.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to the mother’s sorrow. The WEB’s only born describes her one and only son; the account does not require speculation about her future circumstances.

Luke 7:13

13When the Lord saw her, he had compassion on her and said to her, “Don’t cry.”

Arminian

Jesus’ compassion precedes any recorded petition or confession. Arminian readers see grace taking the initiative, not a reward for making the right request.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the Lord’s freely initiated compassion. His comfort will be joined to action; neither tradition should turn this scene into a demand that mourners suppress grief.

Luke 7:14

14He came near and touched the coffin, and the bearers stood still. He said, “Young man, I tell you, arise!”

Arminian

Jesus halts the procession and commands the young man to arise. Arminian readers affirm his life-giving authority without making the miracle settle every question about conversion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see an effective command where human help has ended. The restoration displays power over death, though it is not itself a full account of regeneration.

Luke 7:15

15He who was dead sat up and began to speak. Then he gave him to his mother.

Arminian

The young man speaks, and Jesus restores him to his mother. Arminian readers see resurrection power serving tender, personal restoration rather than spectacle alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress restored life and the mother’s reunion with her son. The gift answers a grief the crowd could share but could not remove.

Luke 7:16

16Fear took hold of all, and they glorified God, saying, “A great prophet has arisen among us!” and, “God has visited his people!”

Arminian

The crowd glorifies God and recognizes a prophetic visitation. Arminian readers see a fitting response to mercy, while noting that their words do not exhaust Jesus’ identity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that the miracle reveals God’s saving presence among his people. Reverent fear leads here to praise rather than rejection.

Luke 7:17

17This report went out concerning him in the whole of Judea and in all the surrounding region.

Arminian

News spreads widely, preparing for John’s disciples to report these events. Arminian readers see testimony carrying the effects of Jesus’ mercy beyond the immediate witnesses.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the link between the public miracle and the coming question. Reports spread, but the following passage still calls for discerning Jesus’ identity.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
