Passage

Luke 7

Verses 18–35 · WEB

John’s messengers ask whether Jesus is the coming one, and Jesus answers through works of restoration and an Isaiah-shaped proclamation of good news. He honors John’s preparatory ministry while contrasting repentant reception with resistance to God’s counsel, ending with the vindication of divine wisdom.

Luke 7:18

18The disciples of John told him about all these things.

Arminian

John’s disciples report Jesus’ works, linking their question to the preceding restorations. Arminian readers see uncertainty brought into conversation with what Jesus does.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to this connection. The report prompts inquiry rather than permitting readers to assume every hearer already understands the Messiah’s mission.

Luke 7:19

19John, calling to himself two of his disciples, sent them to Jesus, saying, “Are you the one who is coming, or should we look for another?”

Arminian

John sends a direct question about Jesus’ identity. Arminian readers allow its force without pretending to know whether it chiefly expresses John’s uncertainty or serves his disciples.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise avoid assigning an unreported motive. The question is carried to Jesus, showing that perplexity can become a petition for clarification rather than final rejection.

Luke 7:20

20When the men had come to him, they said, “John the Baptizer has sent us to you, saying, ‘Are you he who comes, or should we look for another?’ ”

Arminian

The messengers repeat John’s question faithfully. Arminian readers see a request for assurance answered through Jesus’ words and works rather than rebuke of honest inquiry.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that Jesus is asked to identify himself as the expected one. The repetition keeps the issue focused on his mission, not the messengers’ opinions.

Luke 7:21

21In that hour he cured many of diseases and plagues and evil spirits; and to many who were blind he gave sight.

Arminian

Jesus’ healing and deliverance provide the setting for his answer. Arminian readers see mercy addressing suffering while also bearing witness to who he is.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the answer is grounded in what the messengers witness. These kingdom signs are not a guarantee that every illness will be removed immediately.

Luke 7:22

22Jesus answered them, “Go and tell John the things which you have seen and heard: that the blind receive their sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, and the poor have good news preached to them.

Arminian

The answer evokes Isaiah’s promises of restoration and good news, especially Isaiah 35:5–6 and 61:1. Arminian readers see fulfilled hope inviting trust and mercy reaching the poor.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see an Isaiah-shaped account of the Messiah’s work. Jesus identifies his mission through deeds and proclamation rather than conforming to every expectation attached to the coming one.

Luke 7:23

23Blessed is he who finds no occasion for stumbling in me.”

Arminian

Jesus blesses the person who does not stumble over him. Arminian readers hear an invitation to continuing trust when his way differs from anticipated displays of power.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear a gracious summons to rest in the Messiah he reveals himself to be. Faith need not deny the difficulty of disappointed expectations.

Luke 7:24

24When John’s messengers had departed, he began to tell the multitudes about John, “What did you go out into the wilderness to see? A reed shaken by the wind?

Arminian

Jesus honors John after the messengers leave. Arminian readers see that his question does not erase the integrity of his prophetic ministry or justify contempt for him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the public defense of John. The image of a wind-shaken reed challenges the crowd to remember the steadiness of the messenger they heard.

Luke 7:25

25But what did you go out to see? A man clothed in soft clothing? Behold, those who are gorgeously dressed and live delicately are in kings’ courts.

Arminian

John’s ministry contrasts with courtly luxury. Arminian readers see that prophetic credibility does not depend on comfort, impressive clothing, or rulers’ approval.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the contrast between prophetic vocation and royal ease. Jesus asks the crowd to assess John by his calling rather than worldly measures of success.

Luke 7:26

26But what did you go out to see? A prophet? Yes, I tell you, and much more than a prophet.

Arminian

Jesus identifies John as more than an ordinary prophet. Arminian readers see his distinctive preparatory role, not a claim that other prophets were unimportant.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize John’s place at the kingdom’s arrival. His greatness lies in the task God entrusted to him, not in wealth or social prestige.

Luke 7:27

27This is he of whom it is written, ‘Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way before you.’

Arminian

The citation of Malachi 3:1 presents John as the messenger preparing Jesus’ way. Arminian readers see God’s promise meeting a ministry that calls people to repentance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the continuity of God’s purpose and its fulfillment in Jesus. John’s work serves the coming Lord rather than establishing a competing allegiance.

Luke 7:28

28“For I tell you, among those who are born of women there is not a greater prophet than John the Baptizer; yet he who is least in God’s Kingdom is greater than he.”

Arminian

John’s greatness is affirmed, yet the arriving kingdom brings greater privilege. Arminian readers distinguish this new position from a claim that John lacked faith or moral worth.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the comparison in relation to the kingdom’s arrival. Jesus magnifies the gift of participation without inviting contempt for the forerunner.

Luke 7:29

29When all the people and the tax collectors heard this, they declared God to be just, having been baptized with John’s baptism.

Arminian

Those who received John’s baptism acknowledge God’s justice. Arminian readers see repentant response vindicating God’s call, including among tax collectors dismissed by respectable opinion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the contrast between humble reception and religious self-assurance. Their acknowledgment does not make God just; it confesses that his summons is right.

Luke 7:30

30But the Pharisees and the lawyers rejected the counsel of God, not being baptized by him themselves.

Arminian

The Pharisees and lawyers refuse God’s counsel as it concerns themselves by rejecting John’s baptism. Arminian readers see a real divine summons resisted, not merely a lack of information.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish the revealed call to repentance, which people culpably reject, from God’s secret decree and effectual renewal. Their refusal does not imply that God’s ultimate purpose has failed.

Key contrast Both affirm a genuine divine call to repentance and culpable refusal. Arminian readers stress that God’s gracious intention for these hearers can be resisted; Reformed readers distinguish resistance to his revealed counsel from the efficacy of his decree and renewing call.

Luke 7:31

31“To what then should I compare the people of this generation? What are they like?

Arminian

Jesus turns from contrasting responses to a searching description of his generation. Arminian readers see the need to examine resistance rather than excuse every refusal.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear a diagnosis of resistant hearers. The WEB begins with Jesus’ question, without the introductory wording mentioned in its textual footnote.

Luke 7:32

32They are like children who sit in the marketplace and call to one another, saying, ‘We piped to you, and you didn’t dance. We mourned, and you didn’t weep.’

Arminian

The children complain when others will neither dance nor mourn on their terms. Arminian readers see hearers demanding control over the form of God’s appeal.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see inconsistent rejection rather than a sincere search for the right messenger. The following verses show opposite styles meeting the same refusal.

Luke 7:33

33For John the Baptizer came neither eating bread nor drinking wine, and you say, ‘He has a demon.’

Arminian

John’s austerity is dismissed as demonic. Arminian readers see prejudice turning a call to repentance into grounds for accusation rather than a reason to listen.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the perversity of discrediting the messenger instead of facing his message. Jesus reports the accusation; he does not endorse it.

Luke 7:34

34The Son of Man has come eating and drinking, and you say, ‘Behold, a glutton and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!’

Arminian

Jesus’ table fellowship draws the opposite accusation. Arminian readers see mercy misrepresented as indulgence, while friendship offers hope to people treated as outsiders.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish receiving sinners from approving sin. Neither John’s restraint nor Jesus’ hospitality satisfies hearers determined to reject their summons.

Luke 7:35

35Wisdom is justified by all her children.”

Arminian

Wisdom is vindicated by her children: lives shaped by God’s message answer the accusations. Arminian readers see repentant response bearing witness to God’s wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise point to the fruit of divine wisdom rather than hostile critics’ approval. The saying affirms fitting outcomes, not that every popular judgment is reliable.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
