Passage

Luke 7

Verses 36–50 · WEB

At a Pharisee’s table, a woman known as a sinner honors Jesus with tears, kisses, and ointment while the host silently questions him. Jesus’ parable of two forgiven debtors interprets her love in relation to mercy, and his assurance of forgiveness directs her to faith and peace rather than earned acceptance.

Luke 7:36

36One of the Pharisees invited him to eat with him. He entered into the Pharisee’s house and sat at the table.

Arminian

Jesus accepts a Pharisee’s invitation, showing that his welcome crosses social groups. Arminian readers see an opportunity for host and unexpected guest alike to encounter mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that Jesus enters without endorsing every judgment of the owner. The meal exposes self-assurance and grateful dependence.

Luke 7:37

37Behold, a woman in the city who was a sinner, when she knew that he was reclining in the Pharisee’s house, brought an alabaster jar of ointment.

Arminian

The woman comes with ointment despite her reputation as a sinner. Arminian readers see movement toward Jesus that the account does not reduce to her past.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish her known reputation from an invented biography. Luke does not identify her here as Mary Magdalene or specify the sins behind the description.

Luke 7:38

38Standing behind at his feet weeping, she began to wet his feet with her tears, and she wiped them with the hair of her head, kissed his feet, and anointed them with the ointment.

Arminian

Her tears, kisses, and anointing express costly affection. Arminian readers see devotion that will be interpreted through the following parable of freely canceled debt.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand these actions in relation to forgiveness, not as payment for it. Her gratitude contrasts with the host’s restrained hospitality.

Luke 7:39

39Now when the Pharisee who had invited him saw it, he said to himself, “This man, if he were a prophet, would have perceived who and what kind of woman this is who touches him, that she is a sinner.”

Arminian

Simon assumes a prophet would distance himself from such a woman. Arminian readers see a failure to recognize that knowledge of sin can lead to mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that Simon misreads both Jesus and the woman. Jesus answers without accepting his equation of holiness with refusing her touch.

Luke 7:40

40Jesus answered him, “Simon, I have something to tell you.” He said, “Teacher, say on.”

Arminian

Jesus addresses Simon personally and invites him to listen. Arminian readers see correction making room for reconsideration rather than merely exposing him to ridicule.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise notice that Jesus teaches the critic as well as receiving the woman. The parable turns Simon’s own judgment back toward himself.

Luke 7:41

41“A certain lender had two debtors. The one owed five hundred denarii, and the other fifty.

Arminian

Differing debts establish a contrast in magnitude, not between people who can and cannot repay. Arminian readers see a picture of shared need.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress both debtors’ dependence. The figures serve the question about love, not a precise calculation of Simon’s and the woman’s guilt.

Luke 7:42

42When they couldn’t pay, he forgave them both. Which of them therefore will love him most?”

Arminian

Neither debtor can pay, and the lender freely forgives both. Arminian readers see forgiveness preceding grateful love, ruling out love as a purchase price.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the lender’s initiative and the resulting gratitude. The question concerns love awakened by canceled debt, not effort required to earn release.

Luke 7:43

43Simon answered, “He, I suppose, to whom he forgave the most.” He said to him, “You have judged correctly.”

Arminian

Simon identifies the greater debtor as the one likely to love more. Arminian readers see him acknowledge the principle explaining the woman’s devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that Jesus accepts his answer. The contrast challenges Simon to recognize the meaning of actions he has silently condemned.

Luke 7:44

44Turning to the woman, he said to Simon, “Do you see this woman? I entered into your house, and you gave me no water for my feet, but she has wet my feet with her tears, and wiped them with the hair of her head.

Arminian

Jesus asks Simon to see the woman beyond her reputation, contrasting her tears with his omitted water. Arminian readers see gratitude expressed in concrete care.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize a reversal of the host’s evaluation. Jesus compares responses to his presence; he does not present hospitality as a fee for absolution.

Luke 7:45

45You gave me no kiss, but she, since the time I came in, has not ceased to kiss my feet.

Arminian

Her persistent kisses contrast with Simon’s lack of greeting. Arminian readers see continuing affection made intelligible by the parable of forgiveness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see deep gratitude. The contrast exposes the host’s limited reception without authorizing invented motives or claims about customs beyond the account.

Luke 7:46

46You didn’t anoint my head with oil, but she has anointed my feet with ointment.

Arminian

Her ointment exceeds the oil Simon did not offer. Arminian readers see generous love whose meaning is tied to mercy rather than merit.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the action as evidence of gratitude. What the host regards as objectionable becomes a visible contrast with his own welcome.

Luke 7:47

47Therefore I tell you, her sins, which are many, are forgiven, for she loved much. But one to whom little is forgiven, loves little.”

Arminian

Her great love accompanies forgiveness of many sins. Arminian readers commonly understand love as evidence of mercy received, given the canceled debts and Jesus’ concluding word about faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take love as the fruit and sign of forgiveness, not its meritorious cause. The causal wording must be read with the parable, not isolated to make pardon purchasable.

Luke 7:48

48He said to her, “Your sins are forgiven.”

Arminian

Jesus directly assures the woman that her sins are forgiven. Arminian readers see grace addressing her personally rather than leaving her identity fixed by others’ judgments.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize Christ’s authoritative assurance. His declaration brings pardon into the open; it does not announce that tears or ointment have bought it.

Luke 7:49

49Those who sat at the table with him began to say to themselves, “Who is this who even forgives sins?”

Arminian

The guests recognize the startling authority involved in forgiving sins. Arminian readers see attention turning from the woman’s reputation to Jesus’ identity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the controversy concerns who Jesus is. The meal exposes the inadequacy of treating him as merely another guest.

Luke 7:50

50He said to the woman, “Your faith has saved you. Go in peace.”

Arminian

Jesus names faith, not costly ointment, as the means of receiving salvation. Arminian readers see trusting reception of grace issuing in love and peace.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish faith’s receiving role from merit in faith itself. Both ground her peace in Christ’s pardon, not public approval or perfect devotion.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
