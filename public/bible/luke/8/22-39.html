Passage

Luke 8

Verses 22–39 · WEB

Jesus stills a dangerous storm and delivers a man tormented by many demons across the lake from Galilee. The disciples’ fear and the region’s request that Jesus depart contrast with the restored man’s desire to follow and his commission to tell what God has done.

Luke 8:22

22Now on one of those days, he entered into a boat, himself and his disciples, and he said to them, “Let’s go over to the other side of the lake.” So they launched out.

Arminian

Jesus directs the crossing and accompanies his disciples. Arminian readers note that obedience to his direction does not shield them from the danger ahead.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the storm occurring on a journey Jesus initiated. His presence grounds dependence, not a promise to avoid frightening circumstances.

Luke 8:23

23But as they sailed, he fell asleep. A wind storm came down on the lake, and they were taking on dangerous amounts of water.

Arminian

Jesus sleeps while the boat takes on dangerous water. Arminian readers see genuine human weariness and real peril, not an imaginary test without cause for alarm.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm his humanity and the disciples’ danger. The scene prepares revelation of his authority rather than denying that faithful people face distress.

Luke 8:24

24They came to him and awoke him, saying, “Master, Master, we are dying!” He awoke and rebuked the wind and the raging of the water; then they ceased, and it was calm.

Arminian

The disciples cry for help, and Jesus stills wind and water. Arminian readers see their appeal met by authority they cannot exercise themselves.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize Christ’s effective command over creation. The source’s Psalm 107:29 reference highlights the resonance with God’s calming the sea without altering the printed text.

Luke 8:25

25He said to them, “Where is your faith?” Being afraid, they marveled, saying to one another, “Who is this then, that he commands even the winds and the water, and they obey him?”

Arminian

Jesus challenges their faith, and they marvel at his authority. Arminian readers distinguish fearful, faltering trust needing correction from a simple absence of discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ instructing his own amid weakness. Their question directs attention beyond their emotions to the one whom wind and water obey.

Luke 8:26

26Then they arrived at the country of the Gadarenes, which is opposite Galilee.

Arminian

The crossing reaches the region opposite Galilee. Arminian readers see mercy extending beyond familiar settings; the printed WEB place-name Gadarenes is retained.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise follow the selected edition rather than substitute another place-name. The transition introduces deliverance that will lead to local testimony about Jesus.

Luke 8:27

27When Jesus stepped ashore, a certain man out of the city who had demons for a long time met him. He wore no clothes, and didn’t live in a house, but in the tombs.

Arminian

The afflicted man lives unclothed among tombs. Arminian readers see a person needing restoration, not someone reduced to the hostile powers tormenting him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to the human suffering. Luke describes this particular case; the verse should not be used to label every mental illness as possession.

Luke 8:28

28When he saw Jesus, he cried out and fell down before him, and with a loud voice said, “What do I have to do with you, Jesus, you Son of the Most High God? I beg you, don’t torment me!”

Arminian

The cry recognizes Jesus as Son of the Most High God yet fears his authority. Arminian readers distinguish recognition of identity from trusting reception of mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that accurate speech about Jesus need not be saving faith. The encounter reveals supremacy without endorsing the hostile spirit’s account of his intentions.

Luke 8:29

29For Jesus was commanding the unclean spirit to come out of the man. For the unclean spirit had often seized the man. He was kept under guard and bound with chains and fetters. Breaking the bonds apart, he was driven by the demon into the desert.

Arminian

Restraints have failed, but Jesus commands the spirit to leave. Arminian readers see mercy reaching someone whose suffering exceeds the community’s ability to contain it.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s authority where chains and guards fail. The destructive power is real, but it is not equal to the Lord confronting it.

Luke 8:30

30Jesus asked him, “What is your name?” He said, “Legion,” for many demons had entered into him.

Arminian

Legion indicates many demons, as Luke explains. Arminian readers see the affliction’s scale without treating the man as beyond Jesus’ help.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise locate hope in Christ’s authority, not a numerical reconstruction. The account supplies the needed explanation without inviting speculative identifications.

Luke 8:31

31They begged him that he would not command them to go into the abyss.

Arminian

The demons plead not to be sent into the abyss. Arminian readers see hostile powers acknowledging judgment beyond their ability to control.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize their subordination to Jesus. The verse gives no mandate for constructing an elaborate account of the unseen world beyond its stated details.

Luke 8:32

32Now there was there a herd of many pigs feeding on the mountain, and they begged him that he would allow them to enter into those. Then he allowed them.

Arminian

The demons need permission to enter the pigs. Arminian readers affirm Christ’s authority while distinguishing his permission from moral approval of destructive evil.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that hostile powers are not independent rivals. Both should avoid pretending that this verse explains every reason Jesus permits the subsequent loss.

Luke 8:33

33The demons came out of the man and entered into the pigs, and the herd rushed down the steep bank into the lake and were drowned.

Arminian

The herd’s destruction makes the deliverance’s violent setting unmistakable. Arminian readers see rescue from destructive powers without minimizing the loss involved.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hold together deliverance and destruction. Luke reports events without explaining every question about the owners or authorizing indifference to their loss.

Luke 8:34

34When those who fed them saw what had happened, they fled and told it in the city and in the country.

Arminian

The herders report events in town and countryside. Arminian readers see news bringing others to confront both the restored man and their response to Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the public consequences. The report prepares a communal reaction, but hearing about power does not by itself produce welcome.

Luke 8:35

35People went out to see what had happened. They came to Jesus and found the man from whom the demons had gone out, sitting at Jesus’ feet, clothed and in his right mind; and they were afraid.

Arminian

The man sits clothed and sane at Jesus’ feet while others fear. Arminian readers see dignity and relationship restored in contrast to former isolation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the visible transformation. The crowd’s fear shows that witnessing mercy and power can unsettle people rather than immediately produce trust.

Luke 8:36

36Those who saw it told them how he who had been possessed by demons was healed.

Arminian

Witnesses explain the healing to those who arrive. Arminian readers see testimony helping the community understand the transformation before choosing its response.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that people receive an account of rescue, not merely disturbance. Their refusal follows this report of the man’s restoration.

Luke 8:37

37All the people of the surrounding country of the Gadarenes asked him to depart from them, for they were very much afraid. Then he entered into the boat and returned.

Arminian

The people ask Jesus to leave because of fear, and he departs. Arminian readers see a sobering refusal of his presence despite evident mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm their responsibility for rejection. Departure does not end witness, since the delivered man will remain to tell what happened.

Luke 8:38

38But the man from whom the demons had gone out begged him that he might go with him, but Jesus sent him away, saying,

Arminian

The healed man asks to accompany Jesus but receives a different calling. Arminian readers see grateful devotion directed into local service rather than a preferred path.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish refusing this request from rejecting the man. Restored life becomes a vocation shaped by Christ’s command rather than personal preference alone.

Luke 8:39

39“Return to your house, and declare what great things God has done for you.” He went his way, proclaiming throughout the whole city what great things Jesus had done for him.

Arminian

Sent home to declare God’s work, the man proclaims what Jesus has done. Arminian readers see gratitude becoming accessible testimony among those who knew his distress.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the link between God’s action and Jesus’ deliverance. Witness is commissioned here, unlike occasions requiring silence; obedience follows the particular instruction.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
