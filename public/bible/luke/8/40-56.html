Passage

Luke 8

Verses 40–56 · WEB

Jairus pleads for his dying daughter while a woman with twelve years of bleeding seeks healing amid the crowd. Jesus brings the woman’s restoration into the open and answers the child’s death with a life-giving command, calling for trust without turning these particular miracles into a universal promise of immediate healing.

Luke 8:40

40When Jesus returned, the multitude welcomed him, for they were all waiting for him.

Arminian

The returning crowd welcomes Jesus, unlike those across the lake. Arminian readers see different responses without assuming every welcoming person already has mature faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish welcome from a full account of spiritual condition. The scene moves toward two desperate needs within the waiting multitude.

Luke 8:41

41Behold, a man named Jairus came. He was a ruler of the synagogue. He fell down at Jesus’ feet and begged him to come into his house,

Arminian

Jairus, a synagogue ruler, falls before Jesus to plead for help. Arminian readers see status yielding to dependence when his daughter’s life is threatened.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize humble petition rather than confidence in office. Social standing supplies no substitute for Christ’s mercy.

Luke 8:42

42for he had an only born daughter, about twelve years of age, and she was dying. But as he went, the multitudes pressed against him.

Arminian

Jairus’s only daughter is dying as the crowd slows the journey. Arminian readers see urgency and the painful tension when another need interrupts.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to this child and the pressure of delay. The WEB’s only born means his one and only daughter, not a special spiritual rank.

Luke 8:43

43A woman who had a flow of blood for twelve years, who had spent all her living on physicians and could not be healed by any,

Arminian

Long bleeding and exhausted resources reveal the woman’s need. Arminian readers retain the WEB’s statement about physicians without making it a condemnation of medical care.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see failed remedies highlighting helplessness, not a rule against treatment. The selected source prints the full expenditure clause, so it is retained rather than shortened.

Luke 8:44

44came behind him and touched the fringe of his cloak. Immediately the flow of her blood stopped.

Arminian

Her touch is followed by immediate healing. Arminian readers see trusting approach to Jesus, not a magical garment; fringe or tassel concerns clothing terminology.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise locate power in Christ. Contact occasions this gift; it is not a technique obliging God to cure every illness in the same way.

Luke 8:45

45Jesus said, “Who touched me?” When all denied it, Peter and those with him said, “Master, the multitudes press and jostle you, and you say, ‘Who touched me?’”

Arminian

Jesus distinguishes a particular touch from the crowd’s jostling. Arminian readers see a hidden encounter brought into personal recognition rather than left anonymous.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish proximity from this encounter. The WEB includes Peter and those with him and the repeated question; both are retained as printed.

Luke 8:46

46But Jesus said, “Someone did touch me, for I perceived that power has gone out of me.”

Arminian

Jesus identifies the touch through power going out from him. Arminian readers see personal ministry, not an impersonal cure obtained from an object.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that power belongs to Jesus. The statement should not become a theory that his strength is depleted or that he heals involuntarily.

Luke 8:47

47When the woman saw that she was not hidden, she came trembling; and falling down before him declared to him in the presence of all the people the reason why she had touched him, and how she was healed immediately.

Arminian

The woman trembles and publicly tells what happened. Arminian readers see vulnerable honesty met by reassurance rather than condemnation for approaching in need.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize movement from hidden suffering to acknowledged restoration. Her confession reports the cure; it does not purchase a healing already received.

Luke 8:48

48He said to her, “Daughter, cheer up. Your faith has made you well. Go in peace.”

Arminian

Jesus calls her daughter and sends her in peace. Arminian readers see faith as receptive trust, not a meritorious force, and healing as Christ’s gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize Christ as the power faith receives. Neither tradition should turn this woman’s assurance into an accusation that every unhealed person lacks faith.

Luke 8:49

49While he still spoke, one from the ruler of the synagogue’s house came, saying to him, “Your daughter is dead. Don’t trouble the Teacher.”

Arminian

The report of death makes Jairus’s request appear too late. Arminian readers see the severity of the interruption without treating the messenger’s conclusion as Jesus’ limit.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the message’s ordinary human finality. Jesus confronts that conclusion without denying the reality of the family’s bereavement.

Luke 8:50

50But Jesus hearing it, answered him, “Don’t be afraid. Only believe, and she will be healed.”

Arminian

Jesus calls Jairus from fear to faith with a particular promise about his daughter. Arminian readers see trust sustained amid devastating news by Christ’s word.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise ground hope in that promise, not faith as an independent power. This assurance is not a universal guarantee that every dying child will recover now.

Luke 8:51

51When he came to the house, he didn’t allow anyone to enter in, except Peter, John, James, the father of the child, and her mother.

Arminian

Jesus limits entry to three disciples and the parents. Arminian readers see a restrained, personal setting rather than a performance designed for publicity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note his control of the encounter. The witnesses serve this event; the text does not explain every reason for naming these three disciples.

Luke 8:52

52All were weeping and mourning her, but he said, “Don’t weep. She isn’t dead, but sleeping.”

Arminian

Jesus describes death as sleep in view of his coming action. Arminian readers read this with the death report, not as denial that the girl died.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand death as not final before Christ’s life-giving authority. His reassurance rests on what he will do, not on minimizing grief.

Luke 8:53

53They were ridiculing him, knowing that she was dead.

Arminian

The mourners ridicule Jesus because they know the girl died. Arminian readers see unbelief made explicit while the account confirms the reality of death.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that restoration does not depend on the crowd’s confidence. Their mockery cannot overturn Christ’s authority, though it remains an inappropriate response.

Luke 8:54

54But he put them all outside, and taking her by the hand, he called, saying, “Child, arise!”

Arminian

Jesus takes the child’s hand and commands her to arise. Arminian readers see tenderness joined to authority the child cannot supply for herself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the effective, life-giving word. Both affirm the miracle without using it alone to settle every disputed question about the order of salvation.

Luke 8:55

55Her spirit returned, and she rose up immediately. He commanded that something be given to her to eat.

Arminian

Her spirit returns, she rises, and Jesus orders food. Arminian readers see bodily restoration and practical care joined, not an appearance without ordinary needs.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize real, immediate restoration. Concern for nourishment preserves the child’s humanity rather than treating her as an exhibit.

Luke 8:56

56Her parents were amazed, but he commanded them to tell no one what had been done.

Arminian

The parents are astonished but told not to publicize the event. Arminian readers see obedience differing here from the former demoniac’s commission.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise resist inventing a complete explanation for silence. The contrast with verse 39 shows that Christ’s particular command governs response, not a uniform publicity rule.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
