Passage

Luke 22

Verses 54–71 · WEB

Peter’s three denials fulfill Jesus’ warning and lead to bitter tears, while Jesus endures abuse and faces the council. His promise of exaltation stands against the verdict of his opponents. Both traditions take Peter’s sin and repentance seriously and locate restoration in grace rather than self-confidence. The narrative distinguishes hearing Christ from believing him and gives no warrant for blaming Jewish people collectively for the actions of the named authorities.

Luke 22:54

54They seized him and led him away, and brought him into the high priest’s house. But Peter followed from a distance.

Arminian

Jesus is taken to the high priest’s house while Peter follows at a distance. Arminian readers see conflicted loyalty entering danger, not a detail that by itself establishes final apostasy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the beginning of Peter’s foretold failure. His distance neither excuses the denial nor cancels Christ’s prior promise of intercession and return.

Luke 22:55

55When they had kindled a fire in the middle of the courtyard and had sat down together, Peter sat among them.

Arminian

Peter joins the people around the courtyard fire. Arminian readers attend to the ordinary social setting in which fear will test his promised loyalty.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see temptation unfolding through commonplace pressure rather than only spectacular attack. The narrative invites humility about how easily self-confidence can be exposed.

Luke 22:56

56A certain servant girl saw him as he sat in the light, and looking intently at him, said, “This man also was with him.”

Arminian

A servant girl recognizes Peter’s association with Jesus. Arminian readers see a seemingly modest challenge revealing the need for courage supplied by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that Peter’s bold profession meets an ordinary witness rather than a mighty tribunal. His weakness exposes the limits of unaided resolve.

Luke 22:57

57He denied Jesus, saying, “Woman, I don’t know him.”

Arminian

Peter explicitly denies knowing Jesus. Arminian readers take the sin seriously while reading it within the promised possibility of return through Christ’s gracious help.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise refuse to minimize denial. They distinguish this grievous fall from final loss of faith in light of Christ’s intercession and the restoration he announced.

Luke 22:58

58After a little while someone else saw him and said, “You also are one of them!” But Peter answered, “Man, I am not!”

Arminian

A second identification draws another denial. Arminian readers see how fear can deepen sinful choices, strengthening the call to prayer rather than confidence in a past profession.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize repeated failure in a disciple Christ will restore. Preservation does not mean that genuine believers cannot commit grave and repeated sins.

Luke 22:59

59After about one hour passed, another confidently affirmed, saying, “Truly this man also was with him, for he is a Galilean!”

Arminian

Another witness confidently identifies Peter as a Galilean companion of Jesus. Arminian readers see continuing pressure exposing the difference between intended courage and tested fidelity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the mounting evidence around Peter. The narrative unmasks self-reliance without portraying social pressure as an excuse that erases responsibility.

Luke 22:60

60But Peter said, “Man, I don’t know what you are talking about!” Immediately, while he was still speaking, a rooster crowed.

Arminian

Peter’s third denial is immediately followed by the rooster’s crow. Arminian readers see Jesus’ warning confirmed, leaving no room to excuse the failure as insignificant.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize Christ’s truthful foreknowledge amid Peter’s collapse. The fulfilled prediction does not make the denial blameless; it prepares for the remembered word and repentance.

Luke 22:61

61The Lord turned and looked at Peter. Then Peter remembered the Lord’s word, how he said to him, “Before the rooster crows you will deny me three times.”

Arminian

Jesus’ look recalls his warning to Peter’s mind. Arminian readers see gracious confrontation awakening recognition of sin, without speculating beyond what the narrative says about the look.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ’s remembered word interrupting denial. His preserving care does not leave Peter unchanged but brings his failure into the light.

Luke 22:62

62He went out, and wept bitterly.

Arminian

Peter’s bitter tears mark grief over the denial. Arminian readers recognize repentance as a meaningful turn toward restoration, not a payment that makes forgiveness deserved.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise view his sorrow in light of Christ’s promised intercession. Grace brings the fallen disciple toward return without making tears themselves the ground of acceptance.

Luke 22:63

63The men who held Jesus mocked him and beat him.

Arminian

Jesus is mocked and beaten by those holding him. Arminian readers see the innocent Savior enduring cruelty, not an event that makes his abusers morally innocent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that redemptive purpose does not sanctify the perpetrators’ violence. Christ’s suffering exposes sin even as he continues the mission through which sinners are saved.

Luke 22:64

64Having blindfolded him, they struck him on the face and asked him, “Prophesy! Who is the one who struck you?”

Arminian

The captors mock Jesus’ prophetic identity while concealing their blows. Arminian readers see abusive contempt, not a legitimate demand that God prove himself on hostile terms.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the irony that his words about Peter have just been fulfilled. Mockery cannot discredit the Lord’s truthful knowledge or justify the violence.

Luke 22:65

65They spoke many other things against him, insulting him.

Arminian

Further insults compound the abuse. Arminian readers recognize the seriousness of degrading speech and resist treating contempt as harmless when directed at a hated opponent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see sin expressed in words as well as blows. Christ’s patient endurance does not turn his captors’ insults into morally acceptable conduct.

Luke 22:66

66As soon as it was day, the assembly of the elders of the people were gathered together, both chief priests and scribes, and they led him away into their council, saying,

Arminian

The elders, chief priests, and scribes gather at daybreak for council. Arminian readers distinguish institutional authority from a righteous verdict without extending blame to an entire people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize that official procedure can serve hostility rather than truth. The named leadership’s actions do not justify collective condemnation of Jewish people across time.

Luke 22:67

67“If you are the Christ, tell us.” But he said to them, “If I tell you, you won’t believe,

Arminian

The council’s question meets Jesus’ diagnosis of unbelief. Arminian readers emphasize culpable resistance to testimony rather than assume that further words necessarily produce willing trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see unbelief’s moral resistance. The exchange illustrates the need for renewing grace without making the questioners innocent or reducing faith to possession of information.

Luke 22:68

68and if I ask, you will in no way answer me or let me go.

Arminian

Jesus exposes unwillingness to answer fairly or release him. Arminian readers see refusal of honest engagement as responsible resistance, not a neutral search awaiting one more fact.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize hostility shaping the hearing. Sin affects the will as well as understanding, so a formally religious question need not express openness to truth.

Luke 22:69

69From now on, the Son of Man will be seated at the right hand of the power of God.”

Arminian

Jesus answers humiliation with the promise of exaltation at God’s right hand. Arminian readers place hope in the vindicated Son rather than the council’s assessment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise confess the authority of the exalted Christ. The courtroom reversal rests on who he is and God’s purpose, not on his receiving human approval.

Luke 22:70

70They all said, “Are you then the Son of God?” He said to them, “You say it, because I am.”

Arminian

Jesus’ answer acknowledges the identity they put to him. Arminian readers attend to his claim in the flow of the hearing rather than weakening it into mere evasion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect the Son of God question with the preceding promise of exaltation. Christ’s identity remains central even while his hearers use his words against him.

Luke 22:71

71They said, “Why do we need any more witness? For we ourselves have heard from his own mouth!”

Arminian

The council treats Jesus’ own words as sufficient against him. Arminian readers see testimony rejected rather than faithfully received, warning that hearing alone is not saving trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish exposure to Christ’s words from believing reception. The hearing ends in condemnation by his opponents, not in a legitimate refutation of his identity.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
