Passage

Luke 22

Verses 24–38 · WEB

At the table, Jesus corrects rivalry through his own service, promises kingdom fellowship, and warns Peter of satanic testing and denial while assuring him of intercession and return. Both traditions call disciples away from self-confidence toward grace-dependent obedience; they differ over how Peter’s preservation informs the doctrine of perseverance. The difficult sword language must be read beside the coming arrest, Jesus’ healing, and his rejection of armed rescue rather than turned into permission for coercive mission.

Luke 22:24

24A dispute also arose among them, which of them was considered to be greatest.

Arminian

A dispute over greatness interrupts the solemn setting of the meal. Arminian readers recognize that disciples still need grace to resist rivalry and respond to correction.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see remaining sin among Christ’s followers. Their failures expose the need for sanctification rather than justifying rivalry as harmless because they are disciples.

Luke 22:25

25He said to them, “The kings of the nations lord it over them, and those who have authority over them are called ‘benefactors.’

Arminian

Jesus contrasts kingdom leadership with domination disguised by honorable titles. Arminian readers see a challenge to seek others’ good rather than prestige under religious cover.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise reject the use of authority for self-exaltation. Public recognition as generous or important does not itself demonstrate the servant character Christ requires.

Luke 22:26

26But not so with you. Rather, the one who is greater among you, let him become as the younger, and one who is governing, as one who serves.

Arminian

Jesus commands the greater disciple to take the lower place and serve. Arminian readers see grace-enabled humility as a concrete responsibility, not merely an inward sentiment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand service as the fruit of following Christ. Authority in his community should be exercised for others’ benefit rather than personal advancement.

Luke 22:27

27For who is greater, one who sits at the table, or one who serves? Isn’t it he who sits at the table? But I am among you as one who serves.

Arminian

Jesus himself supplies the pattern of service at the table. Arminian readers ground the call to humility in his loving action rather than in earning his acceptance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Lord’s service overturning status competition. Christ’s example forms grateful obedience without reducing his saving mission to moral example alone.

Luke 22:28

28“But you are those who have continued with me in my trials.

Arminian

Jesus acknowledges companions who have remained with him in trials. Arminian readers see continuing loyalty valued without denying the severe failure about to be predicted.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize grace at work in imperfect disciples. Christ’s commendation does not imply sinless constancy or eliminate their continuing need for his intercession.

Luke 22:29

29I confer on you a kingdom, even as my Father conferred on me,

Arminian

The kingdom is conferred as a gift patterned on the Father’s gift to Jesus. Arminian readers see gracious participation, not a reward detached from relationship with Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the initiative of the Father and Son. The disciples receive their place in the kingdom rather than constructing it through status seeking.

Luke 22:30

30that you may eat and drink at my table in my Kingdom. You will sit on thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel.”

Arminian

Table fellowship and governing responsibility replace their immediate rivalry with a larger promise. Arminian readers locate this honor in Christ’s gift rather than personal superiority.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize promised participation in Christ’s reign. Interpretations of the twelve tribes and future judging vary and should not be assigned automatically to either soteriological tradition.

Luke 22:31

31The Lord said, “Simon, Simon, behold, Satan asked to have all of you, that he might sift you as wheat,

Arminian

Satan seeks to sift the disciples, making spiritual danger real rather than imaginary. Arminian readers emphasize dependence on grace and vigilance, not confidence in personal resolve.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take satanic assault seriously while recognizing its limits under God. The plural address concerns the disciples together before Jesus singles out Peter for prayer.

Luke 22:32

32but I prayed for you, that your faith wouldn’t fail. You, when once you have turned again, establish your brothers.”

Arminian

Jesus’ prayer supports Peter through failure toward return and service. Arminian readers see powerful preserving grace that calls for a real repentant response, not denial that believers face danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s effective intercession preserving Peter’s faith through grievous sin. His promised return illustrates restoration secured by grace rather than immunity from serious falls.

Key contrast Both affirm Christ’s intercession and Peter’s real return after grievous failure. Arminian readers emphasize preserving grace and the necessity of repentant response without generalizing Peter’s case into unconditional perseverance for all believers; Reformed readers see his restoration as an illustration of Christ’s effective preservation of his people.

Luke 22:33

33He said to him, “Lord, I am ready to go with you both to prison and to death!”

Arminian

Peter promises prison and death but misunderstands his own weakness. Arminian readers see a warning that sincere resolve cannot replace prayerful dependence on divine help.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish confident professions from sustaining grace. Peter’s courage will fail, exposing how little assurance can safely rest on an estimate of one’s own strength.

Luke 22:34

34He said, “I tell you, Peter, the rooster will by no means crow today until you deny that you know me three times.”

Arminian

Jesus predicts Peter’s three denials with precision. Arminian readers affirm Christ’s foreknowledge without making Peter’s sinful speech involuntary or therefore innocent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm Christ’s knowledge and Peter’s responsibility. The prediction prepares for restoration without minimizing the seriousness of a disciple denying his Lord.

Luke 22:35

35He said to them, “When I sent you out without purse, bag, and sandals, did you lack anything?” They said, “Nothing.”

Arminian

The disciples acknowledge that Jesus provided during their earlier mission. Arminian readers see remembered grace strengthening trust as circumstances change, not a guarantee that hardship will disappear.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize faithful provision through a particular commission. The memory supports dependence while the next instruction shows that practical arrangements can legitimately change.

Luke 22:36

36Then he said to them, “But now, whoever has a purse, let him take it, and likewise a bag. Whoever has none, let him sell his cloak, and buy a sword.

Arminian

Jesus prepares the disciples for a harsher setting using language of supplies and a sword. Arminian readers should read this beside his rebuke of violent action, not as permission for coercive mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise interpret this difficult command within the arrest narrative and its fulfillment theme. Practical readiness must not become a warrant to spread the kingdom by violence.

Luke 22:37

37For I tell you that this which is written must still be fulfilled in me: ‘He was counted with transgressors.’ For that which concerns me is being fulfilled.”

Arminian

Jesus explains the coming crisis through Scripture’s picture of being counted with transgressors. Arminian readers see voluntary redemptive obedience rather than guilt of his own.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the innocent Savior entering the place of the condemned in fulfillment of Scripture. The verse explains his mission, not an endorsement of the disciples becoming violent offenders.

Luke 22:38

38They said, “Lord, behold, here are two swords.” He said to them, “That is enough.”

Arminian

The disciples point to two swords, and Jesus ends the exchange. Arminian readers recognize interpretive uncertainty rather than treating the reply as an unqualified military authorization.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the reply alongside Jesus’ later healing and refusal of armed rescue. Neither tradition should make this brief exchange a license for religious violence.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
