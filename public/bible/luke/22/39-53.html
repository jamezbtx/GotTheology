Passage

Luke 22

Verses 39–53 · WEB

On Olivet Jesus prays in genuine anguish, submits to the Father, and commands his disciples to pray against temptation. Judas arrives with the crowd, a disciple strikes a servant, and Jesus heals rather than securing escape by violence. Both traditions affirm Christ’s real humanity, willing obedience, and mercy without excusing the betrayers. The selected WEB edition includes verses 43–44 in its main text, unbracketed and without a textual-variant footnote; the verse text follows that presentation.

Luke 22:39

39He came out and went, as his custom was, to the Mount of Olives. His disciples also followed him.

Arminian

Jesus follows his customary path to Olivet with the disciples. Arminian readers see deliberate faithfulness as danger approaches, not a Savior accidentally trapped by events.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize Christ’s purposeful movement toward suffering. His ordinary practice of withdrawal becomes the setting for obedient prayer and the coming arrest.

Luke 22:40

40When he was at the place, he said to them, “Pray that you don’t enter into temptation.”

Arminian

Jesus commands prayer against entering temptation. Arminian readers see a genuine means of receiving needed grace, with vigilance required rather than assumed unnecessary.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand prayer as an appointed means of protection. Trust in preservation does not make the disciples’ obedience to this command optional.

Luke 22:41

41He was withdrawn from them about a stone’s throw, and he knelt down and prayed,

Arminian

Jesus withdraws nearby and kneels in prayer. Arminian readers see his real human dependence and communion with the Father, not an appearance of distress without substance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm the incarnate Son’s genuine humanity. His posture expresses willing dependence without implying any defect in his divine identity or holiness.

Luke 22:42

42saying, “Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me. Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done.”

Arminian

Jesus honestly asks for the cup’s removal while submitting to the Father’s will. Arminian readers see genuine human obedience and voluntary love, not rebellion against God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm the sinless human will of Christ freely submitting in the saving purpose. Distress and obedience coexist; suffering does not make trust unreal.

Luke 22:43

43An angel from heaven appeared to him, strengthening him.

Arminian

The angel strengthens Jesus amid his anguish. Arminian readers see divine care sustaining real human weakness, not deliverance from the obedient path of suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm the Father’s provision through an angel. Receiving strength in his humanity does not diminish the Son’s divine dignity or his willing obedience.

Luke 22:44

44Being in agony, he prayed more earnestly. His sweat became like great drops of blood falling down on the ground.

Arminian

Jesus’ agony deepens earnest prayer rather than ending communion with the Father. Arminian readers avoid turning the comparison with drops of blood into an unverified medical diagnosis.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the anguish and embodied suffering seriously. The verse describes costly obedience and should not be embellished with medical certainty the text does not provide.

Luke 22:45

45When he rose up from his prayer, he came to the disciples and found them sleeping because of grief,

Arminian

The disciples sleep from grief rather than remaining alert in prayer. Arminian readers recognize emotional exhaustion without treating it as a reason to ignore Jesus’ warning.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the weakness of those Christ loves. Compassion for grief and sober recognition of spiritual vulnerability belong together rather than cancelling one another.

Luke 22:46

46and said to them, “Why do you sleep? Rise and pray that you may not enter into temptation.”

Arminian

Jesus repeats the call to rise and pray against temptation. Arminian readers hear renewed gracious instruction requiring a response after failure, not immediate rejection of struggling disciples.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize patient correction as part of Christ’s care. The Lord’s preserving purpose includes awakening disciples to pray rather than leaving them comfortably inattentive.

Luke 22:47

47While he was still speaking, a crowd appeared. He who was called Judas, one of the twelve, was leading them. He came near to Jesus to kiss him.

Arminian

Judas leads the crowd and approaches with a familiar sign of affection. Arminian readers see betrayal exploiting intimacy, not satanic influence removing personal accountability.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize deliberate treachery by one outwardly numbered with the twelve. Religious proximity and affectionate gestures cannot substitute for allegiance to Jesus.

Luke 22:48

48But Jesus said to him, “Judas, do you betray the Son of Man with a kiss?”

Arminian

Jesus exposes the contradiction between a kiss and betrayal of the Son of Man. Arminian readers see the betrayer addressed as responsible rather than treated as an impersonal instrument.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm moral accountability within God’s saving purpose. Jesus names the evil truthfully without losing his dignity or control of his response.

Luke 22:49

49When those who were around him saw what was about to happen, they said to him, “Lord, shall we strike with the sword?”

Arminian

The disciples ask whether to use their swords as arrest approaches. Arminian readers see the need to seek Jesus’ direction rather than assume zeal makes violence righteous.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish loyalty to Christ from impulsive defense by force. The unfolding response, not isolated sword language, must guide interpretation of this moment.

Luke 22:50

50A certain one of them struck the servant of the high priest, and cut off his right ear.

Arminian

A disciple’s blow injures the high priest’s servant. Arminian readers recognize that sincere allegiance can express itself wrongly when fear and haste outrun obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise refuse to equate zeal with holiness. The injury sets the scene for Christ’s healing, which exposes the inadequacy of armed rescue for his mission.

Luke 22:51

51But Jesus answered, “Let me at least do this”—and he touched his ear and healed him.

Arminian

Jesus stops the escalation and heals the wounded man. Arminian readers see mercy extending even within a hostile crowd, not approval of retaliation on his behalf.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize sovereign compassion toward an enemy’s servant. Christ’s saving path is not protected by his disciples’ violence but marked by his own merciful obedience.

Luke 22:52

52Jesus said to the chief priests, captains of the temple, and elders, who had come against him, “Have you come out as against a robber, with swords and clubs?

Arminian

Jesus challenges the armed arrest as though he were a robber. Arminian readers see his innocence and the injustice of treating public teaching as concealed criminality.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise contrast Christ’s open ministry with the authorities’ force. His submission to arrest does not make their accusations true or their methods righteous.

Luke 22:53

53When I was with you in the temple daily, you didn’t stretch out your hands against me. But this is your hour, and the power of darkness.”

Arminian

Jesus names both the authorities’ hour and the power of darkness. Arminian readers affirm a permitted season of evil without making God the moral source of their wickedness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hold the limited hour of darkness within God’s providence. Evil acts remain evil even as the passion fulfills the saving purpose of God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
