Passage

Luke 22

Verses 1–23 · WEB

The Passover setting joins the leaders’ plot and Judas’s willing betrayal to Jesus’ deliberate preparation of a final meal. Jesus gives bread and cup in remembrance of his self-offering and identifies the new covenant in his blood. Both traditions affirm voluntary saving love and human responsibility; real differences concern the scope and application of redemption and the relation of divine determination to sinful choices. Outward participation at the table does not by itself settle anyone’s inward spiritual condition.

Luke 22:1

1Now the feast of unleavened bread, which is called the Passover, was approaching.

Arminian

Passover frames the approaching suffering within Israel’s remembrance of deliverance. Arminian readers see Jesus moving toward saving self-giving, not an isolated tragedy without redemptive meaning.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to the feast’s setting as the passion unfolds. The timing prepares readers for the meal and covenant language without making every detail an allegory.

Luke 22:2

2The chief priests and the scribes sought how they might put him to death, for they feared the people.

Arminian

The leaders seek Jesus’ death while fearing popular reaction. Arminian readers see culpable calculation, showing that religious authority does not remove the responsibility to receive God’s truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm the leaders’ moral responsibility. God’s saving purpose does not make murderous plotting innocent or turn concern for public reputation into reverence for him.

Luke 22:3

3Satan entered into Judas, who was also called Iscariot, who was counted with the twelve.

Arminian

Satan’s entry exposes the spiritual evil of betrayal without erasing Judas’s subsequent consent. Arminian readers do not infer his regenerate status merely from his place among the twelve.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish apostolic membership from inward regeneration. Satan’s action does not excuse Judas, and this verse alone does not settle every question about his earlier spiritual condition.

Luke 22:4

4He went away and talked with the chief priests and captains about how he might deliver him to them.

Arminian

Judas actively negotiates a way to hand Jesus over. Arminian readers see responsible participation in evil rather than a human agent portrayed as morally passive.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize willing betrayal within the unfolding passion. Divine sovereignty is not a reason to deny the concrete choices through which Judas collaborates with the leaders.

Luke 22:5

5They were glad, and agreed to give him money.

Arminian

Money joins the leaders’ glad reception of Judas’s plan. Arminian readers see the corrupting alliance of hostility and gain, not an innocent transaction serving religion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the willing cooperation of all involved. Their pleasure in the scheme underscores accountability rather than removing it because redemption will come through Christ’s death.

Luke 22:6

6He consented and sought an opportunity to deliver him to them in the absence of the multitude.

Arminian

Judas consents and searches for a private opportunity. Arminian readers emphasize his ongoing willing involvement; satanic influence does not replace human responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise treat consent and planning as genuine human acts. The passage portrays calculated betrayal, not a blameless person compelled against every inclination.

Luke 22:7

7The day of unleavened bread came, on which the Passover must be sacrificed.

Arminian

The required Passover sacrifice sets the scene for Jesus’ final meal. Arminian readers attend to the redemptive setting without treating the calendar as an independent soteriological proof.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the meal situated within God’s history with Israel. Both traditions can recognize this setting without making every sacrificial detail a separate doctrinal contrast.

Luke 22:8

8Jesus sent Peter and John, saying, “Go and prepare the Passover for us, that we may eat.”

Arminian

Jesus directs Peter and John to prepare the meal. Arminian readers see ordinary obedience participating in his gracious purposes rather than competing with divine initiative.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize Christ’s initiative working through the disciples’ practical service. Preparation and dependence belong together as Jesus gathers his companions before suffering.

Luke 22:9

9They said to him, “Where do you want us to prepare?”

Arminian

The disciples seek Jesus’ direction about the place. Arminian readers see responsive trust expressed in practical questions rather than the expectation of acting without instruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise value dependence that listens before proceeding. The exchange shows disciples learning to follow Christ in ordinary arrangements as well as extraordinary moments.

Luke 22:10

10He said to them, “Behold, when you have entered into the city, a man carrying a pitcher of water will meet you. Follow him into the house which he enters.

Arminian

Jesus supplies a concrete sign and directions for finding the house. Arminian readers see his provision guiding willing disciples toward the appointed meal.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize Christ’s command of the situation. His knowledge and guidance steady the narrative even while others are arranging the betrayal.

Luke 22:11

11Tell the master of the house, ‘The Teacher says to you, “Where is the guest room, where I may eat the Passover with my disciples?” ’

Arminian

The host is asked for a room where Jesus can eat with his disciples. Arminian readers notice fellowship and hospitality serving the coming remembrance of grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the meal as Christ’s deliberate gathering of his disciples. Ordinary hospitality becomes a setting for his instruction without making the host’s motives an occasion for speculation.

Luke 22:12

12He will show you a large, furnished upper room. Make preparations there.”

Arminian

The furnished room provides what the disciples need for preparation. Arminian readers see gracious provision that still calls for action rather than passive observation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise join providential provision with appointed work. The disciples’ preparations are part of Christ’s direction, not an alternative to his care.

Luke 22:13

13They went, found things as Jesus had told them, and they prepared the Passover.

Arminian

The disciples find the circumstances as Jesus described and prepare the Passover. Arminian readers see trust confirmed in obedient action, not grounds for spiritual self-congratulation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the reliability of Christ’s word. His guidance does not bypass the disciples’ labor but gives it a clear place within his purpose.

Luke 22:14

14When the hour had come, he sat down with the twelve apostles.

Arminian

Jesus shares the table with the twelve as the hour arrives. Arminian readers see intimate fellowship without assuming that outward participation by itself guarantees inward faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish membership at the visible table from saving union with Christ. The coming announcement of betrayal prevents a simplistic equation of proximity and regeneration.

Luke 22:15

15He said to them, “I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer,

Arminian

Jesus earnestly desires this fellowship before suffering. Arminian readers emphasize the personal, willing love of the Savior rather than viewing the passion as reluctant compliance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize Christ’s voluntary affection for his companions. His approaching death is not detached from love or from his deliberate care for those he teaches.

Luke 22:16

16for I tell you, I will no longer by any means eat of it until it is fulfilled in God’s Kingdom.”

Arminian

Jesus directs the meal toward fulfillment in God’s kingdom. Arminian readers find future hope grounding present trust rather than fixing a timetable the verse does not provide.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the supper oriented toward the kingdom’s completion. Their confidence in God’s purpose does not require a single interpretation of every detail of the future feast.

Luke 22:17

17He received a cup, and when he had given thanks, he said, “Take this and share it among yourselves,

Arminian

Jesus gives thanks and shares the cup among his companions. Arminian readers see grateful reception and fellowship, not a religious achievement that makes grace unnecessary.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize receiving what Christ gives in thankful communion. Differences about sacramental practice should not obscure the shared recognition of his generosity at the table.

Luke 22:18

18for I tell you, I will not drink at all again from the fruit of the vine, until God’s Kingdom comes.”

Arminian

Jesus connects his abstention with the coming kingdom. Arminian readers see the meal opening toward renewed fellowship beyond suffering, supporting patient hope rather than speculative dates.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise find the kingdom’s promised arrival central to the saying. Christ’s confidence supports the disciples as the apparent defeat of arrest and death approaches.

Luke 22:19

19He took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke and gave it to them, saying, “This is my body which is given for you. Do this in memory of me.”

Arminian

Jesus gives the bread as remembrance of his body given for them. Arminian readers center trust on his self-offering; remembrance is not a human substitute for grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise center the meal on Christ’s gift and call to remembrance. Their sacramental accounts vary, so this verse should not reduce every Reformed reading to bare symbolism.

Luke 22:20

20Likewise, he took the cup after supper, saying, “This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you.

Arminian

The new covenant rests on Jesus’ blood, not human achievement. Arminian readers understand his saving death as provision for all, with covenant benefits received through faith rather than table attendance alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise ground the covenant in Christ’s self-offering. Classical Reformed readings emphasize its effective saving purpose for the elect while distinguishing outward participation from inward reception through faith.

Key contrast Both ground the new covenant in Christ’s death and require faith for saving reception. Arminian readers understand redemption as provided for all and applied to believers; classical Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s definite saving purpose for the elect. The words at this meal should not alone be made to settle every question about the atonement’s extent.

Luke 22:21

21But behold, the hand of him who betrays me is with me on the table.

Arminian

Betrayal is present at the very table of fellowship. Arminian readers see a sober warning that closeness to sacred acts cannot substitute for faithful allegiance to Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish outward fellowship from a renewed heart. The betrayer’s presence must not be used to assume that every participant receives the covenant’s saving benefits identically.

Luke 22:22

22The Son of Man indeed goes as it has been determined, but woe to that man through whom he is betrayed!”

Arminian

Jesus’ death fulfills a determined purpose, yet the betrayer remains guilty. Classical Arminian readers affirm God’s saving plan and foreknowledge while understanding Judas’s sinful choice as permitted rather than morally necessitated.

Reformed

Reformed readers hold God’s determination and Judas’s willing guilt together. God ordains the saving event without becoming the author of sin or making betrayal righteous; providence does not cancel responsibility.

Key contrast Both affirm the determined saving mission and Judas’s culpability. Classical Arminian readers distinguish God’s ordained redemptive purpose from his permission of foreknown sinful choices; Reformed readers affirm an all-encompassing decree working through willing agents without making God the author of sin.

Luke 22:23

23They began to question among themselves which of them it was who would do this thing.

Arminian

The disciples question who could commit the betrayal. Arminian readers see cause for humble self-examination rather than confidence that association with Jesus makes serious sin impossible.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise resist self-assurance based on office or proximity. Dependence on grace, not certainty about one’s own strength, is the fitting response to this disclosure.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
