Passage

Luke 2

Verses 21–40 · WEB

The naming, circumcision, and presentation of Jesus place his early life within the worship and law of Israel. Guided by the Spirit, Simeon recognizes salvation for Israel and the nations while warning of opposition and Mary’s sorrow. Anna adds thanksgiving and public testimony, and the household returns to Nazareth as Jesus grows. Both traditions see fulfilled promise, costly discipleship, and real human development as shared ground rather than reasons to force a doctrinal rivalry.

Luke 2:21

21When eight days were fulfilled for the circumcision of the child, his name was called Jesus, which was given by the angel before he was conceived in the womb.

Arminian

The child is circumcised and receives the name Jesus on the eighth day. Arminian readers see the family receiving God’s prior direction within Israel’s covenant practice. The naming connects the actual child with the announcement before conception, affirming fulfillment rather than a new identity assigned through parental ambition.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the incarnate Son entering the covenant life of his people in a concrete, bodily way. His name has already been given by God. The observance does not supply an identity he previously lacked; it publicly receives the child according to the word and law entrusted to the household.

Luke 2:22

22When the days of their purification according to the law of Moses were fulfilled, they brought him up to Jerusalem to present him to the Lord

Arminian

The parents bring Jesus to Jerusalem in accordance with the law’s purification requirements. Arminian readers see faithful action within the worshiping life of Israel. The language of purification concerns this ritual setting and should not be confused with a claim that the child has committed personal sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the household’s obedience accompanying the arrival of the promised Savior. Divine favor does not lead them to dismiss the law’s directions. The journey provides the setting for Simeon and Anna to recognize the child, joining regular observance with an unexpected disclosure of salvation.

Luke 2:23

23(as it is written in the law of the Lord, “Every male who opens the womb shall be called holy to the Lord”),

Arminian

Luke explains the presentation through the law’s claim upon the firstborn. Arminian readers see the child received as belonging to the Lord, not as a possession defined only by parental hopes. The citation locates the action within God’s existing word rather than a rite invented for the occasion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see continuity between the promise’s fulfillment and Israel’s scriptural worship. Consecration acknowledges God’s claim and gift. The verse describes the reason for presenting the child without suggesting that the Son only now begins to belong to the Father or acquires holiness through the ceremony.

Luke 2:24

24and to offer a sacrifice according to that which is said in the law of the Lord, “A pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons.”

Arminian

The household offers the birds prescribed in the law. Arminian readers see devotion expressed through an actual offering rather than merely inward feeling. The verse’s emphasis is conformity to the given instruction, linking this humble family’s worship with the wider people’s life before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience taking the practical form provided by God’s law. The offering belongs to the family’s worship and purification context, not a purchase of the child’s saving identity. Luke repeatedly emphasizes that the arrival of Jesus is received within a faithful response to God’s instruction.

Luke 2:25

25Behold, there was a man in Jerusalem whose name was Simeon. This man was righteous and devout, looking for the consolation of Israel, and the Holy Spirit was on him.

Arminian

Simeon is righteous, devout, and waiting for Israel’s consolation with the Spirit upon him. Arminian readers see patient faith sustained by divine help. Expectation is neither self-sufficient optimism nor passive indifference; his life is oriented toward the comfort God has promised to bring.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a Spirit-sustained witness prepared to recognize the Messiah. Righteousness and devotion are not offered as prices that force God to reveal the child. They describe a waiting servant whose hope is directed outward to the consolation God will provide for Israel.

Luke 2:26

26It had been revealed to him by the Holy Spirit that he should not see death before he had seen the Lord’s Christ.

Arminian

The Spirit has promised Simeon that he will see the Messiah before death. Arminian readers see a personal assurance resting on revelation, not a general guarantee for every waiting person. The promise addresses his particular calling and prepares the recognition that will interpret the child’s identity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s initiative giving Simeon a definite hope that circumstances cannot create. The revelation is specific, and its fulfillment will display divine faithfulness. His expectation is not the result of controlling the future but of receiving a word from the Spirit.

Luke 2:27

27He came in the Spirit into the temple. When the parents brought in the child, Jesus, that they might do concerning him according to the custom of the law,

Arminian

Simeon comes into the temple in the Spirit as the parents arrive with Jesus. Arminian readers see divine guidance joining the journeys of waiting witness and obedient household. Their meeting is not produced by either side’s complete knowledge; grace brings them together so that recognition and praise can follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providence and the Spirit’s guidance coordinating an ordinary observance with a particular promise. God works through the parents’ actual obedience and Simeon’s actual movement. The account gives significance to their encounter without turning human action into a rival to divine initiative.

Luke 2:28

28then he received him into his arms and blessed God, and said,

Arminian

Simeon receives the child and blesses God. Arminian readers see recognition producing gratitude rather than a claim to personal importance. The promised sight becomes a tender encounter with an infant, joining the Savior’s real humanity with the vast hope that the next words will express.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness acknowledged in praise when the promised meeting occurs. The child in Simeon’s arms is the concrete focus of salvation, not merely a symbol of a better future. Worship responds to the gift already present rather than demanding a more impressive appearance.

Luke 2:29

29“Now you are releasing your servant, Master, according to your word, in peace;

Arminian

Simeon can face release in peace because God’s word has been fulfilled. Arminian readers see trust meeting the reality of mortality without pretending that life is under human control. Peace comes from the Master’s faithfulness, not from Simeon completing an achievement that obliges God to accept him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see assurance grounded in a kept promise. The servant’s life and departure belong to his Master, whose word has proved reliable. The saying expresses readiness in this fulfilled hope rather than contempt for bodily life or a universal timetable for the death of believers.

Luke 2:30

30for my eyes have seen your salvation,

Arminian

Simeon identifies the child he sees with God’s salvation. Arminian readers see rescue centered in Jesus himself rather than an abstract religious system. The infant has not yet begun public ministry, but his identity makes the promised hope present and gives the waiting servant reason to rejoice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see salvation embodied in the person God has given. Simeon’s eyes encounter a child, yet the Spirit’s promise enables him to recognize the significance of that sight. Assurance rests on the Savior’s presence, not on the apparent strength of the circumstances surrounding him.

Luke 2:31

31which you have prepared before the face of all peoples;

Arminian

God has prepared this salvation before all peoples. Arminian readers see a public and far-reaching purpose that cannot be confined to one privileged circle. The scope of the announcement invites broad witness while not asserting that every individual will receive salvation without faith or response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divine preparation preceding the witness that will reach the nations. The saving purpose is not restricted to the first household or audience that recognizes it. Public breadth and God’s initiative belong together without the verse itself resolving every question about individual participation.

Luke 2:32

32a light for revelation to the nations, and the glory of your people Israel.”

Arminian

The child brings revelation to the nations and glory to Israel. Arminian readers see grace extending outward without treating Israel’s hope as meaningless. The blessing is not a competition in which one people’s dignity requires another’s erasure; the same Savior is central to both dimensions of the promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers see one divine saving purpose joining Israel’s promise with the illumination of the nations. Christ is not a replacement for God’s faithfulness but its fulfillment. The verse prepares for a widening mission while locating its origin in the story already celebrated by Mary and Zacharias.

Luke 2:33

33Joseph and his mother were marveling at the things which were spoken concerning him.

Arminian

Joseph and Mary marvel at what Simeon says about Jesus. Arminian readers see earlier revelation leaving room for fresh wonder and growing understanding. Having received a promise does not mean knowing every dimension of its fulfillment; they continue to hear testimony concerning the child entrusted to their care.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the household learning as God unfolds the meaning of the birth. Astonishment is compatible with genuine faith and prior knowledge of the announcement. The narrative keeps the parents human rather than assuming that their special place has given them exhaustive insight.

Luke 2:34

34Simeon blessed them, and said to Mary, his mother, “Behold, this child is appointed for the falling and the rising of many in Israel, and for a sign which is spoken against.

Arminian

Simeon announces both falling and rising in Israel and opposition to the child. Arminian readers see the seriousness of receiving or resisting God’s saving visitation. The statement establishes the divisive significance of Christ without by itself specifying the full relation between divine appointment and each hearer’s response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s saving mission also bringing judgment and exposing opposition. The appointed role assures that rejection does not place him outside God’s purpose. The saying is not an exhaustive account of election and reprobation; its immediate emphasis is the crisis his presence creates within Israel.

Luke 2:35

35Yes, a sword will pierce through your own soul, that the thoughts of many hearts may be revealed.”

Arminian

Mary is warned of personal anguish as hearts are disclosed through the child’s mission. Arminian readers see that divine favor does not exempt her from suffering. The sword is an image of sorrow, and the verse does not make her pain an independent saving work alongside that of Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers see opposition to Christ reaching even the heart of his mother. His coming reveals hidden responses rather than leaving everyone’s stance untouched. Mary’s honored role includes grief, showing why blessing must not be reduced to ease or an absence of costly participation in God’s unfolding purpose.

Luke 2:36

36There was one Anna, a prophetess, the daughter of Phanuel, of the tribe of Asher (she was of a great age, having lived with a husband seven years from her virginity,

Arminian

Anna is introduced as an aged prophetess with a particular family and tribal identity. Arminian readers see another witness drawn from Israel’s ongoing life of hope. Her long personal history matters, while the emphasis falls on her recognition of Jesus rather than on a detailed biography Luke does not provide.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God supplying a further witness through a woman whose life has unfolded over many years. Her prophetic role complements Simeon’s testimony. The naming of her ancestry grounds the encounter in a concrete person, not an anonymous symbol of waiting.

Luke 2:37

37and she had been a widow for about eighty-four years), who didn’t depart from the temple, worshiping with fastings and petitions night and day.

Arminian

Anna’s widowhood is marked by fasting, prayer, and temple worship. Arminian readers see persevering devotion during a long life of loss and expectation. These practices express dependence rather than purchase the right to see the Messiah, and her particular pattern is not imposed as the only faithful form of widowhood.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sustained worship as the fruit of hope in God, not a way to make salvation arrive by human effort. Anna’s presence prepares for a timely encounter with the child. The narrative honors her devotion without making long widowhood or continuous temple attendance a universal requirement.

Luke 2:38

38Coming up at that very hour, she gave thanks to the Lord, and spoke of him to all those who were looking for redemption in Jerusalem.

Arminian

Anna gives thanks and speaks about the child to those awaiting redemption. Arminian readers see received recognition becoming testimony for others. The promised deliverance is no longer merely an inward expectation; she directs fellow worshipers toward the one through whom God is fulfilling their hope.

Reformed

Reformed readers see gratitude and proclamation joined in a Spirit-framed witness. Anna does not make herself the object of attention but speaks of Jesus to the waiting community. The encounter widens public recognition while preserving the focus on God’s redemption rather than the witness’s prestige.

Luke 2:39

39When they had accomplished all things that were according to the law of the Lord, they returned into Galilee, to their own city, Nazareth.

Arminian

After completing the law’s requirements, the household returns to Nazareth. Arminian readers see obedience and ordinary family life continuing after remarkable testimony. The verse gives Luke’s narrative movement without claiming to supply every event between locations, so unrecorded details should not be invented or denied on its basis alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fulfillment of prescribed worship followed by a return to a modest home. God’s purpose continues beyond the temple witnesses and public signs. The simple transition protects the reality of the child’s daily life without requiring an imagined account of all the intervening circumstances.

Luke 2:40

40The child was growing, and was becoming strong in spirit, being filled with wisdom, and the grace of God was upon him.

Arminian

Jesus grows, gains strength and wisdom, and lives under God’s grace. Arminian readers affirm the genuine development of the incarnate Son’s human life. Growth does not imply prior moral corruption; it belongs to true humanity and the favor of God accompanying the child’s unfolding years.

Reformed

Reformed readers see real human maturation without a change in the Son’s divine identity. Wisdom and strength develop within his incarnate life rather than being a mere appearance. Both traditions share this Christological ground and need not turn ordinary growth into a dispute about grace overcoming personal sin in Jesus.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
