Passage

Luke 6

Verses 1–16 · WEB

Two Sabbath disputes show Jesus as Lord of the Sabbath: His disciples pluck grain, and He heals a man’s withered hand in the synagogue. After a night of prayer, He chooses twelve apostles, including Judas Iscariot, who became a traitor. Both traditions see Jesus’ authority over the law and His deliberate choice of the Twelve; they differ in how they relate His choosing to Judas’s fall.

Luke 6:1

1Now on the second Sabbath after the first, he was going through the grain fields. His disciples plucked the heads of grain and ate, rubbing them in their hands.

Arminian

On the second Sabbath after the first, the disciples pluck grain. Arminian readers note that the phrase second Sabbath after the first is uncertain in meaning and absent from many manuscripts.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same textual question. The point is a Sabbath setting for a dispute over the law.

Luke 6:2

2But some of the Pharisees said to them, “Why do you do that which is not lawful to do on the Sabbath day?”

Arminian

Why do what is not lawful? Arminian readers see Pharisees interpreting the Sabbath strictly. They see the disciples’ plucking and rubbing grain as Sabbath work.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a dispute about the meaning of Sabbath rest. The disagreement concerns how to apply the fourth commandment.

Luke 6:3

3Jesus, answering them, said, “Haven’t you read what David did when he was hungry, he and those who were with him,

Arminian

Haven’t you read what David did? Arminian readers see Jesus appealing to Scripture. He answers a legal charge by pointing to a recognized biblical precedent.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus arguing from 1 Samuel 21. Jesus argues from Scripture rather than from personal authority alone.

Luke 6:4

4how he entered into God’s house, and took and ate the show bread, and gave also to those who were with him, which is not lawful to eat except for the priests alone?”

Arminian

David ate the show bread. Arminian readers see mercy overriding ceremonial rules. Need and mercy took priority over ritual restriction in David’s case.

Reformed

Reformed readers see David’s example pointing to Jesus’ authority. If David’s need was honored, how much more the Son of David’s disciples.

Luke 6:5

5He said to them, “The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath.”

Arminian

The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath. Arminian readers see Jesus’ authority over the law. The Sabbath is not abolished but placed under Jesus’ authority.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ divine claim. Only the one who gave the Sabbath can claim lordship over it.

Luke 6:6

6It also happened on another Sabbath that he entered into the synagogue and taught. There was a man there, and his right hand was withered.

Arminian

A man with a withered hand. Arminian readers see need confronting law. Luke notes that it was his right hand, adding to the man’s loss in work and daily life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an opportunity for mercy. Jesus teaches first, then acts, showing word and deed together.

Luke 6:7

7The scribes and the Pharisees watched him, to see whether he would heal on the Sabbath, that they might find an accusation against him.

Arminian

They watch to accuse Him. Arminian readers see hostility. They want evidence to bring charges, not the man’s good.

Reformed

Reformed readers see hardened hearts. Religious zeal without love becomes watchful accusation.

Luke 6:8

8But he knew their thoughts; and he said to the man who had the withered hand, “Rise up and stand in the middle.” He arose and stood.

Arminian

Jesus knows their thoughts and calls the man forward. Arminian readers see courage. Jesus brings the man into the center rather than healing in secret.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divine insight. Jesus knows their hidden motives and still acts in plain view.

Luke 6:9

9Then Jesus said to them, “I will ask you something: Is it lawful on the Sabbath to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?”

Arminian

Is it lawful to do good or harm? Arminian readers see mercy as the law’s intent. Refusing to do good when one can is itself a form of harm.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Sabbath as a day for good. Jesus exposes the leaders’ question by placing it beside their own plotting.

Luke 6:10

10He looked around at them all, and said to the man, “Stretch out your hand.” He did, and his hand was restored as sound as the other.

Arminian

His hand is restored. Arminian readers see healing. He heals with a word, without any act that could be called work.

Reformed

Reformed readers see power displayed. The restored hand displays the goodness of the Sabbath’s Lord.

Luke 6:11

11But they were filled with rage, and talked with one another about what they might do to Jesus.

Arminian

They are filled with rage. Arminian readers see resistance. A miracle of mercy provokes fury, showing how stubborn resistance can be.

Reformed

Reformed readers see hardened hearts. Luke shows opposition turning from objection to plotting.

Luke 6:12

12In these days, he went out to the mountain to pray, and he continued all night in prayer to God.

Arminian

He prays all night. Arminian readers see dependence on God. Luke repeatedly shows Jesus praying before major decisions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see prayer before choosing. The choice of the Twelve follows communion with the Father.

Luke 6:13

13When it was day, he called his disciples, and from them he chose twelve, whom he also named apostles:

Arminian

He chooses twelve and names them apostles. Arminian readers see a choice to office and mission. The Twelve are chosen for a particular mission and given the name apostles, those sent.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sovereign choice for apostleship. The number twelve recalls Israel’s tribes and signals renewal of God’s people.

Luke 6:14

14Simon, whom he also named Peter; Andrew, his brother; James; John; Philip; Bartholomew;

Arminian

Simon Peter, Andrew, James, John, Philip, Bartholomew. Arminian readers see ordinary people. Peter receives a new name, a sign of his new role.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace choosing the unlikely. The list begins with two pairs of brothers called in chapter 5.

Luke 6:15

15Matthew; Thomas; James the son of Alphaeus; Simon who was called the Zealot;

Arminian

Matthew, Thomas, James son of Alphaeus, Simon the Zealot. Arminian readers see diversity. Jesus draws together people from very different backgrounds.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a tax collector and a Zealot together. A former tax collector and a Zealot serve side by side in one band.

Luke 6:16

16Judas the son of James; and Judas Iscariot, who also became a traitor.

Arminian

Judas the son of James, and Judas Iscariot, who became a traitor. Arminian readers see in Judas a sober warning that even someone chosen and close to Jesus could fall away.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s purposes accomplished even through betrayal, compare Acts 1:16. Judas’s guilt remains his own, though his act serves God’s plan.

Key contrast Both see Judas’s betrayal as real and culpable. Arminian readers often take became a traitor as a fall from genuine discipleship. Reformed readers often see Judas as chosen for office but never truly regenerate, with his betrayal within God’s plan.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
