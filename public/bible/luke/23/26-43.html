Passage

Luke 23

Verses 26–43 · WEB

Jesus is led to the place called the Skull and crucified between two criminals. Luke records his words to the women of Jerusalem, his prayer for forgiveness, the mockery, and the promise to the penitent criminal. Both traditions see here the heart of the gospel; the dialogue with the dying criminal raises questions about how grace and response relate.

Luke 23:26

26When they led him away, they grabbed one Simon of Cyrene, coming from the country, and laid the cross on him to carry it after Jesus.

Arminian

Simon of Cyrene is compelled to carry the cross behind Jesus. Arminian readers see a picture of discipleship, bearing the cross after Jesus (Luke 9:23).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same image of following Jesus. They also note that Simon’s involvement was not his choice, yet God used it, a reminder of providence in ordinary events.

Luke 23:27

27A great multitude of the people followed him, including women who also mourned and lamented him.

Arminian

A great multitude follows, including women mourning him. Arminian readers note genuine sorrow among the people, distinct from the shouting crowd at the trial.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Luke gives attention to women throughout his Gospel. Both see real compassion here, though Jesus will redirect it.

Luke 23:28

28But Jesus, turning to them, said, “Daughters of Jerusalem, don’t weep for me, but weep for yourselves and for your children.

Arminian

Jesus tells the daughters of Jerusalem to weep for themselves and their children. Arminian readers see his concern for others even on the way to death, and a call to repentance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same compassion and a prophetic warning. Both agree Jesus points beyond his own suffering to coming judgment on the city (Luke 19:41–44).

Luke 23:29

29For behold, the days are coming in which they will say, ‘Blessed are the barren, the wombs that never bore, and the breasts that never nursed.’

Arminian

He foresees days when the barren will be called blessed. Arminian readers connect this to the fall of Jerusalem, a consequence of the nation’s rejection.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect it to coming judgment. Both traditions read it as historical warning rather than a statement about individual election.

Luke 23:30

30Then they will begin to tell the mountains, ‘Fall on us!’ and tell the hills, ‘Cover us.’

Arminian

Jesus echoes Hosea 10:8: people will ask mountains to fall on them. Arminian readers note the terror of judgment on those who reject God’s visitation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same Old Testament pattern of judgment. Both see it as a serious call to flee to Christ now.

Luke 23:31

31For if they do these things in the green tree, what will be done in the dry?”

Arminian

If they do this in the green tree, what in the dry? Arminian readers commonly read this as: if the innocent suffers so, how much worse for the guilty.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it similarly. Many in both traditions note that the exact referent of the proverb is debated, but the warning is plain.

Luke 23:32

32There were also others, two criminals, led with him to be put to death.

Arminian

Two criminals are led with him. Arminian readers note that Jesus is placed among the lawless.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the fulfillment of Isaiah 53:12, which Jesus quoted in Luke 22:37: he was counted with transgressors.

Luke 23:33

33When they came to the place that is called “The Skull”, they crucified him there with the criminals, one on the right and the other on the left.

Arminian

They crucify him at “The Skull” between the criminals. Arminian readers stress that Luke narrates the crucifixion with restraint, drawing attention to its meaning more than its gore.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same restraint. Both see here the place where sin is dealt with, through the death of the Righteous One.

Luke 23:34

34Jesus said, “Father, forgive them, for they don’t know what they are doing.” Dividing his garments among them, they cast lots.

Arminian

Jesus prays, “Father, forgive them, for they don’t know what they are doing.” The WEB text includes this prayer without a footnote, though some early manuscripts lack it and modern editions often bracket it. Arminian readers see the prayer as revealing God’s sincere desire to forgive even his executioners, a love that reaches all and invites response; Acts 3:17–19 shows many later repenting.

Reformed

Reformed readers also treasure this prayer as a model of love for enemies (compare Stephen, Acts 7:60). Many see its answer especially in those who later repented at Pentecost, while recognizing the prayer as a genuine plea for forgiveness of the ignorant.

Key contrast Shared ground: Jesus truly prays for his enemies’ forgiveness. Arminian readers tend to see the prayer as expressing God’s universal saving will; Reformed readers often see its fulfillment in those God brings to repentance, while both preach it as an offer to all.

Luke 23:35

35The people stood watching. The rulers with them also scoffed at him, saying, “He saved others. Let him save himself, if this is the Christ of God, his chosen one!”

Arminian

The rulers scoff: “He saved others. Let him save himself.” Arminian readers note the irony that he saves others precisely by not saving himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same irony. The mockery unwittingly states the gospel: the Christ, God’s chosen one, stays on the cross to save.

Luke 23:36

36The soldiers also mocked him, coming to him and offering him vinegar,

Arminian

Soldiers mock him and offer vinegar. Arminian readers note that contempt reaches across Jew and Gentile alike.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an echo of Psalm 69:21. Both note that all groups present share in the rejection.

Luke 23:37

37and saying, “If you are the King of the Jews, save yourself!”

Arminian

“If you are the King of the Jews, save yourself!” Arminian readers note the repeated taunt to self-rescue, recalling the wilderness temptations (Luke 4:3, 9).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same echo of temptation. Jesus’ obedience holds firm to the end.

Luke 23:38

38An inscription was also written over him in letters of Greek, Latin, and Hebrew: “THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS.”

Arminian

The inscription reads “THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS.” Arminian readers note that what was intended as mockery or charge proclaims the truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence in the irony. Both traditions read the sign as true testimony to Jesus’ kingship.

Luke 23:39

39One of the criminals who was hanged insulted him, saying, “If you are the Christ, save yourself and us!”

Arminian

One criminal joins the insults. Arminian readers note that he faces the same death yet remains hardened, a free and tragic refusal.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that proximity to Jesus, even in suffering, does not by itself produce faith. The contrast with the other criminal is deliberate.

Luke 23:40

40But the other answered, and rebuking him said, “Don’t you even fear God, seeing you are under the same condemnation?

Arminian

The other rebukes him: “Don’t you even fear God?” Arminian readers see conviction leading to response; the man now fears God and speaks honestly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fruit of grace already at work: a heart changed to fear God. Both agree this is real repentance in the last hour.

Key contrast Both see a dramatic conversion. Arminian readers often stress prevenient grace enabling and inviting the criminal’s free response; Reformed readers often stress that the difference between the two criminals is finally God’s effectual grace.

Luke 23:41

41And we indeed justly, for we receive the due reward for our deeds, but this man has done nothing wrong.”

Arminian

He confesses his guilt and Jesus’ innocence. Arminian readers note confession and recognition as the marks of true repentance.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same: he accepts just condemnation and sees Jesus rightly. Both find here the pattern of repentance and faith.

Luke 23:42

42He said to Jesus, “Lord, remember me when you come into your Kingdom.”

Arminian

“Lord, remember me when you come into your Kingdom.” Arminian readers see simple trust as the response God welcomes, with no works to offer.

Reformed

Reformed readers also highlight that he brings nothing but faith. Both traditions read this as salvation by grace through faith.

Luke 23:43

43Jesus said to him, “Assuredly I tell you, today you will be with me in Paradise.”

Arminian

Jesus promises: today you will be with me in Paradise. Arminian readers stress the immediacy and assurance of the promise to one who turns to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same assurance grounded in Christ’s word. Both agree that salvation here is wholly by grace, apart from baptism or works, and that it brings the believer into Christ’s presence.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
