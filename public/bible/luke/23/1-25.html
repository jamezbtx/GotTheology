Passage

Luke 23

Verses 1–25 · WEB

Luke’s trial narrative moves Jesus from the council to Pilate, to Herod, and back to Pilate. Pilate repeatedly declares Jesus innocent, yet yields to the crowd and releases Barabbas. Arminian and Reformed readers alike see the innocent one handed over in place of the guilty; they differ chiefly in how they relate the human choices here to God’s settled purpose.

Luke 23:1

1The whole company of them rose up and brought him before Pilate.

Arminian

The council brings Jesus to Pilate because only Rome can carry out a death sentence. Arminian readers note that Luke presents a collective, responsible decision: the leaders act freely and are accountable for what they choose.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the whole company acts as one, fulfilling what Jesus had foretold about being handed over to the Gentiles (Luke 18:32). Human hostility is real and culpable, yet it unfolds within God’s announced plan.

Luke 23:2

2They began to accuse him, saying, “We found this man perverting the nation, forbidding paying taxes to Caesar, and saying that he himself is Christ, a king.”

Arminian

The charges are recast in political terms Rome would care about: subversion, refusal of taxes, and a rival kingship. Arminian readers observe that the tax charge is false (Luke 20:25), showing how sin twists truth to reach its goal.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see deliberate misrepresentation. The claim that Jesus is Christ, a king, is true in a sense the accusers do not intend; Luke lets the irony stand, since Jesus is indeed the King, though not of the kind they allege.

Luke 23:3

3Pilate asked him, “Are you the King of the Jews?” He answered him, “So you say.”

Arminian

Jesus answers Pilate briefly: “So you say.” Arminian readers see here a quiet refusal either to deny his identity or to accept Pilate’s political framing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same reserve. Jesus does not defend himself; he entrusts his case to the Father, consistent with the Servant who is silent before his accusers (Isaiah 53:7).

Luke 23:4

4Pilate said to the chief priests and the multitudes, “I find no basis for a charge against this man.”

Arminian

Pilate’s first verdict is clear: no basis for a charge. Arminian readers stress that Luke wants the reader to see Jesus’ innocence plainly established by the Roman authority itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Luke builds a repeated testimony of innocence. Theologically, the spotless one is the fitting substitute, for the righteous suffers for the unrighteous (1 Peter 3:18).

Luke 23:5

5But they insisted, saying, “He stirs up the people, teaching throughout all Judea, beginning from Galilee even to this place.”

Arminian

The leaders insist, widening the charge to stirring up the people from Galilee to Jerusalem. Arminian readers note how persistent pressure shapes decisions, a warning about how communities can talk themselves into injustice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the accusation unwittingly summarizes Jesus’ ministry, which did begin in Galilee and move toward Jerusalem. What is meant as a charge traces the path God had set for him (Luke 9:51).

Luke 23:6

6But when Pilate heard Galilee mentioned, he asked if the man was a Galilean.

Arminian

Hearing “Galilee,” Pilate looks for a way out. Arminian readers see a man seeking to avoid responsibility rather than act on what he knows to be just.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same evasion of duty. Both traditions agree Pilate’s maneuvering does not lessen his responsibility for the outcome.

Luke 23:7

7When he found out that he was in Herod’s jurisdiction, he sent him to Herod, who was also in Jerusalem during those days.

Arminian

Pilate sends Jesus to Herod Antipas, who has jurisdiction over Galilee. Arminian readers note the transfer as another human choice that keeps the trial moving.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Luke alone records the hearing before Herod. Acts 4:27 later names Herod and Pilate together as those who gathered against Jesus to do what God’s hand and plan had predestined.

Key contrast Shared ground: Acts 4:27–28 holds both human guilt and God’s plan together. Arminian readers usually explain the plan as God’s foreknowledge and purposeful use of freely chosen wickedness; Reformed readers usually see God’s decree as ordaining the event while the agents remain fully culpable.

Luke 23:8

8Now when Herod saw Jesus, he was exceedingly glad, for he had wanted to see him for a long time, because he had heard many things about him. He hoped to see some miracle done by him.

Arminian

Herod is glad to see Jesus, hoping for a miracle. Arminian readers see curiosity without repentance: interest in spectacle rather than in the message Jesus preached.

Reformed

Reformed readers draw the same lesson. Herod had long wanted to see Jesus (Luke 9:9), but sight without a changed heart does not bring faith.

Luke 23:9

9He questioned him with many words, but he gave no answers.

Arminian

Jesus gives Herod no answer. Arminian readers suggest that Herod, who had silenced John the Baptist, has hardened himself against the word and now meets silence.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see silence as judgment on a frivolous inquirer, and as the Servant’s willing submission. Both note that Jesus does not perform on demand.

Luke 23:10

10The chief priests and the scribes stood, vehemently accusing him.

Arminian

The chief priests and scribes keep accusing him vehemently. Arminian readers note the intensity of resistance from those who had the Scriptures and still refused him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note how religious privilege does not guarantee spiritual sight. Both traditions read this as a sober warning to religious insiders.

Luke 23:11

11Herod with his soldiers humiliated him and mocked him. Dressing him in luxurious clothing, they sent him back to Pilate.

Arminian

Herod and his soldiers mock Jesus and dress him in luxurious clothing. Arminian readers see the cruelty of contempt toward a king who seems powerless.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the irony: the mock robe proclaims a royalty that is real. The humiliation belongs to the path of the suffering King.

Luke 23:12

12Herod and Pilate became friends with each other that very day, for before that they were enemies with each other.

Arminian

Herod and Pilate become friends that day. Arminian readers note how shared rejection of Jesus can unite former enemies.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with Psalm 2:2, cited in Acts 4:25–27: rulers gather together against the Lord’s Anointed. Their alliance fulfills Scripture even as they act on their own motives.

Luke 23:13

13Pilate called together the chief priests, the rulers, and the people,

Arminian

Pilate gathers the leaders and the people. Arminian readers note that “the people” are now included, widening the circle of those who must decide about Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the broadened assembly. Luke shows that the rejection of Jesus involves leaders and crowd together.

Luke 23:14

14and said to them, “You brought this man to me as one that perverts the people, and behold, having examined him before you, I found no basis for a charge against this man concerning those things of which you accuse him.

Arminian

Pilate restates that he found no basis for the charges. Arminian readers stress that the decision to condemn will be made against clear evidence of innocence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Luke emphasizing innocence again. Jesus dies not for any guilt of his own, which is essential to his death being for others.

Luke 23:15

15Neither has Herod, for I sent you to him, and see, nothing worthy of death has been done by him.

Arminian

Pilate adds Herod’s verdict: nothing worthy of death. Arminian readers note the double witness, satisfying the biblical principle of two testimonies.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same double witness. Two rulers who are no friends of Jesus both find him innocent.

Luke 23:16

16I will therefore chastise him and release him.”

Arminian

Pilate proposes to chastise and release him, a compromise that is itself unjust. Arminian readers see a man trying to satisfy everyone rather than do right.

Reformed

Reformed readers see moral weakness under pressure. Both traditions read Pilate as an example of conscience overridden by fear of people.

Luke 23:17

17Now he had to release one prisoner to them at the feast.

Arminian

This verse explains the custom of releasing a prisoner at the feast. The WEB text includes it but footnotes that the NU text omits it; many modern editions place it in a note. Arminian readers note that nothing doctrinal depends on it, since the custom is attested elsewhere in the Gospels.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise treat the variant without alarm; the same custom appears in Matthew 27:15 and Mark 15:6. Both traditions value honest textual criticism and agree the passage’s meaning is unchanged.

Luke 23:18

18But they all cried out together, saying, “Away with this man! Release to us Barabbas!”—

Arminian

The crowd cries out for Barabbas instead of Jesus. Arminian readers see a free and tragic choice: the people prefer a violent rebel to the Prince of peace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a vivid picture of substitution: the guilty man goes free while the innocent one is condemned. Arminians share this reading; the exchange is a gospel image both traditions preach.

Luke 23:19

19one who was thrown into prison for a certain revolt in the city, and for murder.

Arminian

Luke identifies Barabbas as one imprisoned for revolt and murder. Arminian readers note the stark contrast with Jesus, who has been falsely charged with stirring up the people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the one who truly was guilty of the charges against Jesus is released. The innocent bears the place of the guilty.

Luke 23:20

20Then Pilate spoke to them again, wanting to release Jesus,

Arminian

Pilate speaks again, wanting to release Jesus. Arminian readers note that Pilate’s desire for justice is real but weak, and desire alone does not act.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Good impulses that yield to pressure do not excuse the final decision.

Luke 23:21

21but they shouted, saying, “Crucify! Crucify him!”

Arminian

The crowd shouts for crucifixion. Arminian readers observe how quickly a crowd can turn, and stress each person’s accountability within it.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe the depth of human hostility to God’s Messiah. Both traditions see here a mirror of sin in every human heart, not only of one crowd.

Luke 23:22

22He said to them the third time, “Why? What evil has this man done? I have found no capital crime in him. I will therefore chastise him and release him.”

Arminian

A third time Pilate declares Jesus innocent. Arminian readers note Luke’s threefold emphasis, matching Peter’s threefold denial in the previous chapter.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same pattern. The repeated verdict underlines that Jesus’ death is the just for the unjust.

Luke 23:23

23But they were urgent with loud voices, asking that he might be crucified. Their voices and the voices of the chief priests prevailed.

Arminian

The loud voices prevail. Arminian readers see how persistent pressure overcomes a judge who will not stand for what he knows.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human evil prevailing for a moment, yet within God’s purpose. Both agree it is a real human victory of injustice, and real guilt.

Luke 23:24

24Pilate decreed that what they asked for should be done.

Arminian

Pilate decrees that their demand be done. Arminian readers stress that Pilate cannot shift responsibility; he gives the decision.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree Pilate is fully responsible. They also see the sentence carrying out what had been determined beforehand (Acts 2:23).

Key contrast Both affirm Acts 2:23, Jesus delivered up by God’s definite plan and foreknowledge and killed by lawless hands. Arminian readers often emphasize foreknowledge and permission; Reformed readers emphasize the definite plan as God’s decree, while both insist the human agents are guilty.

Luke 23:25

25He released him who had been thrown into prison for insurrection and murder, for whom they asked, but he delivered Jesus up to their will.

Arminian

He releases the insurrectionist and delivers Jesus to their will. Arminian readers note the bitter summary: the people get what they chose.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same reversal and read it as substitution. Both traditions find in Barabbas a picture of every sinner set free because Jesus is handed over.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
