Passage

Luke 23

Verses 44–56 · WEB

Darkness falls, the temple veil is torn, and Jesus commits his spirit to the Father and dies. The centurion confesses him righteous, the crowds go home grieving, and Joseph of Arimathea buries him while the women watch. Both traditions see Jesus’ trusting death and real burial as central to the gospel.

Luke 23:44

44It was now about the sixth hour, and darkness came over the whole land until the ninth hour.

Arminian

Darkness covers the land from noon to three. Arminian readers see a sign of judgment and cosmic grief at the death of the Son.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a sign of judgment, recalling Amos 8:9. Both agree Luke presents the darkness as meaningful, not merely atmospheric.

Luke 23:45

45The sun was darkened, and the veil of the temple was torn in two.

Arminian

The sun is darkened and the temple veil torn. Arminian readers see access to God opened through Jesus’ death (Hebrews 10:19–20).

Reformed

Reformed readers read the torn veil the same way, and some also see judgment on the old order. Both agree the way into God’s presence is now through Christ.

Luke 23:46

46Jesus, crying with a loud voice, said, “Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!” Having said this, he breathed his last.

Arminian

Jesus cries, “Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!” quoting Psalm 31:5. Arminian readers see trusting surrender, a model for believers in death.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same trust and note that Jesus gives up his life rather than having it taken from him in defeat. Both find comfort here for dying believers.

Luke 23:47

47When the centurion saw what was done, he glorified God, saying, “Certainly this was a righteous man.”

Arminian

The centurion glorifies God, calling Jesus a righteous man. Arminian readers note that a Gentile soldier responds rightly to what he sees.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the final human verdict at the cross confirms Jesus’ innocence, and that a Gentile glorifies God, anticipating the mission to the nations.

Luke 23:48

48All the multitudes that came together to see this, when they saw the things that were done, returned home beating their chests.

Arminian

The crowds go home beating their chests. Arminian readers see the beginnings of remorse that could lead to repentance (Acts 2:37).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same sign of grief. Both note that sorrow is a beginning, not yet full repentance.

Luke 23:49

49All his acquaintances and the women who followed with him from Galilee stood at a distance, watching these things.

Arminian

His acquaintances and the women stand at a distance watching. Arminian readers note the faithfulness of the women who stayed.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that these become witnesses of the death and burial, important for the resurrection testimony. Both honor the women’s steadfastness.

Luke 23:50

50Behold, there was a man named Joseph, who was a member of the council, a good and righteous man

Arminian

Joseph, a council member, is good and righteous. Arminian readers note that not every leader consented; individual choices mattered.

Reformed

Reformed readers note God raising up a faithful man in an unlikely place. Both appreciate Luke’s fair portrait of a dissenting council member.

Luke 23:51

51(he had not consented to their counsel and deed), from Arimathaea, a city of the Jews, who was also waiting for God’s Kingdom.

Arminian

He had not consented to the council’s decision and was waiting for God’s Kingdom. Arminian readers see a man whose expectant faith shaped his action.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same godly expectation, like Simeon and Anna (Luke 2). Both see him as among the faithful remnant.

Luke 23:52

52This man went to Pilate, and asked for Jesus’ body.

Arminian

Joseph asks Pilate for Jesus’ body. Arminian readers note the courage it took to identify with a condemned man.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same courage. Both see his request as an act of love and public allegiance.

Luke 23:53

53He took it down and wrapped it in a linen cloth, and laid him in a tomb that was cut in stone, where no one had ever been laid.

Arminian

He wraps the body and lays it in a new rock-cut tomb. Arminian readers stress that Jesus truly died and was truly buried.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same reality of death and burial, part of the gospel Paul received (1 Corinthians 15:3–4). The new tomb also helps identify the place later found empty.

Luke 23:54

54It was the day of the Preparation, and the Sabbath was drawing near.

Arminian

It was the Day of Preparation, with the Sabbath approaching. Arminian readers note the time pressure that shapes the burial.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both observe that the Sabbath rest sets up the first-day discovery in chapter 24.

Luke 23:55

55The women who had come with him out of Galilee followed after, and saw the tomb and how his body was laid.

Arminian

The women follow and see the tomb and how the body was laid. Arminian readers note that they know exactly where he was buried.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same: their later testimony rests on accurate observation, not confusion.

Luke 23:56

56They returned and prepared spices and ointments. On the Sabbath they rested according to the commandment.

Arminian

They prepare spices and rest on the Sabbath according to the commandment. Arminian readers see devoted, law-keeping disciples who expect to anoint a dead body.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that their faithfulness to the commandment and their preparations show they were not expecting resurrection, which makes their later witness more striking.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
