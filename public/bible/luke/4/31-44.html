Passage

Luke 4

Verses 31–44 · WEB

In Capernaum, Jesus’ authoritative teaching is demonstrated by an exorcism, the healing of Simon Peter’s mother-in-law, and care for many sufferers. He silences demons who recognize his identity and refuses to let one town confine his work. Both traditions affirm his authority over evil and illness and the priority of proclaiming God’s Kingdom in other cities.

Luke 4:31

31He came down to Capernaum, a city of Galilee. He was teaching them on the Sabbath day,

Arminian

After Nazareth, Jesus teaches in Capernaum’s synagogue on the Sabbath. Both traditions see his continuing commitment to proclaiming God’s word despite rejection, now accompanied by a public act of deliverance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that hostility does not divert Jesus from his teaching mission. Capernaum becomes the setting where the authority of his word is demonstrated against an unclean spirit.

Luke 4:32

32and they were astonished at his teaching, for his word was with authority.

Arminian

The congregation is struck by the authority of Jesus’ teaching. Arminian readers understand his word as making a claim upon hearers, not merely supplying religious information or attracting admiration.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm the inherent authority of Christ’s word. The following exorcism displays in action the commanding power already recognized in his teaching.

Luke 4:33

33In the synagogue there was a man who had a spirit of an unclean demon; and he cried out with a loud voice,

Arminian

An unclean spirit interrupts the synagogue gathering through an afflicted man. Both traditions recognize real spiritual opposition and distinguish the sufferer needing deliverance from the hostile power speaking through him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the encounter as conflict with a personal evil power. The synagogue setting shows that religious surroundings themselves do not exclude the need for Christ’s delivering authority.

Luke 4:34

34saying, “Ah! what have we to do with you, Jesus of Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are: the Holy One of God!”

Arminian

The demon recognizes Jesus as God’s Holy One and fears destruction. Both traditions distinguish accurate recognition of his identity from saving faith: hostile spiritual knowledge is not trusting allegiance to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see involuntary acknowledgment of Christ’s holiness and superior power. The demon’s fear exposes the opposition between Jesus’ mission and the evil spirit’s presence in the man.

Luke 4:35

35Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be silent and come out of him!” When the demon had thrown him down in the middle of them, he came out of him, having done him no harm.

Arminian

Jesus silences and expels the spirit by command, preserving the man from harm despite the demon’s violence. Both traditions see liberation through Christ’s authority, not a struggle between equally matched powers.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the effectiveness of Jesus’ word. The demon must depart, and the man’s safety displays the deliverer’s power over both the hostile spirit and its destructive intent.

Luke 4:36

36Amazement came on all and they spoke together, one with another, saying, “What is this word? For with authority and power he commands the unclean spirits, and they come out!”

Arminian

The crowd connects the expulsion with Jesus’ authoritative word. Arminian readers see the miracle confirming his teaching and displaying liberation from evil, while amazement still calls for a response of faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the exorcism as a public demonstration of Christ’s authority. The spirits obey because his command overmasters them, and the witnesses must reckon with who is speaking.

Luke 4:37

37News about him went out into every place of the surrounding region.

Arminian

News of Jesus spreads beyond the immediate witnesses. Both traditions see how a local act of deliverance widens attention to his ministry without treating widespread reputation as equivalent to discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the public impact of Christ’s power. The report carries knowledge of his work throughout the region, preparing for the crowds seeking his help.

Luke 4:38

38He rose up from the synagogue and entered into Simon’s house. Simon’s mother-in-law was afflicted with a great fever, and they begged him to help her.

Arminian

The scene moves from synagogue to Simon Peter’s household, where his mother-in-law suffers a severe fever. Arminian readers see compassionate intercession in the household’s appeal for Jesus to help her.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm Christ’s concern for particular sufferers in ordinary homes. The appeal rests on his ability to act; the woman’s need, not any claim of merit, is brought before him.

Luke 4:39

39He stood over her and rebuked the fever, and it left her. Immediately she rose up and served them.

Arminian

The fever leaves at Jesus’ rebuke, and immediate service shows restored strength. Both traditions see complete healing and a fitting response of service, not payment required to purchase his mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the effectiveness of Christ’s command and the completeness of recovery. Rebuking the fever displays authority over illness without identifying this sickness as a demon.

Luke 4:40

40When the sun was setting, all those who had any sick with various diseases brought them to him; and he laid his hands on every one of them, and healed them.

Arminian

At sunset, the Sabbath’s close, sufferers with varied diseases are brought to Jesus. Arminian readers emphasize his personal care for each one, with no disease among these patients beyond his healing power.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see abundant compassion joined to sovereign power. Jesus heals every person brought in this scene; the account does not promise immediate bodily healing to every believer in every age.

Luke 4:41

41Demons also came out of many, crying out and saying, “You are the Christ, the Son of God!” Rebuking them, he didn’t allow them to speak, because they knew that he was the Christ.

Arminian

Jesus refuses demonic testimony even when its titles are accurate. Both traditions affirm him as Christ and Son of God, while recognizing that hostile spirits cannot dictate how his identity and mission are disclosed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish truthful titles from acceptable witness. Christ commands the demons’ silence as well as their departure, exercising authority over their attempts to publicize what they know.

Luke 4:42

42When it was day, he departed and went into an uninhabited place and the multitudes looked for him, and came to him, and held on to him, so that he wouldn’t go away from them.

Arminian

The crowds try to keep Jesus with them after he withdraws to an uninhabited place. Arminian readers see understandable desire for his presence, but local attachment must yield to the wider reach of his commission.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize that grateful or needy crowds cannot determine the boundaries of Christ’s mission. Their insistence supplies the setting for his explanation of why he must go elsewhere.

Luke 4:43

43But he said to them, “I must preach the good news of God’s Kingdom to the other cities also. For this reason I have been sent.”

Arminian

Jesus’ divine sending requires proclamation in other cities as well. Both traditions affirm that healing serves a mission centered on the good news of God’s Kingdom, which no single community may reserve for itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Father’s commission governing the Son’s movements. His necessity is obedient purpose: announcing God’s reign takes priority over remaining where popularity and immediate demand would keep him.

Luke 4:44

44He was preaching in the synagogues of Galilee.

Arminian

The closing summary presents Jesus continuing his synagogue preaching across Galilee. Arminian readers see faithful pursuit of the commission just stated: other communities, too, are to hear the good news.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the mission advancing through sustained proclamation. Deliverance and healing do not replace teaching; Christ continues announcing God’s Kingdom beyond the place that wished to retain him.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
