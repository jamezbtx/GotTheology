Passage

Luke 4

Verses 1–13 · WEB

After his baptism and genealogy, the Spirit leads Jesus into the wilderness, where forty days of testing recall Israel’s experience. He answers the devil from Deuteronomy, refusing self-serving power, idolatrous dominion, and a presumptuous demand for protection. Both traditions affirm the faithful Son’s obedience and God’s sovereignty over Satan’s limited power.

Luke 4:1

1Jesus, full of the Holy Spirit, returned from the Jordan and was led by the Spirit into the wilderness

Arminian

Following his baptism and genealogy, Jesus enters the wilderness as the Spirit-filled Son. Both traditions affirm that the Spirit leads him into conflict without becoming the author of the devil’s evil.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the Spirit’s holy purpose from Satan’s malice. The Son enters testing under divine direction, beginning his public ministry in obedient dependence on the Spirit.

Luke 4:2

2for forty days, being tempted by the devil. He ate nothing in those days. Afterward, when they were completed, he was hungry.

Arminian

Forty days recall Israel’s wilderness testing, while hunger displays Jesus’ genuine humanity. Arminian readers affirm that his victory answers real temptation through faithful obedience, not freedom from bodily weakness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm Christ’s true humanity and sinless obedience under testing. Where Israel failed in the wilderness, the faithful Son withstands the adversary amid hunger and deprivation.

Luke 4:3

3The devil said to him, “If you are the Son of God, command this stone to become bread.”

Arminian

The temptation presses Jesus to use his sonship to satisfy hunger at the tempter’s command. Both traditions distinguish legitimate bodily need from accepting Satan’s direction about how the Son should act.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the devil exploiting hunger to challenge filial trust. Jesus’ sonship does not mean using divine power independently of the Father’s will or at an enemy’s bidding.

Luke 4:4

4Jesus answered him, saying, “It is written, ‘Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word of God.’ ”

Arminian

Jesus answers from Deuteronomy 8:3, placing dependence on God above immediate appetite. Arminian readers see obedient trust nourished by God’s word; bread is necessary, but bodily provision is not life’s ultimate foundation.

Reformed

Reformed readers share this appeal to Scripture and dependence on God. Christ faithfully embodies the obedience Israel owed, refusing to make hunger the measure of what his Father must provide.

Luke 4:5

5The devil, leading him up on a high mountain, showed him all the kingdoms of the world in a moment of time.

Arminian

The sudden display of kingdoms introduces a temptation to dominion on Satan’s terms. Both traditions see a counterfeit route to glory, offered in place of the Son’s obedient mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the vision as a lure toward kingship detached from obedience. The greatness of the kingdoms does not make the means of acquiring them legitimate.

Luke 4:6

6The devil said to him, “I will give you all this authority and their glory, for it has been delivered to me, and I give it to whomever I want.

Arminian

Satan claims power to distribute worldly authority, but his boast is not the narrator’s endorsement of unlimited ownership. Both traditions recognize real satanic influence while affirming that God remains sovereign over every kingdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise reject Satan’s claim to independent sovereignty. His influence operates under God’s rule, and his offer cannot establish that worldly dominion is his to grant without limit.

Luke 4:7

7If you therefore will worship before me, it will all be yours.”

Arminian

The price of promised authority is worship, exposing the offer as idolatry. Arminian readers see that no desired outcome can justify surrendering allegiance to God, however attractive the promised power.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that worship belongs to God alone. Satan’s condition reveals the spiritual character of the bargain: apparent glory would be purchased through rebellion against the Father.

Luke 4:8

8Jesus answered him, “Get behind me, Satan! For it is written, ‘You shall worship the Lord your God, and you shall serve him only.’ ”

Arminian

Jesus answers with Deuteronomy 6:13, insisting on exclusive worship and service to God. Both traditions see his refusal as faithful covenant obedience rather than negotiation over Satan’s promised reward.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm the Son’s undivided allegiance. Scripture governs worship, and Jesus will not secure a kingdom by serving the very enemy whose dominion his mission will overthrow.

Luke 4:9

9He led him to Jerusalem and set him on the pinnacle of the temple, and said to him, “If you are the Son of God, cast yourself down from here,

Arminian

The temple setting turns the temptation toward a dramatic test of divine protection. Both traditions distinguish trusting God’s care from deliberately creating danger to demand proof of sonship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see presumption rather than faith in the proposed leap. A sacred location cannot sanctify an act undertaken at Satan’s direction to force a demonstration from God.

Luke 4:10

10for it is written, ‘He will put his angels in charge of you, to guard you;’

Arminian

Satan cites Psalm 91, showing that biblical language can be used to promote disobedience. Arminian readers affirm the promise of angelic care while rejecting its use as permission to manufacture a crisis.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish Scripture’s authority from the tempter’s misuse of it. The promise of God’s protection must be received within obedience, not made a warrant for testing him.

Luke 4:11

11and, ‘On their hands they will bear you up, lest perhaps you dash your foot against a stone.’ ”

Arminian

The promise of being borne up is pressed into service of a reckless demand. Both traditions affirm God’s ability to protect without treating that promise as a guarantee for every self-chosen danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that divine protection cannot be manipulated by presumptuous action. Satan’s quotation does not authorize the leap; the proper reading must accord with God’s command against testing him.

Luke 4:12

12Jesus answering, said to him, “It has been said, ‘You shall not tempt the Lord your God.’ ”

Arminian

Jesus answers with Deuteronomy 6:16, recalling Israel’s testing of God at Massah. Arminian readers see obedient trust refusing to make faith conditional on a demanded sign.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Son succeeding where Israel failed. He reads the promise of protection consistently with God’s command, refusing to turn confidence in the Father into a test of him.

Luke 4:13

13When the devil had completed every temptation, he departed from him until another time.

Arminian

The devil withdraws, but only until another opportunity; victory here does not end opposition to Jesus. Both traditions recognize a decisive opening victory within a ministry that will face further satanic hostility.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see this departure as temporary rather than Satan’s final defeat. The obedient Son continues toward his appointed work, with renewed conflict still ahead.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
