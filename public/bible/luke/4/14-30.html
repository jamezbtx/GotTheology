Passage

Luke 4

Verses 14–30 · WEB

Jesus begins his Galilean ministry in the Spirit’s power and reads Isaiah’s promise of good news and release in Nazareth’s synagogue. He declares its fulfillment in his own ministry. Wonder turns to violence when Elijah’s visit to the widow of Zarephath and Elisha’s cleansing of Naaman challenge hometown entitlement. Both traditions affirm the Messiah’s fulfillment of Scripture and mercy beyond Israel.

Luke 4:14

14Jesus returned in the power of the Spirit into Galilee, and news about him spread through all the surrounding area.

Arminian

The Spirit’s power links the wilderness victory with Jesus’ ministry in Galilee. Both traditions see his teaching and works as the unfolding of his divine commission, not the kingdom offered by Satan.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise trace continuity between the Spirit-led testing and public ministry. News spreads because the anointed Son is now exercising his mission among the people.

Luke 4:15

15He taught in their synagogues, being glorified by all.

Arminian

Jesus teaches within Israel’s synagogues and initially receives widespread praise. Arminian readers distinguish this favorable reception from the persevering faith and obedience that his message calls forth.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize that public admiration need not amount to saving faith. The praise sets the scene for Nazareth, where familiarity will soon become resistance to his message.

Luke 4:16

16He came to Nazareth, where he had been brought up. He entered, as was his custom, into the synagogue on the Sabbath day, and stood up to read.

Arminian

Jesus’ customary Sabbath worship places his mission within Israel’s scriptural life. Both traditions see his return to his hometown as an encounter with people who know his upbringing but must still hear his claim.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note Jesus’ regular participation in synagogue worship. His standing to read prepares for a declaration that will challenge the congregation’s assumptions about its familiar neighbor.

Luke 4:17

17The book of the prophet Isaiah was handed to him. He opened the book, and found the place where it was written,

Arminian

The reading from Isaiah supplies the scriptural frame for Jesus’ mission. Arminian readers see him identifying his ministry with God’s promised deliverance, rather than announcing a merely private religious experience.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the selected passage as a public declaration of messianic vocation. The prophetic promise provides the terms in which Jesus explains what his hearers are witnessing.

Luke 4:18

18“The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me to preach good news to the poor. He has sent me to heal the broken hearted, to proclaim release to the captives, recovering of sight to the blind, to deliver those who are crushed,

Arminian

Both traditions identify Jesus as the Spirit-anointed Messiah bringing good news and release. Arminian readers emphasize his gracious approach to the poor, broken, and oppressed, whose need rather than social standing makes the promise good news.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm the Messiah’s Spirit-given mission to the needy. His preaching and acts of restoration reveal divine mercy toward people unable to deliver themselves; spiritual rescue does not erase his concern for bodily suffering.

Luke 4:19

19and to proclaim the acceptable year of the Lord.”

Arminian

The acceptable year evokes God’s gracious time of release, with Jubilee resonance. Both traditions understand this favor as arriving through Jesus’ mission, not as a reward the congregation has earned.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the announcement of divine favor fulfilled in Christ. The language gathers the promised release into a proclamation of God’s saving visitation rather than a timetable supplied by the hearers.

Luke 4:20

20He closed the book, gave it back to the attendant, and sat down. The eyes of all in the synagogue were fastened on him.

Arminian

Jesus returns the scroll and sits to address an attentive congregation. Arminian readers see the narrative pause preparing for his striking claim: the promise just read concerns what God is doing through him now.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note how the scene moves from reading to interpretation. The congregation’s fixed attention heightens the significance of Jesus’ identification of himself with Isaiah’s promise.

Luke 4:21

21He began to tell them, “Today, this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing.”

Arminian

Jesus declares present fulfillment, not merely a distant hope. Both traditions affirm that Isaiah’s promise is realized in the person and ministry of the Spirit-anointed Christ, whose word now confronts his hearers.

Reformed

Reformed readers share this christological fulfillment: Jesus is the promised bearer of good news and release. His hearers stand before the fulfillment itself, not simply an exposition of an absent deliverer.

Luke 4:22

22All testified about him and wondered at the gracious words which proceeded out of his mouth; and they said, “Isn’t this Joseph’s son?”

Arminian

Wonder at gracious speech coexists with questions about Jesus’ familiar origins. Arminian readers see the danger of allowing familiarity to become resistance rather than responding in faith to God’s gracious visitation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish admiration from recognition of Jesus’ divine commission. Identifying him by his local family does not settle who he is or give his neighbors authority over his mission.

Luke 4:23

23He said to them, “Doubtless you will tell me this proverb, ‘Physician, heal yourself! Whatever we have heard done at Capernaum, do also here in your hometown.’ ”

Arminian

Jesus anticipates a demand that his hometown receive the works reported from Capernaum. Both traditions reject the assumption that familiarity entitles Nazareth to miracles on its own terms.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a claim of local privilege behind the proverb. God’s messenger is not bound to prove himself by satisfying demands for benefits claimed as a hometown right.

Luke 4:24

24He said, “Most certainly I tell you, no prophet is acceptable in his hometown.

Arminian

Jesus places Nazareth’s resistance within the pattern of rejected prophets. Arminian readers emphasize the responsibility of hearers who can encounter a gracious message yet refuse the messenger God sends.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize culpable rejection rather than innocent lack of opportunity. Familiarity with the prophet does not overcome unbelief or oblige God to submit his commission to local approval.

Luke 4:25

25But truly I tell you, there were many widows in Israel in the days of Elijah, when the sky was shut up three years and six months, when a great famine came over all the land.

Arminian

The famine in Elijah’s day prepares a challenge to inherited privilege. Both traditions see the many needy widows in Israel as the backdrop to mercy reaching someone outside Israel’s borders.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise notice that need and membership in Israel did not establish a claim on Elijah’s presence. The example prepares the congregation to hear of God’s freedom in sending his prophet.

Luke 4:26

26Elijah was sent to none of them, except to Zarephath, in the land of Sidon, to a woman who was a widow.

Arminian

God freely sends Elijah to a Gentile widow in Zarephath. Arminian readers emphasize grace crossing ethnic boundaries and the danger of unbelief forfeiting blessing; neither Israel nor Nazareth can claim mercy as an entitlement.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm mercy beyond Israel, emphasizing God’s sovereign freedom to distinguish recipients of his benefits. Elijah’s particular sending rests on divine generosity, not an entitlement possessed by the many other widows.

Key contrast Both deny that Israel or Nazareth can claim mercy as an ethnic or local entitlement. Arminian readers emphasize freely extended grace and blessing forfeited through unbelief; Reformed readers emphasize God’s sovereign freedom in distinguishing the recipients of his mercy. The immediate example concerns prophetic aid, not a direct account of each person’s eternal destiny.

Luke 4:27

27There were many lepers in Israel in the time of Elisha the prophet, yet not one of them was cleansed, except Naaman, the Syrian.”

Arminian

Naaman’s cleansing supplies a second example of mercy reaching a Gentile while others remain unhealed. Both traditions see a rebuke to ethnic entitlement, not proof that every Israelite lacked faith or that bodily healing equals eternal salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Syrian’s cleansing as an instance of freely bestowed mercy. Together with the widow, Naaman challenges Nazareth’s demand for privileged treatment without making nationality a measure of who can receive grace.

Luke 4:28

28They were all filled with wrath in the synagogue as they heard these things.

Arminian

The congregation’s anger reveals resistance when grace challenges its presumed privilege. Arminian readers see a concrete rejection of God’s gracious messenger, turning initial admiration into hostility toward his word.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize culpable hostility exposed by Jesus’ teaching. The hearers welcome gracious speech until its implications undermine their claim to control the reach of divine mercy.

Luke 4:29

29They rose up, threw him out of the city, and led him to the brow of the hill that their city was built on, that they might throw him off the cliff.

Arminian

The attempt to throw Jesus from the cliff enacts the rejection of the prophet he has just described. Both traditions condemn the crowd’s violence as human sin, not a faithful defense of Israel’s Scriptures.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the congregation’s outrage hardening into attempted killing. God’s government of Jesus’ mission does not excuse the crowd’s responsibility for trying to destroy the one sent to them.

Luke 4:30

30But he, passing through the middle of them, went his way.

Arminian

Jesus passes through the crowd and continues his mission. Both traditions affirm his deliverance without supplying a mechanism Luke does not describe; Nazareth’s violence does not terminate the work entrusted to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see divine preservation in Jesus’ escape. The rejected Messiah continues his appointed ministry, while Luke leaves the manner of his passage through the crowd unexplained.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
