Passage

Luke 18

Verses 31–43 · WEB

Jesus tells the twelve that suffering, death, and resurrection await him in Jerusalem, but they fail to understand. Near Jericho, a blind man persistently asks for mercy, receives sight, and follows Jesus in praise. The juxtaposition highlights the need for understanding and mercy without reducing the disciples’ difficulty or the man’s bodily suffering to a simple allegory.

Luke 18:31

31He took the twelve aside and said to them, “Behold, we are going up to Jerusalem, and all the things that are written through the prophets concerning the Son of Man will be completed.

Arminian

Jesus describes the journey to Jerusalem as fulfilling the prophets’ witness concerning the Son of Man. Arminian readers see a purposeful mission freely embraced rather than an accidental disaster. Confidence in fulfillment does not turn the later violence into morally innocent behavior.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the coming suffering within God’s redemptive purpose and scriptural promise. Jesus moves knowingly toward it. Both traditions affirm fulfillment without pretending that this verse identifies every prophetic passage or resolves every question about divine purpose and human agency.

Luke 18:32

32For he will be delivered up to the Gentiles, will be mocked, treated shamefully, and spit on.

Arminian

Jesus predicts being handed to Gentiles and subjected to public humiliation. Arminian readers see the depth of his suffering and his willingness to face it. Foreknowledge of these acts does not excuse the people who will carry them out.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Savior’s humiliation as part of the planned road to redemption, while those inflicting shame remain responsible. The prediction should direct attention to Christ’s costly mission rather than encourage contempt toward any people group.

Luke 18:33

33They will scourge and kill him. On the third day, he will rise again.”

Arminian

The prediction joins death with resurrection on the third day. Arminian readers see the promise of victory inseparable from the reality of suffering. Jesus’ mission is not merely an example of courage; it moves through death toward God’s life-giving vindication.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see death and resurrection together at the center of Christ’s saving work. The cross does not defeat God’s purpose. Both traditions should resist separating the comfort of resurrection from the costly humiliation Jesus explicitly announces.

Luke 18:34

34They understood none of these things. This saying was hidden from them, and they didn’t understand the things that were said.

Arminian

The disciples do not understand, and Luke describes the saying as hidden from them. Arminian readers see the need for gracious illumination even among followers. The verse does not specify every cause of the concealment, so it should not be turned into a complete account of spiritual inability.

Reformed

Reformed readers may see divine withholding of understanding until the appropriate time, while recognizing that Luke does not explain the mechanism here. Revelation depends on God’s illumination. The disciples’ temporary incomprehension is not a declaration that they have been finally rejected or cannot later understand.

Luke 18:35

35As he came near Jericho, a certain blind man sat by the road, begging.

Arminian

Near Jericho, a blind beggar sits beside the road. Arminian readers see a person whose need Jesus will not dismiss. His bodily condition is real and should not be reduced to a symbol or assumed to reveal a specific personal sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to the concrete vulnerability of the man Jesus encounters. The narrative may invite reflection on understanding after the disciples’ confusion, but it remains an actual act of mercy toward suffering, not merely an allegory.

Luke 18:36

36Hearing a multitude going by, he asked what this meant.

Arminian

The man hears the passing crowd and asks what is happening. Arminian readers see ordinary inquiry becoming part of his encounter with Jesus. The narrative does not require every preliminary action to function as a technical statement about the order of salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s mercy reaching a person through ordinary events and information. The crowd’s passage creates an occasion for seeking help. The details serve the encounter without requiring an elaborate doctrinal meaning for each stage.

Luke 18:37

37They told him that Jesus of Nazareth was passing by.

Arminian

He is told that Jesus of Nazareth is passing by. Arminian readers see the significance of making Jesus known to someone who cannot see the scene. Information becomes an occasion for appeal, though the report itself is not yet the man’s response of trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a simple report serving as the occasion for calling on Christ. Ordinary human speech can direct a needy person toward the Savior. Luke’s brief wording should not be expanded into an unrecorded sermon or conversion history.

Luke 18:38

38He cried out, “Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!”

Arminian

The beggar addresses Jesus as David’s son and asks for mercy. Arminian readers see trust reaching toward Jesus’ royal authority without making a claim to deserving help. The appeal unites recognition of who Jesus is with honest acknowledgment of need.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see faith expressed as dependence on the promised king’s mercy. The man does not offer achievement or status as a basis for hearing. Both traditions recognize that this petition honors Jesus rather than treating him as an impersonal source of power.

Luke 18:39

39Those who led the way rebuked him, that he should be quiet; but he cried out all the more, “You son of David, have mercy on me!”

Arminian

The crowd tries to silence him, but his appeal becomes more insistent. Arminian readers see persistent trust refusing to let social pressure block the request for mercy. His persistence does not purchase Jesus’ attention; it expresses the urgency of his dependence.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a needy person continuing to seek Christ despite discouragement from others. The scene challenges communities that make access to Jesus harder for the vulnerable. Faith’s persistence is not a merit competing with the grace it seeks.

Luke 18:40

40Standing still, Jesus commanded him to be brought to him. When he had come near, he asked him,

Arminian

Jesus stops and has the man brought near. Arminian readers see compassionate attention answering rather than dismissing an insistent cry. His question honors the petitioner as a person, not merely an obstacle in the journey or an occasion for display.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ’s deliberate mercy at the center of the encounter. The crowd that discouraged the man must now help bring him forward. Jesus’ action shows authority exercised to welcome the needy rather than preserve the convenience of the powerful.

Luke 18:41

41“What do you want me to do?” He said, “Lord, that I may see again.”

Arminian

Jesus invites the man to state his desire, and he asks to see again. Arminian readers see particular need brought openly before the Lord. Prayer is personal dependence, not information supplied to an otherwise unaware God or a formula guaranteeing any desired outcome.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Jesus dignifying a concrete request. Divine knowledge does not make petition meaningless, since Christ himself asks the man to speak. The desired restoration concerns bodily sight and should not be replaced with only a generalized spiritual interpretation.

Luke 18:42

42Jesus said to him, “Receive your sight. Your faith has healed you.”

Arminian

Jesus grants sight and links healing with the man’s faith. Arminian readers understand faith as trusting reception enabled by grace, not a force independent of Jesus. This particular healing does not justify blaming every unhealed person for inadequate faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise locate healing power in Christ rather than the strength of the petitioner’s belief. Faith receives the benefit and gives no ground for boasting. The statement describes this merciful encounter, not an unrestricted promise that all illness yields to sufficient confidence.

Luke 18:43

43Immediately he received his sight and followed him, glorifying God. All the people, when they saw it, praised God.

Arminian

The restored man follows Jesus and glorifies God, and the crowd joins in praise. Arminian readers see mercy leading to grateful discipleship rather than merely a return to private comfort. The response points toward an ongoing life shaped by the gift received.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see praise and following as fitting fruit of Christ’s gracious work. The benefit becomes public testimony to God’s goodness. Both traditions end the scene with gratitude to the giver, not admiration for an independently powerful human faith.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
