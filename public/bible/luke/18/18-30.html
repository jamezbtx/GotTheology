Passage

Luke 18

Verses 18–30 · WEB

A rich ruler asks about eternal life, but Jesus’ call to give to the poor and follow him exposes the hold of wealth. The disciples’ alarm leads to the declaration that salvation depends on God’s ability, not human resources. Jesus then assures those who sacrifice for the Kingdom of God’s generous care and the hope of eternal life.

Luke 18:18

18A certain ruler asked him, saying, “Good Teacher, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?”

Arminian

The ruler asks what he must do to inherit eternal life. Arminian readers see a serious question that Jesus will probe rather than simply affirm. Inheritance directs attention beyond immediate possessions, but his response will expose what he is unwilling to relinquish.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the question opening an examination of confidence in human goodness. Jesus does not answer by offering a manageable religious achievement. The dialogue will lead from apparent obedience and wealth to the necessity of God’s saving power.

Luke 18:19

19Jesus asked him, “Why do you call me good? No one is good, except one: God.

Arminian

Jesus presses the ruler to consider the meaning of goodness in relation to God. Arminian readers do not understand this as a denial of Jesus’ divine identity. The question unsettles a casual compliment and challenges confidence in ordinary human standards of being good.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Jesus directing the ruler toward God as the measure of goodness. He is not confessing personal sin or denying his identity. The exchange prepares the exposure of a heart that has underestimated both goodness and its own attachments.

Luke 18:20

20You know the commandments: ‘Don’t commit adultery,’ ‘Don’t murder,’ ‘Don’t steal,’ ‘Don’t give false testimony,’ ‘Honor your father and your mother.’ ”

Arminian

Jesus recalls commandments governing conduct toward others. Arminian readers see divine instruction confronting the ruler’s understanding of obedience. The list is not a complete plan for earning salvation; the coming challenge will reveal a conflict beneath his confident account.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law exposing the limits of self-assessment rather than providing a works-based alternative to grace. The commandments are good, but merely claiming compliance cannot establish a righteous heart. Jesus will test allegiance in a concrete way.

Luke 18:21

21He said, “I have observed all these things from my youth up.”

Arminian

The ruler says he has observed these commands from youth. Arminian readers see a self-report that Jesus probes further, not an inspired declaration of sinless obedience. Sincere religious commitment can coexist with an attachment that resists a costly call.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the man’s estimate of himself from God’s full judgment. External observance cannot demonstrate freedom from idolatrous attachment. Jesus’ next instruction brings the question from general claims of obedience to the particular hold of his possessions.

Luke 18:22

22When Jesus heard these things, he said to him, “You still lack one thing. Sell all that you have and distribute it to the poor. Then you will have treasure in heaven; then come, follow me.”

Arminian

Jesus calls the ruler to give to the poor and follow him. Arminian readers see a concrete invitation that exposes a rival allegiance and calls for grace-enabled surrender. Selling possessions is not a price that purchases eternal life; following Jesus is the central issue.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see wealth’s hold uncovered by a personal command. Jesus does not establish a monetary transaction for justification or make poverty itself saving. The demand tests whether the ruler will treasure Christ above the security of what he owns.

Luke 18:23

23But when he heard these things, he became very sad, for he was very rich.

Arminian

The ruler becomes sad because he is very rich. Arminian readers see the real resistance that attachment can offer to a gracious summons. Luke records this response but does not here narrate his entire future, so a final verdict beyond the passage should not be supplied.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sorrow revealing the depth of the obstacle wealth presents. The account exposes inability to free oneself through apparent moral success. It does not say everything about the ruler’s later life or authorize certainty about an otherwise unreported future response.

Luke 18:24

24Jesus, seeing that he became very sad, said, “How hard it is for those who have riches to enter into God’s Kingdom!

Arminian

Jesus names the difficulty of entering the Kingdom while held by riches. Arminian readers see a warning against treating prosperity as proof of favor. Wealth can nourish self-reliance and competing loyalty, so the call is to dependence on God rather than confidence in resources.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see riches as a dangerous support for self-sufficiency. The problem is not that God’s grace lacks power over wealthy people, but that human advantage cannot secure entry. Both traditions reject identifying material success with saving faith.

Luke 18:25

25For it is easier for a camel to enter in through a needle’s eye than for a rich man to enter into God’s Kingdom.”

Arminian

The camel and needle image intensifies the impossibility rather than offering an easier technique. Arminian readers see that human resources cannot produce salvation. The saying prepares for God’s ability; it should not be softened with an unsupported story about a special gate.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear a deliberately impossible comparison that defeats confidence in self-rescue. The answer will be God’s power, not a clever adjustment to the image. Both traditions recognize that salvation cannot be reached by wealth, effort, or ingenuity alone.

Luke 18:26

26Those who heard it said, “Then who can be saved?”

Arminian

The hearers ask who can be saved when such apparent advantage proves insufficient. Arminian readers see the dialogue moving beyond one wealthy individual toward universal dependence on grace. Alarm becomes an opening to hear what only God can do.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the question exposing the inadequacy of every merely human basis for salvation. Jesus’ response does not exempt a more deserving class. It redirects hope from social advantage or moral confidence to God’s ability.

Luke 18:27

27But he said, “The things which are impossible with men are possible with God.”

Arminian

What human beings cannot accomplish lies within God’s power. Arminian readers see grace overcoming inability and enabling a genuine response to Christ. They commonly relate this to prevenient grace, which makes faith possible without making its acceptance irresistible; salvation remains entirely dependent on God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divine power doing what fallen human beings cannot do for themselves. They relate this to effectual grace, which renews the will and brings the called to faith. The verse affirms God’s ability without itself supplying every step of either tradition’s account of conversion.

Key contrast Both deny that people can save themselves. Arminian readers emphasize enabling grace that can be resisted; Reformed readers emphasize renewing grace that effectually brings God’s people to faith. The saying establishes divine possibility, not a complete technical account of conversion.

Luke 18:28

28Peter said, “Look, we have left everything and followed you.”

Arminian

Peter contrasts the disciples’ response with the ruler’s attachment: they have left their resources and followed Jesus. Arminian readers see a real costly response rather than self-earned salvation. The next promise answers sacrifice with generosity, not a wage negotiated by deserving servants.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize grace bearing fruit in actual renunciation and following. Peter’s statement does not establish that the disciples have become self-sufficient or flawlessly faithful. Their sacrifice is received within Christ’s gracious promise rather than made the ground of justification.

Luke 18:29

29He said to them, “Most certainly I tell you, there is no one who has left house, or wife, or brothers, or parents, or children, for God’s Kingdom’s sake,

Arminian

Jesus acknowledges losses suffered for God’s Kingdom, including painful disruption of household ties. Arminian readers see the seriousness of costly allegiance. The promise does not encourage careless abandonment of dependents or treat broken relationships as achievements to collect.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ taking the cost of discipleship seriously. Allegiance may entail losses that ordinary measures of success cannot explain. The qualification concerning the Kingdom matters: the verse is not an unrestricted blessing on irresponsibility toward family.

Luke 18:30

30who will not receive many times more in this time, and in the world to come, eternal life.”

Arminian

Jesus promises abundant provision now and eternal life in the coming age. Arminian readers see gracious reassurance for faithful disciples, not a financial formula that guarantees wealth. The gift of life remains God’s generosity rather than the purchase price of their losses.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see reward grounded in Christ’s promise rather than independent merit. Present blessings need not mean a literal replacement of every possession or relationship. Both traditions reject a prosperity guarantee that ignores the passage’s call to costly following.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
