Passage

Luke 18

Verses 1–17 · WEB

Jesus teaches persistent prayer, contrasts self-righteousness with a sinner’s plea for mercy, and welcomes children whom the disciples would turn away. These scenes join patient trust, humble justification, and dependent reception of the Kingdom. Both traditions reject boasting and uphold the necessity of grace; their differences should not obscure the passage’s shared challenge to pride and exclusion.

Luke 18:1

1He also spoke a parable to them that they must always pray and not give up,

Arminian

Luke states the purpose plainly: persistent prayer rather than giving up. Arminian readers see a grace-enabled response during prolonged waiting. The instruction does not promise that repetition controls God; it calls disciples to remain in trusting dependence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see persevering prayer as a means God gives for sustaining faith. Confidence in providence encourages petition instead of replacing it. Both traditions distinguish faithful persistence from a technique meant to force a reluctant God to comply.

Luke 18:2

2saying, “There was a judge in a certain city who didn’t fear God and didn’t respect man.

Arminian

The judge’s lack of reverence and concern makes him an intentionally negative figure. Arminian readers recognize that the later comparison works by contrast: God is not morally like this judge. The story encourages trust in divine justice, not suspicion of God’s character.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the judge as unrighteous rather than a direct portrait of God. His conduct sets up an argument from an unworthy human official to the faithful divine judge. Reading every feature as an attribute of God would reverse the parable’s force.

Luke 18:3

3A widow was in that city, and she often came to him, saying, ‘Defend me from my adversary!’

Arminian

The widow repeatedly seeks protection against an adversary. Arminian readers see persistent appeal from someone needing justice rather than special privilege. Her situation invites compassion for the vulnerable and models bringing need before the one who can act.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see helplessness joined to persistence. The widow’s lack of power sharpens the contrast with the judge’s indifference. Her appeal concerns justice, so the parable should not be converted into an assurance that every personal desire must be granted.

Luke 18:4

4He wouldn’t for a while; but afterward he said to himself, ‘Though I neither fear God nor respect man,

Arminian

The judge delays despite knowing his own lack of moral concern. Arminian readers see the frustration that makes persistence difficult. The story acknowledges delay without teaching that divine silence must mean the same indifference displayed by the judge.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish apparent delay in an answer to prayer from this judge’s corrupt motive. The narrative lets the difficulty of waiting remain real. Trust in God need not pretend that prolonged injustice is painless or easily understood.

Luke 18:5

5yet because this widow bothers me, I will defend her, or else she will wear me out by her continual coming.’ ”

Arminian

The judge acts to escape inconvenience rather than from righteousness. Arminian readers see why his eventual response cannot define God’s motive. If even such a person can be moved to act, disciples have greater reason to trust their just and attentive Father.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the contrast between selfish relief and divine justice. The widow does not make the judge good, and believers do not manufacture God’s goodness through repeated prayer. The parable’s encouragement depends on God being better than this official.

Luke 18:6

6The Lord said, “Listen to what the unrighteous judge says.

Arminian

Jesus directs attention to the judge’s own reasoning before drawing the contrast. Arminian readers see an invitation to consider the argument carefully rather than imitate manipulation. Persistence rests on who God is, not on discovering how to irritate him into action.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise follow Jesus’ explanation rather than assigning theological significance to every incidental detail. The unjust judge’s concession supports confidence in the righteous God. Both traditions should let the stated purpose of prayer without discouragement govern the reading.

Luke 18:7

7Won’t God avenge his chosen ones who are crying out to him day and night, and yet he exercises patience with them?

Arminian

God hears his chosen people crying to him, even amid difficult waiting. Arminian readers understand election in relation to God’s gracious people in Christ and see their prayers as genuine dependence. The focus is confidence in God’s justice, not a complete explanation of election.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s chosen people as the objects of his faithful care and sovereign purpose. Their persistent cries are not obstacles to that purpose but part of its expression. The assurance concerns God’s just response rather than exemption from every season of waiting.

Luke 18:8

8I tell you that he will avenge them quickly. Nevertheless, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on the earth?”

Arminian

The assurance of vindication ends with a searching question about continuing faith. Arminian readers see an urgent call not to abandon trust while waiting. The question warns against complacency; it is not a numerical prediction about exactly how many believers will remain.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a summons to persevering faith rather than permission to rely on a bare profession. God’s promised justice and the call to endure belong together. The question functions as a warning that awakens vigilance, not a denial of divine faithfulness.

Luke 18:9

9He also spoke this parable to certain people who were convinced of their own righteousness, and who despised all others:

Arminian

Luke identifies the target as confidence in personal righteousness joined to contempt for others. Arminian readers see pride corrupting the posture of prayer. The parable challenges every community that measures acceptance by superiority rather than reliance on mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see self-righteousness and contempt as connected errors. The target is not faithful obedience itself but trust in one’s standing over against others. Both traditions must hear the warning as self-examination rather than a way to feel superior to the Pharisee.

Luke 18:10

10“Two men went up into the temple to pray; one was a Pharisee, and the other was a tax collector.

Arminian

Both men enter the temple to pray, but their shared setting does not settle their standing before God. Arminian readers see the importance of the heart’s posture. Religious access and participation cannot replace humble dependence on grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish outward participation in worship from genuine reliance on God. The contrast is not between a religious and a nonreligious location but between two approaches within prayer. The story asks where each man places his confidence.

Luke 18:11

11The Pharisee stood and prayed by himself like this: ‘God, I thank you that I am not like the rest of men: extortionists, unrighteous, adulterers, or even like this tax collector.

Arminian

The Pharisee’s thanksgiving becomes a comparison that exalts himself over others. Arminian readers see how religious language can disguise self-trust. Avoiding particular sins does not justify contempt or turn another person’s guilt into a basis for one’s own acceptance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see apparent gratitude undermined by reliance on moral distinction. The problem is not that honesty or purity are bad, but that he presents himself through superiority. Both traditions recognize that calling a boast a prayer does not make it humble.

Luke 18:12

12I fast twice a week. I give tithes of all that I get.’

Arminian

The Pharisee lists fasting and tithing as evidence of his standing. Arminian readers see good practices distorted when used to establish entitlement. Spiritual disciplines should express dependence and generosity, not become an account of merit presented against a neighbor.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish obedience from trust in obedience as the ground of justification. Religious achievements cannot cure self-exaltation. The list warns that even disciplined practices may coexist with pride when confidence rests in performance rather than God’s mercy.

Luke 18:13

13But the tax collector, standing far away, wouldn’t even lift up his eyes to heaven, but beat his chest, saying, ‘God, be merciful to me, a sinner!’

Arminian

The tax collector acknowledges sin and asks for mercy instead of comparing himself favorably with anyone. Arminian readers see repentant trust awakened and enabled by grace. His appeal receives rather than earns pardon; humility is not a new work that purchases acceptance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a sinner abandoning claims of righteousness and depending entirely on God’s mercy. Such repentance and faith are gifts of grace, not self-generated qualifications. Both traditions affirm the posture without requiring that every penitent reproduce the same physical gestures.

Luke 18:14

14I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other; for everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, but he who humbles himself will be exalted.”

Arminian

Jesus declares the mercy-seeking sinner justified rather than the self-exalting worshiper. Arminian readers see God graciously accepting repentant faith, not rewarding moral superiority. That response is enabled by grace and has no merit of its own; justification remains God’s merciful verdict.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a divine verdict received through faith rather than earned by works. They understand the sinner’s humble trust as the fruit of renewing grace, not its independent cause. The traditions share the rejection of self-righteousness, even while explaining grace’s relation to faith differently.

Luke 18:15

15They were also bringing their babies to him, that he might touch them. But when the disciples saw it, they rebuked them.

Arminian

People bring babies for Jesus’ touch, but the disciples rebuke them. Arminian readers see vulnerable children and their caregivers wrongly treated as interruptions. The scene calls the community to welcome rather than obstruct access to Jesus; it does not narrate a baptism.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see disciples misjudging who may approach Jesus. The babies are recipients of care, not examples of social importance. Those debating baptism must distinguish this welcome from an explicit baptismal instruction, which the passage itself does not provide.

Luke 18:16

16Jesus summoned them, saying, “Allow the little children to come to me, and don’t hinder them, for God’s Kingdom belongs to such as these.

Arminian

Jesus summons the children and forbids hindering them. Arminian readers see the Kingdom overturning assumptions about status and achievement. Views on infant baptism vary among Arminian communities, so welcoming children must not be equated automatically with one baptismal conclusion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see children welcomed rather than dismissed as spiritually insignificant. Paedobaptist and Baptist readers relate this to their wider accounts of the covenant differently. The immediate teaching is Jesus’ welcome; its implications for baptism require arguments beyond this scene.

Luke 18:17

17Most certainly, I tell you, whoever doesn’t receive God’s Kingdom like a little child, he will in no way enter into it.”

Arminian

The Kingdom must be received like a child rather than claimed through standing or achievement. Arminian readers see dependent trust enabled by grace, not an assertion of unaided choice. Childlikeness concerns receptivity; it should not be romanticized as complete innocence or maturity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see receptive dependence excluding merit and self-sufficiency. The Kingdom comes as a gift rather than an accomplishment. The verse does not by itself resolve how infant faith or baptism should be understood, questions on which wider interpretive commitments matter.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
