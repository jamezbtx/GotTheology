Passage

Luke 1

Verses 26–38 · WEB

Gabriel’s announcement moves from Elizabeth’s pregnancy to Mary in Nazareth. The promised son will be the holy Son of God and the Davidic King whose reign never ends; his conception comes through the Holy Spirit’s power. Mary asks for understanding and then entrusts herself to the Lord’s word. Both traditions affirm the virgin birth and the priority of grace, while differing in how they explain grace’s relation to her willing response.

Luke 1:26

26Now in the sixth month, the angel Gabriel was sent from God to a city of Galilee named Nazareth,

Arminian

Gabriel is sent to Nazareth in the sixth month of Elizabeth’s pregnancy. Arminian readers see the two announcements deliberately connected by God’s initiative. The story moves from temple service to a Galilean town, showing that the coming salvation is not limited to one sacred location or social role.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God coordinating the lives of the forerunner and the Messiah before either birth. The repeated sending of Gabriel emphasizes one divine purpose across distinct households. Nazareth becomes the setting for a promise whose significance exceeds the apparent importance of the place.

Luke 1:27

27to a virgin pledged to be married to a man whose name was Joseph, of David’s house. The virgin’s name was Mary.

Arminian

Mary is introduced as a virgin pledged to Joseph, who belongs to David’s house. Arminian readers share the confession that the coming conception is not an ordinary result of marriage. The family setting connects the announcement with promised kingship while preserving the distinctive divine action described next.

Reformed

Reformed readers see both the reality of Mary’s human circumstances and the Davidic connection through Joseph. Her virginity prepares the question about conception and the answer concerning the Spirit. These are shared Christological convictions, not a boundary between Arminian and Reformed readings.

Luke 1:28

28Having come in, the angel said to her, “Rejoice, you highly favored one! The Lord is with you. Blessed are you among women!”

Arminian

The greeting announces divine favor and the Lord’s presence before Mary gives her response. Arminian readers see grace preceding willing obedience. Calling her blessed honors what God is doing for and through her, not a human achievement that forces the divine plan to reward her.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Mary addressed as a recipient of favor rather than the independent source of the blessing. God’s presence grounds the astonishing commission to come. Her honored place should be read within this narrative of grace, not turned into an unrelated dispute between Christian traditions.

Luke 1:29

29But when she saw him, she was greatly troubled at the saying, and considered what kind of salutation this might be.

Arminian

Mary is troubled by the greeting and considers its meaning. Arminian readers see attentive reflection rather than thoughtless acceptance or self-importance. Receiving grace does not exclude questions; the narrative will distinguish her request for understanding from a refusal to trust the God who addresses her.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the favored servant responding with serious wonder, not a claim that such honor was expected or deserved. Her inward consideration belongs to a genuine encounter with divine initiative. The reassurance that follows meets her perplexity rather than dismissing the human significance of the announcement.

Luke 1:30

30The angel said to her, “Don’t be afraid, Mary, for you have found favor with God.

Arminian

The angel reassures Mary that she has found favor with God. Arminian readers see the coming responsibility supported by grace rather than fear. Favor is announced before the difficult explanation of the conception, giving her reason to receive the message as divine kindness even when its consequences are demanding.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s gracious choice grounding the command not to fear. The promise depends on his favor rather than Mary’s ability to control what follows. Divine blessing can include a costly calling, so favor must not be reduced to a guarantee of social comfort or an easy life.

Luke 1:31

31Behold, you will conceive in your womb and give birth to a son, and shall name him ‘Jesus.’

Arminian

Mary is told she will bear a son and name him Jesus. Arminian readers see salvation entering genuine human life through a pregnancy and birth, not a merely apparent humanity. The child’s name is given before the event, emphasizing that the message originates with God rather than parental ambition.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the promised Son taking real human existence while his identity and naming are established by divine announcement. The account joins concrete motherhood with a mission beyond ordinary family expectation. God’s saving purpose works through history rather than bypassing the reality of birth.

Luke 1:32

32He will be great and will be called the Son of the Most High. The Lord God will give him the throne of his father David,

Arminian

The child will be Son of the Most High and receive David’s throne from God. Arminian readers see the promise identifying the royal Savior whose authority is given, not seized. The greatness announced here belongs to Jesus himself and is shared ground for both traditions’ understanding of the Messiah.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to the Davidic promise centered in the coming Son. His kingship is not a temporary success generated by political force. Divine sonship and royal inheritance together prepare for a kingdom whose duration exceeds every earthly dynasty.

Luke 1:33

33and he will reign over the house of Jacob forever. There will be no end to his Kingdom.”

Arminian

Jesus’ reign over Jacob’s house will have no end. Arminian readers see enduring hope grounded in the King’s identity rather than the strength of his followers. The verse promises the permanence of his kingdom; it does not by itself explain every question about how individuals participate in it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the unending kingdom as the accomplishment of God’s faithful purpose. No later opposition can make the Messiah’s reign merely provisional. The promise anchors confidence in Christ rather than in changing circumstances, while its precise implications unfold through the rest of Luke’s narrative.

Luke 1:34

34Mary said to the angel, “How can this be, seeing I am a virgin?”

Arminian

Mary asks how the conception can occur while she is a virgin. Arminian readers see a request for understanding within the encounter, not an announcement that she will first make the promise possible by ordinary means. The answer places the decisive action with God’s Spirit and power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the question making the miraculous character of the conception explicit. Unlike Zacharias, Mary is not rebuked for demanding certainty. The narrative answers her concern by revealing divine agency, preserving both her real humanity and the extraordinary identity of the child.

Luke 1:35

35The angel answered her, “The Holy Spirit will come on you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you. Therefore also the holy one who is born from you will be called the Son of God.

Arminian

The Spirit’s coming and the Most High’s power explain the holy child’s conception. Arminian readers affirm the virgin birth and Jesus’ identity as Son of God. The language describes divine creative action, not a physical union between God and Mary or an achievement of human spiritual power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the incarnation initiated by divine power while the Son truly takes humanity from Mary. The child’s holiness and unique sonship are central to the explanation. Both traditions share these convictions; the scene is not a reason to manufacture a dispute about Christ’s deity or real humanity.

Luke 1:36

36Behold, Elizabeth your relative also has conceived a son in her old age; and this is the sixth month with her who was called barren.

Arminian

Elizabeth’s pregnancy is offered as confirmation of God’s power and a link between the two households. Arminian readers see reassurance graciously supplied rather than earned by superior faith. The reference also gives Mary a concrete person with whom she can share the wonder of what has been announced.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God supporting trust through an already unfolding act of mercy. Elizabeth’s age and former barrenness emphasize the limits of ordinary expectation without making natural weakness the final measure of divine possibility. The two births remain distinct while belonging to one saving purpose.

Luke 1:37

37For nothing spoken by God is impossible.”

Arminian

The assurance concerns the power and reliability of what God speaks. Arminian readers see reason to trust a promise beyond ordinary human capacity. The WEB also gives an alternative rendering that emphasizes the possibility of everything God speaks; neither wording authorizes treating every personal wish as a divine promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s word as effective rather than limited by the conditions that constrain creatures. The promise can be trusted because of the speaker’s power and faithfulness. This confidence answers Mary’s question about the announced birth, not a claim that contradictory human desires must all be granted.

Luke 1:38

38Mary said, “Behold, the servant of the Lord; let it be done to me according to your word.” Then the angel departed from her.

Arminian

Mary receives the promise as the Lord’s servant and willingly entrusts herself to his word. Arminian readers see a genuine consent made possible by prior grace, not an independent contribution that earns the incarnation. They understand such grace as enabling rather than making a willing response irresistible.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s gracious initiative bearing fruit in Mary’s willing trust. Her response is real and personal, yet dependent on divine favor and power rather than an autonomous foundation for the plan. They read her obedience within effective grace without claiming that this verse alone defines every aspect of conversion.

Key contrast Both see Mary’s response as willing and dependent on grace already given. Arminian readers understand grace as enabling a consent that is not coerced or made irresistible. Reformed readers understand willing trust as the fruit of effective divine grace. The narrative does not itself provide a complete account of the order of salvation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
