Passage

Luke 1

Verses 1–25 · WEB

Luke presents an ordered account grounded in eyewitness testimony so that Theophilus may know the certainty of his instruction. The narrative then opens with Zacharias and Elizabeth, whose righteous lives have not removed the sorrow of childlessness. An angel promises John’s birth and preparatory ministry; Zacharias’s unbelief brings temporary silence, while Elizabeth receives the promised mercy. Both traditions see divine initiative, real human weakness, and God’s faithfulness within the worshiping life of Israel.

Luke 1:1

1Since many have undertaken to set in order a narrative concerning those matters which have been fulfilled among us,

Arminian

Luke situates his narrative among earlier efforts to tell what has been fulfilled. Arminian readers see faith attending to God’s action in shared history, not simply inward experience. The opening invites careful hearing of events whose meaning rests in divine promise and fulfillment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see fulfillment giving unity to the events Luke will recount. God’s saving work can be investigated and narrated rather than treated as private speculation. The acknowledgment of earlier accounts places Luke’s writing within an existing witness to what has happened.

Luke 1:2

2even as those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and servants of the word delivered them to us,

Arminian

The account depends on testimony from eyewitnesses who also served the word. Arminian readers see responsible reception of a message entrusted through human witnesses. Faith is not opposed to hearing testimony, and serving the word means transmitting it rather than inventing a more attractive religious story.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God preserving knowledge of his work through eyewitness testimony and faithful ministry. The witnesses both saw and served, joining historical contact with responsibility to the message. Luke’s reliance on them does not diminish divine initiative but shows how the news reaches later hearers.

Luke 1:3

3it seemed good to me also, having traced the course of all things accurately from the first, to write to you in order, most excellent Theophilus;

Arminian

Luke describes careful investigation and an ordered account for Theophilus. Arminian readers see intellectual care serving confidence in the gospel. The stated purpose does not require inventing details about Theophilus’s office or identity; what matters is a reader receiving a reliable presentation of the events.

Reformed

Reformed readers see disciplined human inquiry employed in the service of divine truth. Luke’s attention to accuracy and order is not a rival to confidence in God’s word. The personal address also shows instruction directed toward a real recipient rather than a detached catalogue of religious ideas.

Luke 1:4

4that you might know the certainty concerning the things in which you were instructed.

Arminian

The purpose is certainty about instruction already received. Arminian readers see faith strengthened through understanding rather than protected from examination. Luke seeks a grounded response to the message; learning more about God’s acts can nourish trust without making intellectual mastery the source of salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see assurance supported by the trustworthy account of what God has done. Prior instruction is confirmed and clarified through the narrative. Certainty rests on the truth of the message, not on the reader’s ability to generate confidence without evidence or divine provision.

Luke 1:5

5There was in the days of Herod, the king of Judea, a certain priest named Zacharias, of the priestly division of Abijah. He had a wife of the daughters of Aaron, and her name was Elizabeth.

Arminian

The story begins with named people, priestly responsibilities, and the reign of Herod. Arminian readers see God’s work entering ordinary family and religious life. Zacharias and Elizabeth are not abstractions: their particular setting will become the place where a promise answers a long-standing personal sorrow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the coming events situated within Israel’s worship and a definite political setting. The priestly identities of both spouses connect the announcement with the people awaiting God’s faithfulness. Divine initiative meets them within an existing life of service rather than outside history.

Luke 1:6

6They were both righteous before God, walking blamelessly in all the commandments and ordinances of the Lord.

Arminian

Zacharias and Elizabeth are described as righteous and blameless in their walk. Arminian readers see genuine obedience shaped by faith, not an assertion that they never sinned. Their character shows that the barrenness described next must not be read as evidence of divine rejection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see integrity as the fruit of grace rather than a claim to merit salvation. Blameless conduct describes a faithful manner of life before God. Luke’s commendation matters because their coming affliction cannot be explained by assuming that visible hardship always reveals hidden wickedness.

Luke 1:7

7But they had no child, because Elizabeth was barren, and they both were well advanced in years.

Arminian

Their childlessness continues despite their faithful life, and age makes ordinary hope remote. Arminian readers see suffering that cannot be equated with personal guilt. The verse makes the later birth a mercy rather than a reward that righteous couples can claim as an automatic entitlement.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the limits of human resources preparing the setting for God’s promise. The couple’s righteousness has not removed sorrow from their lives. Their condition neither contradicts God’s care nor licenses judging childless people as less faithful than those who have children.

Luke 1:8

8Now while he executed the priest’s office before God in the order of his division

Arminian

Zacharias serves according to his division’s appointed turn. Arminian readers see patient faithfulness within an ordinary vocation before any extraordinary encounter occurs. Divine initiative does not make established responsibilities irrelevant; the announcement meets him while he is carrying out the service already entrusted to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the regular order of worship forming the setting for an unexpected act of revelation. God’s providence works within ordinary duties as well as remarkable events. Zacharias is not seeking a private sign but attending to the service belonging to his office.

Luke 1:9

9according to the custom of the priest’s office, his lot was to enter into the temple of the Lord and burn incense.

Arminian

The lot assigns Zacharias the task of burning incense in the temple. Arminian readers see an ordinary means of arranging priestly service becoming the occasion of divine encounter. The narrative does not turn the use of lots into a universal method for Christians to secure special revelations.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providence bringing a particular servant into the temple at the appointed moment. The customary process is real, yet the coming announcement is not produced by it. God takes the initiative within an established act of worship rather than responding to a technique that compels him.

Luke 1:10

10The whole multitude of the people were praying outside at the hour of incense.

Arminian

The people pray while Zacharias serves inside. Arminian readers see personal vocation surrounded by corporate dependence on God. The announcement will emerge within worship shared by priest and congregation, reminding readers that private blessing and the good of the wider people can be joined.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the public worship of Israel surrounding the hidden encounter in the sanctuary. Prayer is not displaced by God’s purpose; it belongs to the setting in which that purpose is disclosed. Those outside will soon witness effects they cannot yet explain.

Luke 1:11

11An angel of the Lord appeared to him, standing on the right side of the altar of incense.

Arminian

The angel appears beside the altar while Zacharias ministers. Arminian readers see God initiating communication rather than a priest generating revelation through ritual performance. The messenger’s location belongs to the concrete scene, but the authority of the promise comes from the God who sends him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an unsolicited divine announcement interrupting ordinary service. The angel is a messenger, not the source of the blessing to be promised. The temple setting connects the event with Israel’s worship while directing attention to God’s freedom to speak and act.

Luke 1:12

12Zacharias was troubled when he saw him, and fear fell upon him.

Arminian

The unexpected appearance troubles Zacharias and fills him with fear. Arminian readers see an honest human response that is not hidden by his righteous reputation. Faithful service does not mean automatic composure before revelation, and the messenger’s next words address his fear with reassurance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the distance between ordinary religious familiarity and an actual encounter with heavenly revelation. Zacharias’s alarm does not erase the earlier commendation of his life. The account prepares for gracious reassurance rather than presenting spiritual maturity as an absence of all trembling.

Luke 1:13

13But the angel said to him, “Don’t be afraid, Zacharias, because your request has been heard. Your wife, Elizabeth, will bear you a son, and you shall call his name John.

Arminian

The angel answers fear with news that prayer has been heard and a son will be born. Arminian readers see divine generosity meeting human petition without making prayer a payment. God also names the child, linking this personal mercy with a vocation beyond the parents’ immediate hopes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see promise, answer to prayer, and the child’s appointed identity joined by God’s initiative. Zacharias does not first design John’s role or establish the conditions of the gift. The announcement provides both consolation and direction for receiving what God will do.

Luke 1:14

14You will have joy and gladness, and many will rejoice at his birth.

Arminian

The promised birth will bring joy beyond the parents themselves. Arminian readers see God’s blessing widening into communal gladness. A child long desired is also a servant whose ministry will benefit others, so the promise is not confined to resolving one family’s private disappointment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the joy of salvation beginning to spread through the announcement of its forerunner. God’s mercy to this household serves a larger purpose among his people. The verse joins personal affection with public hope rather than asking the parents to suppress either.

Luke 1:15

15For he will be great in the sight of the Lord, and he will drink no wine nor strong drink. He will be filled with the Holy Spirit, even from his mother’s womb.

Arminian

John’s greatness is measured before the Lord, and his calling is marked by abstinence and the Spirit’s presence from the womb. Arminian readers see grace preceding achievement. This exceptional vocation displays divine initiative without supplying a complete rule about every child’s spiritual experience or every believer’s use of wine.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God setting John apart and equipping him before conscious accomplishment. The Spirit’s early work makes his mission a gift rather than a product of self-development. The particular directions belong to John’s calling and should not be expanded into claims the verse does not make about all people.

Luke 1:16

16He will turn many of the children of Israel to the Lord their God.

Arminian

John will turn many Israelites toward the Lord their God. Arminian readers see repentance sought through a Spirit-enabled ministry that calls for real response. The messenger serves reconciliation; he does not become the object of devotion or a replacement for the God to whom people must return.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God effectively using a human preacher to bring people back to himself. John’s role is instrumental and preparatory, not an independent saving power. The promised fruit assures the purpose of his ministry while leaving the narrative’s focus on the Lord who sends him.

Luke 1:17

17He will go before him in the spirit and power of Elijah, ‘to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children,’ and the disobedient to the wisdom of the just; to prepare a people prepared for the Lord.”

Arminian

John’s Elijah-like ministry will restore relationships and prepare a people for the Lord. Arminian readers see repentance involving practical reconciliation and teachable obedience. The WEB links the family language to Malachi 4:6, grounding the promised preparation in God’s earlier word rather than a novel program of self-improvement.

Reformed

Reformed readers see promised prophetic power renewing a people in readiness for the Lord. Preparation includes changed hearts and conduct, not only increased information. John’s resemblance to Elijah concerns the character and power of his ministry, not a claim that he is simply Elijah returned in another body.

Luke 1:18

18Zacharias said to the angel, “How can I be sure of this? For I am an old man, and my wife is well advanced in years.”

Arminian

Zacharias asks for certainty while emphasizing the couple’s advanced age. Arminian readers see how real obstacles can become grounds for distrusting a promise. The later rebuke identifies unbelief here; his faithful past does not remove the need to trust the word now addressed to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a righteous man’s weakness exposed by a promise beyond ordinary expectation. His objection measures the future by visible capacity rather than the sender’s authority. The account does not pretend that grace has made him incapable of doubt, yet doubt will not cancel God’s announced purpose.

Luke 1:19

19The angel answered him, “I am Gabriel, who stands in the presence of God. I was sent to speak to you and to bring you this good news.

Arminian

Gabriel identifies himself as a servant sent from God’s presence with good news. Arminian readers see the message’s trustworthiness resting in its divine source. The reassurance asks Zacharias to move beyond calculation of natural possibilities toward confidence in the one who has commissioned the announcement.

Reformed

Reformed readers see heavenly authority answering the demand for certainty. Gabriel’s importance lies in the God before whom he stands and from whom he is sent. The promised birth is good news, not an achievement Zacharias must generate by finding stronger resources within himself.

Luke 1:20

20Behold, you will be silent and not able to speak until the day that these things will happen, because you didn’t believe my words, which will be fulfilled in their proper time.”

Arminian

Silence will mark Zacharias’s unbelief until the promise is fulfilled. Arminian readers see real accountability joined to continuing mercy: distrust has consequences, but God does not withdraw the promised child. The sign calls the father to humility without making his lapse the final definition of his relationship with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see corrective discipline within a purpose God will bring to pass in its proper time. Zacharias’s failure does not make the divine word uncertain. The temporary loss of speech both exposes unbelief and becomes a visible sign that the announcement is more than ordinary speculation.

Luke 1:21

21The people were waiting for Zacharias, and they marveled that he delayed in the temple.

Arminian

The waiting congregation notices the unusual delay. Arminian readers see the public effects of an encounter they have not witnessed directly. Their wondering belongs to a story in which God’s activity gradually becomes known, rather than every observer receiving an immediate explanation of what has happened.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the hidden announcement beginning to affect the worshiping community. The delay prompts questions before understanding arrives. God’s work is not confined to Zacharias’s inward experience; it will become recognizable through signs, birth, testimony, and the unfolding fulfillment of the promise.

Luke 1:22

22When he came out, he could not speak to them. They perceived that he had seen a vision in the temple. He continued making signs to them, and remained mute.

Arminian

Zacharias emerges unable to speak, and the people infer that he has seen a vision. Arminian readers see the announced sign becoming public even while his explanation remains limited. His gestures communicate only partially, emphasizing that the full meaning must await the promise’s fulfillment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the messenger’s word confirmed in a visible consequence. The congregation recognizes something extraordinary but does not yet know the complete announcement. The narrative keeps revelation and human understanding distinct: witnessing a sign is real, though it is not the same as grasping everything it means.

Luke 1:23

23When the days of his service were fulfilled, he departed to his house.

Arminian

Zacharias completes his assigned service before returning home. Arminian readers see ordinary duty continuing after an extraordinary event. His coming fatherhood does not erase present responsibility, and the promise will unfold within the patient rhythms of household life rather than through a dramatic public campaign.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priest returning to familiar life under an unfulfilled but certain promise. God’s word does not require him to invent a new vocation while waiting. Completion of his service ties the unusual encounter to the continuing faithfulness of everyday responsibilities.

Luke 1:24

24After these days Elizabeth his wife conceived, and she hid herself five months, saying,

Arminian

Elizabeth conceives as promised and spends five months in seclusion. Arminian readers see the gift becoming concrete in her life. Luke does not specify every reason for her withdrawal, so her response should not be filled out with imagined motives; the next verse gives her own explanation of the mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the announced birth beginning to unfold through God’s faithful action. The conception answers the condition described earlier, where age and childlessness seemed decisive. Elizabeth’s privacy does not make the event less significant for the larger purpose soon joined to Mary’s story.

Luke 1:25

25“Thus has the Lord done to me in the days in which he looked at me, to take away my reproach among men.”

Arminian

Elizabeth recognizes the Lord’s kindness in removing social reproach. Arminian readers see a person long burdened by others’ judgment receiving mercy and renewed dignity. Her gratitude does not validate the assumption that childlessness is disgrace before God; Luke has already described her as righteous.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Elizabeth giving God the credit for a gift she could not create. His attentive mercy answers a painful social condition without endorsing the reproach itself. The blessing is personal and tender, while the child’s announced role reaches beyond the family’s relief.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
