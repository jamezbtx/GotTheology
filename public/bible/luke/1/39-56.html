Passage

Luke 1

Verses 39–56 · WEB

Mary’s visit to Elizabeth brings Spirit-enabled recognition of the Lord she carries and a blessing on her believing response. Mary’s song magnifies God for mercy to a lowly servant, the reversal of human pride and privilege, and faithfulness to Abraham’s offspring. Both traditions share the praise of divine grace and its concern for the lowly, without treating disputes about later Marian doctrines as the passage’s Arminian and Reformed contrast.

Luke 1:39

39Mary arose in those days and went into the hill country with haste, into a city of Judah,

Arminian

Mary travels promptly to the hill country after hearing of Elizabeth’s pregnancy. Arminian readers see trust moving into concrete action and fellowship. The journey links the two promises without requiring the assumption that every detail of her motivation is known; Luke records the visit and the recognition it brings.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providence drawing together the households already linked by Gabriel’s announcements. Mary’s action is purposeful and personal, not erased by divine initiative. The visit will disclose how the two children’s callings relate and turn private experiences into shared praise.

Luke 1:40

40and entered into the house of Zacharias and greeted Elizabeth.

Arminian

Mary enters the household and greets Elizabeth. Arminian readers see ordinary hospitality becoming the setting for extraordinary recognition. Grace is encountered within family relationships and spoken welcome, rather than requiring a public ceremony before the meaning of God’s promise can begin to be shared.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the simple greeting leading into Spirit-given testimony. The household encounter does not create the promise but becomes a means of confirming it. Luke connects divine action with human fellowship rather than treating the two as competing explanations of what happens next.

Luke 1:41

41When Elizabeth heard Mary’s greeting, the baby leaped in her womb; and Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit.

Arminian

The baby’s movement accompanies Elizabeth being filled with the Holy Spirit. Arminian readers see God acting before either child can claim an achievement. This distinctive sign connects the forerunner with the coming Lord, while its particular role should not be expanded into an exhaustive account of every prenatal spiritual experience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit initiating recognition of the Messiah’s presence. The joy is a gift rather than the result of religious accomplishment. The scene confirms the earlier promise concerning John and equips Elizabeth to speak, while keeping the spotlight on the significance of Mary’s child.

Luke 1:42

42She called out with a loud voice and said, “Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb!

Arminian

Elizabeth blesses Mary and the child she carries. Arminian readers see Spirit-enabled joy honoring the recipient of grace and the promised Savior together. Mary’s blessedness is real, yet the scene points beyond social admiration to the action of God that has made this meeting significant.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divine favor publicly recognized through Elizabeth’s testimony. The blessing does not transfer the source of salvation from God to Mary. It celebrates her honored role and the child whose identity will explain why her visit prompts such joy.

Luke 1:43

43Why am I so favored, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?

Arminian

Elizabeth calls Mary the mother of her Lord and marvels at the favor of the visit. Arminian readers see Christ’s identity recognized before his birth. The honor shown to Mary is inseparable from who her child is, making the verse primarily a confession about the Lord coming among his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a striking recognition of the unborn Jesus as Lord. Elizabeth’s humble question emphasizes favor received, not entitlement. Both traditions can honor Mary’s place in the story without turning disputes from other theological settings into a supposed Arminian and Reformed disagreement.

Luke 1:44

44For behold, when the voice of your greeting came into my ears, the baby leaped in my womb for joy!

Arminian

Elizabeth interprets the baby’s movement as joy at Mary’s greeting. Arminian readers see a sign confirming that the two promised births belong together. The narrative invites wonder at God’s action, not a method for interpreting every movement in pregnancy as a distinct prophetic message.

Reformed

Reformed readers see John’s preparatory role anticipated in a Spirit-framed response to the coming Christ. The sign directs attention to the Lord rather than to the child’s independent spiritual capacity. Joy marks the nearness of fulfillment before public ministry has begun.

Luke 1:45

45Blessed is she who believed, for there will be a fulfillment of the things which have been spoken to her from the Lord!”

Arminian

Elizabeth commends Mary’s belief in the Lord’s announced fulfillment. Arminian readers see trust as a meaningful response to prior grace, not a human power that makes the promise true. Blessedness includes receiving God’s word with confidence while its full realization still lies ahead.

Reformed

Reformed readers see believing reception as a fitting fruit of the favor God has shown. The certainty of fulfillment rests on the Lord’s speaking, not on the strength of Mary’s inward resolve. Her faith contrasts with distrust while keeping the credit for what is coming with God.

Luke 1:46

46Mary said, “My soul magnifies the Lord.

Arminian

Mary begins her song by magnifying the Lord. Arminian readers see the proper direction of gratitude: the person called blessed gives honor to God. Her response turns from astonishment at her own circumstances toward praise of the one whose mercy reaches beyond her household.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divine greatness, not Mary’s self-exaltation, at the center of the song. To magnify the Lord is to acknowledge who he is and what he has done. The opening establishes the God-centered character of all that follows.

Luke 1:47

47My spirit has rejoiced in God my Savior,

Arminian

Mary rejoices in God as her Savior. Arminian readers see personal dependence and glad trust within her unique calling. The song does not present her as the source of salvation; she shares in the mercy she celebrates and locates her joy in the God who acts for his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Mary confessing need and gratitude before the God who saves. Her honored role does not place her outside dependence on grace. The verse is shared ground for praising divine mercy rather than a reason to import a separate debate about later Marian definitions.

Luke 1:48

48for he has looked at the humble state of his servant. For behold, from now on, all generations will call me blessed.

Arminian

God’s attention to his lowly servant explains why future generations will call Mary blessed. Arminian readers see dignity given by grace rather than secured through social prominence. Honoring her means recognizing God’s kindness and her place in his work, not relocating the ultimate object of worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the contrast between Mary’s humble condition and the enduring honor God bestows. The blessing originates in his regard, not in a claim that she established the terms of the gift. Her remembrance among believers should continue the praise of God that frames the song.

Luke 1:49

49For he who is mighty has done great things for me. Holy is his name.

Arminian

Mary attributes the great things done for her to the Mighty One and praises his holiness. Arminian readers see power joined with goodness rather than arbitrary force. The verse explains her blessedness through God’s action and character, leaving no place for pride in a self-produced spiritual accomplishment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see might and holiness held together in the God who fulfills the promise. His greatness is displayed in a gracious act toward a lowly servant. The praise directs attention from the recipient to the giver without diminishing the reality of the gift she receives.

Luke 1:50

50His mercy is for generations and generations on those who fear him.

Arminian

Mercy extends across generations to those who fear God. Arminian readers see a living posture of reverent trust rather than a lineage that guarantees favor without response. The song links enduring divine compassion with genuine worship, not with a payment that obliges God to be merciful.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithful mercy gathering worshipers through successive generations. Reverent fear marks those who receive and live within that mercy. The verse celebrates continuity without treating ancestry as sufficient in itself or making human reverence an independent source of saving grace.

Luke 1:51

51He has shown strength with his arm. He has scattered the proud in the imagination of their hearts.

Arminian

God’s strength overturns proud inward calculations. Arminian readers see a warning that status and self-confidence cannot secure the future against him. The song’s reversal reaches the heart as well as outward circumstance, calling hearers toward humility and trust rather than confidence in their own advantage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the strong arm of God exposing the weakness of human pride. No inward scheme can make his saving purpose depend on human permission or prestige. The image celebrates divine power on behalf of the lowly, while judgment falls on self-exaltation rather than on intelligence or planning as such.

Luke 1:52

52He has put down princes from their thrones, and has exalted the lowly.

Arminian

The song contrasts dethroned rulers with the exaltation of the lowly. Arminian readers see God challenging arrangements that treat power as proof of worth. The reversal has a real social dimension and should not be reduced to inward feelings, though the verse is not a timetable for every political event.

Reformed

Reformed readers see earthly authority unable to secure itself against God’s judgment. His mercy honors those without worldly standing, revealing that saving significance does not follow existing rank. The song proclaims God’s rule without giving every human movement a warrant to identify itself uncritically with his verdict.

Luke 1:53

53He has filled the hungry with good things. He has sent the rich away empty.

Arminian

The hungry receive good things while the rich are sent away empty. Arminian readers see God’s attention to material vulnerability and his challenge to complacent abundance. Spiritual application must not erase bodily need, and the reversal does not make wealth or poverty an automatic measure of every person’s final standing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divine generosity exposing the insufficiency of accumulated resources. Those who seem secure cannot purchase the mercy God gives. The verse speaks of genuine reversal for the needy and should retain its concern for hunger rather than becoming only a metaphor for religious experience.

Luke 1:54

54He has given help to Israel, his servant, that he might remember mercy,

Arminian

God helps Israel in remembrance of mercy. Arminian readers see the coming salvation rooted in faithfulness to a people and their history. Remembering does not imply that God had forgotten and needed prompting; it names his action in keeping promises that earlier generations had trusted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see covenant faithfulness giving shape to the surprising reversals of the song. The help comes from God’s remembered mercy rather than Israel’s self-sufficiency. Mary’s personal blessing is thus located within the larger story of divine commitment to his servant people.

Luke 1:55

55as he spoke to our fathers, to Abraham and his offspring forever.”

Arminian

The song traces mercy to the promise given to Abraham and his offspring. Arminian readers see God’s faithfulness linking the present gift to earlier generations. The future rests on his word, while the scope of that promise will unfold as Luke describes salvation reaching beyond the first household.

Reformed

Reformed readers see enduring covenant purpose beneath the new events surrounding Mary. The promise is neither an improvised response nor a private privilege disconnected from Israel. The closing line gathers the song into God’s longstanding commitment and invites confidence in his reliability across time.

Luke 1:56

56Mary stayed with her about three months, and then returned to her house.

Arminian

Mary stays with Elizabeth for about three months and then returns home. Arminian readers see sustained companionship following the extraordinary meeting and praise. The verse gives a simple duration, not a full diary, so neither the exact tasks she performed nor every event she witnessed should be assumed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divine promise lived within ordinary time and household fellowship. The extended visit allows the story’s wonder to remain connected to real relationships. Returning home does not end God’s purpose; the coming births will carry the connected announcements forward.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
