Passage

Luke 5

Verses 12–26 · WEB

Jesus cleanses a man full of leprosy with a touch and a word, then sends him to the priest as the law commands. As crowds grow, He withdraws to pray. Later, before Pharisees and teachers from across the land, friends lower a paralyzed man through the roof. Seeing their faith, Jesus forgives his sins and heals him to prove that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive. Both traditions see here Jesus’ divine authority and compassion.

Luke 5:12

12While he was in one of the cities, behold, there was a man full of leprosy. When he saw Jesus, he fell on his face and begged him, saying, “Lord, if you want to, you can make me clean.”

Arminian

A man full of leprosy says, “Lord, if you want to, you can make me clean.” Arminian readers see humble faith that trusts Jesus’ power and appeals to His willingness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a plea that rests entirely on Jesus’ will. The man does not doubt Jesus’ power, only whether he will be chosen for mercy.

Luke 5:13

13He stretched out his hand and touched him, saying, “I want to. Be made clean.” Immediately the leprosy left him.

Arminian

Jesus touches him and says, “I want to.” Arminian readers see Jesus’ willingness as a revelation of God’s heart toward the suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ sovereign will and compassion together. His touch reverses uncleanness rather than contracting it.

Luke 5:14

14He commanded him to tell no one, “But go your way and show yourself to the priest, and offer for your cleansing according to what Moses commanded, for a testimony to them.”

Arminian

He tells him to show himself to the priest as Moses commanded. Arminian readers see Jesus honoring the law and restoring the man to community.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law’s witness to Jesus’ authority. The cleansing is to be a testimony to them.

Luke 5:15

15But the report concerning him spread much more, and great multitudes came together to hear and to be healed by him of their infirmities.

Arminian

The report spreads, and crowds come. Arminian readers see the spread of interest in Jesus. Jesus’ fame grows as people seek both teaching and healing, which Luke keeps together.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Jesus’ fame grows even when He seeks restraint. Luke shows that the crowds come for healing as well as for hearing, and Jesus meets both needs.

Luke 5:16

16But he withdrew himself into the desert and prayed.

Arminian

He withdraws to the desert to pray. Arminian readers see Jesus’ dependence on the Father as a model for disciples. Even with crowds pressing in, He makes room for communion with the Father.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Luke’s frequent emphasis on Jesus at prayer at key moments. His withdrawal is not escape but renewal for the mission.

Luke 5:17

17On one of those days, he was teaching; and there were Pharisees and teachers of the law sitting by who had come out of every village of Galilee, Judea, and Jerusalem. The power of the Lord was with him to heal them.

Arminian

Pharisees and teachers of the law sit by, and the Lord’s power is present to heal. Arminian readers see God’s power available in the moment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the presence of religious leaders setting up a test of Jesus’ authority.

Luke 5:18

18Behold, men brought a paralyzed man on a cot, and they sought to bring him in to lay before Jesus.

Arminian

Men bring a paralyzed man on a cot and try to lay him before Jesus. Arminian readers see love and determination bringing someone who cannot come alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see friends acting in faith for another. God often uses the care of others to bring people into Christ’s presence.

Luke 5:19

19Not finding a way to bring him in because of the multitude, they went up to the housetop and let him down through the tiles with his cot into the middle before Jesus.

Arminian

Blocked by the crowd, they lower him through the roof tiles. Arminian readers see persistent faith that overcomes obstacles rather than giving up.

Reformed

Reformed readers see bold faith that refuses to be stopped by the crowd. Luke’s detail of tiles fits a house type familiar to his readers.

Luke 5:20

20Seeing their faith, he said to him, “Man, your sins are forgiven you.”

Arminian

Seeing their faith, Jesus says, “Your sins are forgiven.” Arminian readers note that Jesus responds to the faith of the friends, and many see the man’s faith included too.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the friends’ faith honored while forgiveness itself is Jesus’ sovereign gift.

Luke 5:21

21The scribes and the Pharisees began to reason, saying, “Who is this who speaks blasphemies? Who can forgive sins, but God alone?”

Arminian

The leaders ask, “Who can forgive sins but God alone?” Arminian readers see the leaders rightly recognize forgiveness as God’s prerogative. Their question is right; their conclusion about Jesus is wrong.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the question pointing to Jesus’ divine identity. If only God forgives, Jesus’ words either blaspheme or reveal who He is.

Luke 5:22

22But Jesus, perceiving their thoughts, answered them, “Why are you reasoning so in your hearts?

Arminian

Jesus perceives their thoughts. Arminian readers see His insight into human hearts. Jesus addresses hidden objections directly and openly rather than ignoring them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divine knowledge displayed. Only God searches hearts, and Jesus does so here.

Luke 5:23

23Which is easier to say, ‘Your sins are forgiven you,’ or to say, ‘Arise and walk’?

Arminian

Which is easier to say? Arminian readers see Jesus inviting them to think about visible and invisible acts. Saying sins are forgiven cannot be checked; saying arise can be.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the logic: the visible healing will confirm the invisible forgiveness. Jesus chooses the harder test to vindicate the unseen word.

Luke 5:24

24But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins,” he said to the paralyzed man, “I tell you, arise, take up your cot, and go to your house.”

Arminian

The Son of Man has authority to forgive sins. Arminian readers see Jesus claiming divine authority. He links forgiveness and healing so the crowd can see His authority at work.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Daniel 7 behind Son of Man, a figure given authority and dominion. The phrase on earth stresses that God’s authority to forgive is now present in Jesus’ ministry.

Luke 5:25

25Immediately he rose up before them, and took up that which he was laying on, and departed to his house, glorifying God.

Arminian

He rises, takes his cot, and goes home glorifying God. Arminian readers see grateful response to grace. Healing leads straight to worship, which Luke often stresses.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a life restored and God glorified. His glorifying God shows that the gift points back to the giver.

Luke 5:26

26Amazement took hold on all, and they glorified God. They were filled with fear, saying, “We have seen strange things today.”

Arminian

Amazement and fear fill all. Arminian readers see awe at God’s work. Luke records amazement, praise, and fear together in response to Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wonder that does not yet amount to understanding. Strange things today hints that the crowd senses something new without yet grasping it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
