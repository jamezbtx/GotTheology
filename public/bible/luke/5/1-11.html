Passage

Luke 5

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Teaching by the Lake of Gennesaret, Jesus borrows Simon’s boat, then tells the tired fisherman to let down his nets again. The huge catch brings Simon to his knees: “Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, Lord.” Jesus answers, “Don’t be afraid,” and calls him to catch people. Simon, James, and John leave everything and follow. Arminian and Reformed readers share this picture of holiness, grace, and costly discipleship; they describe the call’s inner working differently.

Luke 5:1

1Now while the multitude pressed on him and heard the word of God, he was standing by the lake of Gennesaret.

Arminian

The crowd presses in to hear the word of God. Arminian readers see real hunger for God’s word among ordinary people, and Jesus meets it by teaching openly to all.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Luke calls Jesus’ teaching the word of God. The scene sets up a call that comes through that word.

Luke 5:2

2He saw two boats standing by the lake, but the fishermen had gone out of them and were washing their nets.

Arminian

The fishermen are washing their nets after a failed night. Arminian readers see Jesus meeting people in the middle of ordinary work and disappointment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Lord entering daily life unannounced. The empty nets prepare for a display of His power.

Luke 5:3

3He entered into one of the boats, which was Simon’s, and asked him to put out a little from the land. He sat down and taught the multitudes from the boat.

Arminian

Jesus asks Simon to push out and teaches from his boat. Arminian readers note that Jesus invites Simon’s cooperation before calling him, drawing him in step by step.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus taking the initiative with Simon. Simon hears the teaching before he sees the sign.

Luke 5:4

4When he had finished speaking, he said to Simon, “Put out into the deep and let down your nets for a catch.”

Arminian

“Put out into the deep and let down your nets.” Arminian readers see a command that calls for trust and obedience from an experienced fisherman.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ word carrying authority over Simon’s own trade and expertise.

Luke 5:5

5Simon answered him, “Master, we worked all night and caught nothing; but at your word I will let down the net.”

Arminian

“At your word I will let down the net.” Arminian readers see faith as obedient response to Jesus’ word despite doubt and fatigue.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience grounded in Jesus’ word rather than in Simon’s expectations. Faith rests on who is speaking.

Luke 5:6

6When they had done this, they caught a great multitude of fish, and their net was breaking.

Arminian

The catch is so great that their net begins to break. Arminian readers see abundant blessing following simple obedience, a sign that Jesus can do what human effort could not.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a sign of Jesus’ lordship over creation. The miracle is less about fish than about revealing to Simon who is in his boat.

Luke 5:7

7They beckoned to their partners in the other boat, that they should come and help them. They came and filled both boats, so that they began to sink.

Arminian

Partners come, and both boats fill until they begin to sink. Arminian readers note that the blessing overflows to others, a hint of the shared mission to come.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the overwhelming scale of the catch, beyond any natural explanation. God’s provision exceeds what the fishermen can even hold.

Luke 5:8

8But Simon Peter, when he saw it, fell down at Jesus’ knees, saying, “Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, Lord.”

Arminian

“Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, Lord.” Arminian readers see conviction awakened by encounter with Jesus’ holiness. Grace brings people to honest self-awareness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the response of a sinner before divine holiness, compare Isaiah 6:5. The title shifts from Master to Lord.

Luke 5:9

9For he was amazed, and all who were with him, at the catch of fish which they had caught;

Arminian

Simon and all with him are amazed at the catch. Arminian readers see awe as a doorway to deeper faith, as the disciples begin to sense who Jesus is.

Reformed

Reformed readers see amazement that recognizes power beyond human ability. Luke shows awe turning into recognition of Jesus’ holiness.

Luke 5:10

10and so also were James and John, sons of Zebedee, who were partners with Simon. Jesus said to Simon, “Don’t be afraid. From now on you will be catching people alive.”

Arminian

“Don’t be afraid. From now on you will be catching people alive.” Arminian readers see Jesus answering conviction with grace and a new vocation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ gracious call that turns fear into mission. The phrase catching alive suggests rescue, not capture.

Luke 5:11

11When they had brought their boats to land, they left everything, and followed him.

Arminian

They bring the boats to land, leave everything, and follow. Arminian readers see a wholehearted response to Jesus’ call, freely given at real cost.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the effect of Christ’s call: those He summons leave all to follow Him. The record catch is left behind, showing a new treasure has taken its place.

Key contrast Both see a real call and a real response. Arminian readers usually describe the response as a free choice enabled by grace. Reformed readers usually describe Christ’s call as effectual, bringing about the response it seeks.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
