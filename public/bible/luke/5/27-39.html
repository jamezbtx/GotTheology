Passage

Luke 5

Verses 27–39 · WEB

Jesus calls Levi the tax collector, who leaves everything, follows, and throws a feast for Jesus with tax collectors and others. When the Pharisees and their scribes complain, Jesus says He came to call sinners to repentance, like a physician for the sick. Asked about fasting, He speaks of the bridegroom’s presence and the coming days of fasting, and gives sayings on new cloth and new wine. Both traditions share the grace of this call; they describe how the call to repentance works in different terms.

Luke 5:27

27After these things he went out and saw a tax collector named Levi sitting at the tax office, and said to him, “Follow me!”

Arminian

Jesus says to Levi, “Follow me!” Arminian readers see grace extended to an outcast, inviting a real response. Tax collectors were often despised for collaborating with Rome and for dishonest gain.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ sovereign summons to a sinner who had no claim on Him. Jesus’ call comes before any sign of change in Levi.

Luke 5:28

28He left everything, and rose up and followed him.

Arminian

He leaves everything and follows. Arminian readers see a decisive personal response. Luke notes that he left everything, a costly choice for a man of means.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the call accomplishing its purpose. The obedience is immediate, like that of the fishermen in verse 11.

Luke 5:29

29Levi made a great feast for him in his house. There was a great crowd of tax collectors and others who were reclining with them.

Arminian

Levi hosts a great feast. Arminian readers see joy and witness, bringing friends to Jesus. Levi’s first act is to open his home to Jesus and his old circle.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace creating fellowship with sinners. The table becomes a place where grace meets sinners.

Luke 5:30

30Their scribes and the Pharisees murmured against his disciples, saying, “Why do you eat and drink with the tax collectors and sinners?”

Arminian

Why eat with tax collectors and sinners? Arminian readers see Jesus breaking social barriers. The complaint shows how table fellowship signaled acceptance in that culture.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-righteousness offended by grace. The grumbling recalls Israel’s murmuring in the wilderness.

Luke 5:31

31Jesus answered them, “Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick do.

Arminian

The sick need a physician. Arminian readers see Jesus’ mission to all who recognize their need. Only those who know they are sick seek the physician, so Jesus’ words also invite self-recognition.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sin as sickness that only Christ can heal. The Pharisees’ sense of health keeps them from the physician.

Luke 5:32

32I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance.”

Arminian

“I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance.” Arminian readers see the call extended to all sinners, who are summoned to turn to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see repentance as the goal of Christ’s call, granted by God to those He calls.

Key contrast Both see Jesus calling sinners to repentance. Arminian readers usually stress that the call goes to all and may be accepted or refused. Reformed readers usually stress that God grants repentance to those He effectually calls.

Luke 5:33

33They said to him, “Why do John’s disciples often fast and pray, likewise also the disciples of the Pharisees, but yours eat and drink?”

Arminian

Why don’t your disciples fast? Arminian readers see different expectations of piety. Fasting and prayer were valued practices, and Jesus does not dismiss them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus redefining religious practice around Himself. The question assumes piety is measured by practice, while Jesus measures it by relation to Him.

Luke 5:34

34He said to them, “Can you make the friends of the bridegroom fast while the bridegroom is with them?

Arminian

Friends of the bridegroom do not fast while he is present. Arminian readers see joy in Jesus’ presence. His presence is a season of celebration, like a wedding feast.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus presenting Himself as the bridegroom, an Old Testament image for God. Isaiah and Hosea picture God as Israel’s husband, and Jesus takes up that imagery.

Luke 5:35

35But the days will come when the bridegroom will be taken away from them. Then they will fast in those days.”

Arminian

The bridegroom will be taken away. Arminian readers see a hint of His coming death. Fasting will have a place after His departure, which shapes later Christian practice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an early prediction of the passion. Joy and sorrow both find their meaning in relation to Christ.

Luke 5:36

36He also told a parable to them. “No one puts a piece from a new garment on an old garment, or else he will tear the new, and also the piece from the new will not match the old.

Arminian

New cloth on an old garment. Arminian readers see that the kingdom cannot be patched onto old forms. Jesus warns against trying to fit His new work into old categories without change.

Reformed

Reformed readers see continuity and newness in Christ, but not a mere add-on. The new age in Christ fulfills the old rather than merely repairing it.

Luke 5:37

37No one puts new wine into old wineskins, or else the new wine will burst the skins, and it will be spilled and the skins will be destroyed.

Arminian

New wine bursts old skins. Arminian readers see the newness of the gospel demanding new structures. Rigid forms that cannot stretch will break under the gospel’s life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same point. New wine images the joy and power of the kingdom arriving in Jesus.

Luke 5:38

38But new wine must be put into fresh wineskins, and both are preserved.

Arminian

New wine needs fresh skins. Arminian readers see transformation required. New life requires new forms of devotion and community.

Reformed

Reformed readers see renewal in Christ. God both preserves and renews, giving fitting vessels for His work.

Luke 5:39

39No man having drunk old wine immediately desires new, for he says, ‘The old is better.’ ”

Arminian

No one who drinks old wine wants new. Arminian readers see resistance to change. Only Luke adds this saying, gently explaining why many prefer the familiar.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human preference for the familiar, explaining rejection. Rejection of Jesus often springs from comfort with the old rather than reasoned objection.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
