Passage

Luke 13

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Reports of violent death and accidental disaster prompt Jesus to reject the assumption that the victims were worse sinners than those who survived. He turns the hearers toward repentance and then describes a fruitless fig tree receiving further care before judgment. Warning and patient mercy belong together without making tragedy a simple measure of personal guilt.

Luke 13:1

1Now there were some present at the same time who told him about the Galileans whose blood Pilate had mixed with their sacrifices.

Arminian

A report of Pilate’s killing of Galileans raises the question of what suffering reveals about its victims. Arminian readers see Jesus redirecting attention from judging others toward repentance. The report does not justify treating political violence as evidence of unusual guilt before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise refuse to infer a special degree of guilt from the manner of someone’s death. God’s providence does not give observers permission to diagnose hidden sins behind a tragedy. Jesus’ answer challenges the security of those who assume survival proves their righteousness.

Luke 13:2

2Jesus answered them, “Do you think that these Galileans were worse sinners than all the other Galileans, because they suffered such things?

Arminian

Jesus rejects the inference that these victims suffered because they were worse sinners. Arminian readers stress that all need grace, including those who have escaped disaster. Compassion for sufferers must replace moral speculation about why they were targeted.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the universal reality of sin from a supposed scale of guilt revealed by calamity. The victims are not presented as morally inferior to the survivors. Both traditions should resist blaming people for suffering on the basis of this event.

Luke 13:3

3I tell you, no, but unless you repent, you will all perish in the same way.

Arminian

The call to repentance turns the warning toward every hearer. Arminian readers stress a genuine summons to turn through God’s enabling grace, not self-salvation by moral effort. Perishing warns of judgment without promising that repentant people will never die violently.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear an urgent command that must be answered, understanding repentance as a gift God works through his call. The warning is one means of awakening sinners. Both traditions distinguish rescue from judgment from immunity to every earthly tragedy.

Luke 13:4

4Or those eighteen on whom the tower in Siloam fell and killed them—do you think that they were worse offenders than all the men who dwell in Jerusalem?

Arminian

The tower’s collapse extends the question beyond deliberate human violence to sudden disaster. Arminian readers see that neither sort of death establishes the victims as uniquely guilty. The living must not turn their temporary safety into a verdict of innocence.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise reject a direct equation between catastrophic death and greater personal wickedness. The example exposes everyone’s vulnerability rather than explaining the hidden purpose of each accident. Both traditions should offer care, not presumptuous diagnoses, to those affected by disasters.

Luke 13:5

5I tell you, no, but, unless you repent, you will all perish in the same way.”

Arminian

Jesus repeats the universal summons rather than encouraging comparisons with the dead. Arminian readers stress the urgency of responding to offered grace while opportunity remains. Repentance is a turning toward God, not a technique for avoiding accidents.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear a serious warning directed to all, not merely an observation about those already killed. God’s mercy is needed by survivors no less than victims. Both traditions should connect the call to humility rather than the claim that suffering reliably identifies individual guilt.

Luke 13:6

6He spoke this parable. “A certain man had a fig tree planted in his vineyard, and he came seeking fruit on it and found none.

Arminian

The fruitless fig tree follows the call to repentance and pictures a life failing to answer its privileges. Arminian readers see God seeking a real response to his care. The parable can address the wider covenant community without requiring every detail to represent one named person.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect expected fruit with the seriousness of belonging to a community under God’s care. Religious location alone does not guarantee a faithful life. Both traditions should keep the call to repentance central rather than force a precise allegory onto every feature.

Luke 13:7

7He said to the vine dresser, ‘Behold, these three years I have come looking for fruit on this fig tree, and found none. Cut it down! Why does it waste the soil?’

Arminian

Repeated fruitlessness leads the owner to call for judgment. Arminian readers see patience as substantial but not a promise of endless postponement. Grace is not permission to remain unresponsive, and the warning concerns fruitful repentance rather than accumulating merit.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the justice of judgment after sustained barrenness. Privilege without fruit cannot be treated as assurance of salvation. Both traditions can affirm that God’s patience is real while refusing to turn the three years into an unsupported prophetic calculation.

Luke 13:8

8He answered, ‘Lord, leave it alone this year also, until I dig around it and fertilize it.

Arminian

The vine dresser asks for time and gives further care rather than abandoning the tree immediately. Arminian readers see an image of patient grace creating opportunity for repentance. The tree’s need for cultivation also resists any idea that fruitfulness is self-produced.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see undeserved patience and active provision preceding the fruit sought. The requested delay highlights mercy rather than a right the barren tree can claim. Both traditions should appreciate the picture without constructing a conflict between an unwilling Father and a more merciful Christ.

Luke 13:9

9If it bears fruit, fine; but if not, after that, you can cut it down.’ ”

Arminian

The story leaves fruitfulness as an open question and judgment as a real possibility. Arminian readers hear an urgent invitation to respond to patient grace. The unresolved ending turns the hearer toward repentance rather than satisfying curiosity about the tree’s eventual fate.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the warning calling professing believers to the fruit of genuine faith. God uses such appeals to awaken and preserve his people. The parable does not itself provide a complete account of regeneration or settle every question about perseverance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
