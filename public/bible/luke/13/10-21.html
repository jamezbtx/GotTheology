Passage

Luke 13

Verses 10–21 · WEB

On the Sabbath Jesus releases a woman from a long affliction and defends the fittingness of her healing against a synagogue ruler’s objection. Her restoration and the crowd’s joy lead into two images of the kingdom’s growth and permeating work. The dispute concerns mercy within Israel’s worshipping life, not a contrast between Christian compassion and a supposedly uniformly loveless Judaism.

Luke 13:10

10He was teaching in one of the synagogues on the Sabbath day.

Arminian

Jesus teaches in a synagogue on the Sabbath before the healing that follows. Arminian readers see his mercy operating within Israel’s worshipping life, not in rejection of everything Jewish. The dispute concerns how the day’s purpose is understood.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognise the synagogue setting as part of Jesus’ ministry among his people. The coming confrontation must not become a caricature of Judaism as simply loveless religion. Both traditions should attend to the particular disagreement Luke narrates.

Luke 13:11

11Behold, there was a woman who had a spirit of infirmity eighteen years. She was bent over and could in no way straighten herself up.

Arminian

The woman has endured severe incapacity for eighteen years. Arminian readers take the narrative’s spiritual dimension seriously without inferring that every disability has the same cause or reflects personal sin. Her long suffering calls for compassion rather than speculation about blame.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a person burdened by an affliction she cannot remove for herself. Dependence on divine mercy does not diminish her dignity. Both traditions should avoid converting this particular description into a universal diagnosis of illness or a reason to neglect medical care.

Luke 13:12

12When Jesus saw her, he called her and said to her, “Woman, you are freed from your infirmity.”

Arminian

Jesus sees the woman and initiates her release. Arminian readers stress grace reaching someone before any narrated request, without making the episode a formula for the order of every conversion. His address restores a person whom the story has shown unable to straighten herself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the priority and authority of Christ’s mercy. He is not waiting for her to overcome the condition before helping her. Both traditions can celebrate this initiative without assuming that every physical healing directly illustrates all the mechanics of salvation.

Luke 13:13

13He laid his hands on her, and immediately she stood up straight and glorified God.

Arminian

The woman stands straight and glorifies God after Jesus lays hands on her. Arminian readers see grateful response to a gift she did not earn. The immediacy of this healing should not become a standard by which those still ill are blamed for insufficient faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ’s effective power displayed in a concrete act of restoration. God receives the glory rather than human ability. Both traditions should affirm the healing without promising that every present sufferer will receive the same outcome now.

Luke 13:14

14The ruler of the synagogue, being indignant because Jesus had healed on the Sabbath, said to the multitude, “There are six days in which men ought to work. Therefore come on those days and be healed, and not on the Sabbath day!”

Arminian

The synagogue ruler objects to the timing of the healing and directs the crowd elsewhere. Arminian readers see a rule applied in a way that misses mercy toward this woman. His response must not be made to represent every synagogue or Jewish understanding of Sabbath.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the danger of defending an observance while failing to recognise its life-giving purpose. The objection is particular and will be answered by Jesus’ argument. Both traditions should test their own religious practices rather than use the scene for collective contempt.

Luke 13:15

15Therefore the Lord answered him, “You hypocrites! Doesn’t each one of you free his ox or his donkey from the stall on the Sabbath and lead him away to water?

Arminian

Jesus appeals to the ordinary care given animals on the Sabbath to expose an inconsistency. Arminian readers see mercy toward the woman as at least as fitting as that accepted daily care. The rebuke confronts selective compassion rather than condemning concern for God’s commands.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the comparison as showing that Sabbath observance and merciful action belong together. The issue is not whether human need matters, but why it is neglected here. Both traditions can examine how self-interest distorts their application of religious rules.

Luke 13:16

16Ought not this woman, being a daughter of Abraham whom Satan had bound eighteen long years, be freed from this bondage on the Sabbath day?”

Arminian

Jesus calls the woman a daughter of Abraham and insists that the Sabbath fits her release from bondage. Arminian readers stress her dignity and the fittingness of liberation. Satan’s role in this case does not explain every person’s illness or make disability a sign of exclusion from God’s people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see covenant belonging invoked in defence of mercy rather than used to postpone it. Jesus’ authority breaks a long bondage. Both traditions should affirm the woman’s full worth without treating those who remain disabled as less free to belong to God.

Luke 13:17

17As he said these things, all his adversaries were disappointed; and all the multitude rejoiced for all the glorious things that were done by him.

Arminian

The opponents are put to shame while the crowd rejoices over Jesus’ works. Arminian readers see mercy vindicated against an objection that had overlooked the sufferer. Public enthusiasm is welcome but is not yet a complete account of lasting discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognise a display of the kingdom’s goodness that exposes mistaken judgment. The crowd’s joy contrasts with the objection, not with the worth of the healed woman. Both traditions should celebrate restoration without assuming that popularity always identifies truth.

Luke 13:18

18He said, “What is God’s Kingdom like? To what shall I compare it?

Arminian

Jesus turns from the healing to images explaining God’s Kingdom. Arminian readers see the need to let his teaching define what divine reign looks like. Its character is not measured simply by visible social power.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise receive the comparison as instruction about God’s initiative and purpose. The preceding restoration supplies a setting for hope rather than an exhaustive definition of the kingdom. Both traditions should interpret the images before imposing a political or prophetic programme on them.

Luke 13:19

19It is like a grain of mustard seed which a man took and put in his own garden. It grew and became a large tree, and the birds of the sky live in its branches.”

Arminian

A small seed grows into a tree providing room for birds. Arminian readers see the kingdom’s disproportionate growth from an unassuming beginning and encouragement to faithful witness. The image need not assign a fixed identity to every bird or branch.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the effectiveness of God’s kingdom despite beginnings that seem insignificant. Growth gives confidence in his purpose rather than grounds for human boasting. Both traditions should avoid turning the image into a guarantee that every institution will expand or succeed.

Luke 13:20

20Again he said, “To what shall I compare God’s Kingdom?

Arminian

Jesus offers a second comparison, inviting hearers to recognise another aspect of the kingdom’s work. Arminian readers see patient attention to his images as part of faithful hearing. No single comparison needs to carry an entire theology of salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the paired parables illuminating the kingdom from complementary angles. The invitation to compare encourages reflection rather than an exhaustive code of hidden meanings. Both traditions should keep the immediate picture of growth and permeation in view.

Luke 13:21

21It is like yeast, which a woman took and hid in three measures of flour, until it was all leavened.”

Arminian

Yeast hidden in a large amount of flour permeates the whole batch. Arminian readers see the kingdom’s quiet, extensive influence rather than a requirement for spectacular beginnings. The example uses yeast positively and does not make every occurrence of it in Scripture a symbol of evil.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s reign achieving effects beyond its initially hidden appearance. The image encourages confidence in divine work without specifying a timetable for universal social change. Both traditions can affirm its promise without inferring that every individual will finally be saved.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
