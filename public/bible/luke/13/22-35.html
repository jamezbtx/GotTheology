Passage

Luke 13

Verses 22–35 · WEB

Journeying toward Jerusalem, Jesus answers a question about the number saved by calling his hearers to enter rather than presume on familiarity. The kingdom’s table will overturn expectations, while threats do not divert his mission. His lament over Jerusalem brings genuine compassion, responsible refusal, and judgment into a passage read differently on the relation between divine grace and human response.

Luke 13:22

22He went on his way through cities and villages, teaching, and traveling on to Jerusalem.

Arminian

Jesus continues teaching as he journeys toward Jerusalem. Arminian readers see the coming passion framing the warnings and invitations that follow. The travel setting keeps discussion of salvation connected to following Jesus rather than detached speculation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the passage within Christ’s purposeful movement toward Jerusalem. His teaching prepares hearers for the demands and hopes of the kingdom. Both traditions should resist lifting the next question out of this larger story of discipleship.

Luke 13:23

23One said to him, “Lord, are they few who are saved?” He said to them,

Arminian

A question asks how many are being saved, but Jesus addresses the hearers’ response rather than supplying a statistic. Arminian readers see an invitation to personal repentance and trust. Curiosity about others must not replace receiving the grace offered to oneself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that Jesus does not reveal the size of the elect here. The answer turns the question toward present responsibility. Both traditions should avoid using the verse to calculate a percentage of humanity that will finally enter the kingdom.

Luke 13:24

24“Strive to enter in by the narrow door, for many, I tell you, will seek to enter in and will not be able.

Arminian

Striving calls for urgent, wholehearted response rather than casual religious association. Arminian readers understand the effort as enabled by grace that can be resisted, not an unaided achievement earning entry. The next scene shows why delayed or presumptuous seeking fails; the door’s narrowness is not a quota excluding sincere believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise reject salvation by merit and understand the command as a means through which God draws people to Christ. Effectual grace brings a willing response rather than making human action unnecessary. The warning calls for real repentance, not speculation about a hidden decree or the belief that the elect need not strive.

Key contrast Both reject earning salvation through unaided effort and hear a genuine command to respond. Arminian readers understand striving through enabling grace that can be resisted; Reformed readers understand saving response as brought about by effectual grace. Neither should turn the command into self-salvation or an excuse for passivity.

Luke 13:25

25When once the master of the house has risen up and has shut the door, and you begin to stand outside and to knock at the door, saying, ‘Lord, Lord, open to us!’ then he will answer and tell you, ‘I don’t know you or where you come from.’

Arminian

The closed door makes the opportunity urgent and exposes a claim to familiarity without recognition by the master. Arminian readers see warning against postponing a genuine response to grace. The passage does not portray God refusing someone because too many other people have already entered.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear a solemn boundary to presumption, not an invitation to search for signs of secret rejection. Mere outward association cannot substitute for knowing Christ. Both traditions should take the warning seriously without depicting salvation as a competition for limited seating.

Luke 13:26

26Then you will begin to say, ‘We ate and drank in your presence, and you taught in our streets.’

Arminian

Eating near Jesus and hearing him teach are offered as credentials by those outside. Arminian readers see the insufficiency of exposure without repentant faith. Religious familiarity is a privilege to answer, not a substitute for allegiance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish outward participation from a saving relationship with Christ. The verse challenges reliance on inherited or social proximity. Both traditions should examine complacency without treating ordinary fellowship and hearing the word as worthless.

Luke 13:27

27He will say, ‘I tell you, I don’t know where you come from. Depart from me, all you workers of iniquity.’

Arminian

The master identifies the excluded as workers of wrongdoing, not simply people lacking social standing. Arminian readers connect a genuine response to grace with a changed allegiance. The verse does not teach that enough good deeds can purchase recognition.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see persistent lawlessness exposing the emptiness of outward claims. Good works are fruit rather than the price of acceptance. Both traditions recognise that Jesus’ warning addresses a life at odds with professed fellowship, not believers’ failure to achieve sinless perfection.

Luke 13:28

28There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth when you see Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and all the prophets in God’s Kingdom, and yourselves being thrown outside.

Arminian

The patriarchs and prophets belong within the kingdom while some who presume on privilege remain outside. Arminian readers see warning and sorrow, not grounds for contempt toward Jewish people. The honoured presence of Israel’s faithful ancestors is part of the picture, not something the verse erases.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see inherited privilege unable to guarantee entry without true belonging to God. The image warns Jesus’ hearers rather than assigning a single destiny to every member of a people. Both traditions should reject racial triumphalism in reading this judgment scene.

Luke 13:29

29They will come from the east, west, north, and south, and will sit down in God’s Kingdom.

Arminian

Guests gather from every direction to share the kingdom’s table. Arminian readers stress the breadth of God’s invitation beyond expected boundaries. Grace welcomes outsiders without making a person’s ancestry or social position the condition of acceptance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God gathering a people wider than the hearers may expect. The worldwide welcome fulfils divine purpose rather than depending on human rank. Both traditions hear a rebuke to narrow assumptions about who can belong at God’s table.

Luke 13:30

30Behold, there are some who are last who will be first, and there are some who are first who will be last.”

Arminian

Jesus reverses expectations of priority and status. Arminian readers see a warning against presuming that early privilege secures final honour and encouragement for those treated as outsiders. The saying does not make every reversal automatic or turn low status into saving merit.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress God’s freedom to overturn human rankings. Those confident in their place must receive mercy rather than claim entitlement. Both traditions should let the reversal challenge pride instead of constructing a new hierarchy in which they inevitably come first.

Luke 13:31

31On that same day, some Pharisees came, saying to him, “Get out of here and go away, for Herod wants to kill you.”

Arminian

Some Pharisees warn Jesus that Herod intends to kill him. Arminian readers note the warning without assuming motives Luke does not state. The scene itself resists treating all Pharisees as an undifferentiated group acting in one way.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the reported threat from speculation about the messengers’ sincerity. The danger does not divert Jesus from his mission. Both traditions can attend to the actual encounter rather than import a stereotype about every religious leader.

Luke 13:32

32He said to them, “Go and tell that fox, ‘Behold, I cast out demons and perform cures today and tomorrow, and the third day I complete my mission.

Arminian

Jesus answers the threat by affirming the continuation and completion of his healing mission. Arminian readers see purposeful obedience rather than fearful retreat. Today, tomorrow, and the third day should not be forced into a detailed chronology that the saying does not explain.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ’s mission governed by God’s purpose rather than Herod’s timetable. His work of release and healing continues under threat. Both traditions can recognise resolve and completion without making every temporal phrase a precise prediction of an itinerary.

Luke 13:33

33Nevertheless I must go on my way today and tomorrow and the next day, for it can’t be that a prophet would perish outside of Jerusalem.’

Arminian

Jesus must continue toward Jerusalem and speaks sharply of its history of rejecting prophets. Arminian readers see commitment to his costly mission rather than an excuse for the violence he will suffer. The saying is not a literal assertion that no prophet ever died elsewhere.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the necessity as belonging to Christ’s calling and the scriptural pattern of rejection. The city’s role is a focus for lament, not a warrant for hatred of its people. Both traditions should preserve prophetic rhetoric without treating murder as righteous because it was foreseen.

Luke 13:34

34“Jerusalem, Jerusalem, you who kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to her! How often I wanted to gather your children together, like a hen gathers her own brood under her wings, and you refused!

Arminian

Jesus’ repeated desire to gather meets Jerusalem’s refusal, expressed through a mother bird’s protective care. Arminian readers see genuine gracious willingness that can be resisted, not an insincere offer. Corporate language does not remove the force of refusal, though interpreters differ over the relation of the city’s leaders and its children.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the sincerity of Christ’s compassion and commonly distinguish God’s revealed desire and invitation from his decretive purpose, rather than infer failure of an effectual call. Some also distinguish resistant leaders from the children Jesus would gather. These approaches vary, but they must retain the grief and culpable refusal the lament expresses.

Key contrast Both affirm Christ’s sincere compassion and Jerusalem’s culpable refusal. Arminian readers see saving grace and desire that can be resisted; Reformed readers commonly distinguish revealed invitation from decretive purpose, sometimes also distinguishing the city’s leaders from its children. The disagreement concerns how refusal relates to God’s saving purpose, not whether Jesus truly grieves.

Luke 13:35

35Behold, your house is left to you desolate. I tell you, you will not see me until you say, ‘Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!’ ”

Arminian

Desolation follows refusal, yet Jesus ends with the language of welcome from the psalm. Arminian readers see judgment without permission to dismiss the lament’s desire to gather. Interpretations of the future greeting differ, including its relation to Jesus’ entry and to a later recognition.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise acknowledge both the announced abandonment and the unresolved horizon of recognition. The verse should not become a confident timetable for every detail of Jerusalem’s future. Both traditions must resist using a lament over the city as a justification for hostility toward Jewish people.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
