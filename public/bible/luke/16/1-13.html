Passage

Luke 16

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Jesus tells his disciples a puzzling parable. A manager who is about to be fired for wasting his master’s goods quickly reduces the debts of his master’s debtors so they will welcome him later. The master praises his shrewdness, not his dishonesty. Jesus draws lessons about money: use worldly wealth to make friends for eternity, be faithful in small things, and remember that no one can serve both God and money. This is one of Jesus’ hardest parables to interpret, and interpreters in both traditions offer several readings. Its money lessons are shared ground.

Luke 16:1

1He also said to his disciples, “There was a certain rich man who had a manager. An accusation was made to him that this man was wasting his possessions.

Arminian

A rich man hears that his manager is wasting his property. Arminian readers note that the parable is addressed to disciples, after the parables of chapter 15.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both traditions note that wealth and stewardship are major themes in Luke.

Luke 16:2

2He called him, and said to him, ‘What is this that I hear about you? Give an accounting of your management, for you can no longer be manager.’

Arminian

The master demands an account and fires him. Arminian readers see a picture of the final accounting every steward faces.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point. Both traditions read stewardship as accountability to God.

Luke 16:3

3“The manager said within himself, ‘What will I do, seeing that my lord is taking away the management position from me? I don’t have strength to dig. I am ashamed to beg.

Arminian

The manager thinks through his options: he cannot dig and is ashamed to beg. Arminian readers note his frank assessment of his situation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared ground.

Luke 16:4

4I know what I will do, so that when I am removed from management, they may receive me into their houses.’

Arminian

He makes a plan so that people will welcome him into their homes. Arminian readers note that he thinks ahead about his future.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both traditions see the point to be his forethought, not his ethics.

Luke 16:5

5Calling each one of his lord’s debtors to him, he said to the first, ‘How much do you owe to my lord?’

Arminian

He calls in his master’s debtors one by one. Arminian readers note that the story turns on quick, decisive action.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared narrative ground.

Luke 16:6

6He said, ‘A hundred batos of oil.’ He said to him, ‘Take your bill, and sit down quickly and write fifty.’

Arminian

He cuts one debt of oil from a hundred measures to fifty. Some interpreters suggest he was removing his own commission or hidden interest, others that he was simply cheating his master; the text does not settle it. Arminian readers note the uncertainty honestly.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the debate. Neither tradition rests doctrine on it, and both note that verse 8 calls him “dishonest.”

Luke 16:7

7Then he said to another, ‘How much do you owe?’ He said, ‘A hundred cors of wheat.’ He said to him, ‘Take your bill, and write eighty.’

Arminian

Another debt of wheat drops from a hundred measures to eighty. Arminian readers note the same pattern.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared ground.

Luke 16:8

8“His lord commended the dishonest manager because he had done wisely, for the children of this world are, in their own generation, wiser than the children of the light.

Arminian

The master praises the dishonest manager for acting shrewdly, and Jesus observes that worldly people are often wiser in their affairs than “children of the light.” Arminian readers stress that the praise is for foresight, not for dishonesty.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same distinction. Both traditions read this as a challenge to believers to use the present wisely for eternal ends.

Luke 16:9

9I tell you, make for yourselves friends by means of unrighteous mammon, so that when you fail, they may receive you into the eternal tents.

Arminian

Use “unrighteous mammon,” worldly wealth, to make friends, so they will receive you into eternal dwellings. Arminian readers stress that generous use of money expresses genuine faith and has eternal significance.

Reformed

Reformed readers also stress generosity, adding that it is the fruit of faith, not a way to earn salvation. Both traditions reject reading the verse as buying entry into heaven.

Luke 16:10

10He who is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much. He who is dishonest in a very little is also dishonest in much.

Arminian

Faithfulness in small things reveals faithfulness in large ones. Arminian readers note the steady character that faithfulness builds over time.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point. This verse is broad shared ground.

Luke 16:11

11If therefore you have not been faithful in the unrighteous mammon, who will commit to your trust the true riches?

Arminian

If you are not faithful with worldly wealth, who will trust you with true riches? Arminian readers stress that stewardship now affects what is entrusted later.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree, reading “true riches” as spiritual treasure. Both traditions see money as a test of faithfulness.

Luke 16:12

12If you have not been faithful in that which is another’s, who will give you that which is your own?

Arminian

If you are not faithful with what belongs to another, who will give you what is your own? Arminian readers note that all we have is really God’s.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point. Both traditions see believers as stewards, not owners.

Luke 16:13

13No servant can serve two masters, for either he will hate the one and love the other; or else he will hold to one and despise the other. You aren’t able to serve God and Mammon.”

Arminian

No servant can serve two masters; you cannot serve God and Mammon. “Mammon” means wealth treated like a rival god. Arminian readers stress that this is a choice of loyalty each person must make.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same exclusive claim of God, and the danger that money can become an idol. Both traditions treat this as a clear and serious warning.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
