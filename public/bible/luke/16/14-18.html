Passage

Luke 16

Verses 14–18 · WEB

The Pharisees, described as lovers of money, scoff at Jesus’ teaching on wealth. He tells them that they justify themselves before people, but God knows their hearts, and what people prize can be detestable to God. He then speaks of the law and the prophets until John, the kingdom now being preached, the lasting authority of the law, and divorce. These sayings seem loosely connected, and interpreters link them in different ways. Several are debated in both traditions, and none of them divides Arminians from Reformed readers.

Luke 16:14

14The Pharisees, who were lovers of money, also heard all these things, and they scoffed at him.

Arminian

The Pharisees, who loved money, scoff at Jesus. Arminian readers note that the scoffing shows how the teaching on money struck home.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point. Both traditions note that Luke links love of money to the rejection of Jesus.

Luke 16:15

15He said to them, “You are those who justify yourselves in the sight of men, but God knows your hearts. For that which is exalted among men is an abomination in the sight of God.

Arminian

They justify themselves before people, but God knows their hearts. What people exalt is detestable to God. Arminian readers stress that outward righteousness cannot replace a changed heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect self-justification to the larger biblical theme that God alone justifies (Luke 18:9–14). Both traditions see the verse as a warning against self-righteousness.

Luke 16:16

16“The law and the prophets were until John. From that time the Good News of God’s Kingdom is preached, and everyone is forcing his way into it.

Arminian

The law and the prophets were until John; now the kingdom is preached. The phrase translated “forcing his way into it” is debated: it may mean people press eagerly into the kingdom, or that it is urgently pressed on them, or something else. Arminian readers note the urgency of response.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note the uncertainty in this verse and its parallel (Matthew 11:12). Both traditions see a turning point in God’s plan with John, and neither builds doctrine on the debated wording.

Luke 16:17

17But it is easier for heaven and earth to pass away than for one tiny stroke of a pen in the law to fall.

Arminian

The law will not lose even one tiny stroke. Arminian readers stress that the new era does not throw out God’s word.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point, often reading it alongside Matthew 5:17–18: Christ fulfills the law rather than abolishing it. Both traditions affirm the abiding authority of Scripture.

Luke 16:18

18“Everyone who divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery. He who marries one who is divorced from a husband commits adultery.

Arminian

Divorcing and remarrying is adultery, as is marrying a divorced woman. Arminian and Reformed readers alike read this saying alongside Matthew 5:32 and 19:9 and 1 Corinthians 7, and both traditions contain different views on allowable exceptions.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm the seriousness of marriage, and their confessions allow divorce in limited cases (for example, adultery or desertion). Divorce views vary within both traditions and do not split along Arminian and Reformed lines.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
