Passage

Luke 16

Verses 19–31 · WEB

Jesus tells of a rich man who lived in luxury and a beggar named Lazarus who lay sick and hungry at his gate. Both die. Lazarus is carried to Abraham’s side, while the rich man is in torment in Hades. A great gulf separates them, and Abraham refuses the rich man’s request to send Lazarus to warn his five brothers: if they will not listen to Moses and the prophets, they will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead. Interpreters in both traditions debate whether this is a parable or an account, and how literally to take its picture of the afterlife. Both agree on its main warnings about wealth, judgment, and the sufficiency of Scripture.

Luke 16:19

19“Now there was a certain rich man, and he was clothed in purple and fine linen, living in luxury every day.

Arminian

The rich man wears purple and fine linen and lives in luxury every day. Arminian readers note that he is unnamed, while the poor man is named.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same reversal. Both traditions see the story continuing the theme of verses 13–14.

Luke 16:20

20A certain beggar, named Lazarus, was taken to his gate, full of sores,

Arminian

Lazarus, whose name means “God helps,” lies at the rich man’s gate, covered in sores. Arminian readers note that the rich man daily passed by real need.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both traditions see the danger of indifference to the poor.

Luke 16:21

21and desiring to be fed with the crumbs that fell from the rich man’s table. Yes, even the dogs came and licked his sores.

Arminian

He longs for crumbs from the table, and dogs lick his sores. Arminian readers note the depth of his misery.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared ground.

Luke 16:22

22The beggar died, and he was carried away by the angels to Abraham’s bosom. The rich man also died and was buried.

Arminian

Lazarus dies and is carried by angels to Abraham’s side; the rich man dies and is buried. Arminian readers note the sharp reversal at death.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both traditions stress that Lazarus is not saved by being poor, nor is the rich man condemned simply for being rich; the issue is the heart revealed in how one lives (v. 29).

Luke 16:23

23In Hades, he lifted up his eyes, being in torment, and saw Abraham far off, and Lazarus at his bosom.

Arminian

In Hades the rich man, in torment, sees Abraham far away with Lazarus. Arminian readers note the reality of conscious judgment, while recognizing debate about how literal the imagery is.

Reformed

Reformed readers also affirm real judgment and recognize the same debate. Both traditions agree that the story is not meant to answer every question about the afterlife.

Luke 16:24

24He cried and said, ‘Father Abraham, have mercy on me, and send Lazarus, that he may dip the tip of his finger in water and cool my tongue! For I am in anguish in this flame.’

Arminian

He begs Abraham to send Lazarus to cool his tongue. Arminian readers note that he still treats Lazarus as a servant.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same unchanged attitude. Both traditions find this detail telling.

Luke 16:25

25“But Abraham said, ‘Son, remember that you, in your lifetime, received your good things, and Lazarus, in the same way, bad things. But here he is now comforted and you are in anguish.

Arminian

Abraham reminds him of the reversal: he had good things, Lazarus bad, and now their places are changed. Arminian readers note the echo of Luke 1:52–53 and 6:20–26.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both traditions see Luke’s theme of God lifting the lowly.

Luke 16:26

26Besides all this, between us and you there is a great gulf fixed, that those who want to pass from here to you are not able, and that no one may cross over from there to us.’

Arminian

A great gulf is fixed, and no one can cross it. Arminian readers stress that the choices of this life have lasting results.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point. Both traditions use this verse to reject the idea of a second chance after death.

Luke 16:27

27“He said, ‘I ask you therefore, father, that you would send him to my father’s house—

Arminian

He asks Abraham to send Lazarus to his father’s house. Arminian readers note that he finally thinks of others, though too late.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared ground.

Luke 16:28

28for I have five brothers—that he may testify to them, so they won’t also come into this place of torment.’

Arminian

He wants his five brothers warned so they will not come to this place. Arminian readers see sincere concern for the family.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point, and some note that he seems to blame a lack of warning. Both traditions find it a sobering request.

Luke 16:29

29“But Abraham said to him, ‘They have Moses and the prophets. Let them listen to them.’

Arminian

Abraham answers, “They have Moses and the prophets. Let them listen to them.” Arminian readers stress that Scripture gives sufficient light to repent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the sufficiency of Scripture. Both traditions treat this as a strong statement that God’s word is enough.

Luke 16:30

30“He said, ‘No, father Abraham, but if one goes to them from the dead, they will repent.’

Arminian

The rich man insists that someone returning from the dead would make them repent. Arminian readers note that he still thinks signs will succeed where Scripture failed.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point. This verse is shared ground.

Luke 16:31

31“He said to him, ‘If they don’t listen to Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded if one rises from the dead.’ ”

Arminian

“If they don’t listen to Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded if one rises from the dead.” Luke’s readers know Jesus rose. Arminian readers stress that people can resist even the clearest evidence, and that grace works through Scripture without forcing belief.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that unbelief is not cured by evidence alone; only God’s inner work through his word changes the heart. Both traditions see the resurrection reflected here, and the sad fact that many still did not believe.

Key contrast Both agree evidence alone does not produce faith. Arminians usually explain unbelief as resisting grace offered through the word; Reformed theology usually explains belief as requiring the Spirit’s effectual work through the word.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
