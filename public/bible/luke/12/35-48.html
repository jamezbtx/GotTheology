Passage

Luke 12

Verses 35–48 · WEB

Jesus calls his disciples to readiness for the returning master, who astonishingly serves his watchful servants. Peter’s question leads to a warning for household stewards: entrusted authority must feed and serve others, not excuse abuse during an apparent delay. The master’s judgment distinguishes deliberate defiance from lesser knowledge, while holding both accountable. Both traditions affirm watchfulness, responsible leadership, and proportionate judgment; they differ over how the condemned servant relates to genuine saving faith.

Luke 12:35

35“Let your waist be dressed and your lamps burning.

Arminian

Readiness is pictured through clothing prepared for action and lamps kept burning. Both traditions understand this as active discipleship, not passive speculation about a timetable.

Reformed

Reformed readers see watchfulness as the proper response to promised return. Everyday obedience, rather than date-setting, gives substance to expectation.

Luke 12:36

36Be like men watching for their lord when he returns from the wedding feast, that when he comes and knocks, they may immediately open to him.

Arminian

The servants are ready to open immediately when their master returns. Arminian readers see sustained attentiveness as a fitting expression of love and loyalty.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm expectant service. Both traditions understand the wedding setting as the parable’s scene rather than a code from which every detail of the future must be reconstructed.

Luke 12:37

37Blessed are those servants whom the lord will find watching when he comes. Most certainly I tell you that he will dress himself, make them recline, and will come and serve them.

Arminian

The master astonishingly serves the servants he finds watching. Both traditions see gracious welcome exceeding what ordinary service could claim or earn.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the reversal that reveals the Lord’s generosity. Readiness matters, but the feast remains the master’s gift rather than wages placing him in the servants’ debt.

Luke 12:38

38They will be blessed if he comes in the second or third watch and finds them so.

Arminian

The servants remain ready even when the return comes late in the night. Arminian readers emphasize persevering faithfulness when waiting becomes tiring.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a call to endurance without assuming the delay defeats the promise. Both traditions resist assigning a precise prophetic schedule to the watches of the night.

Luke 12:39

39But know this, that if the master of the house had known in what hour the thief was coming, he would have watched and not allowed his house to be broken into.

Arminian

The householder image stresses the unexpected hour. Both traditions understand the comparison as a warning about readiness, not an attribution of a thief’s moral character to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that advance knowledge would make vigilance easy. The point is continued preparedness when the time is not disclosed.

Luke 12:40

40Therefore be ready also, for the Son of Man is coming in an hour that you don’t expect him.”

Arminian

Jesus directly applies the image to the coming Son of Man. Arminian readers stress present readiness rather than assuming there will always be time for a later response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command as part of Christ’s provision for keeping his people alert. Both traditions refuse to replace obedience with confident predictions of the date.

Luke 12:41

41Peter said to him, “Lord, are you telling this parable to us, or to everybody?”

Arminian

Peter asks about the scope of the parable. Both traditions note that Jesus answers by describing responsible stewardship rather than by giving a simple list of intended hearers.

Reformed

Reformed readers recognize a pointed application to those entrusted with others’ care. The broader summons to readiness is not thereby removed from the rest of the disciples.

Luke 12:42

42The Lord said, “Who then is the faithful and wise steward, whom his lord will set over his household, to give them their portion of food at the right times?

Arminian

The faithful steward distributes food at the proper time. Arminian readers see leadership defined by service to the household, not by privilege or control.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize accountability for resources and people entrusted by the Lord. Both traditions understand faithful teaching and care as responsibilities, not possessions belonging to the leader.

Luke 12:43

43Blessed is that servant whom his lord will find doing so when he comes.

Arminian

Blessing rests on the servant found doing the assigned work. Both traditions see faithfulness embodied in service rather than mere claims to expect the master’s return.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand such obedience as fruit of grace. Assurance must not be converted into an excuse to stop caring for the household.

Luke 12:44

44Truly I tell you that he will set him over all that he has.

Arminian

The master entrusts more to the faithful servant. Arminian readers affirm the reality of reward while locating all service within the master’s prior generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish gracious reward from merit that purchases salvation. Both traditions see entrusted authority as ordered toward faithful responsibility.

Luke 12:45

45But if that servant says in his heart, ‘My lord delays his coming,’ and begins to beat the menservants and the maidservants, and to eat and drink and to be drunken,

Arminian

An apparent delay becomes the servant’s excuse for violence and indulgence. Both traditions recognize an especially grave warning to leaders who exploit vulnerable people under their care.

Reformed

Reformed readers see abuse revealing contempt for the returning master. Delay exposes the servant’s heart; it does not authorize the household to ignore present wrongdoing.

Luke 12:46

46then the lord of that servant will come in a day when he isn’t expecting him and in an hour that he doesn’t know, and will cut him in two, and place his portion with the unfaithful.

Arminian

The abusive servant is judged and assigned a place with the unfaithful. Many Arminian readers see a warning that one genuinely entrusted with grace can abandon faith; others caution that a stewardship role alone does not prove regeneration.

Reformed

Reformed readers typically understand the condemned steward as a professing servant whose conduct reveals the absence of saving faith. Both take the judgment seriously and refuse to make the warning harmless for those presently claiming to serve Christ.

Key contrast Both warn professing disciples against abusive unfaithfulness. Many Arminian readers allow the servant to portray a genuine believer who falls away; Reformed readers typically see a false professor exposed at judgment. Entrusted office by itself does not prove regeneration.

Luke 12:47

47That servant who knew his lord’s will, and didn’t prepare nor do what he wanted, will be beaten with many stripes,

Arminian

Knowledge of the master’s will increases responsibility for disobedience. Arminian readers affirm degrees of judgment and reject the idea that greater religious knowledge is automatically greater spiritual safety.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm proportionate judgment and heightened accountability for informed rebellion. This is shared teaching, not a distinctively Arminian or Reformed claim about punishment.

Luke 12:48

48but he who didn’t know, and did things worthy of stripes, will be beaten with few stripes. To whomever much is given, of him will much be required; and to whom much was entrusted, of him more will be asked.

Arminian

Lesser knowledge brings fewer stripes, not automatic innocence. Both traditions affirm that God judges justly in relation to what has been given; the saying does not supply a complete account of every unevangelized person’s destiny.

Reformed

Reformed readers see gifts, opportunity, and entrusted responsibility increasing what is required. Arminian readers share that principle, and neither tradition should use it to excuse withholding truth or neglecting service.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
