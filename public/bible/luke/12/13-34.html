Passage

Luke 12

Verses 13–34 · WEB

An inheritance request prompts Jesus to warn against greed through the rich fool, whose abundance cannot secure another night of life. Turning to his disciples, Jesus addresses anxiety over food and clothing by pointing to the Father’s care for birds and flowers. Seeking the kingdom and sharing with the needy replace both hoarding and anxious preoccupation. Both traditions affirm that the kingdom is the Father’s gift and that trust should reshape the use of possessions.

Luke 12:13

13One of the multitude said to him, “Teacher, tell my brother to divide the inheritance with me.”

Arminian

A listener asks Jesus to intervene in an inheritance dispute. Both traditions note how concern for property can distract from the urgent teaching just given about faithfulness before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers avoid deciding the brother’s legal guilt when the account supplies no details. Jesus addresses the danger of greed without adjudicating the disputed inheritance.

Luke 12:14

14But he said to him, “Man, who made me a judge or an arbitrator over you?”

Arminian

Jesus declines the requested role of arbitrator. Arminian readers understand this as a refusal to let this demand redirect his mission, not a denial that earthly justice matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish Christ’s present mission from accepting every dispute on a crowd member’s terms. Both traditions resist using the verse to excuse injustice or abolish legitimate legal processes.

Luke 12:15

15He said to them, “Beware! Keep yourselves from covetousness, for a man’s life doesn’t consist of the abundance of the things which he possesses.”

Arminian

Jesus warns against every form of covetousness. Arminian readers see a call to reject the belief that accumulating possessions can define or secure a person’s life.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that human worth and life do not derive from abundance. The warning searches both the wealthy and those consumed by wanting what they lack.

Luke 12:16

16He spoke a parable to them, saying, “The ground of a certain rich man produced abundantly.

Arminian

The rich man’s land produces a large harvest. Both traditions note that abundance itself is not called evil; the problem emerges in how he receives and uses it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providential bounty creating responsibility, not a right to self-absorption. The story invites attention to the giver and to neighbors absent from the man’s plans.

Luke 12:17

17He reasoned within himself, saying, ‘What will I do, because I don’t have room to store my crops?’

Arminian

The man’s concern is where to store his surplus. Arminian readers see the narrowing of vision when prosperity becomes only a private management problem rather than an opportunity for generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that his conversation with himself leaves God and others unmentioned. Both distinguish prudent planning from the self-enclosed security the parable exposes.

Luke 12:18

18He said, ‘This is what I will do. I will pull down my barns, build bigger ones, and there I will store all my grain and my goods.

Arminian

Bigger barns become his answer to abundance. Both traditions recognize that buildings and storage are not inherently sinful; hoarding becomes folly when it serves trust in possessions alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a contrast between entrusted resources and imagined self-sufficiency. The man expands his capacity to store without questioning the purpose for which he has received so much.

Luke 12:19

19I will tell my soul, “Soul, you have many goods laid up for many years. Take your ease, eat, drink, and be merry.” ’

Arminian

Stored goods are made the basis of years of ease. Arminian readers see the error in assuming that material provision controls the future or answers the soul’s deepest need.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the presumption of claiming years one does not possess. Both traditions distinguish receiving ordinary enjoyment gratefully from making leisure and consumption the final good.

Luke 12:20

20“But God said to him, ‘You foolish one, tonight your soul is required of you. The things which you have prepared—whose will they be?’

Arminian

God’s judgment interrupts the man’s plans that very night. Both traditions see mortality exposing the limits of ownership: accumulated goods cannot keep their possessor alive.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that life remains accountable to God. The question about the goods’ next owner reveals how little control the man actually has over his supposed security.

Luke 12:21

21So is he who lays up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God.”

Arminian

Self-directed treasure without richness toward God repeats the rich fool’s error. Arminian readers understand richness toward God as a life oriented to him, expressed in the generosity Jesus soon commands.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect inward trust with outward stewardship. Both deny that giving purchases salvation; the issue is whether possessions serve devotion to God or replace it.

Luke 12:22

22He said to his disciples, “Therefore I tell you, don’t be anxious for your life, what you will eat, nor yet for your body, what you will wear.

Arminian

Jesus turns from greed to anxiety among his disciples. Both traditions recognize that trust in the Father challenges fear over necessities as well as the wealthy person’s craving for surplus.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the teaching as pastoral care rather than dismissal of real need. Dependence on providence includes receiving practical help and giving it to those who lack food or clothing.

Luke 12:23

23Life is more than food, and the body is more than clothing.

Arminian

Life and the body are greater gifts than what sustains and clothes them. Arminian readers see Jesus widening anxious attention toward the God who has already given life.

Reformed

Reformed readers reason from the greater gift to the Father’s care for daily needs. Both traditions resist reducing a human life to the work of acquiring necessities.

Luke 12:24

24Consider the ravens: they don’t sow, they don’t reap, they have no warehouse or barn, and God feeds them. How much more valuable are you than birds!

Arminian

God feeds ravens that lack the rich man’s storage system. Both traditions hear an argument for confidence in the Father’s care, not a command that people cease all labor.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize providence even for creatures outside human systems of provision. The disciples’ greater value encourages trust without promising exemption from hardship.

Luke 12:25

25Which of you by being anxious can add a cubit to his height?

Arminian

Anxiety cannot add the cubit to height described in the WEB. Interpreters differ over whether the image points to stature or length of life; either reading exposes anxiety’s inability to secure life.

Reformed

Reformed readers retain the printed wording rather than silently replacing it with a different rendering. Both traditions distinguish the futility of anxious control from useful care and responsible action.

Luke 12:26

26If then you aren’t able to do even the least things, why are you anxious about the rest?

Arminian

If anxiety cannot accomplish even this, it cannot govern the rest of life. Arminian readers hear a call to relinquish imagined control while continuing responsible dependence on God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see acknowledged creaturely limits as an invitation to trust the Creator. Both traditions should apply the saying gently, not treat involuntary distress as proof that someone lacks faith.

Luke 12:27

27Consider the lilies, how they grow. They don’t toil, neither do they spin; yet I tell you, even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these.

Arminian

The lilies’ beauty surpasses Solomon’s royal splendor. Both traditions see ordinary creation displaying a generosity that human effort cannot manufacture or finally explain.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize God’s adornment of what appears small and passing. The example directs anxious disciples toward the Father’s care rather than toward comparison with human wealth.

Luke 12:28

28But if this is how God clothes the grass in the field, which today exists and tomorrow is cast into the oven, how much more will he clothe you, O you of little faith?

Arminian

God clothes grass destined for the oven and will care much more for his people. Arminian readers see the rebuke to little faith joined to an invitation to stronger trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear correction within fatherly care, not abandonment because faith is weak. Both traditions encourage growing confidence without using material comfort as the measure of God’s favor.

Luke 12:29

29“Don’t seek what you will eat or what you will drink; neither be anxious.

Arminian

Disciples must not let food and drink become an anxious preoccupation. Both traditions read this alongside the prayer for daily bread, so Jesus does not forbid acknowledging bodily needs.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish legitimate concern from being ruled by it. The alternative is dependence on the Father rather than denial that tomorrow’s needs are real.

Luke 12:30

30For the nations of the world seek after all of these things, but your Father knows that you need these things.

Arminian

The Father knows what his disciples need. Arminian readers see a family relationship that frees them from treating acquisition as life’s controlling pursuit.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the Father’s prior knowledge and care. Both traditions contrast patterns of anxious living without implying that every nonbeliever is personally more anxious than every believer.

Luke 12:31

31But seek God’s Kingdom, and all these things will be added to you.

Arminian

Seeking God’s kingdom sets the proper priority for life. Arminian readers stress active trust and obedience, not bargaining with God to obtain material rewards.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm that kingdom allegiance rests on grace and reshapes daily concerns. Both read the promise as assurance of fatherly provision, not a prosperity formula immune to the wider teaching about suffering.

Luke 12:32

32“Don’t be afraid, little flock, for it is your Father’s good pleasure to give you the Kingdom.

Arminian

The little flock need not fear because the Father delights to give the kingdom. Arminian readers affirm a freely given inheritance received through faith and held in continuing trust, not earned by obedient performance.

Reformed

Reformed readers ground assurance in the Father’s gracious purpose to bring his people into the kingdom and preserve them for it. Both hear comfort before the command to give; generosity flows from receiving, not purchasing, the gift.

Key contrast Both receive the kingdom as a free gift, not earned wages. Arminian readers relate assurance to continuing faith sustained by grace; Reformed readers emphasize the Father’s purpose to preserve his people so that they inherit the promised kingdom.

Luke 12:33

33Sell what you have and give gifts to the needy. Make for yourselves purses which don’t grow old, a treasure in the heavens that doesn’t fail, where no thief approaches and no moth destroys.

Arminian

Jesus gives costly, concrete direction to sell possessions and help the needy. Arminian readers stress that trust takes material form; debates over application must not empty the command of sacrificial generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see heavenly treasure contrasting with the rich fool’s insecure barns. Both traditions affirm a real claim on possessions while differing internally about the command’s exact application to every disciple.

Luke 12:34

34For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also.

Arminian

Treasure directs the heart’s attention and loyalty. Both traditions see giving as more than an external financial decision: it exposes and helps shape what a person loves.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect stewardship with worship. The saying asks where security is located, not simply whether a household has adopted an approved financial technique.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
