Passage

Luke 24

Verses 13–35 · WEB

Two disciples walk to Emmaus, downcast, when the risen Jesus joins them unrecognized. He opens the Scriptures to show that the Christ had to suffer and enter his glory, and he is made known in the breaking of bread. Both traditions treasure this passage on Christ in all the Scriptures; they differ somewhat in how they describe the opening of eyes and hearts.

Luke 24:13

13Behold, two of them were going that very day to a village named Emmaus, which was sixty stadia from Jerusalem.

Arminian

Two disciples travel to Emmaus that same day. Arminian readers note that they are ordinary followers, not apostles; the risen Lord seeks out the discouraged.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Christ’s care reaches beyond the inner circle to lesser-known disciples.

Luke 24:14

14They talked with each other about all of these things which had happened.

Arminian

They talk about all that had happened. Arminian readers note honest processing of grief and confusion.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both see value in disciples talking through events in light of what God has done.

Luke 24:15

15While they talked and questioned together, Jesus himself came near, and went with them.

Arminian

Jesus himself draws near and walks with them. Arminian readers see the seeking love of Christ coming alongside the doubting.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Good Shepherd pursuing his own. Both find great pastoral comfort here.

Luke 24:16

16But their eyes were kept from recognizing him.

Arminian

Their eyes are kept from recognizing him. Arminian readers often read this as a temporary divine restraint for teaching purposes, not a permanent hardening.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign control over perception, here serving the disciples’ good. Both agree it is purposeful and temporary.

Key contrast Both see God at work in their not recognizing him. Arminian readers frame it as a pedagogical withholding that respects the disciples’ learning; Reformed readers see it as an instance of God governing when and how spiritual sight is given.

Luke 24:17

17He said to them, “What are you talking about as you walk, and are sad?”

Arminian

Jesus asks what they are discussing; they stand still, sad. Arminian readers see Jesus inviting them to speak, engaging their hearts and minds.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same patience. The risen Lord draws out their grief before he addresses it.

Luke 24:18

18One of them, named Cleopas, answered him, “Are you the only stranger in Jerusalem who doesn’t know the things which have happened there in these days?”

Arminian

Cleopas asks if he is the only visitor unaware of these things. Arminian readers note the irony: the one who knows most is thought to know least.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same irony. Both observe that the events were public and widely known in Jerusalem.

Luke 24:19

19He said to them, “What things?” They said to him, “The things concerning Jesus the Nazarene, who was a prophet mighty in deed and word before God and all the people;

Arminian

They describe Jesus as a prophet mighty in deed and word. Arminian readers note that their view is true but incomplete.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Their confession falls short of seeing him as the suffering and risen Christ.

Luke 24:20

20and how the chief priests and our rulers delivered him up to be condemned to death, and crucified him.

Arminian

They recount how the leaders delivered him to death and crucified him. Arminian readers note the clear naming of human responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same human guilt, which Jesus will soon set within God’s plan in verse 26.

Luke 24:21

21But we were hoping that it was he who would redeem Israel. Yes, and besides all this, it is now the third day since these things happened.

Arminian

“We were hoping that it was he who would redeem Israel.” Arminian readers hear disappointed hope, a hope that was right in substance but wrong in its expected form.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the same. Jesus did redeem Israel, but through suffering, as the Scriptures foretold.

Luke 24:22

22Also, certain women of our company amazed us, having arrived early at the tomb;

Arminian

Some women amazed them, having been early at the tomb. Arminian readers note the report reached them but did not yet persuade them.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Hearing testimony is not yet the same as faith.

Luke 24:23

23and when they didn’t find his body, they came saying that they had also seen a vision of angels, who said that he was alive.

Arminian

The women reported a vision of angels saying he was alive. Arminian readers note that the message was already given clearly.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. The word was present; what was lacking was understanding.

Luke 24:24

24Some of us went to the tomb and found it just like the women had said, but they didn’t see him.”

Arminian

Some went to the tomb and found it as the women said, but did not see him. Arminian readers see evidence confirmed but still not embraced.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that evidence alone does not create faith; the risen Christ must reveal himself.

Luke 24:25

25He said to them, “Foolish people, and slow of heart to believe in all that the prophets have spoken!

Arminian

Jesus calls them foolish and slow of heart to believe the prophets. Arminian readers see a loving rebuke that holds them responsible for responding to Scripture.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a rebuke of real culpability, alongside the need for God to open hearts. Both agree slowness of heart is a moral and spiritual problem, not only an intellectual one.

Key contrast Both see real responsibility here. Arminian readers stress that the disciples could and should have believed the prophets; Reformed readers affirm that while stressing that overcoming slowness of heart requires God’s enabling work, as verses 31–32 and 45 show.

Luke 24:26

26Didn’t the Christ have to suffer these things and to enter into his glory?”

Arminian

Didn’t the Christ have to suffer these things and enter his glory? Arminian readers see the divine plan of suffering then glory, revealed in Scripture.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same necessity grounded in God’s purpose. Both make the cross-then-glory pattern central to reading the Old Testament.

Luke 24:27

27Beginning from Moses and from all the prophets, he explained to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself.

Arminian

Beginning from Moses and all the prophets, he explains the things about himself in all the Scriptures. Arminian readers see Christ as the key to the whole Old Testament.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read all Scripture as pointing to Christ. Both traditions share this Christ-centered reading of the Bible.

Luke 24:28

28They came near to the village where they were going, and he acted like he would go further.

Arminian

They approach the village, and he acts as if going farther. Arminian readers see Jesus giving room for their invitation, not forcing himself on them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a gracious test that draws out their desire. Both note that he waits to be welcomed.

Luke 24:29

29They urged him, saying, “Stay with us, for it is almost evening, and the day is almost over.” He went in to stay with them.

Arminian

They urge him to stay, since it is evening. Arminian readers see a free response of hospitality and longing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see desire already being stirred by his words (verse 32). Both see the invitation as fitting response to Christ.

Luke 24:30

30When he had sat down at the table with them, he took the bread and gave thanks. Breaking it, he gave it to them.

Arminian

At the table he takes bread, blesses, breaks, and gives it. Arminian readers note the echo of the feeding miracle and the Last Supper.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same echoes. Many in both traditions see a link to the Lord’s Supper, though the scene itself is a shared meal.

Luke 24:31

31Their eyes were opened and they recognized him; then he vanished out of their sight.

Arminian

Their eyes are opened; they recognize him, and he vanishes. Arminian readers see grace granting recognition, which they gladly receive.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God opening eyes, the counterpart of verse 16. Both agree recognition of Christ is a gift.

Luke 24:32

32They said to one another, “Weren’t our hearts burning within us while he spoke to us along the way, and while he opened the Scriptures to us?”

Arminian

Their hearts burned as he opened the Scriptures. Arminian readers see Scripture used by the Spirit to stir hearts toward faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the inward work of God through the Word. Both treasure Word and Spirit together.

Luke 24:33

33They rose up that very hour, returned to Jerusalem, and found the eleven gathered together, and those who were with them,

Arminian

They return at once to Jerusalem. Arminian readers see joy prompting immediate action and witness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. Encounter with the risen Christ turns them around, literally and spiritually.

Luke 24:34

34saying, “The Lord is risen indeed, and has appeared to Simon!”

Arminian

The eleven say, “The Lord is risen indeed, and has appeared to Simon!” Arminian readers note Peter’s restoration after his denial.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same, and the confirmation of 1 Corinthians 15:5. Both see the resurrection witness growing.

Luke 24:35

35They related the things that happened along the way, and how he was recognized by them in the breaking of the bread.

Arminian

The two tell how he was known in the breaking of bread. Arminian readers see shared testimony strengthening the community.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. Both note that Christ makes himself known through his word and at the table among his people.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
