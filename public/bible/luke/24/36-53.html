Passage

Luke 24

Verses 36–53 · WEB

Jesus appears to the gathered disciples, shows his hands and feet, and eats before them. He opens their minds to understand the Scriptures, commissions them to preach repentance and forgiveness to all nations, promises the Father’s gift, and ascends while blessing them. Both traditions share deep common ground here; differences surface mainly in how they describe the opening of minds and the universal preaching.

Luke 24:36

36As they said these things, Jesus himself stood among them, and said to them, “Peace be to you.”

Arminian

Jesus stands among them and says, “Peace be to you.” Arminian readers see peace offered to frightened, failing disciples.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same peace won through his death. Both find comfort that his first word is peace.

Luke 24:37

37But they were terrified and filled with fear, and supposed that they had seen a spirit.

Arminian

They are terrified, thinking they see a spirit. Arminian readers note that fear and doubt are natural responses Jesus patiently addresses.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both see the disciples as not credulous; they suspect a ghost rather than inventing a bodily resurrection.

Luke 24:38

38He said to them, “Why are you troubled? Why do doubts arise in your hearts?

Arminian

“Why are you troubled? Why do doubts arise?” Arminian readers see gentle pastoral inquiry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. Jesus addresses doubts directly rather than ignoring them.

Luke 24:39

39See my hands and my feet, that it is truly me. Touch me and see, for a spirit doesn’t have flesh and bones, as you see that I have.”

Arminian

“See my hands and my feet… a spirit doesn’t have flesh and bones.” Arminian readers stress the physical reality of the resurrection body.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same. Both affirm a real bodily resurrection, continuous with the crucified body.

Luke 24:40

40When he had said this, he showed them his hands and his feet.

Arminian

He shows them his hands and feet. The WEB includes this verse without a footnote, though a few manuscripts lack it. Arminian readers see evidence given to support faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. The marks of the cross remain, identifying the risen Lord as the crucified one.

Luke 24:41

41While they still didn’t believe for joy, and wondered, he said to them, “Do you have anything here to eat?”

Arminian

While they still disbelieve for joy, he asks for food. Arminian readers note that joy and disbelief mingle; Jesus is patient.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both find comfort that weak faith is met with kindness.

Luke 24:42

42They gave him a piece of a broiled fish and some honeycomb.

Arminian

They give him a piece of broiled fish. Arminian readers note the simple, ordinary detail.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Ordinary detail supports the reality of what happened.

Luke 24:43

43He took them, and ate in front of them.

Arminian

He eats it before them. Arminian readers see further proof of bodily resurrection.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Acts 10:41 later points to eating and drinking with him after he rose.

Luke 24:44

44He said to them, “This is what I told you while I was still with you, that all things which are written in the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms concerning me must be fulfilled.”

Arminian

Everything written about him in the law, prophets, and psalms must be fulfilled. Arminian readers see the whole Old Testament bearing witness to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same, and the divine necessity of fulfillment. Both share a Christ-centered reading of Scripture.

Luke 24:45

45Then he opened their minds, that they might understand the Scriptures.

Arminian

He opens their minds to understand the Scriptures. Arminian readers see enabling grace that illumines understanding, which the disciples receive and respond to.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a sovereign, effective opening of the mind, without which they would not understand. Both agree that understanding Scripture rightly is a gift of the risen Lord.

Key contrast Both treat this as divine illumination. Arminian readers usually see it as grace that enables and invites understanding; Reformed readers usually see it as God effectually granting understanding, a pattern they link with Acts 16:14.

Luke 24:46

46He said to them, “Thus it is written, and thus it was necessary for the Christ to suffer and to rise from the dead the third day,

Arminian

Thus it is written: the Christ suffers and rises the third day. Arminian readers see the core gospel events anchored in Scripture.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. Both affirm cross and resurrection as the center of biblical revelation.

Luke 24:47

47and that repentance and remission of sins should be preached in his name to all the nations, beginning at Jerusalem.

Arminian

Repentance and remission of sins should be preached in his name to all nations, beginning at Jerusalem. Arminian readers see God’s universal offer of forgiveness to every people, calling each to repent.

Reformed

Reformed readers also affirm the universal preaching of the gospel to all nations, a sincere call to all. Both agree the church must proclaim repentance and forgiveness everywhere.

Key contrast Shared ground: the gospel is to be preached to all nations. Arminian readers see this as reflecting God’s saving intent for all people alike; Reformed readers affirm the free offer to all while holding that God effectually saves his elect from every nation through that preaching.

Luke 24:48

48You are witnesses of these things.

Arminian

“You are witnesses of these things.” Arminian readers see the disciples entrusted with responsibility to testify.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same commission. Both see the church’s mission rooted in eyewitness testimony.

Luke 24:49

49Behold, I send out the promise of my Father on you. But wait in the city of Jerusalem until you are clothed with power from on high.”

Arminian

He will send the promise of the Father; they are to wait until clothed with power from on high. Arminian readers see the Spirit empowering believers for witness and holy living.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same promise fulfilled at Pentecost (Acts 2). Both agree mission depends on the Spirit’s power, not human strength.

Key contrast Both see the promised Spirit as essential. Discussion among both traditions on the Spirit’s work differs more within each tradition than between them; neither reads this verse as settling debates about election.

Luke 24:50

50He led them out as far as Bethany, and he lifted up his hands and blessed them.

Arminian

He leads them out as far as Bethany and lifts up his hands to bless them. Arminian readers see a priestly blessing on his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same priestly act. Both see Christ’s continuing intercession pictured here.

Luke 24:51

51While he blessed them, he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven.

Arminian

While blessing them, he is carried up into heaven. Arminian readers see the ascension as the exaltation of Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same, and Christ’s session at God’s right hand. Both see blessing as his last earthly act.

Luke 24:52

52They worshiped him and returned to Jerusalem with great joy,

Arminian

They worship him and return to Jerusalem with great joy. Arminian readers note that worship of Jesus shows his divine identity.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. Both see joy, not grief, as the fruit of his departure.

Luke 24:53

53and were continually in the temple, praising and blessing God. Amen.

Arminian

They are continually in the temple, praising and blessing God. Arminian readers see the Gospel ending where it began, in the temple, now with praise.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same literary frame. Both see worship as the fitting response to the finished work of Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
