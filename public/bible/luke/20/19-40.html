Passage

Luke 20

Verses 19–40 · WEB

The temple controversies continue as opponents seek words they can use against Jesus before the governor. A question about Caesar’s tax is followed by a challenge to resurrection built around a hypothetical series of marriages. Jesus refuses the political trap and explains that resurrection life is transformed rather than a simple continuation of present arrangements. Both traditions affirm God’s ultimate claim and bodily resurrection; differences mainly concern the relation of grace to receiving that life, while views of civil authority and details of the coming age also vary within each tradition.

Luke 20:19

19The chief priests and the scribes sought to lay hands on him that very hour, but they feared the people—for they knew he had spoken this parable against them.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the leaders recognizing the parable’s application while choosing hostility rather than repentance. Their fear of the crowd restrains their action, but it does not change their intent.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the parable exposing the very resistance it describes. The leaders’ reaction is their own guilty response, while the crowd’s presence temporarily limits what they can do.

Luke 20:20

20They watched him and sent out spies, who pretended to be righteous, that they might trap him in something he said, so as to deliver him up to the power and authority of the governor.

Arminian

Arminian readers see a calculated attempt to turn Jesus’ words into grounds for political accusation. Pretended righteousness becomes especially dangerous when religious language conceals an intention to harm another person.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the spies’ outward appearance set against their hidden purpose. Both traditions distinguish genuine concern for righteousness from strategic piety used to trap someone and hand him over to civil power.

Luke 20:21

21They asked him, “Teacher, we know that you say and teach what is right, and aren’t partial to anyone, but truly teach the way of God.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that the spies say true-sounding things about Jesus while speaking with a dishonest purpose. Respectful religious language is not the same as faith, and believers should examine the intentions behind their own questions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see flattery used as the opening of a trap. Jesus’ truthful and impartial teaching does not become less true because insincere people acknowledge it, but their words alone do not establish genuine allegiance.

Luke 20:22

22Is it lawful for us to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?”

Arminian

Arminian readers see a question designed to force a damaging answer about paying Caesar’s tax. The issue involves life under political authority, but Jesus will refuse the assumption that faithfulness must fit the trap’s two options.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an attempt to make Jesus vulnerable either before the people or before the governor. Both traditions recognize that the question’s hostile setting matters when applying the answer to civic responsibility.

Luke 20:23

23But he perceived their craftiness, and said to them, “Why do you test me?

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus discerning the deceit beneath a question that sounded respectful. His rebuke brings the moral issue into the open instead of allowing the language of inquiry to hide manipulation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus exposing the test before answering it. His knowledge and wisdom frustrate the plot, reminding readers that religious performance cannot conceal a hostile intention from him.

Luke 20:24

24Show me a denarius. Whose image and inscription are on it?” They answered, “Caesar’s.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus turning an abstract dilemma toward the coin actually used in ordinary life. The image and inscription invite his questioners to acknowledge the civic setting they already inhabit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the denarius supplying a concrete answer within the trap itself. The coin’s image helps frame a limited civic obligation, without establishing that Caesar has a claim over everything belonging to God.

Luke 20:25

25He said to them, “Then give to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God the things that are God’s.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus affirming civic obligations while placing them alongside the prior and greater claim of God. Views on church and state vary within the tradition, and the verse does not authorize obedience to every demand a ruler makes.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize a proper but limited place for civil authority under God. Reformed approaches to church and state are diverse, so the saying should not be treated as a complete political system or as giving Caesar ultimate allegiance.

Luke 20:26

26They weren’t able to trap him in his words before the people. They marveled at his answer and were silent.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the attempted trap fail before the people. Admiring Jesus’ answer is not yet the same as receiving his authority, a distinction that remains important when intelligence is praised without obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ wisdom leaving his opponents without the accusation they sought. Their silence reveals the failure of the plot, but the narrative does not say that their amazement amounts to conversion.

Luke 20:27

27Some of the Sadducees came to him, those who deny that there is a resurrection.

Arminian

Arminian readers see Luke identifying the Sadducees’ denial of resurrection before reporting their question. This supplied context explains the challenge without requiring additional claims about everything the group believed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the issue shifting from political authority to the reality of resurrection. Both traditions affirm resurrection and should let Luke’s stated point guide the discussion rather than adding an unverified portrait of the questioners.

Luke 20:28

28They asked him, “Teacher, Moses wrote to us that if a man’s brother dies having a wife, and he is childless, his brother should take the wife and raise up children for his brother.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the questioners appealing to an obligation meant to preserve a deceased brother’s family line. The appeal introduces their hypothetical difficulty; it is not yet Jesus’ account of marriage in the resurrection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an argument built from a Mosaic provision into a challenge about the coming age. Jesus will answer without accepting that present family arrangements define the limits of God’s resurrection power.

Luke 20:29

29There were therefore seven brothers. The first took a wife, and died childless.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the first death establishing the difficult case the questioners want Jesus to solve. This is a constructed scenario, so its details should not be treated as a report about an actual family.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the opening of a hypothetical argument meant to make resurrection seem unworkable. The story begins within present mortality, which Jesus will distinguish from the life of the coming age.

Luke 20:30

30The second took her as wife, and he died childless.

Arminian

Arminian readers see a second marriage ending in the same childlessness and death. The repetition builds the questioners’ puzzle rather than teaching that faithful people are protected from bereavement.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the example accumulating complications under the conditions of this mortal life. Its force depends on assuming that those conditions continue unchanged after resurrection, an assumption Jesus will challenge.

Luke 20:31

31The third took her, and likewise the seven all left no children, and died.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the repeated marriages carrying the scenario to its fullest extent. The increasing difficulty is meant to discredit resurrection, but it cannot establish what life in God’s coming age must be like.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sevenfold repetition sharpening the objection without proving its underlying assumption. Both traditions recognize that imagining an impossible arrangement does not limit God to preserving present social structures unchanged.

Luke 20:32

32Afterward the woman also died.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the woman’s death completing a scenario governed entirely by mortality. Jesus’ answer will offer hope beyond that pattern rather than explain how the same cycle continues indefinitely.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the final death closing the hypothetical case before the question about resurrection. The coming promise that death ends answers a deeper problem than the questioners’ proposed marital conflict.

Luke 20:33

33Therefore in the resurrection whose wife of them will she be? For the seven had her as a wife.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see the question assuming that resurrection must reproduce present marital claims. Jesus will challenge that assumption, so the dilemma should not be allowed to define Christian hope before he has answered.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an attempt to make resurrection appear contradictory through the seven marriages. Both traditions agree that the coming age is not merely the present age restarted with all its arrangements intact.

Luke 20:34

34Jesus said to them, “The children of this age marry and are given in marriage.

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus beginning with a distinction between this age and the age to come. Marriage belongs to present human life, and naming that setting is not a criticism of marriage or of those who enter it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus affirming the reality of present marriage while refusing to make it the pattern for every aspect of future life. The distinction prepares for transformation, not contempt for ordinary human relationships.

Luke 20:35

35But those who are considered worthy to attain to that age and the resurrection from the dead neither marry nor are given in marriage.

Arminian

Arminian readers see participation in resurrection life as God’s gracious gift, not a reward earned by marriage, celibacy, or moral achievement. They commonly connect receiving this life with continuing faith, while recognizing that the passage primarily answers a question about marriage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see being counted worthy as God’s gracious acceptance rather than an independent human qualification. They commonly connect this hope with saving union with Christ, while cautioning that the verse does not itself explain every aspect of election or the order of resurrection events.

Luke 20:36

36For they can’t die any more, for they are like the angels and are children of God, being children of the resurrection.

Arminian

Arminian readers see a life beyond death’s reach, in which God’s children do not become angels but share the freedom from death described here. The comparison answers the marriage puzzle without supplying a complete account of every future relationship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see resurrection life secured in a transformed condition where death no longer governs existence. Readers within both traditions differ over further details, but neither needs to turn the comparison with angels into the loss of embodied humanity.

Luke 20:37

37But that the dead are raised, even Moses showed at the bush, when he called the Lord ‘The God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.’

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus grounding resurrection hope in God’s continuing relationship with the patriarchs. Their deaths do not make his covenant faithfulness an empty title, so trust in the living God looks beyond the grave.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s enduring covenant relationship as the ground of Jesus’ argument for resurrection. The hope rests on who God is to his people, not on the ability of the questioners to imagine the mechanics of future life.

Luke 20:38

38Now he is not the God of the dead, but of the living, for all are alive to him.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see God’s living relationship with his people as stronger than death. Many understand the statement to include their present life before God, while readings of the intermediate state vary; it does not teach that everyone is finally saved.

Reformed

Reformed readers see death unable to cancel God’s covenant bond or his promise to raise the dead. Many also hear an affirmation of the patriarchs’ present life with God, but the verse’s main argument is resurrection, not a detailed description of the intermediate state.

Luke 20:39

39Some of the scribes answered, “Teacher, you speak well.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see some scribes acknowledging that Jesus has answered well. Their response reminds readers that religious leaders do not all speak with one voice, and recognition of a sound answer can be welcomed without assuming full discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see approval from some scribes interrupting a sequence of hostile questions. Both traditions should preserve Luke’s distinction between particular speakers rather than treating every religious leader as equally opposed to Jesus.

Luke 20:40

40They didn’t dare to ask him any more questions.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the questioning brought to a halt after Jesus’ answers. Being silenced is not necessarily the same as being persuaded, so the narrative leaves room to distinguish defeated argument from a believing response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ authority prevailing in the public exchange. The end of questioning clears the way for his own question about the Messiah, moving the discussion beyond the agendas set by his opponents.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
