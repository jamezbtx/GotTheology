Passage

Luke 20

Verses 1–18 · WEB

After entering Jerusalem and confronting the misuse of the temple, Jesus teaches publicly as leaders challenge his authority. His question about John exposes their unwillingness to answer honestly, and the vineyard parable brings rejected messengers and the rejected son into view. The rejected stone then becomes central, showing that human opposition will not have the final word. Both traditions affirm Christ’s authority, human accountability, and God’s vindication of the Son; differences in broader accounts of grace should not obscure the substantial agreement in this exchange.

Luke 20:1

1On one of those days, as he was teaching the people in the temple and preaching the Good News, the priests and scribes came to him with the elders.

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus continuing to teach and announce good news even as opposition approaches him in the temple. His public ministry offers a setting for honest response, but the leaders’ coming challenge will reveal their resistance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus exercising his teaching authority openly rather than retreating from the temple controversies. The leaders’ challenge does not interrupt his mission, and the presence of opposition does not make the message less gracious.

Luke 20:2

2They asked him, “Tell us: by what authority do you do these things? Or who is giving you this authority?”

Arminian

Arminian readers recognize that questions about authority can be proper, but their purpose matters. In this setting the leaders challenge Jesus after his temple action, and the exchange will expose their unwillingness to answer his own question honestly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the leaders questioning the source of an authority Jesus has already been exercising. Both traditions distinguish genuine inquiry from an attempt to discredit him while avoiding the implications of his ministry.

Luke 20:3

3He answered them, “I also will ask you one question. Tell me:

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus inviting the leaders into an exchange that requires their own moral judgment. He is not asking them to suspend thought, but to face the testimony that their question has left unaddressed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus taking control of the exchange with a question that uncovers its deeper issue. His answer will show that their difficulty concerns willingness to acknowledge divine authority, not simply a shortage of information.

Luke 20:4

4the baptism of John, was it from heaven, or from men?”

Arminian

Arminian readers see the question about John bringing divine initiative and human response into focus. If his baptism came from heaven, the appropriate response would have been belief rather than treating his ministry as merely human.

Reformed

Reformed readers see John’s ministry functioning as a test of whether the leaders recognize authority from God. Jesus’ question does not ask them to create that authority, but to acknowledge what has confronted them.

Luke 20:5

5They reasoned with themselves, saying, “If we say, ‘From heaven,’ he will say, ‘Why didn’t you believe him?’

Arminian

Arminian readers see the leaders recognizing the implication of a truthful answer while calculating how to avoid it. Their reasoning shows responsible resistance, warning readers not to protect themselves from a truth they already understand.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the leaders’ deliberation exposing a moral obstacle beneath their public question. They understand that acknowledging John’s divine commission would call their unbelief into question, yet they seek a safer reply.

Luke 20:6

6But if we say, ‘From men,’ all the people will stone us, for they are persuaded that John was a prophet.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see fear of the crowd replacing honest judgment about John’s ministry. The threat described belongs to the leaders’ calculation, not to a command from Jesus or an endorsement of violence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see political caution governing the leaders’ answer as much as theological conviction. Both traditions reject using the crowd’s imagined reaction as permission to harm those who disagree about religious authority.

Luke 20:7

7They answered that they didn’t know where it was from.

Arminian

Arminian readers see a profession of uncertainty used to escape the consequences of answering. The warning is not against admitting genuine limits, but against disguising unwillingness to respond as a lack of knowledge.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the leaders withholding an answer because neither available reply protects their position. Their response contrasts with the honest humility that would acknowledge truth even when it carries a personal cost.

Luke 20:8

8Jesus said to them, “Neither will I tell you by what authority I do these things.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see Jesus refusing to continue an exchange whose participants evade the question he has put to them. Patient grace does not require endless answers to inquiries used as shields against obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus judging the terms of the encounter rather than surrendering his authority. His refusal exposes the leaders’ evasion and prepares for the parable that will address their resistance more directly.

Luke 20:9

9He began to tell the people this parable: “A man planted a vineyard and rented it out to some farmers, and went into another country for a long time.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the vineyard entrusted to tenants rather than surrendered to their ownership. In the setting of the temple controversy, the story warns those charged with spiritual care that entrusted privilege calls for faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a picture of stewardship under an owner whose claim does not disappear during his absence. Both traditions can apply this warning to religious leaders without treating the vineyard as an excuse to condemn an entire people.

Luke 20:10

10At the proper season, he sent a servant to the farmers to collect his share of the fruit of the vineyard. But the farmers beat him and sent him away empty.

Arminian

Arminian readers see a rightful request for fruit met with violence. The tenants’ freedom is not innocence: their rejection of the servant makes them accountable for refusing the owner’s claim.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the tenants’ rebellion expressed against a messenger sent to collect what belongs to the owner. Entrusted responsibility is being used against the giver, exposing the seriousness of resisting God’s messengers.

Luke 20:11

11He sent yet another servant, and they also beat him and treated him shamefully, and sent him away empty.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the owner sending another servant despite the first rejection. This repeated approach displays patience within the story, while the tenants’ renewed abuse shows how resistance can become entrenched.

Reformed

Reformed readers see both the owner’s patience and the tenants’ deepening guilt. A further messenger becomes another occasion for accountable rejection, not evidence that the tenants have acquired a right to the vineyard.

Luke 20:12

12He sent yet a third, and they also wounded him and threw him out.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the third servant’s wounding as an escalation rather than an isolated misunderstanding. Repeated opportunities to respond do not make continued rejection harmless, and religious responsibility cannot excuse mistreating those who speak truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repeated violence establishing the justice of the coming reckoning. The owner’s patience is real, but it does not imply that opposition can continue forever without judgment.

Luke 20:13

13The lord of the vineyard said, ‘What shall I do? I will send my beloved son. It may be that seeing him, they will respect him.’

Arminian

Arminian readers see the sending of the beloved son as the owner’s costly appeal after repeated refusal. The hope for respect belongs to the parable’s dramatic presentation and should not be used to deny God’s foreknowledge of Jesus’ rejection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the beloved son as marking an advance beyond the servants and pointing toward Jesus’ unique place. The owner’s deliberation belongs to the story, not a claim that God lacks knowledge of what will happen to his Son.

Luke 20:14

14“But when the farmers saw him, they reasoned among themselves, saying, ‘This is the heir. Come, let’s kill him, that the inheritance may be ours.’

Arminian

Arminian readers see the tenants knowingly plotting against the heir rather than innocently misunderstanding him. Their plan shows a desire to possess what was entrusted, warning spiritual leaders against treating service as personal ownership.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the murder proposed as a deliberate attempt to seize the inheritance. God’s saving purpose does not make this intention righteous; the parable presents the tenants as responsible for their rebellion.

Luke 20:15

15Then they threw him out of the vineyard and killed him. What therefore will the lord of the vineyard do to them?

Arminian

Arminian readers see rejection of the son reaching its terrible conclusion outside the vineyard. The question about the owner’s response summons listeners to recognize that such violence cannot leave the stewardship unchanged.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the parable anticipating Jesus’ rejection and death while exposing the guilt of those who oppose him. The owner’s coming response means that the son’s murder is not the final word.

Luke 20:16

16He will come and destroy these farmers, and will give the vineyard to others.” When they heard that, they said, “May that never be!”

Arminian

Arminian readers see judgment falling on the violent tenants and responsibility being given to others. The warning concerns rejected stewardship, not permission to declare that every Jewish person has been rejected by God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a transfer of stewardship under the same owner, not the defeat of his purpose. Both traditions must distinguish the judgment on these tenants from claims of collective guilt or a complete account of Israel’s future.

Luke 20:17

17But he looked at them and said, “Then what is this that is written, ‘The stone which the builders rejected was made the chief cornerstone’?

Arminian

Arminian readers see rejection reversed as the despised stone becomes the chief cornerstone. Human refusal is serious but cannot prevent God from establishing Jesus as the one on whom his people depend.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God vindicating the rejected Son and giving him the central place. Both traditions affirm the certainty of this reversal, so the verse does not require a contrast over whether Christ will finally prevail.

Luke 20:18

18Everyone who falls on that stone will be broken to pieces, but it will crush whomever it falls on to dust.”

Arminian

Arminian readers hear a warning that resistance to Jesus brings ruin rather than lasting independence. The stone image presses the seriousness of responding to him; it need not be divided into a detailed sequence of separate judgments.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same Christ who is foundational to salvation bringing judgment on those who oppose him. The images emphasize the certainty and severity of that judgment, not a mandate for his followers to crush opponents.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
