Passage

Luke 9

Verses 51–62 · WEB

Jesus decisively turns toward Jerusalem, beginning the journey that will lead through suffering to his being taken up. A Samaritan village refuses hospitality, but Jesus rejects the disciples’ proposed retaliation and continues toward another village. Three encounters on the road expose the insecurity, urgency, and undivided allegiance involved in following him. The passage joins the Savior’s merciful purpose with a demanding summons, without permitting disciples either to coerce others or postpone obedience indefinitely.

Luke 9:51

51It came to pass, when the days were near that he should be taken up, he intently set his face to go to Jerusalem

Arminian

Jesus’ resolve introduces the journey toward Jerusalem with his being taken up already on the horizon. Arminian readers see purposeful, willing obedience rather than passive surrender to circumstance. His determination supplies the setting for the calls that follow: disciples are invited to join a mission whose direction he has already accepted.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the ordered movement of the saving mission toward its appointed fulfillment. Jerusalem’s suffering will not be the last word, because the narrative already looks toward Jesus’ exaltation. His settled purpose grounds discipleship in the Savior’s action before the text examines the readiness of those who would follow him.

Luke 9:52

52and sent messengers before his face. They went and entered into a village of the Samaritans, so as to prepare for him.

Arminian

Jesus sends messengers to prepare hospitality in a Samaritan village rather than avoiding its people altogether. Arminian readers see an approach that leaves room for genuine reception across a difficult communal boundary. Preparation for ministry includes practical arrangements, but the messengers cannot assume welcome merely because their sender’s purpose is gracious.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Jesus directs the advance party just as he directs the journey itself. Their task is preparation, not control of the village’s response. The Samaritan setting brings inherited tensions into the story, yet Jesus’ initiative refuses to make those boundaries an automatic reason to withhold his approach.

Luke 9:53

53They didn’t receive him, because he was traveling with his face set toward Jerusalem.

Arminian

The village refuses Jesus because his destination is Jerusalem, exposing a communal barrier to hospitality. Arminian readers emphasize that rejection here has a stated human reason rather than being a neutral accident. The narrative will not allow that refusal to become an excuse for the disciples to abandon the merciful character of their mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers recognize responsible hostility toward Jesus while attending to the limited explanation Luke supplies. The verse does not disclose the eternal standing of every villager. Their refusal becomes an occasion for Jesus to correct his own disciples, showing that true zeal must be governed by the Savior rather than injured pride.

Luke 9:54

54When his disciples, James and John, saw this, they said, “Lord, do you want us to command fire to come down from the sky and destroy them, just as Elijah did?”

Arminian

James and John interpret refusal as a reason to request destructive judgment, invoking Elijah in this WEB wording. Arminian readers see zeal that has lost touch with Jesus’ merciful purpose. The selected chapter’s footnotes do not flag a variant here, so the reference to Elijah is retained exactly rather than silently removed.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish confidence in divine power from authorization to wield it against opponents. Invoking a biblical precedent cannot substitute for submission to the Son’s present direction. The disciples’ question exposes their need for correction: sharing Jesus’ journey does not make retaliation an expression of faithful dependence on him.

Luke 9:55

55But he turned and rebuked them, “You don’t know of what kind of spirit you are.

Arminian

Jesus turns his correction toward the disciples rather than granting their request against the village. Arminian readers emphasize that religious hostility must itself be challenged by grace. This WEB text includes “You don’t know of what kind of spirit you are”; its chapter footnotes supply no variant note for that wording.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ exercising authority over his servants’ interpretation of zeal. Their proposed use of power misrepresents the mission he has determined to carry out. The rebuke warns that proximity to Jesus and confidence in a scriptural example do not guarantee that a disciple’s impulses reflect his character.

Luke 9:56

56For the Son of Man didn’t come to destroy men’s lives, but to save them.” They went to another village.

Arminian

Jesus states his saving purpose and moves on rather than destroying the people who refused him. Arminian readers see mercy guiding the treatment of resistant hearers, without turning their rejection into approval. The WEB includes this saying about saving rather than destroying lives, and its chapter footnotes do not flag a variant here.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Christ defines the purpose and manner of his present mission. His refusal of immediate destruction is consistent with the saving journey toward Jerusalem, not a denial that judgment is real. Moving to another village models patient obedience rather than coercion or a demand for instant vindication.

Luke 9:57

57As they went on the way, a certain man said to him, “I want to follow you wherever you go, Lord.”

Arminian

The volunteer offers apparently unrestricted loyalty while Jesus is traveling toward suffering. Arminian readers appreciate the willingness but recognize that a sincere declaration must encounter the realities of discipleship. The following reply tests what “wherever” entails, calling the speaker from enthusiasm toward an informed and durable commitment to the person he addresses.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish an outward promise of loyalty from the enduring faith that follows Christ through cost. Jesus does not simply collect enthusiastic adherents; he exposes their expectations to the truth of his mission. The verse itself does not reveal this person’s final response, so neither conversion nor rejection should be invented.

Luke 9:58

58Jesus said to him, “The foxes have holes and the birds of the sky have nests, but the Son of Man has no place to lay his head.”

Arminian

Jesus names the insecurity of his own earthly circumstances rather than promising the volunteer a comfortable place. Arminian readers see a call to weigh the cost honestly and trust him without guaranteed stability. The contrast with animals’ shelters exposes expectations of advantage, not a command that every disciple must always lack a home.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the humiliation of the Son of Man, whose authority does not appear as ordinary earthly privilege. Following him cannot be made a strategy for acquiring security or status. His words reveal the shape of his mission and direct trust away from comforts that disciples may presume religion will supply.

Luke 9:59

59He said to another, “Follow me!” But he said, “Lord, allow me first to go and bury my father.”

Arminian

Here Jesus initiates the call, while the hearer asks to put a family obligation first. Arminian readers see a genuine summons that confronts competing priorities and requires response. The verse does not explain whether the father has just died or whether a longer delay is intended, so the difficulty should not be resolved by guesswork.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the authority of Christ’s summons over even weighty human claims. The request reveals the tension between following him and retaining control of the timing. Luke supplies too little detail to reconstruct the family situation, but the exchange makes clear that discipleship is not a secondary project added after every other duty.

Luke 9:60

60But Jesus said to him, “Leave the dead to bury their own dead, but you go and announce God’s Kingdom.”

Arminian

Jesus’ startling reply sets announcing the Kingdom above the requested delay. Arminian readers hear an urgent demand for allegiance, not a general release from compassion or family responsibility. The contrast between the dead who bury and the one called to proclaim presses the unique claim of this moment without giving a full rule for every funeral.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand Christ’s authority to reorder obligations in light of the Kingdom’s arrival. The first use of “dead” is commonly understood spiritually, while the burial concerns physical death; the saying works through this contrast. It should not become an excuse for neglecting dependents under the banner of ordinary religious activity.

Luke 9:61

61Another also said, “I want to follow you, Lord, but first allow me to say good-bye to those who are at my house.”

Arminian

The third person offers to follow but attaches a prior condition, returning the issue to divided priorities. Arminian readers see that willing language can coexist with postponement of obedience. The request sounds modest in isolation, yet its place among these encounters asks whether the speaker will let Jesus determine the direction and urgency of discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the repeated tension between calling Jesus Lord and reserving a controlling “first” for oneself. The issue is not that household affection is inherently wrong. In this narrative setting, Christ exposes the tendency to subordinate his summons to another authority, even when the delay appears reasonable or socially expected.

Luke 9:62

62But Jesus said to him, “No one, having put his hand to the plow and looking back, is fit for God’s Kingdom.”

Arminian

The plow image calls for sustained attention to the course already begun, not backward attachment that frustrates the work. Arminian readers who affirm conditional perseverance hear a warning against turning away after beginning to follow. Fitness for the Kingdom involves enduring allegiance enabled by grace, not flawless performance or the purchase of acceptance.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand the image as exposing divided allegiance and distinguishing genuine discipleship from an unstable profession. God preserves true believers in a forward-looking faith that can include painful failures and restoration. The warning itself helps sustain perseverance; it is neither a license for complacency nor a demand that disciples never experience weakness.

Key contrast Arminian readings that affirm possible apostasy take turning back as a danger after genuine discipleship has begun; Reformed readings take final turning back as revealing a profession without persevering, saving faith.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
