Passage

Luke 9

Verses 1–17 · WEB

After displaying authority over storms, demons, sickness, and death, Jesus shares his healing authority with the twelve and sends them to announce God’s Kingdom. Their mission provokes questions about his identity even within Herod’s court. On their return, Jesus welcomes a crowd and provides food through the disciples whose resources are inadequate. Arminian and Reformed readers recognize Christ as the source of the mission and its provision, not merely an example of human resourcefulness.

Luke 9:1

1He called the twelve together and gave them power and authority over all demons, and to cure diseases.

Arminian

Jesus equips the twelve before sending them, so their ministry begins with a gift rather than personal achievement. Arminian readers emphasize that grace enables concrete service and calls its recipients to exercise what Christ supplies. The WEB footnote records the TR wording “his twelve disciples” instead of “the twelve”; this does not change the source of their authority.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s sovereign authority over the powers that threaten human life and his freedom to delegate it. The apostles act as commissioned servants, not independent possessors of supernatural power. Their appointment concerns a particular ministry here and should not automatically be treated as a statement about every member’s final salvation.

Luke 9:2

2He sent them out to preach God’s Kingdom and to heal the sick.

Arminian

Preaching the Kingdom and healing the sick belong together in this commission. Arminian readers see God’s gracious summons addressing people in their spiritual and bodily need. The messengers must actively proclaim and serve, while the saving reign they announce remains God’s work rather than the product of their effort.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that Christ determines both the content and the accompanying signs of this mission. Healing bears witness to the authority of the Kingdom being preached; it does not replace the message. This particular apostolic commission should guide reflection on ministry without becoming a guarantee that every later preacher will heal every illness.

Luke 9:3

3He said to them, “Take nothing for your journey—no staffs, nor wallet, nor bread, nor money. Don’t have two tunics each.

Arminian

The restrictions train the disciples to undertake this journey in dependence rather than protect themselves through accumulated supplies. Arminian readers see obedient trust expressed in practical decisions about money and possessions. The instruction belongs to this mission’s circumstances, not an automatic condemnation of planning or support for every later journey.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the sender will sustain the servants he commissions, often through the hospitality of others. Their lack of reserves makes that dependence visible. Christ’s specific directions shape this journey; the verse should not be converted into a universal requirement that Christian workers refuse ordinary material provision.

Luke 9:4

4Into whatever house you enter, stay there, and depart from there.

Arminian

Remaining in one house encourages stable relationships with those who receive the message. Arminian readers see integrity in accepting hospitality without continually seeking greater comfort or advantage. The missionaries’ conduct should support their proclamation, allowing hosts to participate generously rather than treating households as opportunities for personal advancement.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s provision takes the ordinary form of a welcoming household. Staying there restrains ambition and prevents ministry from becoming a search for better accommodations. The direction concerns faithful stewardship of support, reminding servants that the message, rather than their status, determines their task.

Luke 9:5

5As many as don’t receive you, when you depart from that city, shake off even the dust from your feet for a testimony against them.”

Arminian

Rejection is a real possibility even when the messengers faithfully obey Jesus. Arminian readers emphasize the hearers’ responsibility for refusing the gracious announcement of the Kingdom. Shaking off dust serves as a testimony, not permission for violence; the disciples must depart rather than coerce the response they seek.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish faithful proclamation from control over its reception. Christ authorizes a solemn witness against rejection, leaving judgment with God rather than demanding that the apostles secure acceptance everywhere. The warning upholds human accountability without explaining here the complete relation between divine calling and a person’s response.

Luke 9:6

6They departed and went throughout the villages, preaching the Good News and healing everywhere.

Arminian

The disciples put Jesus’ instructions into practice across the villages, joining good news with healing. Arminian readers emphasize responsive obedience: received authority becomes service rather than unused privilege. Their movement outward displays the generosity of the mission, bringing the announcement to people who have not traveled to Jesus themselves.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s ministry extending through the agents he has equipped. The success described rests on delegated authority rather than a technique the disciples devised. Word and healing together identify the mission as service under the King, not an independent effort to attract followers around the apostles.

Luke 9:7

7Now Herod the tetrarch heard of all that was done by him; and he was very perplexed, because it was said by some that John had risen from the dead,

Arminian

Herod hears reports but cannot make sense of Jesus through the explanations circulating around him. Arminian readers distinguish exposure to religious news from a faithful response to Christ. The suggestion that John has risen expresses public speculation; Luke reports it without presenting it as the truth about Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers notice that Christ’s work reaches the attention of political power without being controlled by it. Herod’s perplexity contrasts with the authority already displayed in the mission. Mere awareness of extraordinary events does not yield understanding, and the ruler’s confusion prepares the narrative’s sharper question about Jesus’ identity.

Luke 9:8

8and by some that Elijah had appeared, and by others that one of the old prophets had risen again.

Arminian

The competing suggestions place Jesus within Israel’s prophetic expectations, but they do not yet identify him adequately. Arminian readers see how genuine religious categories can still fall short of attentive faith. The narrative invites hearers to move beyond circulating opinions and listen to Jesus’ own disclosure of his mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish honoring Jesus as a prophet from recognizing the unique authority he bears. Elijah and the earlier prophets supply familiar categories, yet the following confession and transfiguration will carry the story further. These reported opinions are part of the narrative’s question, not Luke’s own answer.

Luke 9:9

9Herod said, “I beheaded John, but who is this about whom I hear such things?” He sought to see him.

Arminian

Herod’s admission about John joins curiosity about Jesus to responsibility for a prophet’s death. Arminian readers see a warning that wanting an encounter is not the same as repentance. The verse records a desire to see Jesus, but gives no basis for treating that desire as saving faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe the limits of political authority: the ruler who killed John cannot explain or contain Jesus’ work. His inquiry contrasts with the disciples’ growing knowledge of Christ. The narrative exposes curiosity without submission, so a wish to see signs should not be mistaken for spiritual understanding.

Luke 9:10

10The apostles, when they had returned, told him what things they had done. He took them and withdrew apart to a desert region of a city called Bethsaida.

Arminian

The returning apostles report to the one who sent them, and Jesus leads them away from public activity. Arminian readers see ministry joined to accountability and renewed attention to Christ. The WEB footnote says NU omits “a desert region of”; the selected text retains it while locating the withdrawal in relation to Bethsaida.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the apostles remain dependent servants even after exercising remarkable authority. Their return to Jesus keeps the mission centered on its source rather than its accomplishments. The withdrawal prepares for a fresh demonstration that Christ’s ability to provide exceeds the resources or expectations of his messengers.

Luke 9:11

11But the multitudes, perceiving it, followed him. He welcomed them, spoke to them of God’s Kingdom, and he cured those who needed healing.

Arminian

Jesus welcomes those who interrupt the withdrawal and addresses their need through teaching and healing. Arminian readers emphasize the generous character of grace: people are not dismissed because they arrive inconveniently. His welcome also provides the disciples with a pattern of responsive compassion rather than guarded concern for their own schedule.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the King exercising authority with mercy, not merely displaying power before a crowd. His teaching interprets the healings within God’s reign. The scene’s welcome is genuine, though the verse does not identify the final spiritual condition of every person who benefits from his earthly ministry.

Luke 9:12

12The day began to wear away; and the twelve came and said to him, “Send the multitude away, that they may go into the surrounding villages and farms and lodge and get food, for we are here in a deserted place.”

Arminian

The disciples recognize the crowd’s practical needs and the lateness of the hour, but their proposed solution is to disperse them. Arminian readers see understandable concern constrained by limited expectations. Following Jesus requires bringing real problems to him while remaining open to provision beyond the options his followers can presently imagine.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the deserted setting removes ordinary access to food and lodging from the immediate scene. The disciples assess the shortage accurately but have not yet reckoned with the provider beside them. Their limitation creates the setting for a sign grounded in Christ’s sufficiency rather than favorable circumstances.

Luke 9:13

13But he said to them, “You give them something to eat.” They said, “We have no more than five loaves and two fish, unless we should go and buy food for all these people.”

Arminian

Jesus gives the disciples responsibility without pretending their small supply is adequate by itself. Arminian readers see a summons to participate through trust and obedience when resources seem insufficient. Their reply honestly states the difficulty, preparing them to receive and distribute what they cannot produce through their own ability.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the command exposes the gap between human resources and Christ’s power. The five loaves and two fish will become sufficient through his action, not the disciples’ confidence alone. Their participation is real, but it remains the service of recipients rather than the achievement of independent providers.

Luke 9:14

14For they were about five thousand men. He said to his disciples, “Make them sit down in groups of about fifty each.”

Arminian

The crowd’s size makes the shortage substantial, while the instruction to organize groups makes service practical. Arminian readers see that reliance on divine power does not exclude orderly human action. The disciples are not asked to explain the miracle beforehand but to perform the concrete task Jesus gives them.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that Christ governs the distribution as well as supplies the food. Grouping the people prepares for an orderly gift rather than a chaotic scramble. The approximate count establishes the scale of provision without authorizing guesses about the total number of women and children not counted here.

Luke 9:15

15They did so, and made them all sit down.

Arminian

The disciples obey the instruction before the narrative describes the multiplied provision. Arminian readers find a picture of trust taking the next practical step rather than waiting until every difficulty disappears. Their action does not cause the miracle, but it places their service at the disposal of Jesus’ generous purpose.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the fitting connection between Christ’s command and the servants’ compliance. Seating the crowd is ordinary work, yet it belongs within an extraordinary act of provision. God’s power does not render their service irrelevant; it gives that service its direction and its dependence on the giver.

Luke 9:16

16He took the five loaves and the two fish, and looking up to the sky, he blessed them, broke them, and gave them to the disciples to set before the multitude.

Arminian

Jesus blesses, breaks, and gives the food, while the disciples distribute what they receive. Arminian readers emphasize grateful dependence on the Father and the privilege of sharing provision with others. The scene does not explain the miracle’s mechanics, but it clearly distinguishes Christ’s supplying action from the disciples’ serving role.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as the sufficient host whose gift reaches the crowd through appointed servants. His upward look and blessing locate provision within fellowship with the Father rather than autonomous human power. Connections with later meals may enrich reflection, but Luke here describes the feeding of hungry people.

Luke 9:17

17They ate and were all filled. They gathered up twelve baskets of broken pieces that were left over.

Arminian

The meal satisfies the people, and the gathered remnants show that the original scarcity has been overcome. Arminian readers see generous provision rather than bare survival, encouraging gratitude and responsible care for what remains. The bodily satisfaction is explicit; it should not be turned into a claim that every diner has entered saving faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the abundance of Christ’s gift beyond the disciples’ original calculation. Twelve baskets remain after everyone has eaten, showing that his resources were not exhausted by the crowd’s need. The miracle directs attention to the provider’s sufficiency without requiring an elaborate symbolic meaning for every basket.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
