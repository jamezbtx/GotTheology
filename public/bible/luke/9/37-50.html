Passage

Luke 9

Verses 37–50 · WEB

The descent from the mountain brings Jesus into a scene of a child’s suffering and the disciples’ inability to help. He heals the child, but interrupts the crowd’s amazement with another announcement that he will be handed over to human enemies. The disciples’ failure to understand is followed by arguments about greatness and a mistaken attempt to restrict another person’s ministry. Jesus teaches them to receive the lowly and recognize service in his name beyond their own immediate circle.

Luke 9:37

37On the next day, when they had come down from the mountain, a great multitude met him.

Arminian

The movement from the mountain to the crowd brings revealed glory into contact with urgent human need. Arminian readers see discipleship continuing in the world rather than remaining within a privileged experience. The multitude’s arrival prepares for an appeal that the disciples themselves have proved unable to answer.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that the Son’s majesty is not confined to the mountain scene. His authority will be displayed amid weakness and distress below it. The contrast exposes how little the disciples can accomplish apart from him, even after receiving remarkable privileges and earlier authority for ministry.

Luke 9:38

38Behold, a man from the crowd called out, saying, “Teacher, I beg you to look at my son, for he is my only born child.

Arminian

The father pleads for attention to his only child, making the family’s vulnerability unmistakable. Arminian readers see an appeal directed toward Jesus’ mercy rather than a demonstration of religious accomplishment. The WEB footnote explains “only born” as a Greek expression also translated “only begotten” or “one and only”; here it describes this human child.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the personal character of the appeal within a large crowd. Christ’s authority will reach one distressed household rather than remain a public spectacle. The father’s description of his only child heightens the sorrow, while the coming deliverance will direct attention to the compassion and power of Jesus.

Luke 9:39

39Behold, a spirit takes him, he suddenly cries out, and it convulses him so that he foams; and it hardly departs from him, bruising him severely.

Arminian

The father describes repeated violence that leaves his son physically harmed and the family desperate. Arminian readers attend to the boy’s suffering rather than treating him as an occasion for blame. Luke identifies a spirit in this episode; the account does not warrant identifying every seizure or neurological condition today as demonic possession.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the destructive character of the power Jesus will confront. The detailed description makes restoration concrete, not merely a change in social standing or religious interpretation. Recognizing demonic activity in this account need not erase ordinary medical distinctions or justify suspicion toward people living with similar bodily symptoms.

Luke 9:40

40I begged your disciples to cast it out, and they couldn’t.”

Arminian

The father has already sought help from Jesus’ disciples and received none. Arminian readers see a sobering difference between an earlier commission and faithful dependence in a particular situation. Failure should lead servants back to Christ rather than to self-protection, excuses, or blame directed at the afflicted family.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish delegated authority from power possessed independently of its giver. The disciples’ inability exposes their continuing need even after their earlier mission. Luke does not supply a technical explanation here, so the verse should not be used to prescribe a guaranteed method or fault the father for insufficient faith.

Luke 9:41

41Jesus answered, “Faithless and perverse generation, how long shall I be with you and bear with you? Bring your son here.”

Arminian

Jesus names the surrounding unbelief while still inviting the father to bring his son. Arminian readers hold together the seriousness of distrust and the availability of mercy. The rebuke should not be narrowed into an accusation that this suffering child caused his affliction; Jesus immediately moves toward healing rather than abandonment.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s patience within a generation marked by unbelief and distortion. His rebuke reveals the spiritual gravity of the scene, yet his command opens the way for deliverance. Mercy proceeds from his authority despite the inadequacy around him, not because the crowd has first demonstrated worthy understanding.

Luke 9:42

42While he was still coming, the demon threw him down and convulsed him violently. But Jesus rebuked the unclean spirit, healed the boy, and gave him back to his father.

Arminian

The violent convulsion does not prevent Jesus from rebuking the spirit, healing the boy, and restoring him to his father. Arminian readers see mercy reaching a family at the point of helplessness. The restoration is personal and relational, showing that deliverance serves the child’s good rather than the crowd’s appetite for spectacle.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the decisive difference between the demon’s destructive action and Christ’s effective command. Neither resistance nor the disciples’ earlier failure determines the outcome once Jesus intervenes. Returning the healed child to his father displays the gracious purpose of this authority: restoration rather than a display detached from human need.

Luke 9:43

43They were all astonished at the majesty of God. But while all were marveling at all the things which Jesus did, he said to his disciples,

Arminian

The crowd recognizes divine majesty in the healing, but Jesus uses that moment to instruct his disciples further. Arminian readers see that wonder must become teachable faith rather than remain admiration. His next words will challenge any assumption that such power excludes the possibility of suffering and rejection.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect the majesty of God with the Son who is about to announce his betrayal. Divine power and the coming humiliation belong to one mission, not rival accounts of Jesus. The disciples need revelation that interprets the miracle, lest public astonishment become the measure of what God intends.

Luke 9:44

44“Let these words sink into your ears, for the Son of Man will be delivered up into the hands of men.”

Arminian

Jesus insists that the disciples attend closely to the announcement of his coming delivery into human hands. Arminian readers emphasize the responsibility to hear unwelcome truth rather than select only comforting signs. The title Son of Man joins authority with vulnerability, exposing expectations that following him must lead directly to visible triumph.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus knowingly approaching the suffering already announced in this chapter. His delivery into human hands does not mean that the saving purpose has slipped beyond divine rule. The emphatic call to listen prepares the disciples to understand humiliation within his mission, even though they presently fail to grasp it.

Luke 9:45

45But they didn’t understand this saying. It was concealed from them, that they should not perceive it, and they were afraid to ask him about this saying.

Arminian

The disciples’ ignorance includes concealed understanding and fear of asking, so it cannot be reduced to simple lack of intelligence. Arminian readers recognize dependence on further divine illumination alongside their responsibility to seek understanding. The verse does not identify the concealing agent or pronounce a final judgment on their capacity to learn.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that privileged access to Jesus does not itself produce insight apart from revelation. The concealment underscores the dependence of understanding on God’s timing, while their fear remains part of the narrative. This temporary failure among disciples should not be turned into a complete account of reprobation or permanent spiritual blindness.

Luke 9:46

46An argument arose among them about which of them was the greatest.

Arminian

The argument about rank exposes how little the disciples have absorbed the announcement of Jesus’ suffering. Arminian readers see ambition as a temptation within genuine discipleship, requiring correction rather than indulgence. Their association with Jesus does not make every desire holy; grace calls them to relinquish competition for personal importance.

Reformed

Reformed readers recognize the persistence of pride among those granted extraordinary privileges. The problem is not simply inadequate information but a heart inclined to measure worth through status. Jesus’ coming correction addresses that disposition, showing that spiritual formation includes the humbling of servants as well as the enlargement of their knowledge.

Luke 9:47

47Jesus, perceiving the reasoning of their hearts, took a little child, and set him by his side,

Arminian

Jesus answers the hidden reasoning by placing a child beside himself, making a person easily overlooked central to the lesson. Arminian readers see a concrete challenge to status-seeking. Receiving his instruction requires attending to the vulnerable person he welcomes, not merely agreeing in principle that humility is desirable.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s knowledge of the disciples’ hearts and his authority to overturn their standards. The child’s place beside him gives visible form to the lesson before he explains it. Greatness will be measured by relation to Jesus and hospitable service, not by the prestige the disciples seek.

Luke 9:48

48and said to them, “Whoever receives this little child in my name receives me. Whoever receives me receives him who sent me. For whoever is least among you all, this one will be great.”

Arminian

Receiving a child in Jesus’ name becomes a way of receiving Jesus and the Father who sent him. Arminian readers emphasize active welcome toward those who cannot confer status in return. The least person is not an obstacle to ministry but someone through whom costly, humble allegiance to Christ becomes visible.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the reversal of worldly greatness grounded in Christ’s identification with the lowly. The connection to the Father gives this ordinary welcome theological depth, not merely social usefulness. The saying corrects the disciples’ rivalry without offering a formula by which hospitality earns salvation or proves every host’s spiritual condition.

Luke 9:49

49John answered, “Master, we saw someone casting out demons in your name, and we forbade him, because he doesn’t follow with us.”

Arminian

John reports excluding a worker because he does not belong to the disciples’ immediate company, although he acts in Jesus’ name. Arminian readers see the danger of confusing loyalty to Christ with control by one group. The report invites self-examination about whether concern for ministry masks an impulse to protect personal or communal prestige.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish the authority of Christ’s name from the boundaries of the apostles’ traveling circle. John’s criterion concerns affiliation, not an accusation that the man rejects Jesus. The scene warns against claiming ownership of God’s work, while leaving room elsewhere for genuine discernment about false teaching and deceptive claims.

Luke 9:50

50Jesus said to him, “Don’t forbid him, for he who is not against us is for us.”

Arminian

Jesus stops the prohibition and tells the disciples to recognize an ally rather than invent an enemy. Arminian readers emphasize generous recognition of service genuinely directed toward Christ. The statement addresses this particular worker; it should not be expanded into the claim that every belief or practice is equally faithful.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that Christ, rather than the disciples’ group interests, determines whom they may forbid. Work in his name cannot be dismissed simply because its servant is outside their company. The correction encourages humility and discernment together, not indifference to the truth of the message being proclaimed.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
