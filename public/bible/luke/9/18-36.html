Passage

Luke 9

Verses 18–36 · WEB

After the feeding, Jesus asks the disciples to identify him, and Peter confesses him as God’s Christ. Jesus immediately interprets that identity through rejection, death, and resurrection, then extends the pattern of self-denial to everyone who follows him. The transfiguration reveals his glory without canceling the journey to Jerusalem. Moses, Elijah, and the heavenly voice direct attention toward his saving departure and the need to listen to the Son.

Luke 9:18

18As he was praying alone, the disciples were near him, and he asked them, “Who do the multitudes say that I am?”

Arminian

Jesus’ question arises in the setting of prayer, inviting the disciples to distinguish public opinion from personal recognition. Arminian readers emphasize that faithful response requires attention to Christ himself rather than borrowed religious impressions. Prayer frames the inquiry, connecting the confession that follows with dependence on God rather than clever assessment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Luke’s placement of this decisive question alongside Jesus’ prayer. The disciples have witnessed abundant evidence, but its significance must be interpreted through Christ’s own teaching. The question prepares for a confession whose truth exceeds the uncertain judgments of the crowd and the perplexity of Herod.

Luke 9:19

19They answered, “ ‘John the Baptizer,’ but others say, ‘Elijah,’ and others, that one of the old prophets has risen again.”

Arminian

The disciples repeat the explanations already circulating in Herod’s hearing, showing that remarkable works alone have not produced clear public understanding. Arminian readers see the need to respond to further revelation rather than settle for familiar categories. Calling Jesus a revived prophet still falls short of the confession Peter is about to make.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the inadequacy of public speculation to disclose Christ’s full identity. The alternatives honor prophetic figures but do not grasp the person standing before the disciples. The repetition of these reports connects this private conversation with the wider narrative question about who Jesus truly is.

Luke 9:20

20He said to them, “But who do you say that I am?” Peter answered, “The Christ of God.”

Arminian

Peter answers the question directed to the disciples by confessing Jesus as God’s Messiah. Arminian readers see a genuine response to the revelation given through Jesus’ ministry, not a merely inherited opinion. Yet the next prediction shows that correct identification must grow into acceptance of the Messiah’s suffering mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that Jesus is the Christ appointed by God, not a leader whose authority depends on popular recognition. Peter’s confession is true even while his understanding remains incomplete. Christ’s following instruction will govern what this title means, preventing expectations of glory from excluding the cross.

Luke 9:21

21But he warned them and commanded them to tell this to no one,

Arminian

Jesus restricts the announcement immediately after receiving a correct confession. Arminian readers recognize that speaking truth responsibly requires attention to his direction and timing, not only enthusiasm. The following explanation of suffering suggests that the disciples still need formation before they can proclaim his identity without seriously misunderstanding its meaning.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s authority over the disclosure of his identity and mission. A true title can be distorted when separated from the death and resurrection he now predicts. The command therefore guards his revelation from being governed by the disciples’ expectations or the crowd’s preferred account of messiahship.

Luke 9:22

22saying, “The Son of Man must suffer many things, and be rejected by the elders, chief priests, and scribes, and be killed, and the third day be raised up.”

Arminian

The Messiah’s path includes rejection, death, and resurrection, not merely the recognition Peter has just offered. Arminian readers see God’s saving purpose accomplished through suffering while those who reject Jesus remain responsible for their actions. The necessity of this path does not make the rejection morally good or remove the summons to faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the divine necessity expressed by “must”: Christ’s suffering is not an accidental defeat of God’s purpose. Resurrection belongs to the same announced course as rejection and death. God’s plan establishes confidence in redemption without excusing the human authorities who participate in the injustice.

Luke 9:23

23He said to all, “If anyone desires to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me.

Arminian

Jesus addresses all hearers with a call to relinquish self-rule and follow his costly path. Arminian readers emphasize an ongoing, grace-enabled response rather than a single profession detached from obedience. The WEB footnote says TR and NU add “daily”; this edition omits that word, while the call still describes a life of discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand self-denial and cross-bearing as necessary expressions of faith produced by grace, not payments that purchase acceptance. Christ addresses the whole company, confronting empty profession as well as encouraging believers. God preserves his people through such commands, leading them to follow the suffering Savior rather than treat assurance as permission for self-indulgence.

Key contrast Arminian readers stress continuing, grace-enabled obedience as necessary to remain in discipleship; Reformed readers stress such obedience as the fruit and divinely sustained expression of saving faith.

Luke 9:24

24For whoever desires to save his life will lose it, but whoever will lose his life for my sake will save it.

Arminian

Jesus reverses ordinary calculations of security: preserving life at the cost of allegiance to him ends in loss. Arminian readings that affirm conditional perseverance hear a serious warning to continue in trusting loyalty. Losing life for Christ is not self-destruction or earned merit, but faith’s willingness to surrender what fidelity may cost.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish the self-protection that abandons Christ from the self-surrender that manifests saving faith. Those united to him are preserved in a faith that may endure profound earthly loss. The warning is one of God’s means of sustaining that allegiance, not a promise that outward profession alone guarantees life.

Key contrast Arminian readings of conditional perseverance understand final abandonment as a real danger for believers; Reformed readings understand persevering allegiance as the certain fruit of God’s preservation of true believers.

Luke 9:25

25For what does it profit a man if he gains the whole world, and loses or forfeits his own self?

Arminian

The question exposes the failure of gain that destroys the person who pursues it. Arminian readers emphasize responsible choices under grace: no earthly achievement compensates for turning from Christ. The surrounding call to follow him makes the issue ultimate allegiance, not a general prohibition of possessions or responsible participation in ordinary work.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus overturning the scale by which fallen people measure success. Even possession of the world cannot restore a self forfeited through refusal of the Savior. The saying presses hearers toward Christ’s saving sufficiency and warns that outward prosperity offers no evidence of reconciliation with God.

Luke 9:26

26For whoever will be ashamed of me and of my words, of him will the Son of Man be ashamed when he comes in his glory, and the glory of the Father, and of the holy angels.

Arminian

Jesus connects present shame over him and his words with his future acknowledgment of people in glory. Arminian readers who affirm the possibility of apostasy hear a warning against finally repudiating a faith once professed. Temporary fear should not simply be equated with final rejection, but the call to persevering allegiance remains urgent.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand settled repudiation of Christ as incompatible with saving faith, while recognizing that true believers can fail grievously and be restored. The returning Son of Man will expose merely outward allegiance. This warning summons disciples to the endurance God supplies, rather than making their courage an independent ground of salvation.

Key contrast For Arminians who affirm possible apostasy, the warning can concern a believer’s final repudiation of Christ; Reformed readers understand final repudiation as exposing a profession not sustained by saving faith.

Luke 9:27

27But I tell you the truth: There are some of those who stand here who will in no way taste of death until they see God’s Kingdom.”

Arminian

The promise assures some present hearers that the Kingdom will become visible within their lifetime. Arminian readers can see the immediately following transfiguration as a preview of that royal glory. The saying also stands within the wider horizon of Jesus’ resurrection and the Kingdom’s advance, so it need not be reduced to a failed timetable.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s certainty that suffering will not have the final word. The next scene grants selected disciples a sight of glory before the cross, while interpretations also connect the promise with later saving events. Its pastoral force is encouragement to trust the King whose power is not yet fully displayed.

Luke 9:28

28About eight days after these sayings, he took with him Peter, John, and James, and went up onto the mountain to pray.

Arminian

Jesus takes three disciples up the mountain to pray, about eight days after the preceding sayings. Arminian readers see privileged revelation joined to prayer rather than escape from the demanding call to discipleship. The interval links the mountain’s glory with his prediction of suffering and the promise that some will see the Kingdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Jesus chooses the witnesses and initiates the setting of disclosure. They do not ascend by spiritual achievement to discover what God owes them. Prayer again accompanies a decisive moment in Luke, and the coming revelation will confirm the authority of the one who has announced his death.

Luke 9:29

29As he was praying, the appearance of his face was altered, and his clothing became white and dazzling.

Arminian

The alteration of Jesus’ face and clothing reveals glory beyond ordinary human appearance. Arminian readers understand this as gracious confirmation of the one they are being asked to follow through suffering. The transformation occurs during prayer, but Luke does not present a technique by which disciples can reproduce it.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish the disclosure of Christ’s glory from a status conferred on him by the witnesses. They are receiving a revelation, not creating the significance of their teacher. His dazzling appearance prepares for the Father’s voice, which will establish why the disciples must listen to him.

Luke 9:30

30Behold, two men were talking with him, who were Moses and Elijah,

Arminian

Moses and Elijah appear in conversation with Jesus, connecting the scene with Israel’s scriptural story. Arminian readers see continuity rather than a rejection of God’s earlier dealings with his people. Their presence honors that history while preparing for the Father’s command to attend specifically to the Son.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the great servants of God gathered around Christ rather than competing with him for authority. The scene supports the unity of God’s saving purpose across the Scriptures. Their appearance does not authorize speculation about how the dead can be contacted; the initiative and disclosure belong to God.

Luke 9:31

31who appeared in glory and spoke of his departure, which he was about to accomplish at Jerusalem.

Arminian

The conversation concerns Jesus’ departure at Jerusalem, which the WEB footnote identifies literally as his “exodus.” Arminian readers see the coming suffering within God’s liberating purpose rather than as a diversion from messiahship. The glory on the mountain strengthens the call to follow the Savior toward, not away from, that costly mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Jesus will accomplish this departure, presenting the coming events as the fulfillment of his mission rather than circumstances that overpower him. The exodus wording invites attention to deliverance within Israel’s story. The verse does not require assigning a separate symbolic equivalent to every detail of the earlier exodus.

Luke 9:32

32Now Peter and those who were with him were heavy with sleep, but when they were fully awake, they saw his glory, and the two men who stood with him.

Arminian

The disciples’ sleepiness contrasts with the glory they finally see while awake. Arminian readers recognize human weakness even in a moment of exceptional privilege. Their experience is received rather than achieved, and it calls for alert attention to Jesus without suggesting that their earlier drowsiness has placed them beyond his gracious instruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe that the disclosure does not depend on the witnesses’ spiritual strength. Weak disciples are granted a sight of Christ’s glory and of the figures standing with him. Their limitations will continue to shape the narrative, making their need for divine instruction as important as the privilege of this experience.

Luke 9:33

33As they were parting from him, Peter said to Jesus, “Master, it is good for us to be here. Let’s make three tents: one for you, one for Moses, and one for Elijah,” not knowing what he said.

Arminian

Peter wants to preserve the moment through three tents, but Luke explicitly qualifies his speech as uncomprehending. Arminian readers see sincere enthusiasm needing correction by revelation. Delight in spiritual experience must not displace listening to Jesus or resist the movement toward Jerusalem that the conversation about his departure has just announced.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the inadequacy of even a privileged disciple’s spontaneous interpretation. Peter’s proposal places three tents alongside one another, but the heavenly voice will single out the Son as the one to hear. God corrects confused speech by revelation rather than allowing religious excitement to set the scene’s meaning.

Luke 9:34

34While he said these things, a cloud came and overshadowed them, and they were afraid as they entered into the cloud.

Arminian

The overshadowing cloud and the disciples’ fear mark the encounter as more than a conversation among admired teachers. Arminian readers see reverence replacing Peter’s attempt to organize the experience. God’s presence calls for receptive attention, and the next verse will supply the word that interprets what the disciples are seeing.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand the cloud as a sign of divine presence, placing the witnesses under God’s initiative rather than their own control. Fear is an appropriate narrative response to this overwhelming disclosure. The scene moves from human proposals toward authoritative speech, so its meaning rests on what God says.

Luke 9:35

35A voice came out of the cloud, saying, “This is my beloved Son. Listen to him!”

Arminian

The Father identifies Jesus as his beloved Son and commands the disciples to listen to him. Arminian readers emphasize obedient reception of this revelation, especially the teaching about suffering that they find difficult. Their response is not merely admiration of glory but attentive trust in the Son’s words and direction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Father himself establishing the Son’s unique authority over the witnesses. Moses and Elijah do not remain parallel sources of guidance beside him. Listening to Christ therefore includes receiving his account of the cross, allowing God’s testimony rather than human expectations to define messiahship.

Luke 9:36

36When the voice came, Jesus was found alone. They were silent, and told no one in those days any of the things which they had seen.

Arminian

Jesus remains alone after the voice, focusing the disciples’ attention on him rather than the departing figures. Arminian readers note their silence without assuming that they have already understood everything they saw. The experience must be interpreted within the unfolding journey, not immediately turned into public claims beyond their present comprehension.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the revelation culminates in the Son’s enduring presence with his disciples. The mountain does not establish a continuing council of three teachers. Their silence marks a period of withheld testimony, while Christ’s following actions and words will continue to form their understanding of his glory.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
