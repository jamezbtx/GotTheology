Passage

Luke 3

Verses 21–38 · WEB

After closing his account of John's public ministry, Luke presents Jesus praying at his baptism, the Spirit descending, and the Father's affirmation of the beloved Son. The genealogy then traces Jesus' human ancestry backward through David and Abraham to Adam and God. Both traditions affirm his unique sonship, true humanity, and mission with a horizon wider than Israel; the scene prepares for his testing as Son in the wilderness.

Luke 3:21

21Now when all the people were baptized, Jesus also had been baptized and was praying. The sky was opened,

Arminian

Jesus joins the baptized people and prays as heaven opens. Both traditions affirm his solidarity with those he came to save without implying that he needed repentance for personal sin; Luke highlights prayer as his public ministry begins.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the sinless Messiah identifying with his people rather than confessing wrongdoing of his own. His prayer and the opened heaven introduce the divine attestation that follows.

Luke 3:22

22and the Holy Spirit descended in a bodily form like a dove on him; and a voice came out of the sky, saying “You are my beloved Son. In you I am well pleased.”

Arminian

The Spirit visibly descends, and the Father declares his delight in the Son. Both traditions affirm the distinct Father, Son, and Spirit and Jesus' unique sonship; the scene commissions and reveals him rather than making a merely human person divine.

Reformed

Reformed readers share this trinitarian reading: the beloved Son receives the Spirit's anointing for his messianic work. The Father's approval confirms his identity and mission, not adoption into deity at baptism.

Luke 3:23

23Jesus himself, when he began to teach, was about thirty years old, being the son (as was supposed) of Joseph, the son of Heli,

Arminian

Luke gives an approximate age and distinguishes Jesus' supposed relation to Joseph from his divine origin already narrated. Both traditions affirm his real human ancestry without treating Joseph as his biological father.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the qualification about Joseph in light of Luke's account of Jesus' conception. His public ministry begins within a recognizable human family history, not apart from genuine humanity.

Luke 3:24

24the son of Matthat, the son of Levi, the son of Melchi, the son of Jannai, the son of Joseph,

Arminian

Matthat through Joseph continue the ancestry backward from Heli. Both traditions read these generations as part of Jesus' concrete human history, even where Luke supplies no individual biographies.

Reformed

Reformed readers share this grounding of Jesus' humanity in a family line. These names extend the genealogy without making a person's prominence or recorded achievements the measure of their place in it.

Luke 3:25

25the son of Mattathias, the son of Amos, the son of Nahum, the son of Esli, the son of Naggai,

Arminian

Mattathias through Naggai extend the line through people not otherwise described here. Both traditions recognize that the genealogy's testimony depends on continuity across generations, not on every ancestor being famous.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise receive these names as part of Luke's account of ancestry. The lack of biographical detail keeps the emphasis on the line leading to Jesus rather than speculative character portraits.

Luke 3:26

26the son of Maath, the son of Mattathias, the son of Semein, the son of Joseph, the son of Judah,

Arminian

Maath through Judah carry the line farther into Israel's past. Both traditions affirm the Messiah's rootedness in Israel without assuming that repeated ancestral names identify the same individuals elsewhere.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see continuity with Israel's history. Luke records these particular family links, not a general list of biblical heroes whose achievements establish Jesus' worthiness.

Luke 3:27

27the son of Joanan, the son of Rhesa, the son of Zerubbabel, the son of Shealtiel, the son of Neri,

Arminian

The names include Zerubbabel and Shealtiel, connecting the line with names familiar from Israel's restoration after exile. Both traditions see the Messiah's ancestry spanning the nation's upheavals and hopes.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the postexilic setting evoked by Zerubbabel and Shealtiel. Luke includes these links within the longer family history without pausing to resolve every genealogical question.

Luke 3:28

28the son of Melchi, the son of Addi, the son of Cosam, the son of Elmodam, the son of Er,

Arminian

Melchi through Er continue the backward movement toward David. Both traditions see the less familiar generations as integral to the genealogy rather than treating obscurity as spiritual insignificance.

Reformed

Reformed readers share the significance of this continuous human ancestry. Luke's list locates Jesus in a particular family history while withholding biographical claims about these individuals.

Luke 3:29

29the son of Jose, the son of Eliezer, the son of Jorim, the son of Matthat, the son of Levi,

Arminian

Jose through Levi preserve further links in the ancestry. Both traditions affirm that Jesus belongs to a real people across time; these names are genealogical links, not assertions that each ancestor held priestly office.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read these as family names within the line. The appearance of Levi does not itself establish a Levitical priesthood for Jesus; the genealogy is moving toward David and Judah.

Luke 3:30

30the son of Simeon, the son of Judah, the son of Joseph, the son of Jonan, the son of Eliakim,

Arminian

Simeon through Eliakim bring the list nearer to David's family. Both traditions see the cumulative force of the ancestry: Jesus' mission is connected to Israel's history rather than detached from it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize these successive generations as part of the Messiah's human lineage. The repeated names do not authorize assigning each person the biography of a better-known namesake.

Luke 3:31

31the son of Melea, the son of Menan, the son of Mattatha, the son of Nathan, the son of David,

Arminian

Luke reaches David through Nathan. Both traditions affirm Jesus' Davidic ancestry and messianic identity, connecting his human family with God's promises to David.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Davidic ancestry as fitting the promises associated with the Messiah. Luke names Nathan in the line, grounding that connection in a specific genealogy rather than a generic royal title.

Luke 3:32

32the son of Jesse, the son of Obed, the son of Boaz, the son of Salmon, the son of Nahshon,

Arminian

Jesse, Obed, and Boaz connect David's ancestry with the family story told in Ruth. Both traditions see God's saving purpose unfolding through ordinary households and the inclusion of Ruth in Israel's story.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the ancestry familiar from Ruth's conclusion. The line through Boaz and Obed situates David, and ultimately Jesus, within God's providential care across generations.

Luke 3:33

33the son of Amminadab, the son of Aram, the son of Hezron, the son of Perez, the son of Judah,

Arminian

The line passes through Perez to Judah, locating David's family within Judah's tribe. Both traditions see continuity with Israel's ancestral history, not a claim that ancestry alone gives every descendant saving faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the Messiah's historical descent from personal salvation by descent. These names connect the Davidic line to Judah without presenting every ancestor as morally exemplary.

Luke 3:34

34the son of Jacob, the son of Isaac, the son of Abraham, the son of Terah, the son of Nahor,

Arminian

Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham connect Jesus with the patriarchal promises; Terah and Nahor carry the line beyond them. Both traditions affirm promise fulfilled in Christ rather than privilege secured merely by claiming Abraham.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see covenant history within the genealogy. The return to Abraham recalls God's promise while the continuation beyond him prepares for the wider human horizon at Adam.

Luke 3:35

35the son of Serug, the son of Reu, the son of Peleg, the son of Eber, the son of Shelah,

Arminian

Serug through Shelah carry the ancestry beyond Israel's patriarchs into the generations after the flood. Both traditions recognize that Jesus' human connection reaches farther than the boundaries of one nation.

Reformed

Reformed readers share the widening scope of the genealogy. These generations place Abraham himself within an older human family, preparing the reader for the line's conclusion at Adam.

Luke 3:36

36the son of Cainan, the son of Arphaxad, the son of Shem, the son of Noah, the son of Lamech,

Arminian

The line reaches Shem and Noah, linking Jesus with humanity preserved through the flood. Both traditions see the Savior's ancestry extending through the shared history of the human race.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize Noah's place in the history of judgment and preservation. The genealogy continues beyond Israel, giving Jesus' human descent a worldwide horizon.

Luke 3:37

37the son of Methuselah, the son of Enoch, the son of Jared, the son of Mahalaleel, the son of Cainan,

Arminian

Methuselah through Cainan extend the line into the generations before the flood. Both traditions affirm the breadth of Luke's ancestry: the Messiah belongs to humanity's earliest history as well as Israel's story.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see these names continuing the movement toward humanity's beginning. The list emphasizes historical human solidarity rather than attributing special saving power to ancestral longevity or distinction.

Luke 3:38

38the son of Enos, the son of Seth, the son of Adam, the son of God.

Arminian

The genealogy ends with Adam, whose life comes from God, placing Jesus in solidarity with all humanity. Both traditions distinguish Adam's creaturely sonship from Jesus' unique sonship and see the setting for the Son's testing in chapter 4.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect the final reference to God with humanity's created origin. Adam's sonship is not deity; Jesus, already declared the beloved Son, enters humanity's history as its faithful representative.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
