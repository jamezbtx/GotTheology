Passage

Luke 3

Verses 1–20 · WEB

Luke dates John's prophetic ministry amid Roman and regional rulers and Jerusalem's priestly leadership. Isaiah 40 frames his wilderness call to repentance, baptism, and ethical fruit as preparation for the Lord. Ancestry cannot replace repentance; the coming Messiah gives the Spirit and brings judgment. Herod's imprisonment of John closes this account before Jesus takes center stage. Both traditions affirm grace, genuine repentance, and changed conduct, while differing over repentance's relation to regeneration.

Luke 3:1

1Now in the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar, Pontius Pilate being governor of Judea, and Herod being tetrarch of Galilee, and his brother Philip tetrarch of the region of Ituraea and Trachonitis, and Lysanias tetrarch of Abilene,

Arminian

Luke dates John's ministry within Roman imperial rule and the surrounding regional governments. Both traditions affirm that God's saving work enters public history, not an imagined setting detached from political power.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the named rulers as the historical setting for God's word. Imperial and regional authority provide the backdrop, but the decisive initiative belongs to God rather than the powerful.

Luke 3:2

2during the high priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas, the word of God came to John, the son of Zacharias, in the wilderness.

Arminian

Against the priestly leadership in Jerusalem, God's word comes to John in the wilderness. Arminian readers recognize God's initiative in calling the prophet who prepares people for the Messiah.

Reformed

Both traditions hear the language of prophetic commission: God's word comes to Zacharias's son. Reformed readers emphasize that divine appointment, not institutional prominence, authorizes John's ministry.

Luke 3:3

3He came into all the region around the Jordan, preaching the baptism of repentance for remission of sins.

Arminian

John's baptism expresses repentance directed toward God's forgiveness and prepares for the coming Christ. Arminian readers understand repentance as enabled by prior grace, not an unaided achievement or payment for pardon; water alone does not secure remission.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise unite repentance and forgiveness while denying that the outward washing automatically removes sin. John's preparatory baptism calls for a genuine turning to God, whose renewing grace produces repentance.

Luke 3:4

4As it is written in the book of the words of Isaiah the prophet, “The voice of one crying in the wilderness, ‘Make ready the way of the Lord. Make his paths straight.

Arminian

Isaiah 40 supplies the meaning of John's wilderness ministry: he prepares the Lord's way. Both traditions see God's promised coming to save his people fulfilled as John directs attention toward Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers share the identification of John as the prophetic herald. The road-making image announces the Lord's arrival and summons hearers to readiness rather than confidence in their existing religious position.

Luke 3:5

5Every valley will be filled. Every mountain and hill will be brought low. The crooked will become straight, and the rough ways smooth.

Arminian

The transformed landscape pictures obstacles removed for the Lord's coming. Arminian readers share the hope of God's saving intervention and hear a call to readiness; human preparation does not earn his arrival.

Reformed

Both traditions read the valleys, mountains, and crooked paths within Isaiah's promise of restoration. Reformed readers emphasize God's power to clear the way for his saving purpose, not a literal program of road construction.

Luke 3:6

6All flesh will see God’s salvation.’ ”

Arminian

The promise widens the horizon to all humanity, anticipating the gospel's reach beyond Israel. Arminian readers affirm God's saving provision for all without treating this announcement as a guarantee that everyone will be saved.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a worldwide horizon rather than salvation confined to one nation. All flesh seeing God's salvation does not itself assert that every individual receives final salvation.

Luke 3:7

7He said therefore to the multitudes who went out to be baptized by him, “You offspring of vipers, who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?

Arminian

John challenges crowds seeking baptism rather than assuming that participation proves repentance. Both traditions affirm the reality of coming judgment and reject reliance on an outward rite without a changed heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the severe address as exposing false security. Approaching the prophet for baptism does not remove the need for genuine repentance before God's coming wrath.

Luke 3:8

8Therefore produce fruits worthy of repentance, and don’t begin to say among yourselves, ‘We have Abraham for our father;’ for I tell you that God is able to raise up children to Abraham from these stones!

Arminian

Fruit must fit a genuine turning to God; descent from Abraham cannot substitute for repentance. Arminian readers understand this turning as a resistible, grace-enabled response leading into saving faith, with changed conduct displaying its sincerity. God is free to form Abraham's family beyond inherited privilege.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise reject ancestry as sufficient and require conduct consistent with repentance. They understand saving repentance as the fruit of God's regenerating work, inseparable from faith, with good works evidencing renewal. God's power, not natural descent, establishes his people.

Key contrast Both require repentance with fitting fruit and reject ancestry or works as the basis of pardon. Arminian readers understand saving repentance as a resistible response enabled by prior grace; Reformed readers understand it as the response effectually produced by regeneration. Both see changed conduct as evidence of genuine turning to God.

Luke 3:9

9Even now the ax also lies at the root of the trees. Every tree therefore that doesn’t produce good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire.”

Arminian

The ax at the root makes the warning urgent: religious identity without good fruit offers no shelter. Arminian readers see a real summons to respond to enabling grace, not an invitation to earn salvation through moral achievement.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the threatened judgment seriously. Good fruit evidences the renewal that mere outward membership cannot supply; the warning exposes empty profession rather than making works the purchase price of pardon.

Luke 3:10

10The multitudes asked him, “What then must we do?”

Arminian

The crowd asks what repentance should look like in practice. Both traditions see the movement from hearing the warning to seeking concrete obedience, rather than treating repentance as a feeling with no ethical consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the appropriate practical question. John's answer will specify the fruit demanded, showing that truthful proclamation addresses conduct as well as religious observance.

Luke 3:11

11He answered them, “He who has two coats, let him give to him who has none. He who has food, let him do likewise.”

Arminian

Sharing clothing and food makes repentance visible in care for neighbors in need. Arminian readers understand such generosity as grace-enabled obedience, not a donation that buys forgiveness.

Reformed

Both traditions affirm that love of neighbor belongs to repentance's fruit. Reformed readers see generosity as evidence of a heart turned from selfishness, not the meritorious basis of acceptance with God.

Luke 3:12

12Tax collectors also came to be baptized, and they said to him, “Teacher, what must we do?”

Arminian

Tax collectors also seek baptism and ask how they must change. Both traditions see the reach of John's call to people whose occupation raised questions of honesty and justice; they too must repent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that John addresses tax collectors as responsible hearers. Their question leads to a demand for integrity rather than an assumption that baptism leaves their ordinary dealings untouched.

Luke 3:13

13He said to them, “Collect no more than that which is appointed to you.”

Arminian

John forbids collecting beyond the authorized amount, making repentance concrete in financial dealings. Both traditions affirm that turning to God requires abandoning exploitation, not merely adopting religious language.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see occupational honesty as necessary fruit. John does not here require leaving tax collection, but he does require renouncing the unjust gain available within it.

Luke 3:14

14Soldiers also asked him, saying, “What about us? What must we do?” He said to them, “Extort from no one by violence, neither accuse anyone wrongfully. Be content with your wages.”

Arminian

For soldiers, repentance means refusing extortion and false accusations and practicing contentment. Arminian readers join Reformed readers in affirming that power over others must serve justice rather than greed.

Reformed

Both traditions recognize the same ethical demands on those able to coerce others. John addresses the misuse of their position and discontent that feeds it; baptism cannot excuse intimidation or dishonest accusations.

Luke 3:15

15As the people were in expectation, and all men reasoned in their hearts concerning John, whether perhaps he was the Christ,

Arminian

The people's expectation raises the question whether John is the Messiah. Both traditions distinguish a genuine longing for God's deliverance from correctly identifying the deliverer; John will direct attention away from himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the people's speculation as preparing John's testimony to Christ's superiority. The herald's influence does not make him the promised Messiah.

Luke 3:16

16John answered them all, “I indeed baptize you with water, but he comes who is mightier than I, the strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to loosen. He will baptize you in the Holy Spirit and fire.

Arminian

John's water baptism prepares for the stronger Messiah, who gives the Holy Spirit and executes judgment. Both traditions affirm Christ's superiority; the following image makes fire's judicial force prominent, while interpreters also discuss purification within each tradition.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the servant's water ministry from Christ's authority to bestow the Spirit and judge. John disclaims even the lowest claim to worthiness before him; the next verse connects fire with the fate of the unrepentant.

Luke 3:17

17His winnowing fan is in his hand, and he will thoroughly cleanse his threshing floor, and will gather the wheat into his barn; but he will burn up the chaff with unquenchable fire.”

Arminian

Winnowing separates grain from chaff, portraying the Messiah's saving and judging work. Both traditions affirm a real final distinction and the gravity of unquenchable fire; the good news does not cancel accountability.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ gathering his people and judging evil. The harvest image promises secure gathering of the wheat while warning that outward proximity to the harvest does not make chaff into grain.

Luke 3:18

18Then with many other exhortations he preached good news to the people,

Arminian

Luke calls John's message good news even though it includes warnings and ethical demands. Both traditions see repentance as part of preparation for God's saving visitation, not an alternative to divine mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hold together exhortation and good news. John's warnings serve his announcement of the coming Messiah rather than presenting moral reform as an independent way of salvation.

Luke 3:19

19but Herod the tetrarch, being reproved by him for Herodias, his brother’s wife, and for all the evil things which Herod had done,

Arminian

John's reproof reaches Herod's relationship with Herodias and his other evils. Both traditions affirm that repentance's demands bind rulers as well as crowds; political authority does not place wrongdoing beyond prophetic correction.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see John's fidelity in confronting powerful wrongdoing. The same demand for righteousness that shaped his public preaching now brings him into conflict with the tetrarch.

Luke 3:20

20added this also to them all, that he shut up John in prison.

Arminian

Herod answers correction by imprisoning John, adding another evil rather than repenting. Both traditions see costly prophetic faithfulness and responsible resistance to God's word; Luke closes this account of John before focusing on Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize Herod's guilt and John's suffering for speaking truth. This narrative transition does not mean that imprisonment disproves John's divine calling or that rulers can defeat God's saving purpose.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
