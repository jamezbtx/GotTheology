Passage

Leviticus 23

Verses 9–22 · WEB

When Israel reaps in the land, the first sheaf is waved before the LORD, and fifty days later a new grain offering of leavened loaves is presented with abundant sacrifices. The section ends by commanding that field edges and gleanings be left for the poor and foreigner. Both traditions share the New Testament connections of firstfruits with Christ’s resurrection and the later feast with Pentecost, and both join worship to justice.

Leviticus 23:9

9The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses about the firstfruits. Arminian readers see God anticipating Israel’s life in the land and preparing them to honor him there.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s instruction looking ahead to the fulfillment of his promise. Both traditions agree the new section concerns harvest offerings in the land.

Leviticus 23:10

10“Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘When you have come into the land which I give to you, and shall reap its harvest, then you shall bring the sheaf of the first fruits of your harvest to the priest.

Arminian

When Israel enters the land and reaps, they must bring the first sheaf to the priest. Arminian readers see gratitude acknowledging God as the giver of the harvest.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the land and its produce as God’s gift. Both traditions agree that offering the first portion confesses that the whole harvest belongs to him.

Leviticus 23:11

11He shall wave the sheaf before the LORD, to be accepted for you. On the next day after the Sabbath the priest shall wave it.

Arminian

The priest waves the sheaf before the LORD on the day after the Sabbath. Arminian readers see the offering presented for the people’s acceptance before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the sheaf offered on the people’s behalf. Both traditions often link firstfruits with Christ, whom Paul calls the firstfruits of those who sleep in 1 Corinthians 15:20.

Leviticus 23:12

12On the day when you wave the sheaf, you shall offer a male lamb without defect a year old for a burnt offering to the LORD.

Arminian

A year-old male lamb without defect is offered as a burnt offering with the sheaf. Arminian readers see the harvest offering joined to sacrifice, keeping gratitude tied to atonement and dedication.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the burnt offering accompanying the firstfruits. Both traditions agree that the unblemished lamb reflects the standard set in chapter 22. Gratitude and sacrifice go together.

Leviticus 23:13

13The meal offering with it shall be two tenths of an ephah of fine flour mixed with oil, an offering made by fire to the LORD for a pleasant aroma; and the drink offering with it shall be of wine, the fourth part of a hin.

Arminian

A meal offering of fine flour with oil and a drink offering of wine accompany the lamb. Arminian readers see the produce of the land returned to God in worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the full offering pleasing to the LORD. Both note the WEB footnotes giving approximate modern equivalents for the ephah and hin.

Leviticus 23:14

14You must not eat bread, or roasted grain, or fresh grain, until this same day, until you have brought the offering of your God. This is a statute forever throughout your generations in all your dwellings.

Arminian

No bread or grain from the new crop may be eaten until the offering is brought. Arminian readers see God honored first before Israel enjoys the harvest.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the priority of God in all provision. Both traditions agree that this perpetual statute teaches that the first and best belong to the LORD.

Leviticus 23:15

15“ ‘You shall count from the next day after the Sabbath, from the day that you brought the sheaf of the wave offering: seven Sabbaths shall be completed.

Arminian

Israel counts seven Sabbaths from the day the sheaf is waved. Arminian readers see the harvest period structured by counting, linking firstfruits with the coming feast.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the calendar connecting the two harvest festivals. Both traditions recognize that the exact reference of the Sabbath in this counting has been interpreted in more than one way, without making it a doctrinal divide.

Leviticus 23:16

16The next day after the seventh Sabbath you shall count fifty days; and you shall offer a new meal offering to the LORD.

Arminian

After the seventh Sabbath, fifty days are counted and a new meal offering is presented. Arminian readers see the completion of the counting leading to celebration of the wheat harvest.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the feast the New Testament calls Pentecost in Acts 2:1. Both traditions note that Acts 2 records the Spirit’s outpouring on that day, connecting the harvest feast with the gathering of the church.

Leviticus 23:17

17You shall bring out of your habitations two loaves of bread for a wave offering made of two tenths of an ephah of fine flour. They shall be baked with yeast, for first fruits to the LORD.

Arminian

Two loaves baked with yeast are brought as firstfruits. Arminian readers see the produce of daily life offered to God, including ordinary leavened bread. Everyday food becomes a gift to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the loaves presented as firstfruits to the LORD. Both traditions note the WEB footnote on the ephah, and avoid pressing the yeast into a single symbolic meaning.

Leviticus 23:18

18You shall present with the bread seven lambs without defect a year old, one young bull, and two rams. They shall be a burnt offering to the LORD, with their meal offering and their drink offerings, even an offering made by fire, of a sweet aroma to the LORD.

Arminian

Seven lambs, a bull, and two rams are offered as burnt offerings with grain and drink offerings. Arminian readers see abundant worship marking the joy of the harvest.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fullness of offerings appointed for the feast. Both traditions agree the sacrifices express dedication and thanksgiving to God. The harvest feast is rich in worship.

Leviticus 23:19

19You shall offer one male goat for a sin offering, and two male lambs a year old for a sacrifice of peace offerings.

Arminian

A male goat for a sin offering and two lambs for peace offerings are added. Arminian readers see atonement and fellowship included even in a joyful harvest festival.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see sin offering accompanying celebration. Both traditions agree that rejoicing before God still requires provision for sin. Even in joy, Israel does not forget its need for cleansing and peace with God.

Leviticus 23:20

20The priest shall wave them with the bread of the first fruits for a wave offering before the LORD, with the two lambs. They shall be holy to the LORD for the priest.

Arminian

The priest waves the offerings with the bread, holy to the LORD for the priest. Arminian readers see the priests sustained by the people’s offerings in God’s provision.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the offerings dedicated to God and then given to his ministers. Both traditions agree the arrangement reflects God’s care for those who serve at the altar.

Leviticus 23:21

21You shall make proclamation on the same day that there shall be a holy convocation to you. You shall do no regular work. This is a statute forever in all your dwellings throughout your generations.

Arminian

The day is proclaimed a holy convocation with no regular work, a perpetual statute. Arminian readers see corporate worship marking the end of the harvest period.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the feast established as lasting for Israel’s generations. Both traditions agree the day is set apart for gathering before the LORD.

Leviticus 23:22

22“ ‘When you reap the harvest of your land, you must not wholly reap into the corners of your field. You must not gather the gleanings of your harvest. You must leave them for the poor and for the foreigner. I am the LORD your God.’ ”

Arminian

Israel must leave the field’s edges and gleanings for the poor and the foreigner. Arminian readers see worship and justice joined, as harvest gratitude extends to the needy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see care for the poor rooted in God’s identity as Israel’s LORD. Both traditions note the repetition from Leviticus 19:9–10 and the example of Ruth gleaning in Boaz’s field.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
