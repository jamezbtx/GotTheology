Passage

Leviticus 23

Verses 23–32 · WEB

The seventh month opens with a day of trumpet blasts and rest, then the day of atonement on the tenth day, marked by self-denial, complete rest, and severe warnings. Both traditions read the day of atonement, detailed in Leviticus 16, as fulfilled in Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice according to Hebrews, and both caution against speculation about the day of trumpets beyond what the text says.

Leviticus 23:23

23The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses about the seventh month. Arminian readers see the calendar turning toward the autumn festivals with their own distinct emphases. The year turns toward its close.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God appointing a new set of observances. Both traditions agree that the seventh month gathers several significant holy days. The month is marked out as special.

Leviticus 23:24

24“Speak to the children of Israel, saying, ‘In the seventh month, on the first day of the month, there shall be a solemn rest for you, a memorial of blowing of trumpets, a holy convocation.

Arminian

The first day of the seventh month is a solemn rest, a memorial with trumpet blasts. Arminian readers see a call to remember and prepare as the festival month begins.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a sacred day announced with trumpets. Both traditions acknowledge that the text gives little detail about the day’s meaning and avoid speculative claims about its later fulfillment.

Leviticus 23:25

25You shall do no regular work. You shall offer an offering made by fire to the LORD.’ ”

Arminian

No regular work is done, and an offering by fire is presented. Arminian readers see rest and offering marking the day as holy to the LORD.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see worship prescribed for the day of trumpets. Both traditions agree that the pattern of rest and offering parallels the other holy convocations.

Leviticus 23:26

26The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses about the tenth day. Arminian readers see the introduction of the most solemn day in Israel’s calendar. Its seriousness is signaled by the warnings that follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God appointing the day that addresses the sins of the whole community. Both traditions agree that Leviticus 16 gives the fuller ritual for this day.

Leviticus 23:27

27“However on the tenth day of this seventh month is the day of atonement. It shall be a holy convocation to you. You shall afflict yourselves and you shall offer an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

The tenth day of the seventh month is the day of atonement, a holy convocation with self-affliction and offerings. Arminian readers see repentance and humility joined to God’s provision for cleansing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see atonement provided by God and received in humility. Both traditions read Hebrews 9–10 as presenting Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice as the fulfillment of this day.

Leviticus 23:28

28You shall do no kind of work in that same day, for it is a day of atonement, to make atonement for you before the LORD your God.

Arminian

No work is done, for it is a day to make atonement before the LORD. Arminian readers see the people ceasing their labor while God provides for their cleansing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see atonement accomplished through God’s appointed means, not human effort. Both traditions agree that rest on this day underscores that reconciliation is God’s provision.

Leviticus 23:29

29For whoever it is who shall not deny himself in that same day shall be cut off from his people.

Arminian

Anyone who does not deny himself is cut off from his people. Arminian readers see a serious warning that refusing humility before God excludes one from the community’s blessing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the gravity of disregarding God’s appointed day. Both traditions agree the penalty highlights the seriousness of approaching atonement with an unrepentant heart.

Leviticus 23:30

30Whoever does any kind of work in that same day, I will destroy that person from among his people.

Arminian

God will destroy anyone who works on that day. Arminian readers see the solemnity of the day protected by a severe sanction, calling for wholehearted participation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s holiness demanding reverence on the day of atonement. Both traditions agree that the warnings in verses 29–30 express the day’s unique gravity.

Leviticus 23:31

31You shall do no kind of work: it is a statute forever throughout your generations in all your dwellings.

Arminian

No work is to be done; it is a perpetual statute. Arminian readers see the repetition stressing how completely the day is set apart for God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the emphasis on rest as a lasting ordinance for Israel. Both traditions agree that later fulfillment in Christ, as Hebrews teaches, does not lessen the seriousness the day carried for Israel.

Leviticus 23:32

32It shall be a Sabbath of solemn rest for you, and you shall deny yourselves. In the ninth day of the month at evening, from evening to evening, you shall keep your Sabbath.”

Arminian

The day is a Sabbath of solemn rest observed from evening to evening, with self-denial. Arminian readers see a full day devoted to humility and dependence on God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a complete rest focused on atonement. Both traditions note the evening-to-evening reckoning the verse itself specifies, beginning on the ninth day at evening.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
