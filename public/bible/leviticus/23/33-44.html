Passage

Leviticus 23

Verses 33–44 · WEB

The feast of booths follows in the seventh month: seven days of offerings and rejoicing with branches, an eighth-day solemn assembly, and dwelling in temporary shelters to remember the wilderness journey. A summary places all these feasts alongside Israel’s ongoing gifts and offerings, and Moses proclaims them. Both traditions see joyful remembrance of God’s provision as shared ground, and note that Jesus taught at this feast in John 7.

Leviticus 23:33

33The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses about the feast of booths. Arminian readers see the calendar moving from solemnity to joyful celebration later in the same month.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God appointing the final great feast of the year. Both traditions agree that the sequence from atonement to rejoicing is significant within the chapter.

Leviticus 23:34

34“Speak to the children of Israel, and say, ‘On the fifteenth day of this seventh month is the feast of booths for seven days to the LORD.

Arminian

The feast of booths begins on the fifteenth day of the seventh month and lasts seven days. Arminian readers see a festival commemorating God’s care during Israel’s wilderness journey.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a feast of remembrance and thanksgiving. Both note the WEB footnote offering feast of tents or Succoth as alternative renderings. Joy follows atonement.

Leviticus 23:35

35On the first day shall be a holy convocation. You shall do no regular work.

Arminian

The first day is a holy convocation with no regular work. Arminian readers see rest opening the feast with corporate worship. Celebration begins with ceasing from ordinary work.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the sacred assembly marking the feast’s beginning. Both traditions agree that the pattern of rest matches the other appointed feasts. The holy day opens the week.

Leviticus 23:36

36Seven days you shall offer an offering made by fire to the LORD. On the eighth day shall be a holy convocation to you. You shall offer an offering made by fire to the LORD. It is a solemn assembly; you shall do no regular work.

Arminian

Offerings are made for seven days, and the eighth day is a solemn assembly. Arminian readers see the feast extended and concluded with a final holy gathering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the eighth day as a closing assembly before the LORD. Both traditions note that John 7 sets important teaching of Jesus at this feast, including its last great day.

Leviticus 23:37

37“ ‘These are the appointed feasts of the LORD which you shall proclaim to be holy convocations, to offer an offering made by fire to the LORD, a burnt offering, a meal offering, a sacrifice, and drink offerings, each on its own day—

Arminian

These are the appointed feasts, with their offerings, each on its own day. Arminian readers see a summary reminding Israel that all its holy times belong to the LORD.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the chapter’s structure reaffirmed in a concluding formula. Both traditions agree that the summary underlines the order God gave for Israel’s worship.

Leviticus 23:38

38in addition to the Sabbaths of the LORD, and in addition to your gifts, and in addition to all your vows, and in addition to all your free will offerings, which you give to the LORD.

Arminian

These feasts are in addition to Sabbaths, gifts, vows, and freewill offerings. Arminian readers see the festivals adding to, not replacing, Israel’s regular devotion. Special days never excuse neglect of ordinary devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the appointed feasts supplementing ongoing worship. Both traditions agree the verse prevents the feasts from being treated as substitutes for everyday faithfulness.

Leviticus 23:39

39“ ‘So on the fifteenth day of the seventh month, when you have gathered in the fruits of the land, you shall keep the feast of the LORD seven days. On the first day shall be a solemn rest, and on the eighth day shall be a solemn rest.

Arminian

After gathering the harvest, Israel keeps the feast seven days with rest on the first and eighth days. Arminian readers see celebration following the completion of the year’s labor.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see thanksgiving for God’s provision at harvest’s end. Both traditions agree the feast joins agricultural gratitude with historical remembrance. The end of the harvest becomes a season of worship rather than self-congratulation.

Leviticus 23:40

40You shall take on the first day the fruit of majestic trees, branches of palm trees, and boughs of thick trees, and willows of the brook; and you shall rejoice before the LORD your God seven days.

Arminian

Israel takes branches of trees and rejoices before the LORD for seven days. Arminian readers see joyful worship commanded, showing that gladness belongs to faithful devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see rejoicing before God as part of obedience. Both traditions agree that the feast models celebration as a fitting response to God’s goodness.

Leviticus 23:41

41You shall keep it as a feast to the LORD seven days in the year. It is a statute forever throughout your generations. You shall keep it in the seventh month.

Arminian

The feast is kept seven days each year as a perpetual statute in the seventh month. Arminian readers see the annual repetition keeping God’s faithfulness fresh in Israel’s memory.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a lasting ordinance for Israel’s generations. Both traditions agree that the feast’s regularity reinforces its role in teaching the community. Each year renews the lesson.

Leviticus 23:42

42You shall dwell in temporary shelters for seven days. All who are native-born in Israel shall dwell in temporary shelters,

Arminian

All native Israelites dwell in temporary shelters for seven days. Arminian readers see the experience of dwelling in shelters helping each generation share in remembering the journey.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a practice embodying memory. Both note the WEB footnotes offering booths as an alternative rendering for temporary shelters. Living in shelters lets the people feel the story rather than only hear it.

Leviticus 23:43

43that your generations may know that I made the children of Israel to dwell in temporary shelters when I brought them out of the land of Egypt. I am the LORD your God.’ ”

Arminian

The shelters teach future generations that God made Israel dwell in them after the exodus; he is the LORD their God. Arminian readers see memory sustaining faith across generations.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the feast grounded in God’s redemptive history. Both traditions agree that remembrance of deliverance is central to Israel’s worship and identity.

Leviticus 23:44

44So Moses declared to the children of Israel the appointed feasts of the LORD.

Arminian

Moses declares the appointed feasts to Israel. Arminian readers see faithful communication of God’s word as Moses passes on what he received. The people hear the calendar from the mediator God appointed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Moses as the mediator delivering God’s instruction. Both traditions agree that the chapter closes with the feasts proclaimed as the LORD’s appointed times, as it began.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
