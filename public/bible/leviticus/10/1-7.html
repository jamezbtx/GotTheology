Passage

Leviticus 10

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Immediately after the priests’ ordination and the fire from the LORD that consumed the first offerings (Leviticus 8–9), two of Aaron’s sons, Nadab and Abihu, offer “strange fire” that the LORD had not commanded, and fire from the LORD consumes them. Moses interprets the event with God’s word about being shown holy among those who come near him. Aaron is silent, the bodies are carried out of the camp, and the surviving priests are told not to mourn publicly while they remain anointed for service.

Leviticus 10:1

1Nadab and Abihu, the sons of Aaron, each took his censer, and put fire in it, and laid incense on it, and offered strange fire before the LORD, which he had not commanded them.

Arminian

Nadab and Abihu offer incense with fire the LORD had not commanded. The text names the fault plainly: unauthorized worship. Arminian readers note that the priests acted freely and were accountable, and that privilege, including having seen God’s glory at Sinai (Exodus 24:1, 9–11), did not exempt them. Both traditions refrain from claiming more about the fire than the text states.

Reformed

Reformed readers often cite this verse for the principle that God must be worshiped as he commands, not as people devise. The text specifies only that the fire was “strange” and uncommanded; proposed explanations about its source or the priests’ motives remain possibilities. Shared ground: unauthorized worship offends a holy God.

Leviticus 10:2

2Fire came out from before the LORD, and devoured them, and they died before the LORD.

Arminian

The same fire that accepted the offerings in 9:24 now consumes the two priests. Arminian readers see the seriousness of drawing near to God carelessly, and a warning meant to lead others to reverence. Both traditions read the judgment as just, while acknowledging the shock of its suddenness at the very start of Israel’s priestly worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the parallel with 9:24: the fire that signals acceptance also brings judgment. God’s holiness is life to those who approach rightly and danger to those who do not. Shared ground: God is not to be trifled with, and the event at the inauguration of worship establishes his holiness for all who follow.

Leviticus 10:3

3Then Moses said to Aaron, “This is what the LORD spoke of, saying, ‘I will show myself holy to those who come near me, and before all the people I will be glorified.’ ” Aaron held his peace.

Arminian

Moses interprets the event with God’s word: he will show himself holy among those who come near and be glorified before all the people. Arminian readers stress that the closer one’s calling, the greater the responsibility. Aaron’s silence is a striking response of grief and submission. Both traditions read this as the theological key to the passage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s glory as the ultimate concern: he will be honored either in the obedience of those who approach or in judgment on those who profane his presence. Aaron’s silence is submission, not indifference. Shared ground: those nearest to God bear the highest responsibility to treat him as holy.

Leviticus 10:4

4Moses called Mishael and Elzaphan, the sons of Uzziel the uncle of Aaron, and said to them, “Draw near, carry your brothers from before the sanctuary out of the camp.”

Arminian

Moses calls relatives, Mishael and Elzaphan, to carry the bodies out of the camp. Arminian readers note that the priests must not be defiled by contact with the dead during their service. Both traditions see order maintained even in tragedy, with the holy place cleared and the camp’s purity preserved.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the removal follows purity rules that keep death away from the sanctuary. The relatives are named precisely, reflecting the narrative’s careful record. Shared ground: the response is orderly and obedient, and the dead are treated with dignity while the holiness of the sanctuary is protected.

Leviticus 10:5

5So they came near, and carried them in their tunics out of the camp, as Moses had said.

Arminian

The bodies are carried out in their tunics, apparently the priestly garments they had just received. Arminian readers find this detail sobering: the signs of their office could not shield them from judgment. Both traditions note that the men obey Moses’ instructions exactly, in contrast to Nadab and Abihu’s uncommanded act.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priestly tunics, given for glory and beauty, now become their burial garments. Office without obedience offers no protection. Shared ground: the narrative contrasts careful obedience with the presumption that led to the deaths, reinforcing the passage’s call to reverence.

Leviticus 10:6

6Moses said to Aaron, and to Eleazar and to Ithamar, his sons, “Don’t let the hair of your heads go loose, and don’t tear your clothes, so that you don’t die, and so that he will not be angry with all the congregation; but let your brothers, the whole house of Israel, bewail the burning which the LORD has kindled.

Arminian

Aaron and his remaining sons are forbidden to show the usual signs of mourning, while the whole house of Israel may mourn. Arminian readers see the painful cost of priestly service: duty before God takes precedence even over grief. Both traditions recognize that mourning is not condemned; it is assigned to the people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priests’ consecration placing them under special obligations, so that public mourning would seem to protest God’s judgment. The people’s lament affirms that the loss is real. Shared ground: God permits grief, but the priests’ role requires that they not appear to contest his holiness.

Leviticus 10:7

7You shall not go out from the door of the Tent of Meeting, lest you die; for the anointing oil of the LORD is on you.” They did according to the word of Moses.

Arminian

The priests must stay at the Tent of Meeting because the anointing oil is on them, and they obey. Arminian readers note that obedience follows despite grief, showing a renewed commitment to serve rightly. Both traditions see the anointing as setting them apart in a way that governs even their response to family tragedy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the anointing as signifying consecration that places the priests wholly at God’s disposal. Their obedience contrasts with the earlier disobedience. Shared ground: the priests continue their service faithfully, and the narrative closes the episode with the restoration of order under God’s word.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
