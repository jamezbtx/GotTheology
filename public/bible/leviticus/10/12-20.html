Passage

Leviticus 10

Verses 12–20 · WEB

Moses instructs Aaron and his surviving sons about eating their priestly portions from the offerings. When he finds that the goat of the sin offering has been burned rather than eaten in the holy place, he is angry with Eleazar and Ithamar. Aaron responds by pointing to the day’s grief and asking whether eating it would have pleased the LORD, and Moses accepts his answer. The scene shows the law administered with discernment in a time of sorrow.

Leviticus 10:12

12Moses spoke to Aaron, and to Eleazar and to Ithamar, his sons who were left, “Take the meal offering that remains of the offerings of the LORD made by fire, and eat it without yeast beside the altar; for it is most holy;

Arminian

Moses tells Aaron and his surviving sons to eat the remaining meal offering beside the altar, for it is most holy. Arminian readers note that the priests’ ministry continues despite tragedy. Both traditions see God’s provision for the priests even in a time of grief.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the instruction restores the regular order of worship after the judgment. The offering must be eaten without yeast in the holy area. Shared ground: the priestly portions are a gift from God, and the instructions guard the holiness of what is most holy.

Leviticus 10:13

13and you shall eat it in a holy place, because it is your portion, and your sons’ portion, of the offerings of the LORD made by fire; for so I am commanded.

Arminian

The meal offering is to be eaten in a holy place as the priests’ portion. Arminian readers see God’s care in providing for those who serve. Both traditions note that Moses emphasizes he is following God’s command, reinforcing the passage’s theme of obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priests’ portion as part of God’s ordered provision for ministry, later echoed in New Testament teaching on supporting ministers (1 Corinthians 9:13). Shared ground: the food belongs to God and is given to the priests, who must handle it in the place and manner he prescribes.

Leviticus 10:14

14The waved breast and the heaved thigh you shall eat in a clean place, you, and your sons, and your daughters with you: for they are given as your portion, and your sons’ portion, out of the sacrifices of the peace offerings of the children of Israel.

Arminian

The breast and thigh from peace offerings may be eaten in a clean place by the priests and their families, including daughters. Arminian readers note the inclusion of the household in God’s provision. Both traditions see a distinction between most holy portions and holy portions shared with families.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the graded holiness of different offerings: some are restricted to the holy place, others can be eaten in any clean place. Shared ground: God’s provision extends to the priests’ households, and the distinctions protect the holiness of what is offered.

Leviticus 10:15

15They shall bring the heaved thigh and the waved breast with the offerings made by fire of the fat, to wave it for a wave offering before the LORD. It shall be yours, and your sons’ with you, as a portion forever, as the LORD has commanded.”

Arminian

The thigh and breast are waved before the LORD and then belong to the priests as a lasting portion. Arminian readers see a relationship of gift and response: offerings are presented to God, who gives them back to his servants. Both traditions agree that the priests’ share is by God’s command.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the wave offering as acknowledging God’s ownership before the portion is received. The perpetual statute grounds the priests’ livelihood in God’s provision. Shared ground: what is offered to God is received back as his gift, and the command secures the priests’ share.

Leviticus 10:16

16Moses diligently inquired about the goat of the sin offering, and, behold, it was burned. He was angry with Eleazar and with Ithamar, the sons of Aaron who were left, saying,

Arminian

Moses discovers that the goat of the sin offering was burned rather than eaten, and he is angry. The WEB footnote explains that “behold” translates a Hebrew word calling for attention. Arminian readers note Moses’ zeal for obedience after what has just happened. Both traditions read his concern as understandable.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses’ anger as zeal for God’s commands, heightened by the recent judgment. The WEB footnote on “behold” marks the moment of discovery. Shared ground: Moses is concerned that a deviation from God’s instructions might bring further judgment on the priesthood.

Leviticus 10:17

17“Why haven’t you eaten the sin offering in the place of the sanctuary, since it is most holy, and he has given it to you to bear the iniquity of the congregation, to make atonement for them before the LORD?

Arminian

Moses explains that the sin offering was given to the priests to bear the iniquity of the congregation and make atonement. Arminian readers see priestly mediation as God’s provision for the whole people. Both traditions note that eating the offering was part of the atoning ritual, though its exact significance is not spelled out.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priests bearing iniquity as representative mediators, a theme Hebrews develops in relation to Christ. The precise relation of eating to atonement is not explained in the text. Shared ground: the sin offering is a God-given means of atonement, and the priests’ role in it is serious.

Leviticus 10:18

18Behold, its blood was not brought into the inner part of the sanctuary. You certainly should have eaten it in the sanctuary, as I commanded.”

Arminian

Moses notes that the blood was not brought into the inner sanctuary, so the meat should have been eaten in the holy place as commanded. Arminian readers see attention to the specific rules governing different sin offerings. Both traditions agree that Moses’ objection rests on the prescribed procedure (compare 6:30).

Reformed

Reformed readers note the distinction between sin offerings whose blood enters the sanctuary, which are burned, and those whose blood does not, which are eaten (6:26, 30). Moses applies this rule. Shared ground: the procedure matters because God has prescribed it.

Leviticus 10:19

19Aaron spoke to Moses, “Behold, today they have offered their sin offering and their burnt offering before the LORD; and such things as these have happened to me. If I had eaten the sin offering today, would it have been pleasing in the LORD’s sight?”

Arminian

Aaron responds by pointing to the day’s events and asking whether eating the sin offering would have pleased the LORD. Arminian readers see a sincere heart concerned with God’s approval rather than mere form. Both traditions note that Aaron’s reasoning is not fully spelled out, and interpreters offer several explanations.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Aaron weighing the propriety of eating a most holy portion in a day of judgment and grief. The exact logic is debated. Shared ground: Aaron’s concern is to please God, not to ignore the law, and his question reflects reverent discernment.

Leviticus 10:20

20When Moses heard that, it was pleasing in his sight.

Arminian

Moses accepts Aaron’s explanation. Arminian readers see that God’s law is applied with wisdom and attention to the heart, not mechanically. Both traditions note that the chapter ends not with further judgment but with Moses satisfied, contrasting sharply with its opening.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the episode as showing that reverent intent and sound judgment matter in applying God’s law. Moses’ acceptance indicates that Aaron’s decision was not rebellion. Shared ground: the chapter moves from presumptuous worship to reverent discernment, and Moses recognizes the difference.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
