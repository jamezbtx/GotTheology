Passage

Leviticus 14

Verses 21–32 · WEB

This section adapts the restoration rite for someone who is poor. A single lamb, smaller amounts of flour and oil, and two birds take the place of the more costly animals, while every essential step of cleansing, consecration, and atonement remains the same. The repeated phrase about what the person can afford shows God’s care that poverty should not bar anyone from returning to worship and community, a point both traditions share.

Leviticus 14:21

21“If he is poor, and can’t afford so much, then he shall take one male lamb for a trespass offering to be waved, to make atonement for him, and one tenth of an ephah of fine flour mixed with oil for a meal offering, and a log of oil;

Arminian

If the person is poor, a smaller offering is allowed: one lamb, less flour, and oil. God makes restoration affordable.

Arminian readers see grace that does not exclude the poor from coming back. Access to God does not depend on wealth.

Reformed

The law adjusts the cost for the poor. The ephah and log are again measures of quantity.

Reformed readers see God’s care for the vulnerable built into worship. Both traditions read this generosity as shared ground.

Leviticus 14:22

22and two turtledoves, or two young pigeons, such as he is able to afford; and the one shall be a sin offering, and the other a burnt offering.

Arminian

Two turtledoves or young pigeons replace the larger animals, as the person can afford. The phrase ‘such as he is able to afford’ is repeated in the passage.

Arminian readers see God meeting people where they are. A similar provision for the poor appears in Leviticus 12:8, which Luke 2:24 echoes for Mary and Joseph.

Reformed

Birds serve for the sin and burnt offerings. The rite is the same in meaning, smaller in cost.

Reformed readers see that the value of the offering lies in God’s appointment, not its price. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:23

23“On the eighth day he shall bring them for his cleansing to the priest, to the door of the Tent of Meeting, before the LORD.

Arminian

On the eighth day the poor person brings the offerings to the priest at the Tent of Meeting before the LORD. The same day and place apply.

Arminian readers see equal dignity for rich and poor before God.

Reformed

The poor person comes to the same entrance, on the same day, as the wealthier. There is no second-class restoration.

Reformed readers see one way of access for all God’s people. Both traditions agree.

Leviticus 14:24

24The priest shall take the lamb of the trespass offering, and the log of oil, and the priest shall wave them for a wave offering before the LORD.

Arminian

The priest waves the lamb and oil before the LORD. The rite mirrors the fuller version.

Arminian readers see that a smaller offering is fully accepted. God does not reduce the welcome.

Reformed

The wave offering is presented in the same way. The essentials remain.

Reformed readers see continuity in God’s ordered worship. The poor person’s offering is presented exactly as the wealthier person’s is. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:25

25He shall kill the lamb of the trespass offering. The priest shall take some of the blood of the trespass offering and put it on the tip of the right ear of him who is to be cleansed, and on the thumb of his right hand, and on the big toe of his right foot.

Arminian

The lamb is killed, and its blood placed on ear, thumb, and toe. The consecration is identical.

Arminian readers see the whole person restored, regardless of means. Ear, hand, and foot are marked just as in verse 14.

Reformed

The same marking with blood follows. Poverty does not lessen the consecration.

Reformed readers see God’s grace applied equally. The consecration is not scaled down with the cost of the animals. The meaning of the rite stays whole. Both traditions agree.

Leviticus 14:26

26The priest shall pour some of the oil into the palm of his own left hand;

Arminian

The priest pours oil into his left palm. The pattern repeats verse 15.

Arminian readers see that the poor person receives every part of the rite. The text repeats every step rather than summarising it.

Reformed

The repetition stresses that nothing is omitted. Each step is the same.

Reformed readers see faithful repetition of God’s instruction. The repetition itself teaches that the poor lose nothing. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:27

27and the priest shall sprinkle with his right finger some of the oil that is in his left hand seven times before the LORD.

Arminian

The priest sprinkles oil seven times before the LORD. God is honored first, as before.

Arminian readers see worship directed toward God for every restored person. No part of the rite is shortened for the poor.

Reformed

The sevenfold sprinkling repeats the earlier rite. Completeness is signalled again.

Reformed readers see the same divine order for every case. The oil is dedicated to God before it touches the person. Both traditions agree.

Leviticus 14:28

28Then the priest shall put some of the oil that is in his hand on the tip of the right ear of him who is to be cleansed, and on the thumb of his right hand, and on the big toe of his right foot, on the place of the blood of the trespass offering.

Arminian

Oil is placed on ear, thumb, and toe, on the place of the blood. Consecration follows cleansing.

Arminian readers see the poor person fully set apart for life with God.

Reformed

The oil is laid over the blood as before. The sequence is unchanged.

Reformed readers see the pattern of cleansing then consecration. The poor person is consecrated in the same way. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:29

29The rest of the oil that is in the priest’s hand he shall put on the head of him who is to be cleansed, to make atonement for him before the LORD.

Arminian

The rest of the oil goes on the head to make atonement before the LORD. Atonement is the same for rich and poor.

Arminian readers see equal welcome before God.

Reformed

The anointing of the head completes the oil rite. Atonement is made before the LORD.

Reformed readers see God’s provision suited to each person’s means. Nothing about the result depends on wealth. Both traditions agree.

Leviticus 14:30

30He shall offer one of the turtledoves, or of the young pigeons, which ever he is able to afford,

Arminian

One turtledove or pigeon is offered, whichever the person can afford. The phrase about affordability recurs.

Arminian readers hear God’s patience with limited means. The offering fits the person, not a fixed price.

Reformed

The offering is matched to what the person has. The text repeats this point deliberately.

Reformed readers see God’s kindness in not demanding the impossible. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:31

31of the kind he is able to afford, the one for a sin offering, and the other for a burnt offering, with the meal offering. The priest shall make atonement for him who is to be cleansed before the LORD.”

Arminian

One bird for a sin offering, the other for a burnt offering, with the meal offering; the priest makes atonement. The rite is complete.

Arminian readers see full restoration available to all.

Reformed

The two birds fill the roles of the larger animals. Atonement is made before the LORD.

Reformed readers see sufficiency in what God appoints. The smaller offering achieves the same restoration. Both traditions agree.

Leviticus 14:32

32This is the law for him in whom is the plague of leprosy, who is not able to afford the sacrifice for his cleansing.

Arminian

This is the law for the person with skin disease who cannot afford the full sacrifice. The summary names the poor directly.

Arminian readers see that God thought of the poor in advance. Christians often connect this with Jesus’ welcome of the poor and excluded.

Reformed

The closing summary makes the provision official. No one is excluded by poverty.

Reformed readers see God’s law showing compassion. The law names the poor so that no one doubts their welcome. Both traditions share this reading.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
