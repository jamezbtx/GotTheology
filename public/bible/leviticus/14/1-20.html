Passage

Leviticus 14

Verses 1–20 · WEB

Leviticus 14 follows the diagnosis of skin diseases in chapter 13 with the rites for restoring someone who has been healed. The priest goes outside the camp, performs a rite with two birds, and the person washes, shaves, and returns in stages, ending with eighth-day offerings before the LORD. Ritual uncleanness here is not moral sin, and the process welcomes the excluded person back into worship and community. Arminian and Reformed readers share nearly all of this, including cautious Christological readings of sacrifice and cleansing.

Leviticus 14:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses again, now about restoring someone whose skin disease has healed. Chapter 13 diagnosed; chapter 14 restores.

Arminian readers see God’s care for those excluded from community. The law provides a real way back, not just a verdict.

Reformed

God’s word governs both diagnosis and restoration. The same LORD who set the boundaries also opens the way home.

Reformed readers see God’s gracious provision built into the law. Both traditions read this as shared ground.

Leviticus 14:2

2“This shall be the law of the leper in the day of his cleansing: He shall be brought to the priest,

Arminian

This is the law for the day of cleansing: the person is brought to the priest. ‘Leprosy’ here covers various skin conditions, likely not only what is now called Hansen’s disease.

Arminian readers note that the person comes forward to be restored. Being unclean was a ritual condition, not a moral verdict.

Reformed

The priest is the appointed mediator of restoration. The term translated leprosy probably covers several conditions.

Reformed readers stress that ritual impurity is not the same as sin. Both traditions see the rite as God’s ordered way of welcoming the healed back.

Leviticus 14:3

3and the priest shall go out of the camp. The priest shall examine him. Behold, if the plague of leprosy is healed in the leper,

Arminian

The priest goes out of the camp to examine the person. Restoration begins where the excluded person lives.

Arminian readers see a picture of God’s representative going to meet the outsider. The priest confirms healing; he does not cause it.

Reformed

The priest leaves the camp to inspect. Healing has already happened; the rite recognises and declares it.

Reformed readers see God as the healer and the priest as his witness. Both traditions note how Jesus later sends a healed man to the priest (Mark 1:44).

Leviticus 14:4

4then the priest shall command them to take for him who is to be cleansed two living clean birds, cedar wood, scarlet, and hyssop.

Arminian

Two clean birds, cedar wood, scarlet, and hyssop are gathered for the person. The materials are simple and specific.

Arminian readers see God providing the means of cleansing. The person does not invent the rite; it is given.

Reformed

The elements are prescribed by God. Their exact symbolism is debated, though cedar, scarlet, and hyssop often accompany cleansing elsewhere in the law.

Reformed readers see worship ordered by divine command. Both traditions read the details with restraint.

Leviticus 14:5

5The priest shall command them to kill one of the birds in an earthen vessel over running water.

Arminian

One bird is killed in a clay vessel over running water. The rite involves life given and fresh water.

Arminian readers see blood and water as signs of cleansing that God supplies. The picture prepares for the release of the second bird.

Reformed

The death of one bird is part of the cleansing. The Hebrew phrase behind ‘running water’ can also be rendered ‘living water.’

Reformed readers see sacrifice and cleansing joined. Both traditions read it as God’s provision.

Leviticus 14:6

6As for the living bird, he shall take it, the cedar wood, the scarlet, and the hyssop, and shall dip them and the living bird in the blood of the bird that was killed over the running water.

Arminian

The living bird, cedar, scarlet, and hyssop are dipped in the blood of the killed bird. The two birds are linked.

Arminian readers see death and life bound together in the rite. The living bird carries the mark of the one that died.

Reformed

The living bird is identified with the slain bird’s blood. The rite holds death and release together.

Reformed readers often see a picture that Christians connect with death and resurrection, while not pressing every detail. Both traditions share this cautious typology.

Leviticus 14:7

7He shall sprinkle on him who is to be cleansed from the leprosy seven times, and shall pronounce him clean, and shall let the living bird go into the open field.

Arminian

The person is sprinkled seven times, declared clean, and the living bird is released into the open field. Freedom follows cleansing.

Arminian readers see a vivid image of restored life. The released bird pictures the person’s release back into community.

Reformed

Seven sprinklings mark completeness, and the priest pronounces the person clean. The bird’s release dramatises what has happened.

Reformed readers see God’s declaration as decisive. Both traditions value the image of freedom granted, not earned.

Leviticus 14:8

8“He who is to be cleansed shall wash his clothes, and shave off all his hair, and bathe himself in water; and he shall be clean. After that he shall come into the camp, but shall dwell outside his tent seven days.

Arminian

The person washes clothes, shaves, bathes, and may enter the camp, but stays outside the tent seven more days. Restoration happens in stages.

Arminian readers see a gradual return with real steps to take. Cleansing involves responsive obedience.

Reformed

Washing and shaving prepare for full restoration. The person re-enters the camp but not yet home.

Reformed readers see an ordered process set by God. Both traditions read this as practical and ritual, not as earning acceptance.

Leviticus 14:9

9It shall be on the seventh day, that he shall shave all his hair off his head and his beard and his eyebrows. He shall shave off all his hair. He shall wash his clothes, and he shall bathe his body in water. Then he shall be clean.

Arminian

On the seventh day the person shaves all hair again, washes clothes, and bathes, and is clean. The process is thorough.

Arminian readers see careful attention to God’s instruction. Nothing is left half-done.

Reformed

The repeated shaving and washing emphasise complete renewal. The person is now ready for the eighth-day offerings.

Reformed readers see holiness requiring thoroughness. Both traditions read the detail as ritual preparation, not moral reform.

Leviticus 14:10

10“On the eighth day he shall take two male lambs without defect, one ewe lamb a year old without defect, three tenths of an ephah of fine flour for a meal offering, mixed with oil, and one log of oil.

Arminian

On the eighth day the person brings two male lambs, a ewe lamb, flour mixed with oil, and oil. The ephah and the log are measures of quantity.

Arminian readers see worship as the climax of restoration. The healed person returns to God’s presence with offerings.

Reformed

The eighth day marks a new beginning. The offerings are substantial for those who can afford them.

Reformed readers see sacrifice as the way back into fellowship with a holy God. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:11

11The priest who cleanses him shall set the man who is to be cleansed, and those things, before the LORD, at the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

The priest sets the person and the offerings before the LORD at the Tent of Meeting. Restoration is completed in God’s presence.

Arminian readers see the person welcomed back to worship, not only to society.

Reformed

The person stands at the entrance of the sanctuary. Fellowship with God is the goal.

Reformed readers see the priest presenting the person to God. Both traditions see mediation as central to the rite.

Leviticus 14:12

12“The priest shall take one of the male lambs, and offer him for a trespass offering, with the log of oil, and wave them for a wave offering before the LORD.

Arminian

One male lamb is offered as a trespass offering, with the oil, and waved before the LORD. The offering is presented to God.

Arminian readers note that a trespass offering here does not imply the person sinned to cause the disease. It may address the person’s inability to worship during uncleanness, though explanations vary.

Reformed

The trespass offering begins the eighth-day rites. Why it is required is debated among interpreters.

Reformed readers likewise avoid assuming the disease was caused by personal sin. Both traditions read the offering as part of full restoration.

Leviticus 14:13

13He shall kill the male lamb in the place where they kill the sin offering and the burnt offering, in the place of the sanctuary; for as the sin offering is the priest’s, so is the trespass offering. It is most holy.

Arminian

The lamb is killed where sin and burnt offerings are killed, and the trespass offering is most holy. The rite is serious.

Arminian readers see the holiness of the restored person’s approach to God.

Reformed

The offering belongs to the priest like the sin offering. It is treated as most holy.

Reformed readers see the ordered holiness of the sacrificial system. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:14

14The priest shall take some of the blood of the trespass offering, and the priest shall put it on the tip of the right ear of him who is to be cleansed, and on the thumb of his right hand, and on the big toe of his right foot.

Arminian

Blood is placed on the right ear, right thumb, and right big toe. The same pattern was used at the priests’ ordination.

Arminian readers see the whole person, hearing, acting, and walking, consecrated afresh to God.

Reformed

The echo of the priests’ consecration (Leviticus 8:23–24) is striking. The restored person is marked for God.

Reformed readers see the person set apart again for life among God’s people. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:15

15The priest shall take some of the log of oil, and pour it into the palm of his own left hand.

Arminian

The priest pours oil into his left palm. Oil follows blood.

Arminian readers see oil as a sign of blessing and consecration. The rite builds carefully. Even the priest’s hands are part of the ordered rite.

Reformed

The priest prepares the oil for the next action. Each step follows God’s instruction.

Reformed readers see precise obedience in worship. Both traditions read this as part of the consecration rite.

Leviticus 14:16

16The priest shall dip his right finger in the oil that is in his left hand, and shall sprinkle some of the oil with his finger seven times before the LORD.

Arminian

The priest sprinkles oil seven times before the LORD with his right finger. The oil is first presented to God.

Arminian readers see consecration directed toward God before it is applied to the person.

Reformed

Seven sprinklings before the LORD mark completeness. God is honored first.

Reformed readers see worship ordered around God’s presence. The oil is dedicated before it is used for the person. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:17

17The priest shall put some of the rest of the oil that is in his hand on the tip of the right ear of him who is to be cleansed, and on the thumb of his right hand, and on the big toe of his right foot, upon the blood of the trespass offering.

Arminian

Oil is placed on the ear, thumb, and toe, on top of the blood. Oil and blood are layered.

Arminian readers see cleansing followed by consecration. Many Christians have connected oil with the Spirit, though the text does not say so.

Reformed

The oil rests on the blood. The pattern moves from atonement to anointing.

Reformed readers likewise see a sequence that Christians often relate to the Spirit’s work following Christ’s sacrifice, while not overclaiming. Both traditions share this cautious reading.

Leviticus 14:18

18The rest of the oil that is in the priest’s hand he shall put on the head of him who is to be cleansed, and the priest shall make atonement for him before the LORD.

Arminian

The rest of the oil goes on the person’s head, and the priest makes atonement for him before the LORD. The rite approaches its end.

Arminian readers see atonement as restoring the person to fellowship with God.

Reformed

Anointing the head completes the oil rite. The priest makes atonement before the LORD.

Reformed readers see atonement here as ritual purification enabling access to God. Both traditions note that it does not imply the disease was a punishment for sin.

Leviticus 14:19

19“The priest shall offer the sin offering, and make atonement for him who is to be cleansed because of his uncleanness. Afterward he shall kill the burnt offering;

Arminian

The priest offers the sin offering for the person’s uncleanness, then the burnt offering. Uncleanness is dealt with before God.

Arminian readers note the sin offering here addresses uncleanness, not necessarily moral guilt. It is often called a purification offering.

Reformed

The sin offering makes atonement for uncleanness. The burnt offering follows as dedication.

Reformed readers see purification enabling approach to God. Both traditions agree that ritual impurity is not the same as moral sin.

Leviticus 14:20

20then the priest shall offer the burnt offering and the meal offering on the altar. The priest shall make atonement for him, and he shall be clean.

Arminian

The burnt and meal offerings are offered, atonement is made, and the person is clean. Restoration is complete.

Arminian readers see God’s generosity in providing a full path back. Christians often see Christ as the fulfilment of such sacrifices.

Reformed

The final declaration is ‘he shall be clean.’ God’s provision has accomplished its purpose.

Reformed readers see sacrifice as the God-given means of restoration, pointing to Christ’s once-for-all offering. Both traditions share this reading.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
