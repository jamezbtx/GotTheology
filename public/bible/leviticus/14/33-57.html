Passage

Leviticus 14

Verses 33–57 · WEB

The final part of Leviticus 14 addresses mildew or mould in houses in the promised land, followed by a summary of chapters 13–14. The priest inspects, waits, orders repairs, and if necessary has the house torn down; if the remedy works, he cleanses the house with the same bird rite used for a healed person. The law is practical and patient, and the uncleanness it describes is ritual and physical rather than moral. Both traditions read it as God’s care for ordinary homes and life in a holy community.

Leviticus 14:33

33The LORD spoke to Moses and to Aaron, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses and Aaron about a new topic: mildew or mould in houses. The law looks ahead to settled life in the land.

Arminian readers see God’s concern reaching into ordinary homes. Holiness touches daily living, not only the sanctuary.

Reformed

God addresses both the lawgiver and the priest, since priests will inspect the houses. The instruction anticipates life after the wilderness.

Reformed readers see God’s rule extending over every sphere of life. Both traditions read this as shared ground about practical holiness.

Leviticus 14:34

34“When you have come into the land of Canaan, which I give to you for a possession, and I put a spreading mildew in a house in the land of your possession,

Arminian

God says, ‘I put a spreading mildew in a house’ in the land he gives. The land is a gift, and its troubles are not outside his rule.

Arminian readers often read this as God permitting and governing natural processes, without implying the owner sinned. The text gives no moral cause.

Reformed

The LORD speaks of himself as placing the mildew. Reformed readers see this as an expression of God’s providence over all things, including decay.

Both traditions agree the text does not link the mildew to the owner’s sin. Their emphasis on how God governs natural events differs only in degree here.

Leviticus 14:35

35then he who owns the house shall come and tell the priest, saying, ‘There seems to me to be some sort of plague in the house.’

Arminian

The owner reports to the priest: ‘There seems to me to be some sort of plague in the house.’ The owner acts responsibly but does not diagnose.

Arminian readers see honest reporting as a model. The community trusts the appointed process rather than private judgment.

Reformed

The owner notices and speaks up, but leaves the verdict to the priest. The wording is tentative.

Reformed readers see the value of God-appointed authority in judging uncleanness. Both traditions share this reading of humble, responsible action.

Leviticus 14:36

36The priest shall command that they empty the house, before the priest goes in to examine the plague, that all that is in the house not be made unclean. Afterward the priest shall go in to inspect the house.

Arminian

The house is emptied before the priest goes in, so its contents will not be made unclean. The rule protects the family’s goods.

Arminian readers see practical mercy in the law. Uncleanness is declared only after inspection, so the timing spares possessions.

Reformed

Uncleanness begins with the priest’s declaration, not before. Emptying the house first is a sensible kindness.

Reformed readers see God’s law as wise and humane. Both traditions read this verse as shared ground on the law’s care for ordinary people.

Leviticus 14:37

37He shall examine the plague; and behold, if the plague is in the walls of the house with hollow streaks, greenish or reddish, and it appears to be deeper than the wall,

Arminian

The priest looks for greenish or reddish streaks that seem deeper than the wall’s surface. The signs are specific and observable.

Arminian readers see careful discernment, not hasty judgment. The priest examines before declaring anything.

Reformed

The description resembles the inspection of skin in chapter 13. The same careful method is applied to a house.

Reformed readers see order and patience in God’s law. Both traditions share this reading of careful, evidence-based judgment.

Leviticus 14:38

38then the priest shall go out of the house to the door of the house, and shut up the house seven days.

Arminian

The priest goes out to the door and shuts the house for seven days. Waiting allows the truth to become clear.

Arminian readers see patience built into judgment. No one is condemned on a first glance.

Reformed

The seven-day quarantine parallels the skin inspections. Time tests the diagnosis.

Reformed readers see wisdom in the law’s restraint. Both traditions agree that the process is protective and fair, not punitive.

Leviticus 14:39

39The priest shall come again on the seventh day, and look. If the plague has spread in the walls of the house,

Arminian

On the seventh day the priest returns to see whether the plague has spread. The decision depends on what he finds.

Arminian readers see judgment following real evidence. The process responds to actual conditions.

Reformed

The second inspection determines the next step. Spreading is the key sign.

Reformed readers see an orderly procedure set by God. Both traditions read this as practical wisdom rather than a theological dispute.

Leviticus 14:40

40then the priest shall command that they take out the stones in which is the plague, and cast them into an unclean place outside of the city.

Arminian

If it has spread, the affected stones are removed and taken to an unclean place outside the city. The problem is dealt with precisely.

Arminian readers see that only the affected parts are removed at first. The aim is to save the house if possible.

Reformed

Removal is targeted, not total. Unclean material is taken outside the community.

Reformed readers see holiness requiring separation from what defiles, while showing restraint. The aim is to save the house where possible. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:41

41He shall cause the inside of the house to be scraped all over. They shall pour out the mortar that they scraped off outside of the city into an unclean place.

Arminian

The inside of the house is scraped, and the scraped mortar is dumped outside the city. Cleansing is thorough.

Arminian readers see careful effort to restore what can be saved. Christians sometimes apply this image to dealing seriously with spiritual corruption, though the text itself is practical.

Reformed

Scraping removes the visible traces of the mildew. The waste is put outside the city.

Reformed readers likewise note that later Christian application is analogy rather than the text’s direct meaning. Both traditions read this as shared ground.

Leviticus 14:42

42They shall take other stones, and put them in the place of those stones; and he shall take other mortar, and shall plaster the house.

Arminian

New stones and new plaster replace the old. The house is repaired and given a fresh start.

Arminian readers see restoration as the goal. God’s law seeks renewal before destruction.

Reformed

Replacement follows removal. The house is rebuilt rather than abandoned.

Reformed readers see God’s patience in providing a remedy. The law hopes for recovery before it orders destruction. Both traditions share this reading of repair and hope.

Leviticus 14:43

43“If the plague comes again, and breaks out in the house after he has taken out the stones, and after he has scraped the house, and after it was plastered,

Arminian

If the plague returns after the stones are replaced and the house replastered, further action is needed. Repair does not always succeed.

Arminian readers see honest realism in the law. Some problems go deeper than first thought.

Reformed

The return of the mildew shows that the problem was not solved. The law anticipates this outcome.

Reformed readers see the seriousness of persistent defilement. Both traditions read this as a practical case, not a statement about moral guilt.

Leviticus 14:44

44then the priest shall come in and look; and behold, if the plague has spread in the house, it is a destructive mildew in the house. It is unclean.

Arminian

If it has spread, it is a destructive mildew and the house is unclean. The verdict is now firm.

Arminian readers see judgment only after every opportunity for remedy. The priest declares what the evidence shows.

Reformed

The priest pronounces the house unclean after repeated inspection. The declaration follows the evidence.

Reformed readers see God’s standard applied consistently. Both traditions agree that the uncleanness is ritual and physical, not a judgment on the household’s character.

Leviticus 14:45

45He shall break down the house, its stones, and its timber, and all the house’s mortar. He shall carry them out of the city into an unclean place.

Arminian

The house is torn down, and all its materials are taken out of the city to an unclean place. This is a heavy loss.

Arminian readers see that some things must be removed for the community’s health. The law does not pretend the loss is small.

Reformed

Destruction is the last resort after repair fails. The whole house goes outside the city.

Reformed readers see the seriousness of uncleanness in a holy community. Both traditions share this reading while noting the owner is not condemned morally.

Leviticus 14:46

46“Moreover he who goes into the house while it is shut up shall be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Anyone who enters the house while it is shut up is unclean until evening. Contact brings temporary uncleanness.

Arminian readers see clear boundaries that protect the community. The uncleanness is brief and remedied by time.

Reformed

Entering the closed house conveys ritual uncleanness. It lasts only until evening.

Reformed readers see holiness affecting even ordinary movement. Time, not sacrifice, ends this brief impurity. Both traditions agree that such uncleanness is not sin.

Leviticus 14:47

47He who lies down in the house shall wash his clothes; and he who eats in the house shall wash his clothes.

Arminian

Anyone who lies down or eats in the house must wash their clothes. Longer contact needs more cleansing.

Arminian readers see proportion in the law. The remedy matches the degree of exposure.

Reformed

Sleeping or eating in the house requires washing. The law is precise about degrees of contact.

Reformed readers see God’s orderly care for purity. Both traditions read this as practical ritual instruction.

Leviticus 14:48

48“If the priest shall come in, and examine it, and behold, the plague hasn’t spread in the house, after the house was plastered, then the priest shall pronounce the house clean, because the plague is healed.

Arminian

If the plague has not spread after replastering, the priest declares the house clean because the plague is healed. Good news is announced.

Arminian readers see the law’s hope for restoration. Recovery is expected, not only failure.

Reformed

The house is pronounced clean on the evidence. The language of healing is used even for a house.

Reformed readers see God’s gracious outcome when the remedy works. Both traditions share this hopeful reading.

Leviticus 14:49

49To cleanse the house he shall take two birds, cedar wood, scarlet, and hyssop.

Arminian

To cleanse the house, the priest takes two birds, cedar wood, scarlet, and hyssop. The same rite used for a healed person is applied.

Arminian readers see the house restored to the community like a person. Cleansing is ritual, not only practical.

Reformed

The parallel with 14:4 is deliberate. Home and person are restored in similar ways.

Reformed readers see the holiness of the whole life of Israel, including homes. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:50

50He shall kill one of the birds in an earthen vessel over running water.

Arminian

One bird is killed in a clay vessel over running water. The rite repeats verse 5.

Arminian readers see blood and water as signs of cleansing God provides for homes as well as people.

Reformed

The same action is performed for the house. Death and running water are again joined.

Reformed readers see God’s provision of cleansing extended to ordinary life. Both traditions read this repetition as shared ground about God’s care for daily living.

Leviticus 14:51

51He shall take the cedar wood, the hyssop, the scarlet, and the living bird, and dip them in the blood of the slain bird, and in the running water, and sprinkle the house seven times.

Arminian

The cedar, hyssop, scarlet, and living bird are dipped in the blood and water, and the house is sprinkled seven times. The rite is complete and thorough.

Arminian readers see full cleansing applied to the home.

Reformed

Seven sprinklings mark completeness, as with the person in 14:7. The house is treated with the same seriousness.

Reformed readers see God’s ordered cleansing. Christians sometimes connect sprinkling with Hebrews’ language of cleansing by blood, while not overclaiming. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 14:52

52He shall cleanse the house with the blood of the bird, and with the running water, with the living bird, with the cedar wood, with the hyssop, and with the scarlet;

Arminian

The verse lists every element used to cleanse the house. The emphasis is on the full set of means God provides.

Arminian readers see that cleansing comes through God-given means, not human ingenuity.

Reformed

The repetition reinforces that each element matters in the rite. The law is careful and complete.

Reformed readers see worship ordered by God’s word. Both traditions share this reading of the rite’s detail as purposeful.

Leviticus 14:53

53but he shall let the living bird go out of the city into the open field. So shall he make atonement for the house; and it shall be clean.”

Arminian

The living bird is released outside the city, atonement is made for the house, and it is clean. The house is restored.

Arminian readers see freedom and restoration pictured again. Atonement here means ritual purification.

Reformed

Atonement is made for a house, showing the term’s use for purification, not only for moral guilt. The release of the bird closes the rite.

Reformed readers see God restoring what was defiled. Both traditions agree that this atonement concerns ritual uncleanness.

Leviticus 14:54

54This is the law for any plague of leprosy, and for an itch,

Arminian

This is the law for every case of skin disease and for an itch. The summary begins.

Arminian readers see the law gathered into a clear teaching. God wants his people to understand.

Reformed

The closing summary collects chapters 13–14. Each condition is named.

Reformed readers see God’s law as complete for its purpose. Both traditions read this summary as shared ground and a reminder that the categories are ritual, not moral.

Leviticus 14:55

55and for the destructive mildew of a garment, and for a house,

Arminian

The summary includes mildew in garments and in houses. The law covers people, clothing, and homes.

Arminian readers see holiness touching every part of ordinary life. Clothing and homes are included, not only bodies.

Reformed

The range of the law is wide. Garments and houses are named alongside skin conditions.

Reformed readers see the comprehensiveness of God’s rule. Both traditions share this reading of a holy people living in a holy land.

Leviticus 14:56

56and for a swelling, and for a scab, and for a bright spot;

Arminian

The summary lists swellings, scabs, and bright spots. Each sign from chapter 13 is named.

Arminian readers see careful teaching meant to be understood and followed. The summary helps priests and people remember the whole teaching.

Reformed

The list completes the categories. Nothing is left vague.

Reformed readers see order and clarity in God’s law. Both traditions agree that the categories describe ritual status, not the moral worth of the person affected.

Leviticus 14:57

57to teach when it is unclean, and when it is clean. This is the law of leprosy.

Arminian

The purpose is ‘to teach when it is unclean, and when it is clean.’ The law exists to instruct.

Arminian readers see God giving clear guidance so that people can respond rightly. The distinction between clean and unclean shapes Israel’s life with God.

Reformed

Teaching is the goal of the law. Priests were to help Israel distinguish clean from unclean.

Reformed readers see the law as a guide to holiness, pointing beyond itself to Christ’s greater cleansing. Both traditions share this reading.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
