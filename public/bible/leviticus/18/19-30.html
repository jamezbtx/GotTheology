Passage

Leviticus 18

Verses 19–30 · WEB

The chapter widens to other prohibited practices: relations during menstrual impurity, adultery, child sacrifice to Molech, same-sex intercourse, and bestiality. It closes by warning that the land itself vomited out the nations who practiced these things and would do the same to Israel. Both traditions affirm the sexual ethics taught here with moral clarity. Christians in both read some elements, such as the purity regulation in verse 19, through a distinction between moral, ceremonial, and civil law, with internal diversity on details.

Leviticus 18:19

19“ ‘You shall not approach a woman to uncover her nakedness, as long as she is impure by her uncleanness.

Arminian

Relations during menstrual impurity are forbidden. Arminian readers place this within Israel’s purity system, tied to holiness and the sanctuary. Many Christians see it as ceremonial rather than a permanent moral law, though views differ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise often classify this as a ceremonial purity law fulfilled in Christ, while some see continuing wisdom in it. Both traditions show internal diversity, and neither camp has a distinctive position here.

Leviticus 18:20

20“ ‘You shall not lie carnally with your neighbor’s wife, and defile yourself with her.

Arminian

Adultery with a neighbor’s wife is forbidden. Arminian readers see the seventh commandment applied. Adultery defiles the one who commits it and wrongs the neighbor.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Decalogue expanded in the holiness laws. Jesus deepens the command in Matthew 5:27–28. Both traditions share this reading. Faithfulness in marriage is a shared conviction.

Leviticus 18:21

21“ ‘You shall not give any of your children as a sacrifice to Molech. You shall not profane the name of your God. I am the LORD.

Arminian

Offering children to Molech is forbidden and profanes God’s name. Arminian readers see the horror of child sacrifice condemned. Scholars debate whether Molech names a deity or a type of offering, but the practice is clearly abhorrent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see idolatry and the destruction of children joined. Worship and ethics cannot be separated. Both traditions affirm this condemnation as abiding moral teaching.

Leviticus 18:22

22“ ‘You shall not lie with a man as with a woman. That is detestable.

Arminian

A man is not to lie with a man as with a woman; the text calls it detestable. Arminian readers, like the church historically, read this as abiding moral teaching about sexuality, reaffirmed in passages such as Romans 1 and 1 Corinthians 6:9–11. The same Corinthian passage (6:11) offers washing and new life to all sinners.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the verse as moral law rather than merely ceremonial. They pair moral clarity with the gospel’s welcome to every repentant sinner, as 1 Corinthians 6:11 shows. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 18:23

23“ ‘You shall not lie with any animal to defile yourself with it. No woman may give herself to an animal, to lie down with it: it is a perversion.

Arminian

Bestiality is forbidden for men and women as a perversion. Arminian readers see the created order between humans and animals protected. The law addresses both sexes equally.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the confusion of created kinds condemned. Both traditions affirm this moral boundary. The verse condemns a practice that degrades human dignity and distorts the order God made at creation.

Leviticus 18:24

24“ ‘Don’t defile yourselves in any of these things; for in all these the nations which I am casting out before you were defiled.

Arminian

Israel must not defile itself with these practices, which defiled the nations being driven out. Arminian readers see God judging the nations for real moral wrong. Israel is not exempt from the same standard.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the moral basis of the conquest narrative. Judgment fell on sin, not ethnicity. Both traditions share this reading. God’s standard is the same for all peoples.

Leviticus 18:25

25The land was defiled. Therefore I punished its iniquity, and the land vomited out her inhabitants.

Arminian

The land was defiled, and God punished it, so the land vomited out its inhabitants. Arminian readers see vivid imagery of moral consequences. Sin affects even the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s judgment working through creation itself. The image recalls Genesis 15:16 and the iniquity of the Amorites. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 18:26

26You therefore shall keep my statutes and my ordinances, and shall not do any of these abominations; neither the native-born, nor the stranger who lives as a foreigner among you

Arminian

Native-born and foreigner alike must keep these statutes. Arminian readers see one moral standard for all who live in the land. Holiness is not limited to ethnic Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the universal reach of these moral laws within Israel. Many see this as a sign that they reflect God’s abiding moral will. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 18:27

27(for the men of the land that were before you had done all these abominations, and the land became defiled),

Arminian

The previous inhabitants committed these abominations and defiled the land. Arminian readers see the parenthesis explaining the reason for judgment. The warning draws on recent history.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the past serving as a warning for the present. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. Israel is meant to learn from what happened to those before them.

Leviticus 18:28

28that the land not vomit you out also, when you defile it, as it vomited out the nation that was before you.

Arminian

Israel too will be vomited out if it defiles the land. Arminian readers see covenant privilege bringing greater responsibility. Israel’s later exile fulfilled this warning.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s even-handed justice. Election does not give license to sin. Both traditions share this reading. The exile later shows that God’s warning to his own people was not idle.

Leviticus 18:29

29“ ‘For whoever shall do any of these abominations, even the souls that do them shall be cut off from among their people.

Arminian

Whoever does these abominations will be cut off from the people. Arminian readers see the seriousness of these sins within the covenant community. The exact meaning of being cut off is debated.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a grave covenant penalty, whether divine judgment or exclusion. Both traditions read the verse as a serious warning. Interpreters debate the exact penalty, but its seriousness is clear.

Leviticus 18:30

30Therefore you shall keep my requirements, that you do not practice any of these abominable customs which were practiced before you, and that you do not defile yourselves with them. I am the LORD your God.’ ”

Arminian

Israel must keep God’s requirements and avoid these customs. Arminian readers see a closing summons to faithfulness. The chapter ends where it began, with I am the LORD your God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the covenant relationship framing the whole chapter. Holiness flows from belonging to God. Both traditions share this reading. The closing refrain ties holiness to relationship with God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
