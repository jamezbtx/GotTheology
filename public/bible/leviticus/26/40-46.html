Passage

Leviticus 26

Verses 40–46 · WEB

After the curses comes a door of hope. If the exiles confess their iniquity and their fathers’, and if their uncircumcised heart is humbled and they accept the punishment of their iniquity, the LORD will remember his covenant with Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham, and will remember the land. Yet even before that turning, he will not reject them in the enemies’ land so as to destroy them utterly or to break his covenant, for he is the LORD their God; he will remember the covenant of the ancestors he brought out of Egypt. The chapter closes as the statutes made at Sinai between the LORD and Israel by Moses. Both traditions find here shared ground about God’s faithfulness: confession is real and called for, and the LORD’s memory of his covenant is the hope of a people who could not save themselves.

Leviticus 26:40

40“ ‘If they confess their iniquity and the iniquity of their fathers, in their trespass which they trespassed against me; and also that because they walked contrary to me,

Arminian

The hope is introduced with an “if”: if they confess their iniquity and the iniquity of their fathers, in the trespass they committed, and that they walked contrary to the LORD.

Arminian readers see a genuine call to confession, including the fathers’ sin named in verse 39. Restoration begins with an honest owning of the contrary walk.

Reformed

Reformed readers also take the confession as real and necessary. The people must name their trespass and their contrary walk.

Both traditions read this “if” as a true summons to repentance, and they do not set it against the faithfulness of God that verses 42–45 will announce. The two belong together.

Leviticus 26:41

41I also walked contrary to them, and brought them into the land of their enemies; if then their uncircumcised heart is humbled, and they then accept the punishment of their iniquity,

Arminian

The LORD had also walked contrary to them and brought them into the enemies’ land. If then their uncircumcised heart is humbled and they accept the punishment of their iniquity, the way is opened for what follows.

Arminian readers hear the need for a humbled heart, not only spoken words. “Uncircumcised heart” describes a resistant inner life that must be bowed, and accepting the punishment means they stop kicking against the judgment as undeserved.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the humbled heart and the acceptance of iniquity’s punishment. The exile is owned as the LORD’s just dealing.

Both traditions take heart-humbling as part of the promised turning, and they refrain from treating the image of the uncircumcised heart as a point of contrast between them. Deuteronomy 30 also joins exile, return, and the heart in closely related terms.

Leviticus 26:42

42then I will remember my covenant with Jacob, my covenant with Isaac, and also my covenant with Abraham; and I will remember the land.

Arminian

Then the LORD will remember his covenant with Jacob, with Isaac, and with Abraham, and he will remember the land.

Arminian readers see hope located in God’s memory, not in Israel’s power to restore itself. The patriarchal covenant and the land are both held in his care, and he remembers them when the people humble themselves.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that “I will remember” is God’s own faithful act. He names Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham in reverse order, going back to the promise’s root, and he remembers the land as well.

Both traditions find shared ground here: the people’s future rests on the covenant God remembers, the covenant he made with the fathers.

Leviticus 26:43

43The land also will be left by them, and will enjoy its Sabbaths while it lies desolate without them; and they will accept the punishment of their iniquity because they rejected my ordinances, and their soul abhorred my statutes.

Arminian

The land will be left by them and will enjoy its Sabbaths while it lies desolate; and they will accept the punishment of their iniquity because they rejected his ordinances and their soul abhorred his statutes.

Arminian readers hear again the land’s Sabbath rest and the people’s acceptance of their punishment. The reason is restated: they rejected and abhorred what God had given.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the desolation of verses 34–35 and the confession of verses 40–41 are held in one frame. Judgment and acknowledgment belong together.

Both traditions read the verse as tying land, Sabbath, and accepted punishment to the same unfaithfulness already described, not as a new dispute.

Leviticus 26:44

44Yet for all that, when they are in the land of their enemies, I will not reject them, neither will I abhor them, to destroy them utterly and to break my covenant with them; for I am the LORD their God.

Arminian

Yet for all that, when they are in the land of their enemies, the LORD will not reject them, nor abhor them, so as to destroy them utterly and to break his covenant with them; for he is the LORD their God.

Arminian readers hear this as the beating heart of the chapter’s hope. Even in exile, God will not finish the story as total rejection or as the breaking of his covenant. His character, “I am the LORD their God,” holds them still.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s faithfulness as the last word over the curses. He will not utterly destroy them or break his covenant. The “yet for all that” stands even before the full fruit of their return is described.

Both traditions take this verse as shared ground about God’s covenant faithfulness, not as a place to argue conditionality against grace. He remains their God in the enemies’ land.

Leviticus 26:45

45But I will for their sake remember the covenant of their ancestors, whom I brought out of the land of Egypt in the sight of the nations, that I might be their God. I am the LORD.’ ”

Arminian

For their sake he will remember the covenant of their ancestors, whom he brought out of Egypt in the sight of the nations, that he might be their God. The speech closes: “I am the LORD.”

Arminian readers see God remembering the exodus covenant for the people’s sake, in the sight of the nations. The God who broke the yoke in verse 13 still means to be their God.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same memory and purpose: “that I might be their God.” The public exodus and the ancestral covenant are grounds of hope that Israel did not lay down for itself.

Both traditions again find God’s faithfulness here, and they take the closing “I am the LORD” as the seal of that pledge. The quotes end the speech that has run through the chapter.

Leviticus 26:46

46These are the statutes, ordinances, and laws, which the LORD made between him and the children of Israel in Mount Sinai by Moses.

Arminian

A narrative close follows: these are the statutes, ordinances, and laws which the LORD made between himself and the children of Israel in Mount Sinai by Moses.

Arminian readers see the whole of the preceding instruction, including the blessings and curses, located at Sinai and mediated by Moses. The covenant is historical and corporate.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the colophon as placing this chapter among the Sinai statutes. What God “made between him and the children of Israel” is a real covenant at a real mountain.

Both traditions take the verse as a literary and historical close, and they do not divide over it. Chapter 27 will add further Sinai commandments on vows.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
