Passage

Leviticus 26

Verses 27–39 · WEB

A further “if” raises the curse to its height. If the people still will not listen, the LORD will walk contrary to them in wrath: siege will bring unspeakable hunger, high places and sanctuaries will be destroyed, cities and land will be desolate, and the nation will be scattered among the peoples. Only then does the land enjoy the Sabbaths it was denied. Those who remain in enemy lands will be faint-hearted and will pine away in their iniquity and in their fathers’ iniquities. Both traditions read this as the climax of Sinai’s covenant curse, still aimed at a nation that walks contrary to God. They take the threats as real, and they are cautious about assigning every line a single later fulfillment or a settled typological meaning.

Leviticus 26:27

27“ ‘If you in spite of this won’t listen to me, but walk contrary to me,

Arminian

If in spite of this they will not listen, but walk contrary to the LORD, the last escalation begins.

Arminian readers hear yet another real “if.” Even after sword and famine, the people might still refuse, and that refusal is treated as their own continued walk against God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same stubborn contrary walk meeting a final wave of wrath. The pattern of verses 14–26 is not spent; further refusal brings a heavier stroke.

Both traditions again read the condition as a genuine warning to the nation under the Sinai covenant.

Leviticus 26:28

28then I will walk contrary to you in wrath. I will also chastise you seven times for your sins.

Arminian

Then the LORD will walk contrary to them in wrath and will chastise them seven times for their sins.

Arminian readers note the new word “wrath.” The opposition is no longer only disciplinary delay; it is anger against persistent rebellion.

Reformed

Reformed readers also mark “in wrath.” The LORD himself remains the one who chastises, and the “seven times” again signals a full, intensified judgment.

Both traditions take the verse as the heading for the horrors that follow, without claiming more for the number than the text states.

Leviticus 26:29

29You will eat the flesh of your sons, and you will eat the flesh of your daughters.

Arminian

They will eat the flesh of their sons and of their daughters.

Arminian readers let the verse stand in its grim sense: siege hunger so severe that parents consume their children. It is the inversion of the fruitfulness promised in verse 9.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise refuse to soften the line. The covenant curse reaches the most intimate bonds of the family.

Both traditions read this as a threatened horror of siege, not as a symbol to be decoded, and they do not treat it as a point of doctrinal contrast.

Leviticus 26:30

30I will destroy your high places, and cut down your incense altars, and cast your dead bodies upon the bodies of your idols; and my soul will abhor you.

Arminian

God will destroy high places, cut down incense altars, and cast the people’s dead bodies on the bodies of their idols; his soul will abhor them.

Arminian readers see idolatry judged in the very shrines where it was practiced. The dead are thrown on the idols that could not save them, and the acceptance of verse 11 is reversed: “my soul will abhor you.”

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the LORD himself dismantles the high places and altars. The idols become a resting place for corpses, and his abhorrence answers their earlier abhorrence of his ordinances (verse 15).

Both traditions read the verse as judgment on false worship and as the dreadful counterpart of God’s pledged presence.

Leviticus 26:31

31I will lay your cities waste, and will bring your sanctuaries to desolation. I will not take delight in the sweet fragrance of your offerings.

Arminian

Cities will be laid waste and sanctuaries brought to desolation; the LORD will not take delight in the sweet fragrance of their offerings.

Arminian readers hear that even the holy places and the offerings of chapters 1–7 will not avail when the people persist in a contrary walk. Ritual without listening does not delight God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the LORD himself refuses the pleasing aroma that earlier chapters attached to appointed offerings. Desolate sanctuaries are his doing, not only an enemy’s.

Both traditions take the lost “sweet fragrance” as rejected worship, the inverse of the accepted offerings of Leviticus 1–3.

Leviticus 26:32

32I will bring the land into desolation, and your enemies who dwell in it will be astonished at it.

Arminian

The LORD will bring the land into desolation, and the enemies who dwell in it will be astonished at it.

Arminian readers see the land itself, once promised abundance, become a shock to its occupiers. The curse is so complete that even enemies stare at the ruin.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the desolation is the LORD’s act. Astonished enemies do not control the meaning of the empty land; God does.

Both traditions read the verse as the land’s devastation under covenant judgment, preparing for the exile of the next verse.

Leviticus 26:33

33I will scatter you among the nations, and I will draw out the sword after you. Your land will be a desolation, and your cities shall be a waste.

Arminian

God will scatter them among the nations and draw out the sword after them. The land will be a desolation, and the cities a waste.

Arminian readers hear exile as the climactic loss: removal from the land of blessing, with the sword still pursuing them abroad.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see scattering as the LORD’s act, not a mere political accident. The sword drawn after them means judgment does not end at the border.

Both traditions take deportation and a wasted land as the Sinai covenant’s most severe earthly sanction, still corporate and historical.

Leviticus 26:34

34Then the land will enjoy its Sabbaths as long as it lies desolate and you are in your enemies’ land. Even then the land will rest and enjoy its Sabbaths.

Arminian

Then the land will enjoy its Sabbaths as long as it lies desolate and the people are in their enemies’ land. Even then the land will rest and enjoy its Sabbaths.

Arminian readers see a hard mercy for the soil: the Sabbaths Israel would not keep (verse 2; chapter 25) are at last given to the land, at the cost of the people’s absence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same irony of rest. The land’s Sabbath is the LORD’s claim being honored even in judgment.

Both traditions recognize the link with the land Sabbaths of chapter 25. 2 Chronicles 36:21 later describes the land enjoying its Sabbaths while it lay desolate, in language very close to this verse.

Leviticus 26:35

35As long as it lies desolate it shall have rest, even the rest which it didn’t have in your Sabbaths when you lived on it.

Arminian

As long as the land lies desolate it will have rest, even the rest it did not have in Israel’s Sabbaths when they lived on it.

Arminian readers hear the charge made plain: the people had lived on the land without giving it the rest God required. Exile exposes that withheld obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see neglected Sabbaths repaid in the years of desolation. The land’s rest is measured against what was denied it.

Both traditions treat the verse as explanation of verse 34, not as a separate riddle, and they do not divide over it.

Leviticus 26:36

36“ ‘As for those of you who are left, I will send a faintness into their hearts in the lands of their enemies. The sound of a driven leaf will put them to flight; and they shall flee, as one flees from the sword. They will fall when no one pursues.

Arminian

As for those left, God will send faintness into their hearts in the enemies’ lands. The sound of a driven leaf will put them to flight; they will flee as from the sword and fall when no one pursues.

Arminian readers see inward collapse added to outward exile: leftover survivors live in panic, startled by a leaf. Safety of verse 6 is wholly gone.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the faintness is sent by God into their hearts. Fear itself is part of the covenant curse, not only foreign armies.

Both traditions read the driven leaf and the causeless flight as the terror already foreshadowed in verse 17, now located in enemy lands.

Leviticus 26:37

37They will stumble over one another, as it were before the sword, when no one pursues. You will have no power to stand before your enemies.

Arminian

They will stumble over one another as before the sword when no one pursues, and they will have no power to stand before their enemies.

Arminian readers hear a people so broken that they trip over one another in panic and cannot stand. Community itself becomes a snare.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same powerlessness. The strength and victory of verses 7–8 have been withdrawn by the LORD.

Both traditions take the verse as a continuation of the faint heart of verse 36, still without a doctrinal fork.

Leviticus 26:38

38You will perish among the nations. The land of your enemies will eat you up.

Arminian

They will perish among the nations, and the land of their enemies will eat them up.

Arminian readers see exile as a place of wasting, not a neutral new home. The enemy land “eats” the people who once ate to the full in their own land.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear perishing among the nations as the LORD’s threatened end for a people that will not turn.

Both traditions read the line in the context of the next verses: some are left, and even in this ruin God has not yet said the last word (verses 40–45).

Leviticus 26:39

39Those of you who are left will pine away in their iniquity in your enemies’ lands; and also in the iniquities of their fathers they shall pine away with them.

Arminian

Those left will pine away in their iniquity in the enemies’ lands, and also in the iniquities of their fathers they will pine away with them.

Arminian readers hear lingering guilt, their own and their fathers’, as the atmosphere of exile. The pining of verse 16 is now located abroad and tied to confessed national sin in the verses that follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the remnant wastes away under both personal and ancestral iniquity. The verse states a corporate moral solidarity without explaining every mechanism of it.

Both traditions let the text name that double iniquity, and they wait for verses 40–45, where confession of both and God’s remembrance of the covenant are held together.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
