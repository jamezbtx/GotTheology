Passage

Leviticus 26

Verses 14–26 · WEB

The blessing “if” is now matched by a warning: if Israel will not listen, rejects the LORD’s statutes, and breaks his covenant, he will appoint terror, disease, defeat, and famine. The judgments come in waves. If the people still will not turn, the chastisement is intensified “seven times” more, until the sword executes “the vengeance of the covenant” and bread itself is broken and rationed. Both traditions take these warnings as real corporate judgments on a people in the land, not as empty threats. They read the section under the same Sinai terms already noted at verse 3, and they do not treat every plague as a separate type or as a direct map of individual salvation.

Leviticus 26:14

14“ ‘But if you will not listen to me, and will not do all these commandments,

Arminian

The turn comes with another “if”: if they will not listen and will not do all these commandments.

Arminian readers take the warning as genuinely contingent, the counterpart of verse 3. Refusal to listen is a real path a covenant people may take, and it brings real loss.

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear a genuine warning to the nation. They typically keep this condition within the Sinai administration for life in the land, as at verse 3, without treating every later believer’s salvation as hanging on the same terms.

Both traditions agree that the threat is not theatrical. Not listening is a breach God will answer.

Leviticus 26:15

15and if you shall reject my statutes, and if your soul abhors my ordinances, so that you will not do all my commandments, but break my covenant,

Arminian

The refusal is described more deeply: they reject the statutes, their soul abhors the ordinances, they will not do the commandments, and they break the covenant.

Arminian readers hear a willful turning of the heart, not a mere slip. To abhor God’s ordinances and break his covenant is a real rupture of the relationship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see covenant-breaking as Israel’s act of rejection. The language of the soul abhorring God’s ordinances is the grim inverse of verse 11.

Both traditions take “break my covenant” as a real national unfaithfulness, while also holding, with verses 44–45, that God can still refuse to destroy his people utterly or to break his covenant from his side.

Leviticus 26:16

16I also will do this to you: I will appoint terror over you, even consumption and fever, that shall consume the eyes, and make the soul to pine away. You will sow your seed in vain, for your enemies will eat it.

Arminian

The LORD will appoint terror, consumption, and fever that consume the eyes and make the soul pine away. Seed will be sown in vain, for enemies will eat it.

Arminian readers see the first wave of curse in body and field: disease, despair, and lost harvest. The land’s blessing is reversed when the people will not listen.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that terror and wasting disease are appointed by God, and that enemies eat what Israel sows. The covenant Lord governs even these losses.

Both traditions read the verse as national judgment in the land, and they do not press each symptom into an independent symbol.

Leviticus 26:17

17I will set my face against you, and you will be struck before your enemies. Those who hate you will rule over you; and you will flee when no one pursues you.

Arminian

God will set his face against them. They will be struck before enemies; those who hate them will rule over them; they will flee when no one pursues.

Arminian readers hear the loss of God’s favor as the heart of the curse: when his face is against the people, courage and victory fail.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that defeat and panic come because the LORD sets his face against them, not merely because enemies are strong.

Both traditions see foreign rule and causeless flight as covenant judgment, the reverse of the peace and victory promised in verses 6–8.

Leviticus 26:18

18“ ‘If you in spite of these things will not listen to me, then I will chastise you seven times more for your sins.

Arminian

If in spite of these things they will not listen, the LORD will chastise them seven times more for their sins.

Arminian readers see judgment as discipline meant to turn the people, and further refusal as bringing a heavier hand. The “if” remains a real opportunity still refused.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see an escalating chastisement. “Seven times more” marks a full and intensified stroke, though the phrase itself is not given a detailed explanation here.

Both traditions treat the number as intensification of real judgment rather than as a code whose exact meaning is settled.

Leviticus 26:19

19I will break the pride of your power, and I will make your sky like iron, and your soil like bronze.

Arminian

God will break the pride of their power and make the sky like iron and the soil like bronze.

Arminian readers hear pride judged and the rain of verse 4 withdrawn. A hard sky and a hard earth leave the people unable to bless themselves.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that strength, sky, and soil are all under the LORD’s command. He can shut the heavens as surely as he can open them.

Both traditions read iron and bronze as images of locked-up rain and barren ground, the opposite of the seasonal rains in verse 4.

Leviticus 26:20

20Your strength will be spent in vain; for your land won’t yield its increase, neither will the trees of the land yield their fruit.

Arminian

Strength will be spent in vain: the land will not yield its increase, nor the trees their fruit.

Arminian readers see labor without fruit as the curse matching the blessing of verses 4–5 and 10. Effort cannot replace the LORD’s gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the emptiness of work when God withholds the yield. The land does not pay out of its own store.

Both traditions keep the verse within the agricultural reversal already underway.

Leviticus 26:21

21“ ‘If you walk contrary to me, and won’t listen to me, then I will bring seven times more plagues on you according to your sins.

Arminian

If they walk contrary to the LORD and will not listen, he will bring seven times more plagues according to their sins.

Arminian readers hear “walk contrary” as a chosen stance against God, met by a further wave of plagues. The door to turn back is still in view, and still refused.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same refrain of refused listening and intensified judgment. The plagues are “according to your sins,” measured by the God they oppose.

Both traditions again take “seven times” as a mark of severe, complete chastisement, without claiming a single agreed symbolism for the number.

Leviticus 26:22

22I will send the wild animals among you, which will rob you of your children, destroy your livestock, and make you few in number. Your roads will become desolate.

Arminian

Wild animals will be sent among them, robbing them of children, destroying livestock, and making them few; the roads will become desolate.

Arminian readers see the peace of verse 6 reversed: the beasts once removed now ravage family and flock, and the land’s paths empty out.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the animals are sent by the LORD. Even this desolation is covenant judgment, not a random disaster.

Both traditions read the verse as a grim, this-worldly curse on population, livelihood, and public life in the land.

Leviticus 26:23

23“ ‘If by these things you won’t be turned back to me, but will walk contrary to me,

Arminian

If by these things they will not be turned back to the LORD, but walk contrary to him, the judgments have failed, so far, of their reforming purpose.

Arminian readers underline that the blows were meant to turn the people back. Refusal is a real resistance to a genuine call to return.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see chastisement aimed at turning the nation. The verse does not treat judgment as merely retributive; it was meant to bring them back.

Both traditions agree that “turned back to me” is the desired end of these curses, and that walking contrary is a willful alternative.

Leviticus 26:24

24then I will also walk contrary to you; and I will strike you, even I, seven times for your sins.

Arminian

Then the LORD will also walk contrary to them and will himself strike them seven times for their sins.

Arminian readers hear a fearful correspondence: those who walk contrary to God find God walking contrary to them. The relationship itself is now marked by opposition.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the “even I”: it is the LORD himself who strikes. The nation’s opposite walk is met by his opposite walk.

Both traditions take the mirroring language as covenant justice, still intensified “seven times,” without pressing the number into a settled code.

Leviticus 26:25

25I will bring a sword upon you that will execute the vengeance of the covenant. You will be gathered together within your cities, and I will send the pestilence among you. You will be delivered into the hand of the enemy.

Arminian

A sword will come that executes “the vengeance of the covenant.” The people will be gathered into their cities; pestilence will be sent; they will be delivered to the enemy.

Arminian readers hear siege and plague as the covenant’s own avenging. Breaking the covenant (verse 15) is not without an answer.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the sword carries out the covenant’s vengeance, not a whim. Pestilence inside the cities and delivery to the enemy are the LORD’s acts.

Both traditions read “vengeance of the covenant” as legal, covenantal judgment on a people that has broken its pledged life with God.

Leviticus 26:26

26When I break your staff of bread, ten women shall bake your bread in one oven, and they shall deliver your bread again by weight. You shall eat, and not be satisfied.

Arminian

When God breaks the staff of bread, ten women will bake in one oven and deliver bread by weight; the people will eat and not be satisfied.

Arminian readers see famine in a household image: fuel, ovens, and rations fail, and even what is eaten does not satisfy. The full bread of verse 5 is gone.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the “staff of bread” is broken by the LORD. Scarcity and unsatisfied hunger are his appointed curse.

Both traditions take the verse as severe famine under siege or drought, the close of this wave of judgments, without inventing a further symbolism for the ten women or the one oven.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
