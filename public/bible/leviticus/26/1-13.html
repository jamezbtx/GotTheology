Passage

Leviticus 26

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Following the Jubilee laws of chapter 25, the LORD sets out the blessings and curses of the Sinai covenant. Idols are forbidden, Sabbaths are to be kept, and the sanctuary is to be revered. Then comes the governing “if”: if Israel walks in the LORD’s statutes, the land will be fruitful, the people will live in peace, and God will walk among them as their God. The blessing section closes by recalling the exodus: God has already broken Egypt’s yoke. Both traditions read these promises as gracious terms for the nation’s life in the land, grounded in prior redemption. Where a real difference appears, it is over how the covenant “if” relates to God’s saving purpose, not over whether the exodus was earned.

Leviticus 26:1

1“ ‘You shall make for yourselves no idols, and you shall not raise up a carved image or a pillar, and you shall not place any figured stone in your land, to bow down to it; for I am the LORD your God.

Arminian

Israel is forbidden to make idols, raise a carved image or a pillar, or place a figured stone in the land to bow down to it, “for I am the LORD your God.”

Arminian readers hear a call to exclusive loyalty. The people already belong to the LORD; they must not give the land’s worship to images.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the ban rests on who God is: “I am the LORD your God.” The first command of the Decalogue (Exodus 20:2–5) is applied to life in the land.

Both traditions see idolatry as a breach of the relationship just named, not as a mere artistic mistake.

Leviticus 26:2

2“ ‘You shall keep my Sabbaths, and have reverence for my sanctuary. I am the LORD.

Arminian

Israel is to keep the LORD’s Sabbaths and have reverence for his sanctuary. The word closes again: “I am the LORD.”

Arminian readers see time and place set apart for God. Sabbaths and the sanctuary train a willing people to honor the God who dwells among them.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see holy time and holy space as the LORD’s claim on the nation’s life. Reverence for the sanctuary matches the presence promised later in this chapter.

Both traditions take these commands as the fitting preface to the blessings and curses that follow.

Leviticus 26:3

3“ ‘If you walk in my statutes and keep my commandments, and do them,

Arminian

The blessing section opens with a condition: “If you walk in my statutes and keep my commandments, and do them.”

Arminian readers take the “if” as a real call to faithful response. Covenant blessing in the land is held out to a people who continue to walk in what God has spoken, by the grace he supplies, not as a wage that purchases the exodus already given.

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear a genuine call to obedience, but they typically read this “if” as the condition of the Sinai administration for life in the land, not as the terms by which God elects or finally saves individuals. They differ among themselves on how the Mosaic covenant relates to the covenant of grace.

Both traditions insist that the condition responds to redemption rather than earning it, as verse 13 will make plain.

Key contrast Both affirm that these are God’s gracious terms for Israel’s life in the land and that the “if” is a real call to obedience. Arminian readers typically take the condition as genuinely contingent: the nation may enjoy or forfeit these blessings by its response, and some also see here a pattern relevant to perseverance. Reformed readers typically distinguish this national, Sinai administration, conditional as to life in the land, from God’s purpose to save his elect, and they differ internally on how the Mosaic covenant relates to the covenant of grace.

Leviticus 26:4

4then I will give you your rains in their season, and the land shall yield its increase, and the trees of the field shall yield their fruit.

Arminian

If the people walk in his statutes, the LORD will give rains in their season, and the land and the trees will yield their increase.

Arminian readers see ordinary fruitfulness as God’s gift in response to a faithful walk. The blessing is earthly and communal: rain, fields, and orchards.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that even the rain is the LORD’s to give. The land’s yield is not an automatic natural cycle detached from the covenant God.

Both traditions read the promise as national and this-worldly blessing in the land, not as a private guarantee of ease to every later believer.

Leviticus 26:5

5Your threshing shall continue until the vintage, and the vintage shall continue until the sowing time. You shall eat your bread to the full, and dwell in your land safely.

Arminian

Threshing will last until vintage, and vintage until sowing. The people will eat bread to the full and dwell in the land safely.

Arminian readers hear abundance that overflows from one season into the next, and safety in the land God has given. The promise invites trust and continued obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same overflow as the LORD’s covenant care: food to the full and a safe dwelling are his to grant.

Both traditions keep the blessing tied to life in the land under the Sinai terms just stated.

Leviticus 26:6

6“ ‘I will give peace in the land, and you shall lie down, and no one will make you afraid. I will remove evil animals out of the land, neither shall the sword go through your land.

Arminian

God will give peace in the land. The people will lie down unafraid; evil animals will be removed, and the sword will not go through the land.

Arminian readers see peace as a gift that covers daily rest, the threat of wild beasts, and the threat of war. Security is received, not seized.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that peace, beasts, and the sword are all under the LORD’s hand. He both grants rest and withholds the weapons of judgment.

Both traditions treat the promised peace as covenantal well-being for the nation in its land.

Leviticus 26:7

7You shall chase your enemies, and they shall fall before you by the sword.

Arminian

Israel will chase its enemies, and those enemies will fall by the sword.

Arminian readers see victory as part of the same blessing: a faithful people is not left helpless before hostile powers.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the falling of enemies is the LORD’s doing under the covenant, not the fruit of Israel’s strength alone, as the next verse will underline.

Both traditions read the verse as a national military blessing, not as a standing warrant for later private violence.

Leviticus 26:8

8Five of you shall chase a hundred, and a hundred of you shall chase ten thousand; and your enemies shall fall before you by the sword.

Arminian

Five will chase a hundred, and a hundred will chase ten thousand; enemies will fall by the sword.

Arminian readers hear a promise that a small, obedient people can prevail beyond its numbers. The odds themselves show that the victory is God’s.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see disproportionate victory as the LORD’s work. The numbers describe his help, not Israel’s prowess.

Both traditions take the verse as an intensification of the blessing in verse 7, still within the Sinai “if.”

Leviticus 26:9

9“ ‘I will have respect for you, make you fruitful, multiply you, and will establish my covenant with you.

Arminian

The LORD will have respect for the people, make them fruitful, multiply them, and establish his covenant with them.

Arminian readers see God’s favorable regard and the gift of children as covenant kindness, and “establish my covenant” as the LORD upholding the relationship he has made.

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear fruitfulness and a confirmed covenant as God’s own act. He establishes what he has already begun.

Both traditions read the verse as God confirming his covenant with a blessed people, not as Israel creating the covenant by obedience.

Leviticus 26:10

10You shall eat old supplies long kept, and you shall move out the old because of the new.

Arminian

They will eat old supplies long kept, and will have to move out the old because of the new.

Arminian readers see provision so full that last year’s store must make room for the new harvest. The blessing is concrete and plentiful.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same overflow as the LORD’s faithfulness in the land’s produce.

Both traditions keep the line within the agricultural blessings of verses 4–5 and do not spiritualize it away.

Leviticus 26:11

11I will set my tent among you, and my soul won’t abhor you.

Arminian

God will set his tent among them, and his soul will not abhor them.

Arminian readers hear the heart of the blessing: God himself dwells with his people and does not reject them. Presence is the gift toward which the land’s peace points.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the tent among them is the LORD’s own act. “My soul won’t abhor you” is his pledge not to cast them off while they walk in his ways.

Both traditions see dwelling and acceptance as the covenantal center of this section, later set over against verse 30, where abhorrence is threatened.

Leviticus 26:12

12I will walk among you, and will be your God, and you will be my people.

Arminian

The LORD will walk among them and will be their God, and they will be his people.

Arminian readers hear the covenant formula as a living relationship: God walks with a people who belong to him, and they are called to walk with him in return.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the heart of the covenant: God “will be your God, and you will be my people.” The promise is God’s to make good.

Both traditions also recognize that Paul applies this promise to the church in 2 Corinthians 6:16: God will dwell and walk among his people and be their God.

Leviticus 26:13

13I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, that you should not be their slaves. I have broken the bars of your yoke, and made you walk upright.

Arminian

The LORD identifies himself as the God who brought them out of Egypt so that they should not be slaves. He has broken the bars of their yoke and made them walk upright.

Arminian readers stress the order already seen at Sinai: redemption comes first. The blessings of this chapter rest on a liberation God has already accomplished, which calls for a grateful walk.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the exodus as the ground of the covenant, not its reward. God broke the yoke; Israel did not free itself.

Both traditions read this verse as the gracious foundation of 26:1–12. The “if” of verse 3 does not purchase a salvation already given.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
