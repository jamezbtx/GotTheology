Passage

Leviticus 20

Verses 22–27 · WEB

The chapter closes by returning to its main theme: Israel must keep the LORD’s statutes so that the land will not vomit them out, as it did the nations before them (compare 18:24–28). God has set Israel apart from the peoples, and the distinction between clean and unclean animals serves as a daily sign of that separation. A final law on mediums and wizards brackets the chapter with its opening concern about turning to spirits. Both traditions read this as covenant holiness for Israel, and both commonly see the food distinctions as fulfilled and set aside in Christ (Mark 7:19; Acts 10:15) while the call to be holy remains (1 Peter 1:15–16). Their differences here are minor and mostly concern emphasis rather than interpretation.

Leviticus 20:22

22“ ‘You shall therefore keep all my statutes and all my ordinances, and do them, that the land where I am bringing you to dwell may not vomit you out.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the warning that the land might vomit out Israel as a reminder that covenant blessings came with real responsibility. Israel could forfeit its place in the land through persistent disobedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same warning as part of God’s covenant dealings with Israel as a nation (compare 18:25–28). Both traditions agree that the verse speaks of Israel’s life in the land rather than directly of individual salvation.

Leviticus 20:23

23You shall not walk in the customs of the nation which I am casting out before you; for they did all these things, and therefore I abhorred them.

Arminian

Arminian readers see God’s judgment on the nations as a response to their practices, not arbitrary favoritism. Israel is warned not to imitate them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s justice in removing nations whose sins had reached their full measure. Both traditions agree that the verse calls God’s people to resist the surrounding culture’s sins.

Leviticus 20:24

24But I have said to you, “You shall inherit their land, and I will give it to you to possess it, a land flowing with milk and honey.” I am the LORD your God, who has separated you from the peoples.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the gift of the land as grounded in God’s promise and grace, followed by the call to live as a distinct people. God gives first and then asks for faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize “who has separated you from the peoples,” seeing God’s gracious choice of Israel as the basis of its holiness. Both traditions agree that Israel’s separation was for God’s purposes and rooted in his initiative.

Leviticus 20:25

25“ ‘You shall therefore make a distinction between the clean animal and the unclean, and between the unclean fowl and the clean. You shall not make yourselves abominable by animal, or by bird, or by anything with which the ground teems, which I have separated from you as unclean for you.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the distinctions between clean and unclean animals as a daily reminder of Israel’s separation. These were ceremonial markers fulfilled in Christ (Mark 7:19).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the food laws as part of the ceremonial law that pointed to holiness and has been set aside in Christ (Acts 10:15). Both traditions agree that Christians are not bound by these distinctions but learn from the call to be distinct.

Leviticus 20:26

26You shall be holy to me, for I, the LORD, am holy, and have set you apart from the peoples, that you should be mine.

Arminian

Arminian readers hear in “that you should be mine” the relational heart of holiness. God sets his people apart so that they may belong to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s holiness and his act of setting Israel apart as the foundation of the people’s calling. Both traditions treasure this verse, echoed for the church in 1 Peter 1:15–16 and 2:9.

Leviticus 20:27

27“ ‘A man or a woman that is a medium or is a wizard shall surely be put to death. They shall be stoned with stones. Their blood shall be upon themselves.’ ”

Arminian

Arminian readers see the closing law on mediums and wizards as framing the chapter with the danger of turning from God to spirits. The penalty was administered within Israel’s covenant courts.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the seriousness of occult practice in a nation where God dwelt. Both traditions agree that Christians should reject such practices, while generally not applying the civil penalty today.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
