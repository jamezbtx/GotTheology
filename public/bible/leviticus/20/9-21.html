Passage

Leviticus 20

Verses 9–21 · WEB

This section lists capital and other penalties for cursing parents and for a range of sexual offenses, many already forbidden in Leviticus 18. The laws protected the family, the integrity of marriage, and the holiness of a people among whom God dwelt, and they were administered within Israel’s covenant community and courts. The passage is hard for modern readers, and it should be read carefully, without softening its seriousness or detaching it from its covenant setting. Both traditions have commonly distinguished the moral concerns of these laws, which they regard as continuing, from their civil penalties, which they see as tied to Israel as a nation and not carried out by the church, though each tradition contains some diversity on how far that distinction reaches. Both also read these verses in light of the gospel, which calls all people to repentance and offers cleansing in Christ (1 Corinthians 6:11).

Leviticus 20:9

9“ ‘For everyone who curses his father or his mother shall surely be put to death. He has cursed his father or his mother. His blood shall be upon himself.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the severe penalty for cursing parents as showing the weight Israel’s law placed on honoring father and mother (compare Exodus 21:17). “His blood shall be upon himself” places responsibility on the offender.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Jesus cites this command when rebuking those who evaded care for parents (Matthew 15:4). Both traditions treat the honor of parents as a continuing moral duty while generally not applying the civil penalty today (a small theonomic minority within the Reformed world argues otherwise).

Leviticus 20:10

10“ ‘The man who commits adultery with another man’s wife, even he who commits adultery with his neighbor’s wife, the adulterer and the adulteress shall surely be put to death.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the death penalty for adultery as a sign of how seriously God guards marriage and the covenant community. Both partners are held responsible.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see adultery as an offense against God, the spouse, and the community. Both traditions continue to teach marital faithfulness as God’s will, while understanding the civil penalty as belonging to Israel’s covenant order.

Leviticus 20:11

11“ ‘The man who lies with his father’s wife has uncovered his father’s nakedness. Both of them shall surely be put to death. Their blood shall be upon themselves.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the prohibition of relations with a father’s wife as protecting family boundaries and trust. Paul’s concern over a similar case in 1 Corinthians 5:1 shows the moral concern carried into the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the New Testament treats this sin with church discipline rather than civil execution. Both traditions agree on the continuing moral wrong while recognizing a change in how the covenant community responds.

Leviticus 20:12

12“ ‘If a man lies with his daughter-in-law, both of them shall surely be put to death. They have committed a perversion. Their blood shall be upon themselves.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the law against relations with a daughter-in-law as guarding the integrity of the household. The text calls it a perversion, a confusion of family roles.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s design for family relationships protected by firm boundaries. Both traditions read the moral concern as lasting and the civil penalty as tied to Israel’s nationhood.

Leviticus 20:13

13“ ‘If a man lies with a male, as with a woman, both of them have committed an abomination. They shall surely be put to death. Their blood shall be upon themselves.

Arminian

Arminian readers in the classical tradition read this verse, together with New Testament teaching such as 1 Corinthians 6:9–11, as affirming that sexual relations are meant for the marriage of a man and a woman. They also stress that the gospel offers forgiveness and new life to all, and that the civil penalty belonged to Israel’s covenant order, not to the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers in the classical tradition hold the same moral reading and the same distinction between the moral concern and Israel’s civil penalty. Both traditions call for the dignity of every person to be respected, and both point to the words “Some of you were such, but you were washed” (1 Corinthians 6:11) as the hope offered to everyone.

Leviticus 20:14

14“ ‘If a man takes a wife and her mother, it is wickedness. They shall be burned with fire, both he and they, that there may be no wickedness among you.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the extreme penalty for taking a woman and her mother as reflecting the gravity of confusing family relationships. The stated purpose is that wickedness not remain among the people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the concern for purging evil from the covenant community. Whether burning followed death by other means is not stated in the verse and is debated, and both traditions read the penalty as part of Israel’s civil law.

Leviticus 20:15

15“ ‘If a man lies with an animal, he shall surely be put to death; and you shall kill the animal.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the law against bestiality as protecting the created distinction between humans and animals. The animal is put to death as well, removing the occasion of the offense.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an affirmation of the order of creation and human dignity. Both traditions agree on the moral wrong while not applying Israel’s civil penalty in the church.

Leviticus 20:16

16“ ‘If a woman approaches any animal and lies with it, you shall kill the woman and the animal. They shall surely be put to death. Their blood shall be upon them.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the same law applies to a woman, showing equal accountability. The repetition stresses the seriousness of the offense.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the parallel structure as emphasizing that both men and women are responsible before God. Both traditions read the verse’s moral concern as lasting and its penalty as covenant-specific.

Leviticus 20:17

17“ ‘If a man takes his sister—his father’s daughter, or his mother’s daughter—and sees her nakedness, and she sees his nakedness, it is a shameful thing. They shall be cut off in the sight of the children of their people. He has uncovered his sister’s nakedness. He shall bear his iniquity.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the prohibition of relations with a sister as protecting the family from exploitation and shame. The offender bears his own iniquity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the verse’s concern for family purity under God. What “cut off in the sight of the children of their people” involved in practice is debated, and both traditions treat the moral boundary as continuing.

Leviticus 20:18

18“ ‘If a man lies with a woman having her monthly period, and uncovers her nakedness, he has made her fountain naked, and she has uncovered the fountain of her blood. Both of them shall be cut off from among their people.

Arminian

Arminian readers see this law, which echoes 15:24 and 18:19, as tied to Israel’s purity system concerning blood. Its purpose was reverence for life and for God’s presence among the people.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly see this law as more ceremonial than the others in the list, tied to the purity rules fulfilled in Christ. Both traditions note that Christians differ somewhat on how much of this verse carries over, while agreeing that the purity system pointed to God’s holiness.

Leviticus 20:19

19“ ‘You shall not uncover the nakedness of your mother’s sister, nor of your father’s sister, for he has made his close relative naked. They shall bear their iniquity.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the prohibition of relations with an aunt as guarding the extended family. Those who do so bear their iniquity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see these boundaries as reflecting God’s ordering of kinship relations. Both traditions agree that the verse protects relationships of trust within the family.

Leviticus 20:20

20If a man lies with his uncle’s wife, he has uncovered his uncle’s nakedness. They shall bear their sin. They shall die childless.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the consequence “they shall die childless” as a judgment that touches what Israel deeply valued, the continuation of the family line. The exact meaning, whether no children or children not counted as heirs, is debated.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the penalty as fitting the offense against family integrity. Both traditions hold the precise sense with caution while agreeing on the moral concern.

Leviticus 20:21

21“ ‘If a man takes his brother’s wife, it is an impurity. He has uncovered his brother’s nakedness. They shall be childless.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the prohibition of taking a brother’s wife as protecting family relationships, a concern John the Baptist raised with Herod (Mark 6:18). Deuteronomy 25:5 provides a specific exception for a brother who died without a son.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read this verse alongside the levirate provision in Deuteronomy 25:5, which applied in a particular situation. Both traditions read the two laws together, with the levirate duty as a specific exception, though exactly how they fit together is discussed.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
