Passage

Leviticus 20

Verses 1–8 · WEB

Leviticus 20 belongs to a stretch of the book in which the LORD repeatedly calls Israel to be holy because he is holy (compare 19:2). Where chapter 18 listed forbidden practices, chapter 20 attaches penalties to many of them, beginning with giving children to Molech and turning to mediums and wizards. These laws belong to the covenant God made with Israel as a nation, with its own courts and penalties, and they show how seriously he regarded idolatry that destroyed children and defiled his sanctuary. Arminian and Reformed readers share the main framework for applying such laws: the holiness God requires still binds believers, the civil penalties belonged to Israel’s covenant order rather than to the church, and Christ fulfills the law (Matthew 5:17). They differ mainly in emphasis on how God’s sanctifying work and human obedience relate in verses 7–8, not over the meaning of the passage.

Leviticus 20:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

Arminian readers note that this chapter, like those around it, begins with the LORD speaking. The penalties that follow are presented as God’s word to his covenant people, not as Moses’ own invention.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that the laws come from God himself. Both traditions agree that the chapter must be read as covenant instruction given to Israel through Moses.

Leviticus 20:2

2“Moreover, you shall tell the children of Israel, ‘Anyone of the children of Israel, or of the strangers who live as foreigners in Israel, who gives any of his offspring to Molech shall surely be put to death. The people of the land shall stone that person with stones.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the death penalty for giving children to Molech as a measure of how gravely God viewed idolatry that sacrificed the vulnerable. The law applied to Israelites and resident foreigners alike, showing that the land’s holiness bound everyone living in it. The WEB footnote notes that “offspring” can also be rendered “seed.”

Reformed

Reformed readers see the community’s duty to act as a sign that idolatry endangered the whole covenant people (compare 18:21). The exact nature of the Molech practice is debated, but both traditions agree the passage condemns harming children in the name of a false god.

Leviticus 20:3

3I also will set my face against that person, and will cut him off from among his people, because he has given of his offspring to Molech, to defile my sanctuary, and to profane my holy name.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that God himself sets his face against the offender, beyond any human punishment. Worship of Molech is described as defiling God’s sanctuary and profaning his name, not merely as a private wrong.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s personal opposition as a reminder that sin is first an offense against him. Both traditions read “cut off” as serious divine judgment, though its exact form here is not spelled out and is debated.

Leviticus 20:4

4If the people of the land all hide their eyes from that person when he gives of his offspring to Molech, and don’t put him to death,

Arminian

Arminian readers see a warning that turning a blind eye to grave wrongs shares in their guilt. The community is responsible for how it responds to evil in its midst.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the corporate dimension of covenant life, in which the people as a whole answer for tolerating idolatry. Both traditions agree that complicity through silence is a real moral failure.

Leviticus 20:5

5then I will set my face against that man and against his family, and will cut him off, and all who play the prostitute after him to play the prostitute with Molech, from among their people.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that God’s judgment reaches those who protect or follow the offender, not innocent bystanders. The language of playing the prostitute portrays idolatry as unfaithfulness to the LORD.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the image of spiritual adultery as central to how Scripture describes idolatry. Both traditions agree that God treats the worship of other gods as a betrayal of a covenant relationship.

Leviticus 20:6

6“ ‘The person that turns to those who are mediums and wizards, to play the prostitute after them, I will even set my face against that person, and will cut him off from among his people.

Arminian

Arminian readers see turning to mediums and wizards as seeking guidance and power apart from God. It is pictured as unfaithfulness, and God sets his face against it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see occult practices as a rejection of the LORD’s sufficiency and word (compare Deuteronomy 18:10–11). Both traditions agree that Christians should avoid such practices, and here the penalty is God’s own judgment rather than a court sentence.

Leviticus 20:7

7“ ‘Sanctify yourselves therefore, and be holy; for I am the LORD your God.

Arminian

Arminian readers hear a call to active consecration: the people must sanctify themselves and be holy because of who their God is. Holiness involves real choices and effort in response to grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the same summons and note that it is grounded in God’s identity, “I am the LORD your God.” Both traditions see this call to be holy repeated for Christians in 1 Peter 1:15–16.

Leviticus 20:8

8You shall keep my statutes, and do them. I am the LORD who sanctifies you.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that keeping God’s statutes is set beside his declaration that he sanctifies his people. They commonly emphasize grace that enables and invites a cooperative response in growing holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize “I am the LORD who sanctifies you,” seeing God as the decisive agent who makes his people holy, while they truly obey. Both traditions hold God’s sanctifying work and human obedience together; they differ in how they describe that relationship, a question this verse raises but does not settle.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
