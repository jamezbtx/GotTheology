Passage

Leviticus 6

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Leviticus 6:1–7 completes the trespass, or guilt, offering of chapter 5. Versification: English 6:1–7 is Hebrew 5:20–26; this page keeps the WEB numbers. It deals with sins against a neighbor that are also a trespass against the LORD: fraud over a deposit, robbery, extortion, keeping lost property, and false oaths. The order matters. Full restitution plus a fifth comes first, then the ram, then atonement and forgiveness. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that the passage ties reconciliation with God to repairing wrong done to people, and both read the offering as pointing ahead to Christ.

Leviticus 6:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The speech formula marks this as God’s word, not human invention. Arminians stress that the same God who requires holiness also provides the way back for the one who has sinned, an expression of his gracious will toward his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the speech formula grounds the law of atonement in divine revelation, so worship is regulated by what God commands. Arminians share this conviction, and both see Leviticus as God graciously initiating the means of restored fellowship.

Leviticus 6:2

2“If anyone sins, and commits a trespass against the LORD, and deals falsely with his neighbor in a matter of deposit, or of bargain, or of robbery, or has oppressed his neighbor,

Arminian

The wrongs listed are against a neighbor, yet the text calls them ‘a trespass against the LORD’. Arminians see here the unity of the two great commandments: wronging a neighbor is never merely a social matter but offends God, whose image the neighbor bears.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters likewise stress that sin against people is first sin against God (compare Psalm 51:4). Both traditions read the list (deposit, bargain, robbery, oppression) as covering hidden and open fraud alike, so no wrong done to a neighbor is beneath God’s notice.

Leviticus 6:3

3or has found that which was lost, and lied about it, and swearing to a lie—in any of these things that a man sins in his actions—

Arminian

Keeping lost property and confirming it with a false oath adds deceit to theft and drags God’s name into the lie. Arminians note that the law addresses the sinner as responsible for actions he could and should have done differently.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the false oath makes the sin worse because it invokes God as witness to a falsehood (compare Exodus 20:7, 16). Both traditions agree that the sinner is fully responsible, and they agree on what the verse condemns.

Leviticus 6:4

4then it shall be, if he has sinned, and is guilty, he shall restore that which he took by robbery, or the thing which he has gotten by oppression, or the deposit which was committed to him, or the lost thing which he found,

Arminian

‘If he has sinned, and is guilty’: conviction of guilt leads to concrete action. Arminians see repentance here as more than a feeling. It involves turning from the wrong and setting it right, as Zacchaeus later did (Luke 19:8).

Reformed

Reformed interpreters likewise see that genuine repentance bears fruit in restitution. They add that the awareness of guilt is itself a work of God’s grace in the conscience. Arminians would also credit prevenient grace for this awakening, so both see God at work before the sinner acts.

Leviticus 6:5

5or any thing about which he has sworn falsely: he shall restore it in full, and shall add a fifth part more to it. He shall return it to him to whom it belongs in the day of his being found guilty.

Arminian

Restitution comes first: return in full, add a fifth, and do it ‘in the day’ guilt is established. Arminians stress that being reconciled to God does not cancel duties to the person wronged. Jesus applies the same order in Matthew 5:23–24.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the added fifth shows restitution should exceed the bare loss, honoring the victim and checking greed. Both traditions read the sequence (restitution, then offering) as teaching that worship cannot cover a wrong the worshiper is unwilling to set right.

Leviticus 6:6

6He shall bring his trespass offering to the LORD: a ram without defect from the flock, according to your estimation, for a trespass offering, to the priest.

Arminian

After making things right with his neighbor, the guilty party still owes a ram to the LORD. Arminians note that restitution, however necessary, does not itself atone. God provides the sacrifice through which forgiveness is granted.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters stress the same point: human restitution repairs the neighbor’s loss but cannot remove guilt before God, which requires a sacrifice ‘without defect’. Both traditions connect the guilt offering (asham) with Isaiah 53:10 and fulfill it in Christ.

Leviticus 6:7

7The priest shall make atonement for him before the LORD, and he will be forgiven concerning whatever he does to become guilty.”

Arminian

‘He will be forgiven’ is God’s promise attached to the offering, not something the offerer earns. Arminians generally read Christ, the fulfillment, as making atonement sufficient for all and provided for all, received by the penitent through faith. Classical Arminians commonly affirmed substitution, while some later Arminians emphasized a governmental view.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priest’s atonement as a picture of Christ’s substitutionary satisfaction, which actually secures forgiveness for those for whom it is made. Both agree that forgiveness rests on God’s appointed sacrifice, that animal blood pointed beyond itself (Hebrews 10:4), and that the penitent truly receives pardon.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
