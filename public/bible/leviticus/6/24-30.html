Passage

Leviticus 6

Verses 24–30 · WEB

Leviticus 6:24–30 gives the priests’ law of the sin, or purification, offering. Versification: English 6:24–30 is Hebrew 6:17–23; this page keeps the WEB numbers. It is ‘most holy’, killed where the burnt offering is killed, and eaten by the priests in a holy place, and anything it touches is affected by its holiness. But when its blood is brought into the Holy Place, the flesh must be burned instead (compare 4:1–21; 10:16–20; 16:27). Both traditions read the passage as teaching how seriously sin and holiness are taken, and Hebrews 13:11–12 links the burned offering with Jesus suffering outside the gate.

Leviticus 6:24

24The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The divine speech introduces the law of the sin, or purification, offering. Arminians note that God himself provides the means of dealing with sin, so the initiative is gracious from the start.

Reformed

Reformed readers also emphasize God’s initiative in appointing a sacrifice for sin. Both traditions read the following rules as God’s provision, not human attempts to placate him.

Leviticus 6:25

25“Speak to Aaron and to his sons, saying, ‘This is the law of the sin offering: in the place where the burnt offering is killed, the sin offering shall be killed before the LORD. It is most holy.

Arminian

Killed where the burnt offering is killed, ‘before the LORD’, and ‘most holy’. Arminians see the shared place of slaughter as linking dedication and cleansing, the two great needs of worshipers.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters note the same link and stress that what deals with sin is not unclean but ‘most holy’. Both see this fulfilled in Christ, who was made a sin offering yet remained the Holy One (2 Corinthians 5:21).

Leviticus 6:26

26The priest who offers it for sin shall eat it. It shall be eaten in a holy place, in the court of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

The officiating priest eats it in a holy place. Arminians follow interpreters who connect this eating with the priests’ role in bearing the people’s iniquity (compare 10:17), while recognizing the precise meaning is debated.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that Leviticus 10:17 links the priests’ eating with bearing iniquity and making atonement. Both traditions treat the exact mechanism with caution and agree that the meal is part of God’s ordained ritual, not a mere perk.

Leviticus 6:27

27Whatever shall touch its flesh shall be holy. When there is any of its blood sprinkled on a garment, you shall wash that on which it was sprinkled in a holy place.

Arminian

Its holiness affects what touches it, and blood on a garment must be washed in a holy place. Arminians see a vivid lesson that dealing with sin and holiness is never casual.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and note that the blood is washed out within holy space, so that holiness does not spread carelessly into the common world. Both traditions agree on what the verse means.

Leviticus 6:28

28But the earthen vessel in which it is boiled shall be broken; and if it is boiled in a bronze vessel, it shall be scoured, and rinsed in water.

Arminian

An earthen pot used for boiling is broken, and a bronze one is scoured and rinsed. Arminians note that porous clay could not be fully cleansed, which teaches the thoroughness God requires.

Reformed

Reformed readers draw the same lesson about thoroughness. Both traditions note that the rule reflects practical differences between materials and both caution against over-allegorizing the vessels.

Leviticus 6:29

29Every male among the priests shall eat of it. It is most holy.

Arminian

Every male among the priests may eat it, for ‘it is most holy’. Arminians see God’s care in providing for the whole priestly community, not only the officiant.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters note that the wider priestly family shares the most-holy portion within the sanctuary. Both see a reminder that holy food is kept for those set apart to serve, and they agree on this verse.

Leviticus 6:30

30No sin offering, of which any of the blood is brought into the Tent of Meeting to make atonement in the Holy Place, shall be eaten. It shall be burned with fire.

Arminian

If the blood is brought into the Holy Place, the flesh is not eaten but burned (compare 4:1–21; 16:27). Arminians follow Hebrews 13:11–12 in seeing Christ, who suffered outside the gate to sanctify the people through his own blood.

Reformed

Reformed readers draw the same connection, and many also cite Hebrews 13:10. Both traditions agree that the burned sin offering points to Christ’s sacrifice, which brings his people into God’s presence, and they agree on this verse.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
