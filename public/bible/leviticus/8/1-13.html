Passage

Leviticus 8

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Leviticus 8 carries out the ordination instructions the LORD gave in Exodus 29, using the priestly garments described in Exodus 28. After the sacrificial laws of Leviticus 1–7, Moses gathers the congregation at the Tent of Meeting, washes Aaron and his sons, clothes Aaron as high priest, anoints the tabernacle and altar, and anoints Aaron. The repeated refrain “as the LORD commanded Moses” presents the whole ceremony as obedience to God’s word.

Leviticus 8:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The familiar formula introduces a new section: the laws of offering (chapters 1–7) give way to narrative as the priesthood is actually installed. Both traditions agree that the initiative here is God’s; Israel does not invent its priesthood.

Arminian readers see the LORD graciously providing a way for a redeemed people to continue in fellowship with him.

Reformed

“The LORD spoke to Moses” marks what follows as revelation. The priesthood rests on God’s word, not on human appointment (cf. Heb 5:4, “nobody takes this honor on himself, but he is called by God, just like Aaron was”).

Reformed readers stress that God himself defines how he will be approached and who will mediate, which is shared ground with Arminian readers.

Leviticus 8:2

2“Take Aaron and his sons with him, and the garments, and the anointing oil, and the bull of the sin offering, and the two rams, and the basket of unleavened bread;

Arminian

The list gathers everything Exodus 28–29 prescribed: people, garments, oil, sacrifices and bread. Nothing is improvised.

Arminian readers note that the priests do not qualify themselves; what consecrates them is supplied by God’s command and provision.

Reformed

Every item corresponds to the instructions of Exodus 29:1–4. Reformed readers see in the completeness of the list the sufficiency of God’s appointed means.

Both traditions read Aaron’s priesthood as a God-given pattern that points beyond itself to Christ’s priesthood (Heb 8:5; 9:11–12).

Leviticus 8:3

3and assemble all the congregation at the door of the Tent of Meeting.”

Arminian

The ordination is public. The whole congregation is assembled, so the people see that Aaron’s office is from God and not seized.

Arminian readers value this corporate setting: the priesthood exists to serve the people’s relationship with God.

Reformed

The congregation witnesses the installation at the door of the Tent of Meeting, the boundary between the people and the holy place.

Reformed readers note the public, covenantal character of the event; the mediator is appointed before the whole people for their sake. Arminian readers share this emphasis.

Leviticus 8:4

4Moses did as the LORD commanded him; and the congregation was assembled at the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

Moses’ obedience is immediate and exact, setting the tone for the chapter.

Arminian readers see willing, faithful human response to God’s word, which the narrative repeatedly commends.

Reformed

The phrase “as the LORD commanded him” will recur throughout the chapter.

Reformed readers emphasize the regulative force of God’s word in worship: the ceremony is valid because it follows his instruction. Both traditions read the refrain as commending careful obedience.

Leviticus 8:5

5Moses said to the congregation, “This is the thing which the LORD has commanded to be done.”

Arminian

Moses explains to the people what is happening and why. The ritual is accompanied by a word that interprets it.

Arminian readers note that God’s people are meant to understand and consent to what is done in his name.

Reformed

Moses’ words echo the pattern of 9:6: what is done is “the thing which the LORD has commanded.”

Reformed readers stress that word precedes and explains rite. Both traditions affirm that worship should be grounded in and explained by God’s revealed command.

Leviticus 8:6

6Moses brought Aaron and his sons, and washed them with water.

Arminian

Washing comes first. Before Aaron and his sons are clothed and anointed, they are cleansed (cf. Exod 29:4; 40:12).

Arminian readers see a picture of the cleansing God provides for those he calls to serve him.

Reformed

The washing at the entrance marks the priests as needing purification before they can minister.

Reformed readers note that even those set apart to mediate are not clean in themselves. Both traditions often connect this imagery with New Testament language of washing (e.g., Heb 10:22), while recognizing that the rite itself is an ordination washing, not a sacrament of the new covenant.

Leviticus 8:7

7He put the tunic on him, tied the sash on him, clothed him with the robe, put the ephod on him, and he tied the skillfully woven band of the ephod on him and fastened it to him with it.

Arminian

Aaron is clothed piece by piece as Exodus 28 prescribed. He does not dress himself; Moses puts the garments on him.

Arminian readers see the honor of the office as something received, not earned.

Reformed

The garments are “for glory and for beauty” (Exod 28:2). Reformed readers note that Aaron is clothed by another, a detail many interpreters in both traditions take as a picture of God providing what his servants need to stand before him.

The ephod and its band belong to the high priest alone.

Leviticus 8:8

8He placed the breastplate on him. He put the Urim and Thummim in the breastplate.

Arminian

The breastplate bears the names of Israel’s tribes over Aaron’s heart (Exod 28:29), and the Urim and Thummim are placed in it.

Arminian readers note that the priest carries the people before God; the office is relational and representative.

Reformed

The Urim and Thummim were used for seeking the LORD’s decision (Exod 28:30; Num 27:21), though how they functioned is not described and remains uncertain.

Reformed readers note that the priest both represents the people and receives guidance from God for them. Both traditions share this reading of the priest’s representative role.

Leviticus 8:9

9He set the turban on his head. He set the golden plate, the holy crown, on the front of the turban, as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

The golden plate on the turban is inscribed “HOLY TO THE LORD” (Exod 28:36–38), and Aaron bears it so the people’s gifts may be accepted.

Arminian readers see God graciously providing for the acceptance of imperfect worshipers.

Reformed

Exodus 28:38 says Aaron “shall bear the iniquity of the holy things” the people offer. Reformed readers see here a representative bearing of guilt that the New Testament fulfills in Christ’s priesthood.

Arminian readers also affirm that Christ fulfills this representative role; the difference between the traditions does not lie here.

Leviticus 8:10

10Moses took the anointing oil, and anointed the tabernacle and all that was in it, and sanctified them.

Arminian

Moses anoints the tabernacle and its contents, setting them apart for God’s use (Exod 30:26–29; 40:9).

Arminian readers note that holiness here is consecration to God, not a quality the objects had on their own.

Reformed

To “sanctify” the tabernacle is to make it holy, belonging to the LORD. Reformed readers stress that holiness is conferred by God’s appointed means.

Both traditions agree that the place, like the people, is holy because God claims it and dwells there.

Leviticus 8:11

11He sprinkled it on the altar seven times, and anointed the altar and all its vessels, and the basin and its base, to sanctify them.

Arminian

The altar is sprinkled seven times, a number that often marks completeness in Israel’s worship.

Arminian readers see special care for the altar, the place where Israel will meet God through sacrifice.

Reformed

The altar, the basin and their furnishings are anointed so they can serve in holy use.

Reformed readers note the thoroughness of the consecration. Both traditions read the sevenfold sprinkling as emphasizing completeness rather than as carrying a hidden meaning that divides them.

Leviticus 8:12

12He poured some of the anointing oil on Aaron’s head, and anointed him, to sanctify him.

Arminian

Oil is poured on Aaron’s head, an image later celebrated in Psalm 133:2. Aaron alone of the priests is anointed in this way at this point.

Arminian readers see God setting apart a mediator for the people’s good.

Reformed

Anointing sets Aaron apart for his office. Reformed readers often link anointed offices in Israel with the title Messiah, “anointed one,” fulfilled in Christ.

Arminian readers share this Christ-centered reading; both traditions see Aaron as a type, not a rival, of the true High Priest.

Leviticus 8:13

13Moses brought Aaron’s sons, and clothed them with tunics, and tied sashes on them, and put headbands on them, as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

Aaron’s sons are clothed with simpler garments: tunics, sashes and headbands.

Arminian readers note that the ordinary priests share in the ministry, though with a different role from the high priest.

Reformed

The sons are clothed “as the LORD commanded Moses.” Reformed readers note the ordered distinction between high priest and priests.

Both traditions often connect this with the New Testament calling of believers as a priesthood (1 Pet 2:5, 9; Rev 1:6), while holding that Christ alone is the great High Priest (Heb 4:14).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
