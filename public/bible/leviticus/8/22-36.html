Passage

Leviticus 8

Verses 22–36 · WEB

The second ram is the “ram of consecration,” a term related to the Hebrew idiom of “filling the hand” for installing a priest. Its blood marks the priests’ ear, hand and foot; portions are placed in their hands and offered; and the remaining meat is eaten at the Tent. The chapter closes with a seven-day period of consecration at the door of the Tent and a summary of complete obedience (8:36).

Leviticus 8:22

22He presented the other ram, the ram of consecration. Aaron and his sons laid their hands on the head of the ram.

Arminian

The second ram is the ram of consecration, the sacrifice that marks the actual installation into office.

Arminian readers note that the priests again identify themselves with the offering by laying on hands.

Reformed

The Hebrew for “consecration” here is related to the idiom of “filling the hand,” used for installing a priest (cf. Exod 29:9; Judg 17:5, 12).

Reformed readers see office conferred through God’s appointed rite. Both traditions agree on this basic meaning.

Leviticus 8:23

23He killed it; and Moses took some of its blood, and put it on the tip of Aaron’s right ear, and on the thumb of his right hand, and on the great toe of his right foot.

Arminian

Blood is put on Aaron’s right ear, thumb and great toe. Many interpreters see the whole person consecrated: hearing, doing and walking.

Arminian readers often apply this to the call for a life fully devoted to God.

Reformed

The same ear, thumb and toe marking appears in the cleansing of a healed leper (Lev 14:14), which suggests purification as well as consecration.

Reformed readers note the extremities as representing the whole person. This is a common interpretive suggestion in both traditions, not something the text states outright.

Leviticus 8:24

24He brought Aaron’s sons; and Moses put some of the blood on the tip of their right ear, and on the thumb of their right hand, and on the great toe of their right foot; and Moses sprinkled the blood around on the altar.

Arminian

Aaron’s sons receive the same marking, and the remaining blood is sprinkled on the altar.

Arminian readers note that every priest is consecrated personally, not only the high priest.

Reformed

The blood links the priests with the altar they will serve.

Reformed readers see the priests bound to the place of sacrifice. Arminian readers share this reading.

Leviticus 8:25

25He took the fat, the fat tail, all the fat that was on the innards, the cover of the liver, the two kidneys and their fat, and the right thigh;

Arminian

The fat portions and the right thigh are set aside. Normally the right thigh belongs to the priest (Lev 7:32–33), but here it is burned.

Arminian readers note that at their ordination the priests do not yet receive their usual portion.

Reformed

Because the priests are being installed and are not yet officiating, the portion that would ordinarily be theirs is given to God (cf. Exod 29:22–25).

Reformed readers see the priests offering to God before receiving from the altar. Both traditions read the detail in the same way.

Leviticus 8:26

26and out of the basket of unleavened bread that was before the LORD, he took one unleavened cake, one cake of oiled bread, and one wafer, and placed them on the fat and on the right thigh.

Arminian

Bread from the basket is added: an unleavened cake, an oiled cake and a wafer.

Arminian readers see the ordinary food of life joined to the offering, marking the priests’ whole livelihood as dedicated to God.

Reformed

The bread offerings follow Exodus 29:23. Reformed readers note the integration of grain and animal offerings in one act of consecration.

This is descriptive shared ground.

Leviticus 8:27

27He put all these in Aaron’s hands and in his sons’ hands, and waved them for a wave offering before the LORD.

Arminian

Moses places the portions in the priests’ hands and they are waved before the LORD. Their hands are literally “filled.”

Arminian readers see this as a vivid picture of being entrusted with ministry.

Reformed

The filled hands enact the idiom of ordination. The priests hold before God what they will later offer on the people’s behalf.

Reformed readers note that the office is received from God, not taken. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 8:28

28Moses took them from their hands, and burned them on the altar on the burnt offering. They were a consecration offering for a pleasant aroma. It was an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

The wave offering is burned on top of the burnt offering, another pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian readers see God accepting the priests’ dedication.

Reformed

The consecration offering ascends to God. Reformed readers see the priests’ installation sealed by an offering God accepts.

Both traditions agree that acceptance rests on God’s appointed sacrifice.

Leviticus 8:29

29Moses took the breast, and waved it for a wave offering before the LORD. It was Moses’ portion of the ram of consecration, as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

Moses receives the breast as his portion. He acts as priest for this one ceremony.

Arminian readers note Moses’ unique role as mediator of the covenant.

Reformed

Moses officiates here before the Aaronic priesthood begins. Reformed readers note his singular office as the one through whom the LORD gave the law and installed the priests.

Both traditions recognize this unique, transitional role.

Leviticus 8:30

30Moses took some of the anointing oil, and some of the blood which was on the altar, and sprinkled it on Aaron, on his garments, and on his sons, and on his sons’ garments with him, and sanctified Aaron, his garments, and his sons, and his sons’ garments with him.

Arminian

Oil and blood together are sprinkled on the priests and their garments. Holiness comes through both anointing and sacrifice.

Arminian readers often connect oil with the Spirit’s enabling and blood with forgiveness, though the text does not make this explicit.

Reformed

The combination of blood and oil completes the sanctifying of persons and garments (Exod 29:21).

Reformed readers commonly read the pairing typologically of Christ’s atoning work and the Spirit’s anointing; Arminian readers make the same connection. The typology is shared, and both recognize it as interpretation rather than explicit statement.

Leviticus 8:31

31Moses said to Aaron and to his sons, “Boil the meat at the door of the Tent of Meeting, and there eat it and the bread that is in the basket of consecration, as I commanded, saying, ‘Aaron and his sons shall eat it.’

Arminian

The priests eat the meat and bread at the Tent. Sharing the sacrificial meal expresses fellowship with God.

Arminian readers see communion with God as the goal of sacrifice.

Reformed

The meal takes place in the holy precinct, as commanded. Reformed readers note that God himself provides the meal for those he consecrates.

Both traditions see the meal as a sign of fellowship with God; neither treats it as a direct prescription for the Lord’s Supper.

Leviticus 8:32

32What remains of the meat and of the bread you shall burn with fire.

Arminian

Leftovers are burned, not kept or treated as ordinary food (cf. Exod 29:34).

Arminian readers note that what is holy is to be honored as holy.

Reformed

The command guards the holiness of the consecration offering.

Reformed readers stress reverence for what God has set apart. Arminian readers share this emphasis.

Leviticus 8:33

33You shall not go out from the door of the Tent of Meeting for seven days, until the days of your consecration are fulfilled: for he shall consecrate you seven days.

Arminian

The priests remain at the Tent for seven days. “He shall consecrate you” is literally “he shall fill your hand.”

Arminian readers see a sustained period of preparation; ordination is not a single moment only.

Reformed

The seven days echo the pattern of creation and other weeklong rites. The process is completed by God’s appointed time, not by the priests’ readiness.

Reformed readers note the patience of the process. Both traditions read the seven days as completeness and preparation.

Leviticus 8:34

34What has been done today, so the LORD has commanded to do, to make atonement for you.

Arminian

What was done is repeated over the seven days (cf. Exod 29:35–37) to make atonement for the priests.

Arminian readers note that even those set apart to serve depend on atonement.

Reformed

The priests themselves need atonement before they can make atonement for others. Hebrews 7:27 contrasts this with Christ, who needed no sacrifice for himself.

Reformed and Arminian readers share this contrast between the repeated Aaronic offerings and Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice (Heb 10:11–14).

Leviticus 8:35

35You shall stay at the door of the Tent of Meeting day and night seven days, and keep the LORD’s command, that you don’t die: for so I am commanded.”

Arminian

The warning “that you don’t die” shows the seriousness of approaching the holy God.

Arminian readers note that faithful obedience is required of those who serve.

Reformed

Holiness is life-giving to those who approach rightly and dangerous to those who do not. Chapter 10 will show the danger concretely.

Reformed readers stress reverence before God’s holiness. Both traditions read the warning as real and serious.

Leviticus 8:36

36Aaron and his sons did all the things which the LORD commanded by Moses.

Arminian

The chapter ends with full obedience. Aaron and his sons do everything commanded through Moses.

Arminian readers see a model of wholehearted response to God’s word.

Reformed

The summary underlines that the ordination was complete and according to command.

Reformed readers note the contrast with chapter 10, where an unauthorized act brings judgment. Both traditions read the contrast as instructive.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
