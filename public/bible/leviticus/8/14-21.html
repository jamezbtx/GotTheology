Passage

Leviticus 8

Verses 14–21 · WEB

Before Aaron and his sons can serve at the altar, sacrifice is offered on their behalf. Moses, still acting as officiant, offers a bull as a sin offering, which also purifies the altar, and then a ram as a whole burnt offering. The procedures follow the pattern already given in Exodus 29:10–18 and in the offering laws of Leviticus 1 and 4.

Leviticus 8:14

14He brought the bull of the sin offering, and Aaron and his sons laid their hands on the head of the bull of the sin offering.

Arminian

Before they can offer for others, Aaron and his sons need a sin offering themselves. Laying hands on the bull identifies them with the offering.

Arminian readers see grace providing a remedy for the very servants who will minister it.

Reformed

The laying on of hands is widely understood as identification with the animal, and many interpreters see a transfer of guilt as well, though the text does not explain the gesture here.

Reformed readers emphasize substitution in sacrifice; Arminian readers also affirm that Christ’s death was substitutionary, even where their theories of atonement are framed differently.

Leviticus 8:15

15He killed it; and Moses took the blood, and put it around on the horns of the altar with his finger, and purified the altar, and poured out the blood at the base of the altar, and sanctified it, to make atonement for it.

Arminian

The blood purifies the altar and makes atonement “for it.” Even the place of meeting needs cleansing because it stands among a sinful people.

Arminian readers note how seriously God treats the defilement of sin.

Reformed

Atonement is made for the altar itself. Hebrews 9:21–22 recalls Moses sprinkling the tabernacle and vessels with blood and says that “apart from shedding of blood there is no remission.”

Reformed readers stress that approach to God depends on blood atonement; Arminian readers share this conviction.

Leviticus 8:16

16He took all the fat that was on the innards, and the cover of the liver, and the two kidneys, and their fat; and Moses burned it on the altar.

Arminian

The fat portions belong to the LORD and are burned on the altar, as the offering laws specified (Lev 3:16; 4:8–10).

Arminian readers see the best of the offering given to God.

Reformed

The choicest parts are offered to God in smoke. Reformed readers see an acknowledgment that the best belongs to the LORD.

Both traditions treat these details as part of a God-given pattern without reading theological disputes into them.

Leviticus 8:17

17But the bull, and its skin, and its meat, and its dung, he burned with fire outside the camp, as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

The rest of the bull is burned outside the camp, as required for a priest’s sin offering (Lev 4:11–12; Exod 29:14).

Arminian readers note that Hebrews 13:11–12 connects this practice with Jesus suffering “outside of the gate.”

Reformed

What is associated with sin is removed from the holy camp. Reformed readers likewise follow Hebrews 13:11–13, which links the burning outside the camp with Christ’s suffering and calls believers to go out to him.

This typological connection is shared ground.

Leviticus 8:18

18He presented the ram of the burnt offering. Aaron and his sons laid their hands on the head of the ram.

Arminian

A ram is presented as a burnt offering, and again the priests lay hands on it.

Arminian readers see the burnt offering as an act of whole devotion that follows the removal of sin.

Reformed

The order matters: sin offering first, then burnt offering. Reformed readers note that atonement precedes dedication and acceptance.

Arminian readers also observe this order, and both traditions read it as describing the logic of the rite rather than taking a side in later debates.

Leviticus 8:19

19He killed it; and Moses sprinkled the blood around on the altar.

Arminian

The blood is sprinkled around the altar, following Leviticus 1:5.

Arminian readers see the life of the animal given up to God, in line with Leviticus 17:11.

Reformed

Leviticus 17:11 ties blood to atonement: “the life of the flesh is in the blood.” Reformed readers read the burnt offering in that light.

Both traditions understand blood in Leviticus as signifying life given up in God’s appointed way.

Leviticus 8:20

20He cut the ram into its pieces; and Moses burned the head, and the pieces, and the fat.

Arminian

The ram is cut up and burned in its parts, as in Leviticus 1:6–9.

Arminian readers note the care and order with which God’s worship is conducted.

Reformed

The detail reflects the prescribed procedure. Reformed readers note that nothing is left to human preference.

Both traditions treat the procedure as obedience to command, without assigning disputed meanings to each part.

Leviticus 8:21

21He washed the innards and the legs with water; and Moses burned the whole ram on the altar. It was a burnt offering for a pleasant aroma. It was an offering made by fire to the LORD, as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

The whole ram is burned, a “pleasant aroma” to the LORD. The burnt offering expresses total dedication and God’s acceptance.

Arminian readers often link this language with Romans 12:1, the call to offer ourselves as living sacrifices.

Reformed

Ephesians 5:2 uses the same aroma language for Christ, who “gave himself up for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling fragrance.”

Reformed readers stress Christ’s perfect self-offering; Arminian readers affirm the same. Both see the burnt offering fulfilled in Christ and echoed in the believer’s devotion.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
