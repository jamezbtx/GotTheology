Passage

Leviticus 1

Verses 10–13 · WEB

The burnt-offering law now covers an animal from the flock, a sheep or a goat. The requirements remain a male without defect, the sprinkling of blood, the washing of the innards and legs, and the burning of the whole as a pleasant aroma to the LORD. The animal is killed on the north side of the altar. Both traditions read this section as the same offering made available through a less costly animal, still fulfilled in Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice, and they do not invent a contrast over the typology or the added location detail.

Leviticus 1:10

10“ ‘If his offering is from the flock, from the sheep or from the goats, for a burnt offering, he shall offer a male without defect.

Arminian

If the burnt offering is from the flock, from the sheep or the goats, it must still be a male without defect.

Arminian readers see God opening a way for those who cannot bring a bull. The standard of an offering without defect remains, but the flock is a true burnt offering, not a lesser class of worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the same “without defect” requirement governs the flock as the herd. God does not lower holiness when the animal is smaller.

Both traditions agree that the offerer’s means may differ, while the character of the offering and its fulfillment in the unblemished Christ remain the same.

Leviticus 1:11

11He shall kill it on the north side of the altar before the LORD. Aaron’s sons, the priests, shall sprinkle its blood around on the altar.

Arminian

The animal is killed on the north side of the altar before the LORD, and the priests sprinkle its blood around on the altar.

Arminian readers see the same costly approach as with the bull, now located at a stated side of the altar. The blood is still applied in God’s presence for the one who comes.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the added detail of the north side and that the death is again “before the LORD.” The blood rite does not change with the species.

Both traditions treat the location as part of the prescribed order rather than a symbol the text explains, and they again share a substitutionary reading fulfilled in Christ (Hebrews 9–10).

Leviticus 1:12

12He shall cut it into its pieces, with its head and its fat. The priest shall lay them in order on the wood that is on the fire which is on the altar,

Arminian

The offerer cuts the animal into its pieces, with its head and its fat, and the priest lays them in order on the wood.

Arminian readers see the same careful preparation as in the herd offering. The worshiper and the priest each do the part appointed to them.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the close match with verses 6 and 8. The rite is repeated so that the flock offering is clearly the same burnt offering.

Both traditions again decline to press each piece into a separate type.

Leviticus 1:13

13but the innards and the legs he shall wash with water. The priest shall offer the whole, and burn it on the altar. It is a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian

The innards and legs are washed, and the priest offers the whole and burns it on the altar. It is a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian readers hear the same conclusion as in verse 9: the entire animal is given, and God is pleased with the offering he has appointed.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the flock animal, like the bull, is offered whole and received as a pleasant aroma.

Both traditions see no diminishing of acceptance when the gift is from the flock, and they again read the pleasant aroma as fulfilled in Christ’s fragrant self-offering (Ephesians 5:2), while the repeated sacrifices themselves could not take away sins (Hebrews 10:4, 11).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
