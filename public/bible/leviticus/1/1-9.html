Passage

Leviticus 1

Verses 1–9 · WEB

After the Tent of Meeting is set up at the end of Exodus, Leviticus opens with the LORD calling to Moses from that tent and giving Israel its first sacrificial instructions. Chapter 1 treats the burnt offering, beginning with a male from the herd, without defect, presented at the door so that the offerer may be accepted. The offerer lays a hand on the animal’s head; it is accepted to make atonement; the priests handle the blood and the altar fire; and the whole is burned as a pleasant aroma to the LORD. Both traditions read these offerings as fulfilled in Christ (Hebrews 9–10) and share a substitutionary reading of the rite. They do not divide here over atonement typology or over the ritual details.

Leviticus 1:1

1The LORD called to Moses, and spoke to him from the Tent of Meeting, saying,

Arminian

Leviticus begins with God’s own call. The LORD speaks to Moses from the Tent of Meeting, the place of his presence among a redeemed people.

Arminian readers stress that instruction for worship starts with God drawing near and addressing Moses, so that the people may approach him as he provides.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the book opens with divine speech, not human invention. Worship will be received on terms the LORD sets from the tent he has filled.

Both traditions see continuity with the end of Exodus: God now tells a delivered people how to live and worship in his presence.

Leviticus 1:2

2“Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘When anyone of you offers an offering to the LORD, you shall offer your offering of the livestock, from the herd and from the flock.

Arminian

Moses is to tell Israel that when anyone offers an offering to the LORD, it is to come from the livestock, the herd or the flock.

Arminian readers hear an open invitation: “when anyone of you offers.” The approach is real and personal, and it is made possible by God’s prior word.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that even these gifts are regulated. The offering must be from the livestock God names, not whatever the worshiper prefers.

Both traditions agree that drawing near is governed by God’s instruction, not by human experiment in worship.

Leviticus 1:3

3“ ‘If his offering is a burnt offering from the herd, he shall offer a male without defect. He shall offer it at the door of the Tent of Meeting, that he may be accepted before the LORD.

Arminian

If the burnt offering is from the herd, it must be a male without defect, offered at the door of the Tent of Meeting, “that he may be accepted before the LORD.”

Arminian readers see a willing worshiper bringing an unblemished animal to the place God has appointed, seeking acceptance on the terms God has given.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that acceptance is tied to an offering without defect, presented where God meets his people. The standard is God’s, not the offerer’s estimation of what is good enough.

Both traditions read the unblemished male and the appointed door as God’s provision for a people who could not invent a way to stand accepted before him.

Leviticus 1:4

4He shall lay his hand on the head of the burnt offering, and it shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him.

Arminian

The offerer lays a hand on the head of the burnt offering, and it is accepted for him to make atonement for him.

Arminian readers see a personal act of identification: the worshiper is bound to the animal that will die in his place. The text itself says the offering makes atonement for the one who comes.

Reformed

Reformed readers also read the hand-laying as identification and substitution: the animal stands for the offerer and is accepted “for him.”

Both traditions share this substitutionary reading and see it fulfilled in Christ, who offered himself once to put away sin (Hebrews 9:26, 28; 10:10–12). They do not treat this verse as a place to argue later debates about the extent of the atonement.

Leviticus 1:5

5He shall kill the bull before the LORD. Aaron’s sons, the priests, shall present the blood and sprinkle the blood around on the altar that is at the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

The offerer kills the bull before the LORD; Aaron’s sons present the blood and sprinkle it around the altar at the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian readers feel the cost of approach: life is given in God’s presence, and the priests serve so that the worshiper may come near.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the death and the blood are “before the LORD” and applied to the altar he appointed. Hebrews 9:22 affirms that apart from shedding of blood there is no remission, while Hebrews 10:4 adds that the blood of bulls and goats could not itself take away sins.

Both traditions hold these together: the rite is truly appointed for atonement, and it points beyond itself to Christ.

Leviticus 1:6

6He shall skin the burnt offering and cut it into pieces.

Arminian

The burnt offering is skinned and cut into pieces.

Arminian readers see careful, complete preparation. Nothing is offered in a hurried or careless way; the whole animal is made ready for the altar.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the procedure is thorough because the whole offering will go up to God.

Both traditions read the dividing of the animal as part of an ordered rite, without pressing each step into an independent symbol the text does not explain.

Leviticus 1:7

7The sons of Aaron the priest shall put fire on the altar, and lay wood in order on the fire;

Arminian

The sons of Aaron the priest put fire on the altar and lay wood in order on the fire.

Arminian readers see appointed ministers preparing what God has required, so that the people’s offering may be presented rightly.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the fire and the wood are arranged by those God has set apart for this service. The altar does not burn at human whim.

Both traditions see the priests as servants of a worship God himself has ordered.

Leviticus 1:8

8and Aaron’s sons, the priests, shall lay the pieces, the head, and the fat in order on the wood that is on the fire which is on the altar;

Arminian

The priests lay the pieces, the head, and the fat in order on the wood that is on the fire on the altar.

Arminian readers notice the care of the arrangement: the offering is placed, not thrown, on the fire God has appointed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see an ordered placement on the altar. The repeated “in order” keeps the rite under the word that commanded it.

Both traditions refrain from assigning a separate meaning to each part, and they agree that the whole is given to the LORD.

Leviticus 1:9

9but he shall wash its innards and its legs with water. The priest shall burn all of it on the altar, for a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian

The innards and legs are washed, and the priest burns all of it on the altar, a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian readers see total consecration: the entire animal goes up to God, and the washing shows that even the hidden parts are made fit to be offered.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the whole is burned and that the “pleasant aroma” is the language of God’s acceptance of what he has appointed. Ephesians 5:2 describes Christ’s self-giving as an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling fragrance.

Both traditions affirm that the burnt offering’s total surrender is fulfilled in Christ, and that the animal rite itself could not perfect those who draw near (Hebrews 10:1, 4).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
