Passage

Leviticus 1

Verses 14–17 · WEB

The burnt offering may also be of birds, turtledoves or young pigeons. The priest carries more of the rite: he brings the bird to the altar, wrings off its head, drains the blood, removes the crop and feathers, and tears the bird by its wings without dividing it apart, then burns it as a pleasant aroma to the LORD. Both traditions emphasize that God makes a way for poorer offerers to bring the same kind of offering, and both still read the rite as pointing to Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice rather than as a reduced atonement.

Leviticus 1:14

14“ ‘If his offering to the LORD is a burnt offering of birds, then he shall offer his offering from turtledoves or of young pigeons.

Arminian

If the burnt offering is of birds, the offerer brings turtledoves or young pigeons.

Arminian readers see a merciful provision: those with little may still draw near with a burnt offering to the LORD. The invitation of verse 2 is not limited to owners of cattle.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the bird offering as God’s appointment, not a human substitute for the rite. The offering remains a burnt offering to the LORD.

Both traditions reject any suggestion that the poor meet a different God or bring an offering of a different nature; the same fulfillment in Christ holds here as in the herd and the flock.

Leviticus 1:15

15The priest shall bring it to the altar, and wring off its head, and burn it on the altar; and its blood shall be drained out on the side of the altar;

Arminian

The priest brings the bird to the altar, wrings off its head, and burns it; its blood is drained out on the side of the altar.

Arminian readers notice that the priest does more of the work here, so that a small offering is still handled with full seriousness at the altar.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that blood still reaches the altar, even when the animal is a bird. The need for blood is not waived.

Both traditions again hold Hebrews 9:22 and 10:4 together: God appoints blood for atonement, and the blood of these creatures points to the one sacrifice that can take away sins.

Leviticus 1:16

16and he shall take away its crop and its feathers, and cast it beside the altar on the east part, in the place of the ashes.

Arminian

The crop and feathers are taken away and cast beside the altar on the east, in the place of the ashes.

Arminian readers see a further act of preparation: what is not to be burned as the offering is set in the appointed place.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the stated location (the east side, the place of the ashes) as part of the ordered rite.

Both traditions refrain from inventing a symbolism for the crop and feathers that the text does not give, and they read the verse as keeping the altar offering distinct and clean.

Leviticus 1:17

17He shall tear it by its wings, but shall not divide it apart. The priest shall burn it on the altar, on the wood that is on the fire. It is a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian

The priest tears the bird by its wings but does not divide it apart, then burns it on the wood on the fire. It is a burnt offering, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian readers hear the same closing formula as with the herd and the flock. The bird, though handled differently, is accepted as the same kind of offering.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the bird is not split completely, a distinctive of this form of the rite, yet the conclusion is unchanged: a burnt offering of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Both traditions see the chapter closing with one offering in three economic registers, all fulfilled in Christ’s complete self-offering, and they do not treat the undivided bird as a separate doctrinal fork.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
