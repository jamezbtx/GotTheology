Passage

Leviticus 24

Verses 1–9 · WEB

The LORD commands Israel to supply pure olive oil so the lamps on the gold lampstand burn continually before him, and to bake twelve loaves set in two rows on the gold table each Sabbath as a memorial, to be eaten by the priests in a holy place. These are standing provisions for ongoing worship in the Tent of Meeting (see Exod 25:23–40; 27:20–21). Both traditions read them as signs of God’s continual presence with his covenant people, and many Christians in both see them fulfilled in Christ, with no real Arminian–Reformed dispute here.

Leviticus 24:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The familiar formula marks a new divine instruction. Arminian readers note that the law comes as God’s word to Moses, not as human religious invention.

What follows is not a one-time event but a standing order for Israel’s worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that worship is regulated by what God commands. The details of the sanctuary come from him.

The formula reminds readers that these provisions carry divine authority.

Leviticus 24:2

2“Command the children of Israel, that they bring to you pure olive oil beaten for the light, to cause a lamp to burn continually.

Arminian

The people are to bring pure, beaten olive oil so that a lamp burns continually. Arminian readers note that the whole community shares in maintaining worship through its offerings (cf. Exod 27:20).

The best oil is given for God’s house.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this repeats Exodus 27:20 almost exactly. The repetition fixes the light as a permanent feature of Israel’s worship.

God’s people supply the means; the light signifies his presence among them.

Leviticus 24:3

3Outside of the veil of the Testimony, in the Tent of Meeting, Aaron shall keep it in order from evening to morning before the LORD continually. It shall be a statute forever throughout your generations.

Arminian

Aaron tends the lamp outside the veil from evening to morning as a lasting statute. Arminian readers see the priest’s faithful, ordinary service as part of how God’s presence is honored.

The light burns through the night, a picture of watchful devotion (cf. 1 Sam 3:3).

Reformed

Reformed readers see Aaron’s priestly ministry as pointing ahead to Christ’s priestly work. Hebrews lists the lampstand among the sanctuary furnishings that foreshadowed better things (Heb 9:2, 8–9).

The “statute forever” belonged to the old covenant order; Christians read its lasting meaning in Christ.

Leviticus 24:4

4He shall keep in order the lamps on the pure gold lamp stand before the LORD continually.

Arminian

The lamps on the pure gold lampstand are kept in order continually. Arminian readers note the repeated “continually” in this passage: worship is sustained, not occasional.

The lampstand is described in Exodus 25:31–40.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note the emphasis on continual light before the LORD. Many Christians connect it with Christ as the light of the world (John 8:12) and the church’s calling to shine (Matt 5:14–16; Rev 1:20).

Those connections are readings drawn from the wider canon.

Leviticus 24:5

5“You shall take fine flour, and bake twelve cakes of it: two tenths of an ephah shall be in one cake.

Arminian

Twelve cakes of fine flour are baked, each two tenths of an ephah. Arminian readers note that the number twelve naturally suggests the twelve tribes of Israel.

The bread represents the whole people before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the twelve tribes represented. The bread is set before God on their behalf.

The measurement is substantial, and the WEB footnote gives a modern equivalent for the ephah.

Leviticus 24:6

6You shall set them in two rows, six on a row, on the pure gold table before the LORD.

Arminian

The loaves are set in two rows of six on the pure gold table before the LORD. Arminian readers see orderliness in worship.

The table and bread are described in Exodus 25:23–30, where it is called the bread of the presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the bread is “before the LORD,” in his presence. It speaks of fellowship between God and his people.

Many Christians see in it a foreshadowing of Christ, the bread of life (John 6:35), as a reading of the type.

Leviticus 24:7

7You shall put pure frankincense on each row, that it may be to the bread for a memorial, even an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

Frankincense on each row serves as a memorial, an offering by fire. Arminian readers note the link with the memorial portion in the grain offering (Lev 2:2).

The incense rises to God while the bread remains for the priests.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the memorial as setting Israel before God and calling his covenant promises to remembrance on their behalf.

The frankincense is burned while the bread itself goes to the priests (v. 9), so the one offering serves both worship and the support of those who minister.

Leviticus 24:8

8Every Sabbath day he shall set it in order before the LORD continually. It is an everlasting covenant on the behalf of the children of Israel.

Arminian

The bread is renewed each Sabbath as an everlasting covenant for Israel. Arminian readers note that weekly renewal keeps the covenant before God.

The Sabbath rhythm ties this ritual to Israel’s whole calendar.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the covenant language: the bread is a sign of God’s commitment to his people.

Hebrews treats the earthly sanctuary as a shadow fulfilled in Christ (Heb 8:5; 9:2), which shapes how Christians read “everlasting” here.

Leviticus 24:9

9It shall be for Aaron and his sons. They shall eat it in a holy place; for it is most holy to him of the offerings of the LORD made by fire by a perpetual statute.”

Arminian

The bread is eaten by Aaron and his sons in a holy place, as most holy. Arminian readers note that holiness governs who eats and where (cf. Lev 6:16–18).

Jesus later refers to David eating this bread in need (1 Sam 21:1–6; Matt 12:3–4).

Reformed

Reformed readers also note Jesus’ use of David’s example to teach about mercy and the Son of Man’s authority (Matt 12:3–8).

The holiness rules show the seriousness of approaching God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
