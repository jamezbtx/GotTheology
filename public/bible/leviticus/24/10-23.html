Passage

Leviticus 24

Verses 10–23 · WEB

A man with an Israelite mother and an Egyptian father blasphemes the Name during a fight; he is held in custody until the LORD’s will is known, and the LORD commands that he be stoned outside the camp. The ruling is followed by laws on murder, killing animals, and injury, with proportional penalties applying equally to native-born and foreigner, and the people carry out the sentence. Both traditions read this as a sober case about the holiness of God’s name and equal justice within Israel’s civil law, and they share the view that its penalties belong to Israel’s covenant order; differences over how such laws apply today run within each tradition.

Leviticus 24:10

10The son of an Israelite woman, whose father was an Egyptian, went out among the children of Israel; and the son of the Israelite woman and a man of Israel strove together in the camp.

Arminian

The narrative introduces a man of mixed parentage in a fight in the camp. Arminian readers note that the text identifies him carefully, perhaps because the ruling will address whether the law applies to such a person.

A mixed multitude left Egypt with Israel (Exod 12:38).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the detail as preparing for the ruling in verse 22 that the same law applies to foreigner and native-born.

The story begins with an ordinary quarrel that turns into a grave offense.

Leviticus 24:11

11The son of the Israelite woman blasphemed the Name, and cursed; and they brought him to Moses. His mother’s name was Shelomith, the daughter of Dibri, of the tribe of Dan.

Arminian

He blasphemes the Name and curses. Arminian readers note that the law already forbade this (Exod 20:7; 22:28), and the text treats it as a serious, responsible act.

Naming his mother and her tribe roots the case in real community life.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the gravity of blasphemy against God’s name, which represents God himself.

The careful naming may underline that this was a member of the community, not an outsider.

Leviticus 24:12

12They put him in custody until the LORD’s will should be declared to them.

Arminian

They hold him until the LORD’s will is declared. Arminian readers note the community’s restraint: they do not act on anger but seek God’s direction (cf. Num 15:34).

The pause models seeking God before judging.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a people waiting for God’s word rather than improvising justice (cf. Num 9:8). The case was not plainly settled by earlier law, so they ask.

The authority for the judgment comes from God, not from the crowd or even from Moses’ own judgment.

Leviticus 24:13

13The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD answers Moses. Arminian readers note that God responds to the people’s honest inquiry and does not leave them to guess.

The ruling that follows carries divine authority and becomes a standing law for later cases (vv. 15–22).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God speaks to resolve a case not yet covered explicitly, and that his word governs his people’s judgments.

The formula marks direct revelation. The narrative case becomes the occasion for general law, a pattern also seen in Numbers 15:32–36.

Leviticus 24:14

14“Bring him who cursed out of the camp; and let all who heard him lay their hands on his head, and let all the congregation stone him.

Arminian

The blasphemer is taken outside the camp, witnesses lay hands on him, and the congregation stones him. Arminian readers note that the witnesses bear responsibility first (cf. Deut 17:7).

The community as a whole carries out the judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the laying on of hands as the witnesses’ testimony that the guilt rests on him.

Execution outside the camp may reflect keeping the camp holy. Christians recall that Jesus, condemned for alleged blasphemy, suffered outside the gate (Matt 26:65; Heb 13:12).

Leviticus 24:15

15You shall speak to the children of Israel, saying, ‘Whoever curses his God shall bear his sin.

Arminian

Whoever curses his God bears his sin. Arminian readers stress personal accountability: the guilt is the offender’s own.

The phrase recurs in Leviticus for those responsible for their own wrongdoing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see personal guilt before God. To curse God is to reject the Creator and covenant Lord himself.

“Bear his sin” places the responsibility squarely on the offender; the community does not share his guilt when it carries out God’s sentence.

Leviticus 24:16

16He who blasphemes the LORD’s name, he shall surely be put to death. All the congregation shall certainly stone him. The foreigner as well as the native-born shall be put to death when he blasphemes the Name.

Arminian

Blasphemy of the LORD’s name is a capital crime, for foreigner and native-born alike. Arminian readers note the equality before the law.

In the New Testament, Stephen is stoned on a blasphemy charge (Acts 7:58), and Jesus speaks of blasphemy and forgiveness (Mark 3:28–29).

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the holiness of God’s name. The severity reflects Israel’s status as a holy nation under God’s direct rule.

Many in both traditions hold that the civil penalties of Israel’s law are not binding on states today, though there is internal diversity on how the principles apply.

Leviticus 24:17

17“ ‘He who strikes any man mortally shall surely be put to death.

Arminian

Whoever strikes a person mortally is put to death. Arminian readers connect this with Genesis 9:6, which grounds the penalty in human beings bearing God’s image.

Human life has unique worth, so taking it is treated differently from any loss of property. Other laws distinguish deliberate killing from accidental killing (Num 35:9–28).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Genesis 9:6 behind the law. God protects human life because it bears his image.

Christian views on whether and how this applies to modern capital punishment vary within both traditions, so this is not an Arminian–Reformed line.

Leviticus 24:18

18He who strikes an animal mortally shall make it good, life for life.

Arminian

Killing an animal requires restitution, life for life. Arminian readers note the distinction between human and animal life.

Restitution, not death, is required for an animal.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note the distinction between human and animal life. Killing someone’s animal is a property loss and is made good by restitution.

Here “life for life” means replacing the animal, which shows that the phrase in this law is about fitting compensation, not literal killing in every case.

Leviticus 24:19

19If anyone injures his neighbor, it shall be done to him as he has done:

Arminian

Whoever injures a neighbor receives the same. Arminian readers see judicial proportion: the penalty matches the injury.

This principle limits revenge rather than encouraging it.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a principle of equity in judgment: the penalty must fit the offense, neither more nor less.

In a world prone to escalating feuds (cf. Gen 4:23–24), the law sets a limit. Both traditions treat this as shared ground.

Leviticus 24:20

20fracture for fracture, eye for eye, tooth for tooth. It shall be done to him as he has injured someone.

Arminian

Fracture for fracture, eye for eye, tooth for tooth (cf. Exod 21:23–25; Deut 19:21). Arminian readers see this as a rule for judges, limiting penalties to what is proportionate.

Jesus addresses this principle in Matthew 5:38–39, calling his followers not to personal retaliation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a judicial standard, not permission for private revenge (cf. Rom 12:19; 13:4).

Jesus’ teaching in Matthew 5:38–39 addresses personal response. Both traditions see this as shared ground; how literally the formula was applied in ancient Israel is discussed by interpreters.

Leviticus 24:21

21He who kills an animal shall make it good; and he who kills a man shall be put to death.

Arminian

The penalties for killing an animal and killing a person are restated. Arminian readers note that the repetition frames the laws on injury on both sides (vv. 17–18, 21).

The structure keeps the main distinction in view: animals are compensated, human life is protected by the gravest sanction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a careful literary structure in this section, which returns to its opening principles.

The distinction between human and animal life is reaffirmed, keeping the image of God at the center of the law on violence.

Leviticus 24:22

22You shall have one kind of law for the foreigner as well as the native-born; for I am the LORD your God.’ ”

Arminian

One law for foreigner and native-born, for I am the LORD your God. Arminian readers see God’s impartial justice (cf. Exod 12:49; Num 15:16).

The ruling answers the question raised by the blasphemer’s mixed parentage.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s own character as the ground of equal justice. The foreigner living in Israel is protected by the same law and accountable to it.

The phrase “I am the LORD your God” anchors the law in God himself, not in ethnic belonging.

Leviticus 24:23

23Moses spoke to the children of Israel; and they brought him who had cursed out of the camp, and stoned him with stones. The children of Israel did as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

The people carry out the sentence as the LORD commanded. Arminian readers note the community’s obedience to God’s revealed will in a hard case.

The narrative closes by returning to the blasphemer, so the laws in verses 15–22 are read in light of a real event.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience to God’s command, however sobering. The account shows the seriousness of God’s holiness in Israel’s covenant life.

Many in both traditions hold that Israel’s civil penalties are not carried over to the church or modern states, though they reason about this in different ways within each tradition.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
