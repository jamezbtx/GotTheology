Passage

Leviticus 15

Verses 19–33 · WEB

The second half of Leviticus 15 addresses women’s bodily discharges, both the normal monthly cycle and prolonged bleeding from illness. The rules closely parallel those for men in verses 2–18, with washing, waiting, and offerings for restoration after healing. Verse 31 explains the purpose: God’s tabernacle is among Israel, so ritual impurity must be handled carefully. The chapter treats women and their bodies with the same plain, non-moral categories as men; ritual uncleanness is not sin. Christians often recall Jesus healing the woman who had bled for twelve years.

Leviticus 15:19

19“ ‘If a woman has a discharge, and her discharge in her flesh is blood, she shall be in her impurity seven days. Whoever touches her shall be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

A woman with her regular monthly flow is in her impurity seven days, and anyone touching her is unclean until evening. This is a normal bodily cycle.

Arminian readers stress that menstruation is not sinful. The law treats it as ritual status, not moral fault.

Reformed

The rule parallels the men’s laws earlier in the chapter. The remedy for others is brief.

Reformed readers agree that ritual impurity is not the same as sin. Both traditions affirm the dignity of women and of the body God created.

Leviticus 15:20

20“ ‘Everything that she lies on in her impurity shall be unclean. Everything also that she sits on shall be unclean.

Arminian

Anything she lies or sits on during her impurity becomes unclean. The pattern mirrors verse 4.

Arminian readers see consistency between the laws for men and women. The rules are practical, not punitive.

Reformed

Bedding and seats are affected as with the man’s discharge. The parallel structure is deliberate.

Reformed readers see order and fairness in God’s instruction. Both traditions read this as shared ground on ritual categories that do not imply moral blame.

Leviticus 15:21

21Whoever touches her bed shall wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Whoever touches her bed must wash, bathe, and be unclean until evening. The remedy is simple.

Arminian readers see that family life continues with clear, temporary remedies. Touching a bed does not make anyone guilty; it only calls for washing.

Reformed

The same rule applies as for the man’s bed in verse 5. The law is balanced.

Reformed readers see God’s even-handed law for men and women. Both traditions agree that this impurity is brief and ritual, and that contact with family members is not sinful.

Leviticus 15:22

22Whoever touches anything that she sits on shall wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Touching anything she sits on requires washing, bathing, and waiting until evening. The rule repeats.

Arminian readers see clear guidance so that ordinary life can be lived faithfully. The rule protects the community without shaming the woman.

Reformed

The law mirrors verse 6. Consistency is maintained.

Reformed readers see God’s ordered care for the community. Both traditions read this as practical ritual instruction rather than a statement about anyone’s worth or morality.

Leviticus 15:23

23If it is on the bed, or on anything she sits on, when he touches it, he shall be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Whether on the bed or the seat, touching it brings uncleanness until evening. The rule is clarified.

Arminian readers see attention to real questions people would ask. Even indirect contact is explained so no one is left guessing.

Reformed

The verse removes ambiguity about indirect contact. The remedy is the same brief wait.

Reformed readers see the law’s concern for clarity. Both traditions share this reading and note again that ritual impurity is not the same as sin.

Leviticus 15:24

24“ ‘If any man lies with her, and her monthly flow is on him, he shall be unclean seven days; and every bed he lies on shall be unclean.

Arminian

If a man lies with her during her flow, he is unclean seven days, and his bed is unclean. The impurity is shared.

Arminian readers note this verse describes ritual consequences. Leviticus 18:19 and 20:18 address the act separately with stronger language.

Reformed

Here the law states a ritual effect: seven days of impurity. Other passages treat the matter differently.

Reformed readers likewise distinguish this ritual rule from the prohibitions elsewhere. Both traditions read the verse carefully without adding claims the text does not make.

Leviticus 15:25

25“ ‘If a woman has a discharge of her blood many days not in the time of her period, or if she has a discharge beyond the time of her period, all the days of the discharge of her uncleanness shall be as in the days of her period. She is unclean.

Arminian

A woman with prolonged or irregular bleeding is unclean as during her period, for as long as it lasts. This describes illness.

Arminian readers often recall the woman in the Gospels who had bled for twelve years and reached out to touch Jesus (Mark 5:25–34).

Reformed

Long-term bleeding extends the ritual impurity. The condition could isolate a woman for years.

Reformed readers likewise see Jesus’ healing of that woman as compassionate and powerful: instead of being made unclean, he makes her whole. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 15:26

26Every bed she lies on all the days of her discharge shall be to her as the bed of her period. Everything she sits on shall be unclean, as the uncleanness of her period.

Arminian

Every bed and seat during her discharge is unclean, as during her period. The same rules extend.

Arminian readers see the real burden such illness placed on a woman. The law describes it honestly.

Reformed

The rules for regular menstruation apply to prolonged bleeding. The burden is real.

Reformed readers see the law exposing human weakness and need. Both traditions agree that the woman is not sinful because she is ill, and see compassion in Christ’s response.

Leviticus 15:27

27Whoever touches these things shall be unclean, and shall wash his clothes and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Whoever touches these things is unclean, washes, bathes, and waits until evening. The rule repeats.

Arminian readers see the community living carefully alongside those who are ill. The rules let the household live alongside illness with simple remedies.

Reformed

The same remedy applies for contact. The law is consistent.

Reformed readers see order in God’s instruction for a holy people. Both traditions read this as shared ground on temporary ritual uncleanness, which does not make anyone guilty before God.

Leviticus 15:28

28“ ‘But if she is cleansed of her discharge, then she shall count to herself seven days, and after that she shall be clean.

Arminian

If she is healed, she counts seven days and is clean. Restoration follows healing.

Arminian readers see hope written into the law. The impurity is not permanent. Her recovery is marked and celebrated by restoration.

Reformed

The seven days confirm her healing, as with the man in verse 13. The law is parallel.

Reformed readers see God’s gracious path back to full participation. Both traditions share this reading of restoration after illness.

Leviticus 15:29

29On the eighth day she shall take two turtledoves, or two young pigeons, and bring them to the priest, to the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

On the eighth day she brings two turtledoves or pigeons to the priest at the Tent of Meeting. She comes herself.

Arminian readers see the woman welcomed back to worship as fully as the man.

Reformed

The offering matches the man’s in verse 14. Men and women are treated alike in restoration.

Reformed readers see equal access to God’s presence. Both traditions share this reading of the woman’s dignity before God.

Leviticus 15:30

30The priest shall offer the one for a sin offering, and the other for a burnt offering; and the priest shall make atonement for her before the LORD for the uncleanness of her discharge.

Arminian

The priest offers the sin and burnt offerings, making atonement for her uncleanness. Restoration is complete.

Arminian readers note that atonement here addresses impurity, not moral guilt. She is restored to worship.

Reformed

Atonement is made ‘for the uncleanness of her discharge.’ This is purification.

Reformed readers agree that the offering does not imply she sinned. Both traditions see this as God’s provision for drawing near to him, and many connect it to the cleansing Christ brings.

Leviticus 15:31

31“ ‘Thus you shall separate the children of Israel from their uncleanness, so they will not die in their uncleanness when they defile my tabernacle that is among them.’ ”

Arminian

The purpose: to separate Israel from uncleanness so they will not die by defiling God’s tabernacle among them. The stakes are high because God dwells among them.

Arminian readers see God’s presence as the reason for the rules. Holiness protects, not just restricts.

Reformed

God’s tabernacle in Israel’s midst makes purity serious. Approaching in uncleanness is dangerous.

Reformed readers see the holiness of God at the center of the law. Both traditions agree, and Christians see Christ as the one who opens access to God.

Leviticus 15:32

32This is the law of him who has a discharge, and of him who has an emission of semen, so that he is unclean by it;

Arminian

This is the law for men with discharges and emissions. The summary begins.

Arminian readers see clear teaching gathered for the people’s understanding. The summary keeps the rules for men clear and memorable. It treats these conditions as ritual matters only.

Reformed

The closing summary lists the cases from verses 2–18. The law is orderly.

Reformed readers see God’s care in making his instructions plain. Both traditions read this summary as shared ground and as a reminder that these categories concern ritual status, not moral standing.

Leviticus 15:33

33and of her who has her period, and of a man or woman who has a discharge, and of him who lies with her who is unclean.

Arminian

The summary includes women’s periods, discharges, and a man who lies with an unclean woman. The chapter closes.

Arminian readers see the whole community covered equally by the law. No one is singled out for shame.

Reformed

The summary completes the list from verses 19–30. Men and women are named alike.

Reformed readers see God’s even-handed law for his people. Both traditions read the chapter as practical instruction for life near God’s presence, with ritual impurity distinct from sin.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
