Passage

Leviticus 15

Verses 1–18 · WEB

Leviticus 15 deals with bodily discharges, beginning with men. Verses 2–15 describe an abnormal, probably illness-related discharge, its effects on beds, seats, and those who touch them, and the offerings for restoration after healing. Verses 16–18 address normal emissions of semen, including marital intercourse, which require only washing and waiting until evening. The chapter treats the body plainly and without shame; the uncleanness it describes is ritual, not moral sin. Both traditions read it as God’s care for a holy people living near his sanctuary.

Leviticus 15:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses and to Aaron, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses and Aaron about bodily discharges. The topic is private, but God addresses it openly and plainly.

Arminian readers see that no part of human life is outside God’s concern. Holiness reaches ordinary bodily experience without shame.

Reformed

God gives this instruction to both lawgiver and priest. The priests will need to teach and apply it.

Reformed readers see God’s word ordering all of life, including the body. Both traditions read this as shared ground and handle it with respect.

Leviticus 15:2

2“Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘When any man has a discharge from his body, because of his discharge he is unclean.

Arminian

When a man has a discharge from his body, he is unclean. The context suggests an abnormal discharge, likely from illness.

Arminian readers stress that this uncleanness is ritual, not moral. The man has not sinned by being ill.

Reformed

The law describes a physical condition and its ritual effect. It does not accuse the man of wrongdoing.

Reformed readers agree that ritual impurity is not the same as moral sin. Both traditions see the law teaching that God’s holiness is distinct from ordinary bodily weakness.

Leviticus 15:3

3This shall be his uncleanness in his discharge: whether his body runs with his discharge, or his body has stopped from his discharge, it is his uncleanness.

Arminian

Whether the discharge is flowing or blocked, it is still uncleanness. The law defines the condition carefully.

Arminian readers see clarity that avoids confusion. The person knows where they stand.

Reformed

The precise definition prevents arbitrary judgments. The condition, not the person’s character, determines status.

Reformed readers see order in God’s law for a holy community. Both traditions share this reading of careful, non-moral ritual categories.

Leviticus 15:4

4“ ‘Every bed on which he who has the discharge lies shall be unclean; and everything he sits on shall be unclean.

Arminian

Any bed he lies on or seat he sits on becomes unclean. Uncleanness spreads through contact.

Arminian readers see a picture of how impurity affects surroundings. The rules protect others and the sanctuary.

Reformed

The law teaches that uncleanness is contagious in a ritual sense. Furniture becomes a carrier.

Reformed readers see the seriousness of approaching a holy God. Both traditions agree the rule is about ritual status and community care, not moral blame.

Leviticus 15:5

5Whoever touches his bed shall wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Whoever touches his bed must wash clothes, bathe, and be unclean until evening. The remedy is simple and temporary.

Arminian readers see that secondary contact is easily cleansed. The law is not harsh toward helpers or family.

Reformed

Touching the bed brings brief uncleanness. Washing and waiting resolve it.

Reformed readers see proportion in God’s law. Both traditions note that caring for a sick person is not sinful, even when it brings temporary ritual uncleanness.

Leviticus 15:6

6He who sits on anything on which the man who has the discharge sat shall wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Anyone who sits where the man sat must wash, bathe, and be unclean until evening. The pattern repeats.

Arminian readers see consistent, understandable rules. Daily life continues with clear remedies.

Reformed

The law applies the same rule to seats. Consistency marks the instructions.

Reformed readers see order and fairness. Both traditions share this reading of practical ritual instruction that keeps the community aware of God’s holiness.

Leviticus 15:7

7“ ‘He who touches the body of him who has the discharge shall wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Touching the man’s body brings the same temporary uncleanness. Direct contact is addressed plainly.

Arminian readers see that care for the sick was possible with simple remedies. Christians often recall Jesus touching the unclean and making them whole.

Reformed

Physical contact conveys ritual uncleanness until evening. The remedy is washing.

Reformed readers likewise see Jesus’ willingness to touch the unclean as a sign of his power to cleanse rather than be defiled. Both traditions share this connection without overclaiming.

Leviticus 15:8

8“ ‘If he who has the discharge spits on him who is clean, then he shall wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

If the man spits on a clean person, that person must wash, bathe, and be unclean until evening. Even small contact counts.

Arminian readers see the law making people mindful of one another’s ritual status.

Reformed

The rule covers accidental or minor contact. The remedy remains simple.

Reformed readers see attention to detail in a holy community. Both traditions read this as ritual instruction, not a moral judgment on either person.

Leviticus 15:9

9“ ‘Whatever saddle he who has the discharge rides on shall be unclean.

Arminian

Any saddle he rides on becomes unclean. Travel is covered too.

Arminian readers see the law addressing everyday activities realistically. Life does not stop, but it is lived carefully. The rules cover ordinary movement as well as rest.

Reformed

Even a saddle is included. The law is thorough about objects in contact with the discharge.

Reformed readers see God’s concern for purity in all areas. Both traditions share this reading of practical, temporary ritual rules.

Leviticus 15:10

10Whoever touches anything that was under him shall be unclean until the evening. He who carries those things shall wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Touching or carrying anything that was under him brings uncleanness until evening; carrying requires washing and bathing. Degrees of contact are distinguished.

Arminian readers see proportionate remedies that make the law livable.

Reformed

The verse distinguishes touching from carrying. Each has its own remedy.

Reformed readers see careful order in God’s instructions. Both traditions agree these are ritual categories, not moral ones, and that the remedies are simple and temporary.

Leviticus 15:11

11“ ‘Whomever he who has the discharge touches, without having rinsed his hands in water, he shall wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

If he touches someone without rinsing his hands, that person must wash and bathe. Rinsing hands limits the spread.

Arminian readers see personal responsibility for others’ purity. A simple act of care protects neighbours.

Reformed

Handwashing reduces the transfer of uncleanness. The law gives the man a practical way to protect others.

Reformed readers see love of neighbour expressed through ritual care. Both traditions share this reading of the law’s considerate purpose.

Leviticus 15:12

12“ ‘The earthen vessel, which he who has the discharge touches, shall be broken; and every vessel of wood shall be rinsed in water.

Arminian

A clay vessel he touches must be broken; a wooden one rinsed. Materials are treated differently.

Arminian readers see practical rules for household items. The law is detailed and concrete.

Reformed

Clay absorbs and cannot be cleansed, so it is broken; wood can be rinsed. The same distinction appears in Leviticus 11:32–33.

Reformed readers see consistency in the purity laws. Both traditions read this as ritual instruction for daily life.

Leviticus 15:13

13“ ‘When he who has a discharge is cleansed of his discharge, then he shall count to himself seven days for his cleansing, and wash his clothes; and he shall bathe his flesh in running water, and shall be clean.

Arminian

When the man is healed, he counts seven days, washes clothes, and bathes in running water, and is clean. Recovery leads to restoration.

Arminian readers see hope built into the law. Uncleanness is not permanent.

Reformed

The seven days confirm healing. Running water marks cleansing, as in chapter 14.

Reformed readers see God’s ordered path back to full participation. Both traditions share this reading of restoration after illness, with no hint of moral blame.

Leviticus 15:14

14“ ‘On the eighth day he shall take two turtledoves, or two young pigeons, and come before the LORD to the door of the Tent of Meeting, and give them to the priest.

Arminian

On the eighth day he brings two turtledoves or pigeons to the priest at the Tent of Meeting before the LORD. The offerings are modest.

Arminian readers see that restoration is accessible. The man returns to worship.

Reformed

The eighth day again marks a new beginning. The offering is within reach of most people.

Reformed readers see God’s kindness in requiring little for restoration. Both traditions read this as shared ground about God’s welcome after illness.

Leviticus 15:15

15The priest shall offer them, the one for a sin offering, and the other for a burnt offering. The priest shall make atonement for him before the LORD for his discharge.

Arminian

The priest offers a sin offering and a burnt offering, making atonement for his discharge. Atonement addresses the ritual uncleanness.

Arminian readers note that the sin offering here deals with impurity, not guilt for a sin. It restores access to God.

Reformed

Atonement is made ‘for his discharge,’ showing purification rather than forgiveness of a moral fault. The offering is often called a purification offering.

Reformed readers agree. Both traditions see this as God’s provision for approaching his presence after impurity.

Leviticus 15:16

16“ ‘If any man has an emission of semen, then he shall bathe all his flesh in water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

A man with an emission of semen must bathe and be unclean until evening. This is a normal bodily event.

Arminian readers stress that normal sexual function is not sinful. The law treats it as a matter of ritual status only.

Reformed

The remedy is simple: bathing and waiting until evening. No sacrifice is required.

Reformed readers likewise affirm that the body and sexuality are good gifts of God. Both traditions agree that ritual impurity here is not the same as moral sin.

Leviticus 15:17

17Every garment and every skin which the semen is on shall be washed with water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Garments or skins with semen on them must be washed and are unclean until evening. The rule is practical.

Arminian readers see ordinary cleanliness folded into ritual life. The law is matter-of-fact.

Reformed

Items are washed and wait until evening. The remedy is simple.

Reformed readers see God’s law addressing daily life without shame. Both traditions share this reading of ritual instruction that does not imply wrongdoing.

Leviticus 15:18

18If a man lies with a woman and there is an emission of semen, they shall both bathe themselves in water, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

When a husband and wife have intercourse, both bathe and are unclean until evening. Marital intimacy is assumed to be normal.

Arminian readers stress that the law does not condemn marital sex. It marks a ritual boundary related to approaching the sanctuary.

Reformed

The rule applies to both spouses equally. Bathing and waiting until evening are enough.

Reformed readers affirm marriage and sexuality as good in God’s design. Both traditions agree that this temporary impurity is ritual, not moral, and is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
