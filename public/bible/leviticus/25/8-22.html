Passage

Leviticus 25

Verses 8–22 · WEB

After seven Sabbath years, the fiftieth year is proclaimed a Jubilee with a trumpet on the Day of Atonement: liberty is proclaimed, people return to their family property, the land rests, and land sales are priced by the years left until the Jubilee so no one wrongs another. God promises enough in the sixth year to carry Israel through. Both traditions read this as law rooted in God’s ownership and redemption; whether the Jubilee was ever fully practiced is debated, and Christian readings of it as pointing to Christ (see Luke 4:18–19) are offered as readings.

Leviticus 25:8

8“ ‘You shall count off seven Sabbaths of years, seven times seven years; and there shall be to you the days of seven Sabbaths of years, even forty-nine years.

Arminian

Seven sevens of years, forty-nine years, are counted. Arminian readers note the pattern of sevens that runs through Israel’s calendar.

The counting builds toward a special year.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Sabbath structure magnified: seven days, seven years, and now seven times seven years.

Time itself is ordered by God’s rest. The count leads to a year of release that many Christians see as pointing beyond itself (cf. Isa 61:1–2), as a reading.

Leviticus 25:9

9Then you shall sound the loud trumpet on the tenth day of the seventh month. On the Day of Atonement you shall sound the trumpet throughout all your land.

Arminian

The trumpet sounds on the Day of Atonement. Arminian readers note the link between atonement and release.

Many readers see significance in liberty being proclaimed on the day of cleansing from sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note the timing: release is proclaimed on the day when atonement is made for the people’s sin (Lev 16:29–34).

Many Christians read this as pointing to freedom grounded in Christ’s atoning work. That is a reading of the canon, not something the verse itself states.

Leviticus 25:10

10You shall make the fiftieth year holy, and proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants. It shall be a jubilee to you; and each of you shall return to his own property, and each of you shall return to his family.

Arminian

The fiftieth year is holy; liberty is proclaimed and people return to property and family. Arminian readers see restoration of families and their inheritance.

Isaiah 61:1 also speaks of proclaiming liberty, and Jesus reads Isaiah 61:1–2 in Luke 4:18–19. Many readers see Jubilee echoes there.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Luke 4:18–19 as drawing on Jubilee imagery, and read Christ as bringing the liberty the Jubilee pictured.

That connection is a reading of the canon. The verse itself concerns land and family in Israel.

Leviticus 25:11

11That fiftieth year shall be a jubilee to you. In it you shall not sow, neither reap that which grows of itself, nor gather from the undressed vines.

Arminian

The Jubilee year is also a year without sowing or reaping, following directly after the forty-ninth year, itself a Sabbath year. Arminian readers note that this means two fallow years in a row.

The text anticipates the obvious worry and answers it in verses 20–22. Whether, and how fully, the Jubilee was ever practiced in Israel’s history is debated.

Reformed

Reformed readers see deepened dependence on God’s provision as the Sabbath year is extended.

They also note that later Scripture records Israel’s failure to keep the land’s Sabbaths (Lev 26:34–35; 2 Chron 36:21), though how far that applies to the Jubilee in particular is debated.

Leviticus 25:12

12For it is a jubilee; it shall be holy to you. You shall eat of its increase out of the field.

Arminian

The year is holy, and the people eat what the field yields directly. Arminian readers note that holiness here takes the form of rest and trust rather than extra ritual.

The food still comes from the land, but as God’s provision rather than the result of that year’s work.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s provision sustaining his people while they rest.

The Jubilee is set apart for God, and its holiness shapes the community’s economic life as well as its worship.

Leviticus 25:13

13“ ‘In this Year of Jubilee each of you shall return to his property.

Arminian

Each person returns to his property in the Year of Jubilee. Arminian readers see the restoration of each family’s inheritance as the heart of the Jubilee.

The repetition from verse 10 underlines the central point before the detailed rules begin.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see family inheritance restored by God’s law. Naboth’s refusal to sell his ancestral vineyard reflects how seriously inheritance was taken (1 Kings 21:3).

No family is meant to be permanently cut off from its share of the land.

Leviticus 25:14

14“ ‘If you sell anything to your neighbor, or buy from your neighbor, you shall not wrong one another.

Arminian

In buying and selling, Israelites must not wrong one another. Arminian readers see fairness required in every transaction.

Economic life falls under God’s moral law, not outside it.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see justice in trade as part of covenant faithfulness (cf. Prov 11:1).

The command introduces the pricing rules, which are designed to make wronging a neighbor harder.

Leviticus 25:15

15According to the number of years after the Jubilee you shall buy from your neighbor. According to the number of years of the crops he shall sell to you.

Arminian

The price depends on how many years remain until the Jubilee. Arminian readers note that what is actually sold is the use of the land for a set time.

The land itself returns, so the buyer is paying for harvests.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the land as God’s, so it is in effect leased rather than sold outright (cf. v. 23).

The system builds fairness into the market itself.

Leviticus 25:16

16According to the length of the years you shall increase its price, and according to the shortness of the years you shall diminish its price; for he is selling the number of the crops to you.

Arminian

More years mean a higher price, fewer years a lower one. Arminian readers note the logic stated in the verse: the seller is selling a number of crops.

Transparent pricing guards both parties from exploitation.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see justice in proportion.

What changes hands is a series of harvests, not the family’s inheritance, which belongs finally to God.

Leviticus 25:17

17You shall not wrong one another, but you shall fear your God; for I am the LORD your God.

Arminian

Do not wrong one another, but fear your God. Arminian readers note that reverence for God is the motive for fairness toward neighbors.

The repetition from verse 14 frames the pricing rules with a moral command.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the fear of God as the root of right dealing with others.

“I am the LORD your God” grounds the command in God’s own character and covenant claim.

Leviticus 25:18

18“ ‘Therefore you shall do my statutes, and keep my ordinances and do them; and you shall dwell in the land in safety.

Arminian

Keeping God’s statutes brings safety in the land. Arminian readers note the conditional promise.

Obedience and blessing are linked in the covenant (cf. Lev 26:3–13).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see covenant blessing promised to obedience. They usually read such promises as given within a relationship God established by grace, not as wages earned.

Safety in the land is God’s gift, and the curses of Leviticus 26 show the other side.

Leviticus 25:19

19The land shall yield its fruit, and you shall eat your fill, and dwell therein in safety.

Arminian

The land will yield its fruit, and they will eat their fill in safety. Arminian readers note the concrete promise of abundance attached to keeping God’s statutes.

God’s blessing reaches ordinary needs like food and security.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to provide for his covenant people.

The promise prepares for the question in verse 20 about how they will eat when they do not sow.

Leviticus 25:20

20If you said, “What shall we eat the seventh year? Behold, we shall not sow, nor gather in our increase;”

Arminian

The anticipated question: what shall we eat in the seventh year? Arminian readers note that God addresses real anxieties.

Jesus addresses similar worry in Matthew 6:31–33.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Matthew 6:31–33 in the background. God anticipates the question and invites trust rather than rebuking the asker.

The question is natural for farmers asked to leave fields unsown; the answer is God’s promise, not human calculation.

Leviticus 25:21

21then I will command my blessing on you in the sixth year, and it shall bear fruit for the three years.

Arminian

God will command blessing in the sixth year for three years’ supply. Arminian readers see God’s generous provision for those who obey.

The double portion of manna is similar (Exod 16:22–29).

Reformed

Reformed readers also note the parallel with the double manna before the Sabbath (Exod 16:22–29). God’s provision makes the commanded rest possible.

He “commands” his blessing (cf. Deut 28:8), so the harvest depends on his word rather than on the farmer’s effort alone.

Leviticus 25:22

22You shall sow the eighth year, and eat of the fruits from the old store until the ninth year. Until its fruits come in, you shall eat the old store.

Arminian

They sow in the eighth year and eat the old store until the ninth. Arminian readers note the extended time of provision.

Interpreters discuss how the three years of verse 21 are counted, especially when the Jubilee follows a seventh-year Sabbath.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sustained provision through the whole transition, from the sixth year until the new crop comes in.

The promise covers the full period of need. God’s care is not a single gift but continued faithfulness over time.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
