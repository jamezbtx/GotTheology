Passage

Leviticus 25

Verses 23–34 · WEB

Because the land belongs to the LORD and Israel lives on it as foreigners and temporary residents with him, land may not be sold permanently: a near kinsman may redeem it, the seller may buy it back, and otherwise it returns at the Jubilee. Houses in walled cities have a one-year redemption window, while village houses and Levite property follow special rules. Both traditions read this as law grounded in God’s ownership, family inheritance, and redemption, and many Christians see the kinsman-redeemer as pointing to Christ, as a reading.

Leviticus 25:23

23“ ‘The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine; for you are strangers and live as foreigners with me.

Arminian

The land is God’s; Israel lives as foreigners with him. Arminian readers stress that ownership belongs to God, and people are stewards (cf. Ps 24:1).

This grounds the redemption laws.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s ownership: Israel holds the land as his guests. Scripture later uses the same language of believers as strangers and sojourners (1 Chron 29:15; Heb 11:13; 1 Pet 2:11).

The land law teaches humility, dependence, and a loose grip on possessions.

Leviticus 25:24

24In all the land of your possession you shall grant a redemption for the land.

Arminian

Redemption must be allowed for all land. Arminian readers note that the right to buy back is written into every sale, so no family can be permanently shut out of its inheritance.

The principle stated here governs the detailed cases that follow (vv. 25–34).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the language of redemption, which becomes central to the Bible’s account of salvation, appears here in an economic and family setting.

That later theological use is built on concrete practices like this one, where something lost is bought back.

Leviticus 25:25

25“ ‘If your brother becomes poor, and sells some of his possessions, then his kinsman who is next to him shall come, and redeem that which his brother has sold.

Arminian

If a brother grows poor and sells part of his land, his nearest kinsman is to redeem it. Arminian readers note the family’s responsibility to act for a relative in need.

Ruth 4 and Jeremiah 32:6–15 show related practices of redeeming family land, though Ruth combines this with other customs.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the kinsman-redeemer. Many read Christ as the near kinsman who becomes our brother in order to redeem (Heb 2:11, 14–15), as a reading.

The law itself concerns keeping family land within the family.

Leviticus 25:26

26If a man has no one to redeem it, and he becomes prosperous and finds sufficient means to redeem it,

Arminian

If no kinsman can redeem the land but the seller later prospers, he may redeem it himself. Arminian readers note that the seller never loses the right to recover his inheritance.

Redemption can come through a relative or through the seller’s own recovery.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law’s mercy in keeping a way back open for the one who fell into poverty.

The provision covers situations where no family member is able or willing to help.

Leviticus 25:27

27then let him reckon the years since its sale, and restore the surplus to the man to whom he sold it; and he shall return to his property.

Arminian

The seller counts the years since the sale and refunds the balance to the buyer. Arminian readers note fairness to the buyer, who is repaid for the harvests he will not receive.

Recovery of the land does not come at the buyer’s unjust expense.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see equity on both sides. The poor man recovers his inheritance, and the buyer receives what is fair for the harvests he gives up.

The calculation follows the Jubilee pricing principle of verses 15–16.

Leviticus 25:28

28But if he isn’t able to get it back for himself, then what he has sold shall remain in the hand of him who has bought it until the Year of Jubilee. In the Jubilee it shall be released, and he shall return to his property.

Arminian

If the seller cannot redeem it, the land stays with the buyer until the Jubilee and is then released. Arminian readers note the safety net: no family’s loss of land is meant to be permanent.

The Jubilee guarantees what personal means could not.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s provision for restoration that does not depend on the poor person’s ability.

The release comes by God’s appointed year, not by the seller’s effort.

Leviticus 25:29

29“ ‘If a man sells a dwelling house in a walled city, then he may redeem it within a whole year after it has been sold. For a full year he shall have the right of redemption.

Arminian

A house in a walled city may be redeemed only within a year after its sale. Arminian readers note that urban property is treated differently from farmland.

City houses were not part of the agricultural inheritance in the same way.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note the distinction, which shows the law applying its principles with practical judgment.

The Jubilee rule is tied mainly to land and livelihood.

Leviticus 25:30

30If it isn’t redeemed within the space of a full year, then the house that is in the walled city shall be made sure in perpetuity to him who bought it, throughout his generations. It shall not be released in the Jubilee.

Arminian

If not redeemed within a year, the city house belongs permanently to the buyer and is not released at the Jubilee. Arminian readers note this exception to Jubilee release.

The law recognizes that different kinds of property serve different purposes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wise distinctions within the law. Not all property is treated alike.

The exception confirms that the Jubilee’s main concern is family land.

Leviticus 25:31

31But the houses of the villages which have no wall around them shall be accounted for with the fields of the country: they may be redeemed, and they shall be released in the Jubilee.

Arminian

Houses in unwalled villages are treated like the fields: they may be redeemed and are released at the Jubilee. Arminian readers note that village homes are bound up with the surrounding farmland.

Rural life ties home and inheritance together.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the agricultural link: unwalled village houses belong with the fields that sustain the family.

The Jubilee therefore protects both the family land and the homes that go with it, while walled-city houses follow a separate rule.

Leviticus 25:32

32“ ‘Nevertheless, in the cities of the Levites, the Levites may redeem the houses in the cities of their possession at any time.

Arminian

Levites may redeem houses in their cities at any time. Arminian readers note their special status: they received cities rather than a tribal territory (Num 35:1–8).

The law protects those set apart for God’s service.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see provision for the Levites, whose cities are listed in Joshua 21.

God cares for those devoted to his service by securing their homes.

Leviticus 25:33

33The Levites may redeem the house that was sold, and the city of his possession, and it shall be released in the Jubilee; for the houses of the cities of the Levites are their possession among the children of Israel.

Arminian

Levite houses are released at the Jubilee because they are the Levites’ possession among Israel. Arminian readers note that this keeps the Levites from losing their homes permanently.

The walled-city exception does not apply to them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Levites’ houses functioning like the fields of the other tribes.

Their inheritance is protected in a form suited to their calling.

Leviticus 25:34

34But the field of the pasture lands of their cities may not be sold, for it is their perpetual possession.

Arminian

The pasture lands around Levite cities may not be sold at all. Arminian readers note that this is their perpetual possession.

The Levites’ livelihood is put beyond the reach of the market.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a permanent provision for those who serve at God’s sanctuary.

The law guards their support so that their ministry is not undermined by poverty.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
