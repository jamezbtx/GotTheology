Passage

Leviticus 25

Verses 1–7 · WEB

On Mount Sinai the LORD commands that when Israel enters the land, the land itself is to keep a Sabbath every seventh year: no sowing, pruning, or organized harvest, with whatever grows on its own serving as food for the household, workers, foreigners, and animals. The law teaches that the land belongs to God and that Israel lives by his provision. Both traditions read it as social and economic law grounded in God’s ownership and care, with broad shared ground.

Leviticus 25:1

1The LORD said to Moses on Mount Sinai,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses on Mount Sinai. Arminian readers note that the setting places these laws within the covenant given at Sinai (cf. Lev 26:46).

The land laws are part of God’s covenant instruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the Sinai setting as anchoring these laws in the covenant God made with the people he redeemed from Egypt.

They are God’s law for his people’s life in his land, not merely economic policy, and their motive clauses keep pointing back to the LORD (vv. 17, 38, 55).

Leviticus 25:2

2“Speak to the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘When you come into the land which I give you, then the land shall keep a Sabbath to the LORD.

Arminian

When Israel enters the land God gives, the land is to keep a Sabbath. Arminian readers note that the land is God’s gift and is to be treated according to his rhythm.

The weekly Sabbath now extends to the land.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God gives the land and sets the terms for its use (cf. Ps 24:1). Israel receives the land as a gift before it ever works it.

The Sabbath principle, rooted in creation (Gen 2:2–3), shapes all of life, including agriculture and the economy.

Leviticus 25:3

3You shall sow your field six years, and you shall prune your vineyard six years, and gather in its fruits;

Arminian

Six years of sowing, pruning, and harvesting are permitted. Arminian readers note that the pattern mirrors the six working days of the week (Exod 20:9), so ordinary farming is affirmed, not suspected.

Rest in the seventh year makes sense only against the background of honest, diligent work in the other six.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the six-and-one rhythm of creation (Gen 2:2–3) written into the life of the land.

Labor is part of God’s good design, and its fruit is received from him. The law does not set work and worship against each other.

Leviticus 25:4

4but in the seventh year there shall be a Sabbath of solemn rest for the land, a Sabbath to the LORD. You shall not sow your field or prune your vineyard.

Arminian

The seventh year is a Sabbath of solemn rest for the land. Arminian readers note the trust required to stop farming for a year.

The law is repeated in shorter form in Exodus 23:10–11.

Reformed

Reformed readers see dependence on God’s provision taught through rest.

The Sabbath year differs in emphasis from the release of debts in Deuteronomy 15:1–2, though both occur in the seventh year.

Leviticus 25:5

5What grows of itself in your harvest you shall not reap, and you shall not gather the grapes of your undressed vine. It shall be a year of solemn rest for the land.

Arminian

Even what grows on its own is not harvested in the usual way, and the vines are left undressed. Arminian readers note that the owner gives up control of the produce for a year.

The point is not neglect of the land but trust in God, who gives the land its rest.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the true owner of the land’s increase, so the farmer’s claim is limited by God’s command.

The repeated phrase “solemn rest for the land” shows that the rest is the land’s, as the weekly Sabbath is for people and animals.

Leviticus 25:6

6The Sabbath of the land shall be for food for you; for yourself, for your servant, for your maid, for your hired servant, and for your stranger, who lives as a foreigner with you.

Arminian

The Sabbath produce feeds everyone: owner, servants, hired workers, and foreigners. Arminian readers see social concern: the vulnerable share in the land’s yield.

Exodus 23:11 also mentions the poor.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see care for the whole household, including servants, hired workers, and the foreigner living among Israel.

In the Sabbath year the owner eats from the same unworked produce as the rest. God’s provision is shared, not hoarded, and the year humbles the landowner.

Leviticus 25:7

7For your livestock also, and for the animals that are in your land, shall all its increase be for food.

Arminian

Livestock and wild animals also eat from what the land produces. Arminian readers note God’s care for his creatures, which the law protects directly.

The Sabbath year reaches beyond human beings to the wider creation (cf. Exod 23:11).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence over all creation, which he feeds (Ps 104:14, 27–28).

The law reflects the Creator’s care for the whole order he made, not only for Israel’s households.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
