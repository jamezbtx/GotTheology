Passage

Leviticus 25

Verses 35–55 · WEB

If a fellow Israelite becomes poor, Israel must support him without interest or profit, and if he sells himself he must be treated as a hired worker, not a slave, and released with his family at the Jubilee, because Israelites are the LORD’s servants whom he brought out of Egypt. The law allows Israelites to acquire slaves from the surrounding nations and resident foreigners, but forbids harsh rule over fellow Israelites, and provides for redeeming an Israelite who sells himself to a foreigner. Both traditions read the passage soberly in its ancient setting, grounding it in God’s redemption from Egypt, and share the view that the New Testament moves beyond this order (see Gal 3:28; Philem 16).

Leviticus 25:35

35“ ‘If your brother has become poor, and his hand can’t support himself among you, then you shall uphold him. He shall live with you like an alien and a temporary resident.

Arminian

If a brother becomes poor and cannot support himself, Israel must uphold him. Arminian readers see active compassion required toward a fellow Israelite.

He is to be supported as a resident alien would be, living among the community rather than pushed out of it.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see care for the poor as a covenant duty, not an optional charity.

The poor man remains “your brother” throughout the passage (vv. 35, 36, 39, 46, 47). His poverty does not cancel his place in God’s people.

Leviticus 25:36

36Take no interest from him or profit; but fear your God, that your brother may live among you.

Arminian

Take no interest or profit from him; fear your God. Arminian readers note the ban on profiting from a brother’s need (cf. Exod 22:25).

The fear of God restrains greed, and the purpose is stated: “that your brother may live among you.”

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Exodus 22:25 and Psalm 15:5. Deuteronomy 23:19–20 permits interest from a foreigner, which shows that this law concerns loans to needy covenant kin.

How these rules bear on modern commercial lending is debated within both traditions.

Leviticus 25:37

37You shall not lend him your money at interest, nor give him your food for profit.

Arminian

Do not lend money at interest or give food for profit. Arminian readers note the concrete application to the two things a poor person most needs.

The poor are not to be turned into a source of gain.

Reformed

Reformed readers see generosity commanded, an attitude Jesus extends in calling for lending without expecting return (Luke 6:35).

Nehemiah 5:1–13 records a later crisis in which this kind of exploitation had to be confronted.

Leviticus 25:38

38I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, to give you the land of Canaan, and to be your God.

Arminian

I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of Egypt. Arminian readers note that redemption grounds the ethics: the law is the response of a rescued people.

Israel received land and freedom as gifts, so they must not use a brother’s poverty against him.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see grace preceding law. God’s saving act comes first, and the commands follow from it.

The verse ties the economic laws to the exodus and to God’s purpose “to be your God,” the heart of the covenant.

Leviticus 25:39

39“ ‘If your brother has grown poor among you, and sells himself to you, you shall not make him to serve as a slave.

Arminian

A poor brother who sells himself must not be made to serve as a slave. Arminian readers see a firm line between debt service and slavery for an Israelite.

His dignity is protected even in severe poverty.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the protection of fellow Israelites.

The relationship of this law to the six-year release laws in Exodus 21:2–6 and Deuteronomy 15:12–18 is discussed by interpreters, and different harmonizations have been proposed.

Leviticus 25:40

40As a hired servant, and as a temporary resident, he shall be with you; he shall serve with you until the Year of Jubilee.

Arminian

He is to live as a hired servant and temporary resident and serve until the Jubilee. Arminian readers note the limits: he works, but as a worker, not as property.

His service is bounded in time and character.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a bounded arrangement meant to preserve a brother’s dignity in hardship. He is a hired worker and a resident, not property.

The Jubilee sets the end point, so no Israelite family is permanently absorbed into another’s household.

Leviticus 25:41

41Then he shall go out from you, he and his children with him, and shall return to his own family, and to the possession of his fathers.

Arminian

He and his children go free and return to their family and ancestral property. Arminian readers see full restoration of family and inheritance.

Poverty does not permanently remove a household from its place in Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the Jubilee’s restoring purpose. Release includes his children and his return to his family.

The ancestral possession is recovered, not merely the man’s personal freedom, so the household’s future is restored.

Leviticus 25:42

42For they are my servants, whom I brought out of the land of Egypt. They shall not be sold as slaves.

Arminian

They are God’s servants, brought out of Egypt; they must not be sold as slaves. Arminian readers stress that redemption from Egypt defines Israel’s identity.

God’s ownership forbids human ownership of his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s claim on his redeemed people.

Paul uses related language: believers are bought with a price and should not become slaves of men (1 Cor 7:23).

Leviticus 25:43

43You shall not rule over him with harshness, but shall fear your God.

Arminian

Do not rule over him harshly, but fear your God. Arminian readers see a limit set on any authority one person holds over another.

The New Testament gives masters a similar charge because they too have a Master (Eph 6:9; Col 4:1).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Colossians 4:1 in the background. Authority is held under God and answerable to him.

The fear of God restrains power, and the Hebrew word rendered “harshness” (perek) is the one used for Egypt’s ruthless treatment of Israel (Exod 1:13–14).

Leviticus 25:44

44“ ‘As for your male and your female slaves, whom you may have from the nations that are around you, from them you may buy male and female slaves.

Arminian

Slaves may be bought from the surrounding nations. Arminian readers acknowledge that this reflects an ancient institution permitted in Israel’s law.

It contrasts sharply with the protections for Israelites.

Reformed

Reformed readers also read this soberly in its historical setting.

The law regulates rather than originates the institution; there are protections elsewhere (Exod 21:16, 20–21, 26–27; Deut 23:15–16).

Leviticus 25:45

45Moreover, of the children of the aliens who live among you, of them you may buy, and of their families who are with you, which they have conceived in your land; and they will be your property.

Arminian

Children of resident foreigners may also be bought and become property. Arminian readers acknowledge the hard reality of the text and do not soften it.

The distinction from Israelite kin is stark, and it should be read soberly within its ancient setting.

Reformed

Reformed readers also face the text honestly as part of Israel’s ancient civil order.

They note that the New Testament condemns slave-traders (1 Tim 1:10) and speaks of a slave as a brother in Christ (Philem 16), pointing toward a different order.

Leviticus 25:46

46You may make them an inheritance for your children after you, to hold for a possession. Of them you may take your slaves forever, but over your brothers the children of Israel you shall not rule, one over another, with harshness.

Arminian

Foreign slaves may be passed on as an inheritance, but Israelites are not to rule harshly over one another. Arminian readers note the stark contrast between foreign slaves and Israelite kin.

Many readers see the New Testament moving toward brotherhood in Christ that breaks down such divisions (Gal 3:28; Philem 16).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see that trajectory and read the law soberly in its ancient setting.

Readers in both traditions generally treat this law as part of Israel’s civil order rather than a pattern to follow, though they explain the law’s relation to Christians in different ways within each tradition.

Leviticus 25:47

47“ ‘If an alien or temporary resident with you becomes rich, and your brother beside him has grown poor, and sells himself to the stranger or foreigner living among you, or to a member of the stranger’s family,

Arminian

If an Israelite sells himself to a resident foreigner who has become rich, the law still provides for him. Arminian readers note that Israelites are protected even under foreign masters.

The right of redemption remains.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s concern for his people in every circumstance, including when a foreigner holds economic power.

The law anticipates situations where roles are reversed and keeps the Israelite’s right of redemption intact.

Leviticus 25:48

48after he is sold he may be redeemed. One of his brothers may redeem him;

Arminian

After the sale he may be redeemed; one of his brothers may redeem him. Arminian readers note the duty of kin to act.

Redemption is a family responsibility, not left to chance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the kinsman-redeemer pattern again.

Many Christians read Christ in this pattern as the one who redeems his brothers (Heb 2:11, 14–15), as a reading.

Leviticus 25:49

49or his uncle, or his uncle’s son, may redeem him, or any who is a close relative to him of his family may redeem him; or if he has grown rich, he may redeem himself.

Arminian

An uncle, a cousin, or another close relative may redeem him, or he may redeem himself if he prospers. Arminian readers note the wide circle of responsibility and the possibility of self-redemption.

The law keeps every path to freedom open.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the family circle at work.

They note that in the wider biblical story redemption is chiefly accomplished by another on the person’s behalf, while the law here allows both.

Leviticus 25:50

50He shall reckon with him who bought him from the year that he sold himself to him to the Year of Jubilee. The price of his sale shall be according to the number of years; he shall be with him according to the time of a hired servant.

Arminian

The price is calculated from the sale to the Jubilee, and he is treated like a hired worker. Arminian readers note that the foreign buyer cannot charge more than the remaining service is worth.

The Jubilee principle governs the price.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see equity applied across ethnic lines. The foreign buyer is bound by the same Jubilee arithmetic as an Israelite.

The man’s status is that of a hired worker, not a permanent slave.

Leviticus 25:51

51If there are yet many years, according to them he shall give back the price of his redemption out of the money that he was bought for.

Arminian

If many years remain, he repays more of the purchase price. Arminian readers note the proportional calculation.

The buyer is treated fairly while the man’s freedom is secured.

Reformed

Reformed readers see justice in the arithmetic: the redemption price matches the service still owed.

The calculation applies the rule of verse 50 and keeps the buyer from profiting unfairly from a brother’s freedom.

Leviticus 25:52

52If there remain but a few years to the year of jubilee, then he shall reckon with him; according to his years of service he shall give back the price of his redemption.

Arminian

If only a few years remain, he repays less. Arminian readers note the symmetry with verse 51.

The nearer the Jubilee, the cheaper the redemption.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see equity. As the Jubilee approaches, the cost of freedom steadily falls.

The law treats every case by the same measure, whether many or few years remain.

Leviticus 25:53

53As a servant hired year by year shall he be with him. He shall not rule with harshness over him in your sight.

Arminian

He is to be treated as a hired worker, and the master must not rule harshly “in your sight.” Arminian readers note the community’s responsibility to watch for abuse.

Israel must not look away from mistreatment of a brother.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see community oversight. The phrase “in your sight” makes the whole community responsible.

Even a foreign master living in Israel is under Israel’s law, and harsh treatment of a brother may not be tolerated.

Leviticus 25:54

54If he isn’t redeemed by these means, then he shall be released in the Year of Jubilee: he and his children with him.

Arminian

If not redeemed, he and his children are released in the Jubilee. Arminian readers see the guarantee of freedom.

No Israelite was meant to stay in permanent bondage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Jubilee release as certain and not dependent on the man’s resources or relatives.

God’s law ensures that he and his children return. Release finally rests on God’s appointed year, not on human means.

Leviticus 25:55

55For to me the children of Israel are servants; they are my servants whom I brought out of the land of Egypt. I am the LORD your God.

Arminian

The children of Israel are God’s servants whom he brought out of Egypt. Arminian readers stress the redemption that defines Israel.

The chapter ends with its theological foundation.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see God’s ownership of his redeemed people as the final reason for every protection in the chapter.

Many Christians read this in light of redemption in Christ, in which believers become God’s servants (Rom 6:22), as a reading.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
