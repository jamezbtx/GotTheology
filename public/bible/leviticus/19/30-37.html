Passage

Leviticus 19

Verses 30–37 · WEB

The chapter closes with Sabbath and sanctuary, rejection of mediums, honor for the elderly, love for the foreigner, and honest weights and measures. The final reminder that the LORD brought Israel out of Egypt grounds ethics in redemption. Both traditions share this reading: grace precedes and motivates obedience, and love for the stranger reflects Israel’s own history.

Leviticus 19:30

30“ ‘You shall keep my Sabbaths, and reverence my sanctuary; I am the LORD.

Arminian

Keep Sabbaths and reverence the sanctuary. Arminian readers see holy time and holy space honored. Worship shapes life. Sabbath and sanctuary belong together as marks of a people devoted to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see reverence for God’s presence. Views on Sabbath vary within both traditions. The reverence is shared. The command also echoes verse 3, framing the chapter with worship.

Leviticus 19:31

31“ ‘Don’t turn to those who are mediums, nor to the wizards. Don’t seek them out, to be defiled by them. I am the LORD your God.

Arminian

Do not turn to mediums or wizards. Arminian readers see reliance on God alone for guidance. Seeking spirits defiles. The LORD alone is Israel’s guide, and other sources of guidance lead astray.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the occult as rival worship. Both traditions share this reading. Deuteronomy 18:10–12 lists similar practices. Turning to mediums betrays trust in the LORD.

Leviticus 19:32

32“ ‘You shall rise up before the gray head and honor the face of the elderly; and you shall fear your God. I am the LORD.

Arminian

Rise before the elderly and honor them; fear God. Arminian readers see respect for age as part of holiness. Honoring the old reflects reverence for God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see dignity given to the elderly. Both traditions share this reading. The fear of God is linked to respect for those whose age gives them honor in the community.

Leviticus 19:33

33“ ‘If a stranger lives as a foreigner with you in your land, you shall not do him wrong.

Arminian

Do not wrong the foreigner. Arminian readers see protection for vulnerable outsiders. God cares for the stranger. Foreigners were especially vulnerable to mistreatment in ancient societies.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see justice extended beyond Israel. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. Exodus 22:21 and 23:9 give the same command, grounding it in Israel’s own experience.

Leviticus 19:34

34The stranger who lives as a foreigner with you shall be to you as the native-born among you, and you shall love him as yourself; for you lived as foreigners in the land of Egypt. I am the LORD your God.

Arminian

Treat the foreigner as native-born and love him as yourself, remembering Egypt. Arminian readers see neighbor love extended to outsiders. Memory of grace shapes compassion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see redemption motivating love for the stranger. Both traditions share this reading. Love for the stranger extends verse 18 beyond Israel to anyone living among them.

Leviticus 19:35

35“ ‘You shall do no unrighteousness in judgment, in measures of length, of weight, or of quantity.

Arminian

Do no injustice in measures of length, weight, or quantity. Arminian readers see honesty in commerce as holiness. Fraud wrongs neighbors. God cares about fairness in ordinary trade.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s justice in everyday trade. Both traditions share this reading. Honest measures protect buyers who cannot verify what they are given, especially the poor.

Leviticus 19:36

36You shall have just balances, just weights, a just ephah, and a just hin. I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt.

Arminian

Use just balances and measures, for the LORD brought you out of Egypt. Arminian readers see redemption grounding ethics. The WEB footnotes estimate the ephah and hin; exact ancient values are approximate.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see grace preceding obedience. Both traditions share this reading. The reminder of the Exodus grounds honest trade in the God who freed Israel from oppression.

Leviticus 19:37

37“ ‘You shall observe all my statutes and all my ordinances, and do them. I am the LORD.’ ”

Arminian

Observe all God’s statutes and ordinances. Arminian readers see a closing call to wholehearted obedience. The refrain ends the chapter. Obedience is to be complete, not selective.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the law as the shape of a grateful life. Both traditions share this reading. The final refrain I am the LORD gathers the chapter’s many commands under God’s authority.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
