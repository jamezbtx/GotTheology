Passage

Leviticus 19

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Leviticus 19 gathers wide-ranging commands under one heading: be holy, for the LORD your God is holy. Family honor, Sabbath, rejection of idols, proper sacrifice, and leaving harvest edges for the poor all belong together. Both traditions see holiness here as touching worship and daily life alike, and 1 Peter 1:15–16 applies the call to the church.

Leviticus 19:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses again. Arminian readers see a new collection of instruction for the whole community. God’s word continues to shape Israel’s life.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see divine authority behind the commands that follow. Both traditions read the opening as shared ground. The chapter that follows is often seen as a summary of the law’s heart.

Leviticus 19:2

2“Speak to all the congregation of the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘You shall be holy; for I, the LORD your God, am holy.

Arminian

Israel must be holy because the LORD is holy. Arminian readers see holiness as a real calling for God’s people, enabled by grace. Wesleyan Arminians especially stress growth in holiness of heart and life.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see holiness as the goal of redemption, worked out through sanctification. 1 Peter 1:16 applies the verse to the church. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 19:3

3“ ‘Each one of you shall respect his mother and his father. You shall keep my Sabbaths. I am the LORD your God.

Arminian

Respect parents and keep the Sabbath. Arminian readers see family honor and worship placed side by side at the head of the list. Holiness begins in the home and in honoring God’s time.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fifth and fourth commandments echoed. Views on Sabbath observance today vary within both traditions. The respect for parents is shared ground.

Leviticus 19:4

4“ ‘Don’t turn to idols, nor make molten gods for yourselves. I am the LORD your God.

Arminian

Do not turn to idols or make molten gods. Arminian readers see the first and second commandments restated. True holiness rejects false worship. Idols cannot save.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see idolatry as the root of other sins. The memory of the golden calf lies behind molten gods. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 19:5

5“ ‘When you offer a sacrifice of peace offerings to the LORD, you shall offer it so that you may be accepted.

Arminian

Peace offerings must be offered so as to be accepted. Arminian readers see worship following God’s instructions. Acceptance depends on approaching God his way. Peace offerings expressed fellowship with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the regulation of worship by God’s word. Christians in both traditions read the sacrifices as fulfilled in Christ. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 19:6

6It shall be eaten the same day you offer it, and on the next day. If anything remains until the third day, it shall be burned with fire.

Arminian

The offering is eaten on the first and second days, and leftovers are burned on the third. Arminian readers see clear limits on the sacred meal. The rule guards against treating holy food as ordinary.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see holiness protected by specific boundaries. Both traditions read this as a ceremonial regulation. The time limits may also have protected the meal from spoiling, though the text stresses holiness rather than hygiene.

Leviticus 19:7

7If it is eaten at all on the third day, it is an abomination. It will not be accepted;

Arminian

Eating on the third day is an abomination and not accepted. Arminian readers see disregard for God’s instructions undermining the offering. Careless worship is no small matter.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see that worship must follow God’s appointed order. Both traditions share this reading. The verse warns against disregarding the limits God set, a principle of reverence shared by both traditions.

Leviticus 19:8

8but everyone who eats it shall bear his iniquity, because he has profaned the holy thing of the LORD, and that soul shall be cut off from his people.

Arminian

Whoever eats it bears iniquity and is cut off for profaning what is holy. Arminian readers see the seriousness of profaning sacred things. Holiness carries responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the gravity of treating God’s holy things lightly. Both traditions read this as shared ground. The penalty of being cut off underlines how seriously Israel was to treat holy things.

Leviticus 19:9

9“ ‘When you reap the harvest of your land, you shall not wholly reap the corners of your field, neither shall you gather the gleanings of your harvest.

Arminian

Leave the corners of the field and do not gather the gleanings. Arminian readers see God building care for the poor into economic life. Generosity is structured, not left to impulse.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s concern for the needy in Israel’s law. The book of Ruth shows this law in practice. Both traditions share this reading.

Leviticus 19:10

10You shall not glean your vineyard, neither shall you gather the fallen grapes of your vineyard. You shall leave them for the poor and for the foreigner. I am the LORD your God.

Arminian

Leave grapes for the poor and the foreigner. Arminian readers see provision for the vulnerable. The refrain grounds generosity in who God is. God himself is the reason for the command.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see love for neighbor expressed in ordinary farming. Both traditions see the principle of generosity carrying into Christian ethics. Leaving gleanings preserves the dignity of the poor, who gather for themselves.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
