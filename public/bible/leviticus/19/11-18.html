Passage

Leviticus 19

Verses 11–18 · WEB

These commands address honesty, fair wages, care for the disabled, impartial justice, and the heart’s posture toward a neighbor. The unit climaxes in verse 18: you shall love your neighbor as yourself. Jesus names this as the second great commandment, and Paul and James echo it. Arminian and Reformed readers stand on shared ground throughout.

Leviticus 19:11

11“ ‘You shall not steal. “ ‘You shall not lie. “ ‘You shall not deceive one another.

Arminian

Do not steal, lie, or deceive one another. Arminian readers see honesty as essential to community life. The commands restate the eighth and ninth commandments.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see truthfulness grounded in God’s character. Both traditions share this reading. The source prints the three commands as separate lines, joined here.

Leviticus 19:12

12“ ‘You shall not swear by my name falsely, and profane the name of your God. I am the LORD.

Arminian

Do not swear falsely by God’s name and profane it. Arminian readers see oaths as binding before God. Using his name to deceive dishonors him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the third commandment applied. Jesus addresses oaths in Matthew 5:33–37. Both traditions share this reading. Truthfulness in speech honors the God whose name is invoked in oaths.

Leviticus 19:13

13“ ‘You shall not oppress your neighbor, nor rob him. “ ‘The wages of a hired servant shall not remain with you all night until the morning.

Arminian

Do not oppress or rob your neighbor, and pay hired workers promptly. Arminian readers see justice for laborers who depend on daily wages. Delay can harm the poor.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see economic justice as part of holiness. James 5:4 condemns withheld wages. Both traditions share this reading. Prompt payment protects those who live from day to day on their wages.

Leviticus 19:14

14“ ‘You shall not curse the deaf, nor put a stumbling block before the blind; but you shall fear your God. I am the LORD.

Arminian

Do not curse the deaf or trip the blind; fear God. Arminian readers see protection for those who cannot defend themselves. God sees what the victim cannot.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fear of God restraining cruelty toward the disabled. Both traditions share this reading. Cruelty toward those who cannot see or hear is an offense against God, who sees all.

Leviticus 19:15

15“ ‘You shall do no injustice in judgment. You shall not be partial to the poor, nor show favoritism to the great; but you shall judge your neighbor in righteousness.

Arminian

Judge impartially, favoring neither poor nor great. Arminian readers see justice that does not bend to sympathy or power. Fairness reflects God’s own impartiality. Neither class nor pity may tilt the scales.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see righteousness in judgment as essential. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. Partiality in either direction corrupts justice, so judges must decide according to what is right.

Leviticus 19:16

16“ ‘You shall not go around as a slanderer among your people. “ ‘You shall not endanger the life of your neighbor. I am the LORD.

Arminian

Do not slander or endanger your neighbor’s life. Arminian readers see words and actions that threaten others condemned. The WEB footnote notes the Hebrew literally says blood, suggesting standing against a neighbor’s life.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see protection of reputation and life. The exact sense of the second line is debated. Both traditions share the moral point. Gossip harms communities.

Leviticus 19:17

17“ ‘You shall not hate your brother in your heart. You shall surely rebuke your neighbor, and not bear sin because of him.

Arminian

Do not hate your brother in your heart, but rebuke him so you do not share his sin. Arminian readers see honest correction as an act of love. Hidden hatred is forbidden.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see love expressed in loving confrontation, as in Matthew 18:15. Both traditions share this reading. Loving rebuke prevents resentment from festering and protects the community from shared guilt.

Leviticus 19:18

18“ ‘You shall not take vengeance, nor bear any grudge against the children of your people; but you shall love your neighbor as yourself. I am the LORD.

Arminian

Do not take vengeance or bear a grudge; love your neighbor as yourself. Arminian readers see the heart of the law here, as Jesus teaches in Matthew 22:39. Love is enabled by grace and expressed in action.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see this command as summarizing the second table of the law, echoed in Romans 13:9, Galatians 5:14, and James 2:8. Both traditions stand on shared ground here.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
