Passage

Leviticus 9

Verses 1–7 · WEB

On the eighth day, after the seven days of ordination (8:33), Aaron begins his public ministry. Moses gives instructions for offerings for Aaron himself and for the people, and he attaches a promise: today the LORD will appear, and his glory will be seen.

Leviticus 9:1

1On the eighth day, Moses called Aaron and his sons, and the elders of Israel;

Arminian

“The eighth day” follows the seven days of consecration. A new stage begins: the priests now start their ministry.

Arminian readers see God’s timing in bringing his people into ordered worship.

Reformed

The eighth day marks the inauguration of the priestly service. Reformed readers note the movement from preparation to ministry.

Some interpreters in both traditions see eighth-day symbolism of new beginning; the text itself simply marks the sequence.

Leviticus 9:2

2and he said to Aaron, “Take a calf from the herd for a sin offering, and a ram for a burnt offering, without defect, and offer them before the LORD.

Arminian

Aaron must first bring a sin offering and burnt offering for himself.

Arminian readers note that the newly ordained priest still stands in need of atonement.

Reformed

The high priest offers for himself before he offers for the people (cf. Heb 5:3).

Reformed readers highlight the limitation of the Aaronic priesthood that Hebrews draws out. Arminian readers share this reading.

Leviticus 9:3

3You shall speak to the children of Israel, saying, ‘Take a male goat for a sin offering; and a calf and a lamb, both a year old, without defect, for a burnt offering;

Arminian

The people bring offerings too: a goat for sin and animals for burnt offering.

Arminian readers see the whole community invited into renewed fellowship with God.

Reformed

The people’s offerings correspond to Aaron’s, showing that both priest and people need atonement and dedication.

Reformed readers note the covenant community gathered before God. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 9:4

4and a bull and a ram for peace offerings, to sacrifice before the LORD; and a meal offering mixed with oil: for today the LORD appears to you.’ ”

Arminian

Peace offerings and a meal offering complete the set, and the reason is given: “today the LORD appears to you.”

Arminian readers see the offerings as preparation for meeting God.

Reformed

The promise of God’s appearing frames the whole day. Reformed readers stress that the sacrifices are the God-given way to receive his presence safely.

Both traditions agree that God’s appearing is the goal of the ritual.

Leviticus 9:5

5They brought what Moses commanded before the Tent of Meeting. All the congregation came near and stood before the LORD.

Arminian

The people bring what was commanded and stand before the LORD at the Tent of Meeting. This is the whole congregation’s first public approach after the priests’ consecration in chapter 8.

Arminian readers note the people’s ready participation: worship is not left to the priests alone.

Reformed

The congregation “came near and stood before the LORD,” language of approach to God’s presence.

Reformed readers see the covenant people assembled for worship. Both traditions read the scene in the same way.

Leviticus 9:6

6Moses said, “This is the thing which the LORD commanded that you should do; and the LORD’s glory shall appear to you.”

Arminian

Moses ties obedience to the promise of God’s glory. Doing what God commanded is the setting for meeting him.

Arminian readers see a genuine relationship between obedience and blessing.

Reformed

God’s glory will appear in connection with the worship he has commanded.

Reformed readers stress that the appearing is God’s gracious initiative, received through his appointed means. Both traditions affirm that obedience and blessing are joined here, while grounding both in God’s grace.

Leviticus 9:7

7Moses said to Aaron, “Draw near to the altar, and offer your sin offering, and your burnt offering, and make atonement for yourself, and for the people; and offer the offering of the people, and make atonement for them, as the LORD commanded.”

Arminian

Aaron makes atonement “for yourself, and for the people.” The priest’s work serves the whole congregation.

Arminian readers often see in the priest’s ministry for all the people a pattern fulfilled in Christ, whose atoning sacrifice is offered for all (1 John 2:2; 1 Tim 2:6) and received by faith.

Reformed

The high priest acts on behalf of the covenant people of God. Hebrews draws on this pattern for Christ’s priesthood, while noting that unlike Aaron he offered no sacrifice for himself (Heb 7:27).

Many Reformed readers see the unity of the priest’s offering and his intercession for a defined people as a pattern for Christ’s work for those the Father gives him (cf. John 17:9). Both traditions recognize that Leviticus 9 itself does not settle the question of the atonement’s extent.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
