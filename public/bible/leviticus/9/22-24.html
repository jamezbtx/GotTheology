Passage

Leviticus 9

Verses 22–24 · WEB

The inaugural service ends with blessing, the appearing of the LORD’s glory, and fire from before the LORD consuming the offerings on the altar. The scene answers the promise of 9:4 and 9:6 and parallels the glory filling the tabernacle at its completion (Exodus 40:34–35). The next chapter uses similar wording, fire coming out from before the LORD, for the judgment on Nadab and Abihu (10:1–2).

Leviticus 9:22

22Aaron lifted up his hands toward the people, and blessed them; and he came down from offering the sin offering, and the burnt offering, and the peace offerings.

Arminian

Aaron lifts his hands and blesses the people. The text does not give the words; the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24–26 is a natural comparison but is not stated here.

Arminian readers see blessing as the goal of sacrifice: God wants to bless his people.

Reformed

Sacrifice ends in blessing. Many interpreters in both traditions note Luke 24:50–51, where Jesus lifts his hands and blesses his disciples as he is taken up.

Reformed readers see Christ fulfilling the priestly work of blessing. Arminian readers share this reading.

Leviticus 9:23

23Moses and Aaron went into the Tent of Meeting, and came out, and blessed the people; and the LORD’s glory appeared to all the people.

Arminian

Moses and Aaron go into the Tent and come out to bless the people again. Then the LORD’s glory appears, as promised in 9:6.

Arminian readers see God keeping his word to a people who have obeyed.

Reformed

The promised glory appears “to all the people.” Reformed readers stress God’s faithfulness to his promise and his gracious condescension in dwelling among his people.

Both traditions see the appearing as an act of grace.

Leviticus 9:24

24Fire came out from before the LORD, and consumed the burnt offering and the fat upon the altar. When all the people saw it, they shouted, and fell on their faces.

Arminian

Fire from before the LORD consumes the offering. The people shout and fall on their faces in awe and joy.

Arminian readers see God’s acceptance of the worship and a fitting response of reverent praise. Similar divine fire marks acceptance at the temple dedication (2 Chr 7:1) and at Mount Carmel (1 Kgs 18:38).

Reformed

The fire shows God’s approval of the sacrifice and his presence with his people. Reformed readers note that the same holy fire will strike Nadab and Abihu in 10:2, showing that God’s presence brings both blessing and judgment.

Arminian readers also draw this connection. Both traditions read worship and awe as the right response to God’s holy presence.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
