Passage

Leviticus 11

Verses 41–47 · WEB

The chapter closes by forbidding creeping things and then grounding all the food laws in God’s own holiness: “be holy, for I am holy,” tied to his redemption of Israel from Egypt. A summary states the law’s purpose, to distinguish between unclean and clean. Peter quotes the holiness command (1 Peter 1:16), and both Arminian and Reformed readers hear its call as still binding even though the dietary rules that once expressed it have been fulfilled in Christ.

Leviticus 11:41

41“ ‘Every creeping thing that creeps on the earth is an abomination. It shall not be eaten.

Arminian

Creeping things are an abomination and must not be eaten. Arminian readers note that the chapter returns to eating after the contact rules. Both traditions agree that the prohibition completes the dietary boundaries. The rule sets the final boundary before the chapter states its reason.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the section closes the food laws with creeping things. Shared ground: the prohibition marks the final category, and the chapter moves toward its theological climax in God’s holiness. The word abomination again marks something Israel must treat as off limits.

Leviticus 11:42

42Whatever goes on its belly, and whatever goes on all fours, or whatever has many feet, even all creeping things that creep on the earth, them you shall not eat; for they are an abomination.

Arminian

Creatures that go on their belly, on all fours, or with many feet are forbidden. Arminian readers note the comprehensive description. Some readers connect creatures going on the belly with the serpent of Genesis 3:14, though the text does not say so. Both traditions avoid pressing such connections.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the comprehensive categories and avoid overconfident symbolic readings. The possible echo of Genesis 3:14 is an inference, not a stated link. Shared ground: the rule sets a clear boundary, and its rationale is given in the following verses.

Leviticus 11:43

43You shall not make yourselves abominable with any creeping thing that creeps. You shall not make yourselves unclean with them, that you should be defiled by them.

Arminian

Israel must not make itself abominable or unclean with creeping things. Arminian readers note that the people are responsible for their own purity. Both traditions agree that the language moves from describing animals to addressing Israel’s own condition.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the focus shifts from the animals to the people, preparing for the holiness command. Shared ground: Israel’s practice of purity reflected their identity as God’s people, and the rules served to shape that identity.

Leviticus 11:44

44For I am the LORD your God. Sanctify yourselves therefore, and be holy; for I am holy. You shall not defile yourselves with any kind of creeping thing that moves on the earth.

Arminian

God grounds the laws in his identity: “I am the LORD your God,” and calls Israel to sanctify themselves and be holy, for he is holy. Arminian readers stress the call to active, wholehearted holiness, and many in the Wesleyan tradition see the possibility of entire sanctification in this life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see holiness as God’s call and gift: he sanctifies his people and calls them to pursue holiness. They typically hold that sanctification is progressive and remains incomplete in this life. Both traditions see Peter’s use of this command (1 Peter 1:16) as binding on Christians.

Key contrast Both affirm that God’s holiness is the ground and pattern of his people’s holiness, that grace makes holiness possible, and that believers must pursue it. Wesleyan Arminians commonly teach that believers may be made perfect in love in this life (entire sanctification), though not all Arminians hold this; Reformed readers typically hold that sanctification is real but progressive and incomplete until glorification.

Leviticus 11:45

45For I am the LORD who brought you up out of the land of Egypt, to be your God. You shall therefore be holy, for I am holy.

Arminian

God grounds holiness in redemption: he brought Israel out of Egypt to be their God. Arminian readers stress that holiness responds to grace already given. Both traditions see the order of redemption first, then holiness, as central.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that holiness flows from God’s redeeming act, not as a means to earn redemption. The same pattern appears in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:2). Shared ground: God’s saving act comes first, and holiness is the response of a redeemed people.

Leviticus 11:46

46“ ‘This is the law of the animal, and of the bird, and of every living creature that moves in the waters, and of every creature that creeps on the earth,

Arminian

The law covers land animals, birds, water creatures, and creeping things. Arminian readers note the summary’s comprehensive scope. Both traditions agree that the summary recapitulates the chapter’s structure. Its four categories mirror the order of the chapter’s sections and make the law easy to remember and teach.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the summary covers every category discussed. Shared ground: the law addresses all living creatures in Israel’s environment, showing that holiness extended to every part of daily life. Some readers hear an echo of the creature groupings in Genesis 1, though the text does not draw the link.

Leviticus 11:47

47to make a distinction between the unclean and the clean, and between the living thing that may be eaten and the living thing that may not be eaten.’ ”

Arminian

The purpose is to distinguish between unclean and clean, and between what may and may not be eaten. Arminian readers see the call to discernment as lasting, even though the specific food laws are fulfilled in Christ. Both traditions commonly read Mark 7:19 and Peter’s vision in Acts 10 as setting the dietary distinctions aside.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the distinction parallels the priestly task in 10:10. The New Testament declares all foods clean (Mark 7:19) and applies the deeper meaning to holiness of life. Shared ground: the call to holiness remains, while the dietary rules are fulfilled in Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
