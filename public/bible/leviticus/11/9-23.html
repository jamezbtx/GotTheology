Passage

Leviticus 11

Verses 9–23 · WEB

The food laws continue with creatures of the water, the air, and flying insects. Water creatures must have fins and scales; certain birds, mostly birds of prey and scavengers, are listed as forbidden; most flying insects are forbidden, but certain locusts and similar insects are allowed. Many of the Hebrew names of birds and insects are difficult to identify, so translations vary. The point is a God-given boundary that marked Israel’s daily life as belonging to him.

Leviticus 11:9

9“ ‘You may eat of all these that are in the waters: whatever has fins and scales in the waters, in the seas, and in the rivers, that you may eat.

Arminian

Water creatures with fins and scales may be eaten. Arminian readers see clear, simple guidance for daily life. Both traditions note that the criterion, like the land-animal rules, is based on observable features and serves to mark Israel as distinct.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the twofold criterion of fins and scales, paralleling the twofold test for land animals. Shared ground: the rule is practical and God-given, and its purpose is the holiness of Israel rather than a scientific or hygienic theory.

Leviticus 11:10

10All that don’t have fins and scales in the seas and rivers, all that move in the waters, and all the living creatures that are in the waters, they are an abomination to you,

Arminian

Water creatures without fins and scales are an abomination to Israel. Arminian readers note the strong language, which marks a firm boundary. Both traditions agree that “abomination” here describes ritual detestability for Israel, not intrinsic evil in the creatures God made.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the term expresses what Israel must treat as detestable within its covenant life. God’s creation remains good (Genesis 1:31). Shared ground: the categories are covenantal and ceremonial, and the creatures are not morally evil.

Leviticus 11:11

11and you shall detest them. You shall not eat of their meat, and you shall detest their carcasses.

Arminian

Israel must detest these creatures and their carcasses. Arminian readers see the repetition as emphasizing the importance of the boundary. Both traditions agree that the command trains Israel to observe God’s distinctions consistently in everyday life.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that both eating and touching carcasses are covered, reinforcing the boundary. Shared ground: the command shaped Israel’s practice of holiness and is understood by both traditions as part of the ceremonial law fulfilled in Christ.

Leviticus 11:12

12Whatever has no fins nor scales in the waters is an abomination to you.

Arminian

Whatever lacks fins and scales is an abomination. The repetition summarizes the rule. Arminian readers note that repetition aids memory and obedience. Both traditions agree that the summary closes the section on water creatures clearly and firmly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repetition as a characteristic of legal instruction in Leviticus. Shared ground: the rule is restated for clarity, emphasizing that Israel’s distinction from the nations extended even to what was eaten from the waters.

Leviticus 11:13

13“ ‘You shall detest these among the birds; they shall not be eaten because they are an abomination: the eagle, the vulture, the black vulture,

Arminian

Certain birds are forbidden, beginning with the eagle, vulture, and black vulture. Many of the Hebrew bird names are hard to identify, and translations differ. Arminian readers note that many listed birds are predators or scavengers. Both traditions avoid overconfident symbolic explanations.

Reformed

Reformed readers acknowledge the uncertainty of identifying the birds and note that many are birds of prey or carrion eaters. Some suggest a link with contact with blood and death, but the text does not explain. Shared ground: the list defines Israel’s boundary.

Leviticus 11:14

14the red kite, any kind of black kite,

Arminian

The red kite and black kite are listed. Arminian readers note that the phrase “any kind of” extends the prohibition to related species. Both traditions recognize that exact identification of these birds is uncertain and that translations vary.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the inclusive phrase “any kind of,” which covers related birds. The names are difficult to identify. Shared ground: the list functions as a practical boundary for Israel, and its precise ornithology is less important than its role in marking holiness.

Leviticus 11:15

15any kind of raven,

Arminian

Any kind of raven is forbidden. Arminian readers note that ravens elsewhere serve God’s purposes, as with Elijah (1 Kings 17:4–6), showing that unclean does not mean worthless to God. Both traditions agree that ritual uncleanness concerns Israel’s food, not the creature’s value.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same contrast: God used ravens to feed Elijah, though they were unclean for eating. God’s care for ravens is also noted elsewhere (Psalm 147:9). Shared ground: unclean animals remain God’s creatures.

Leviticus 11:16

16the horned owl, the screech owl, the gull, any kind of hawk,

Arminian

The horned owl, screech owl, gull, and hawks are forbidden. Arminian readers note the variety of birds included. Both traditions agree that the identifications are uncertain and that the list’s purpose is to set clear boundaries for Israel’s diet.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that many of these birds are predators. Translations vary in the names given. Shared ground: the list marks the limits of permitted food, and both traditions read it in light of its ceremonial purpose rather than as a modern classification.

Leviticus 11:17

17the little owl, the cormorant, the great owl,

Arminian

The little owl, cormorant, and great owl are forbidden. Arminian readers note that the list continues with more birds associated with desolate places or predation. Both traditions agree that the exact species are uncertain. The repeated pattern shows a consistent category rather than isolated rules.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the continued uncertainty of identification and avoid building theology on specific bird names. Shared ground: the list belongs to the ceremonial law and served to mark Israel’s distinctiveness in its historical setting.

Leviticus 11:18

18the white owl, the desert owl, the osprey,

Arminian

The white owl, desert owl, and osprey are forbidden. Arminian readers note that several owls appear in the list. Both traditions agree that the Hebrew names are difficult and translations differ, and that the point is a God-given boundary.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the identifications remain uncertain. Shared ground: the list functions as part of Israel’s ceremonial purity laws, and both traditions read it as fulfilled in Christ rather than binding on Christians today.

Leviticus 11:19

19the stork, any kind of heron, the hoopoe, and the bat.

Arminian

The stork, heron, hoopoe, and bat are forbidden. The bat, though a mammal by modern classification, is grouped with flying creatures. Arminian readers see the categories as based on ancient observation. Both traditions agree that the list reflects how Israel grouped creatures, not modern science.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the bat’s inclusion shows the categories are based on flying rather than biological class. Shared ground: the law uses observational categories suited to its original audience, and its purpose is holiness and separation.

Leviticus 11:20

20“ ‘All flying insects that walk on all fours are an abomination to you.

Arminian

Flying insects that walk on all fours are an abomination. “On all fours” likely describes how they move rather than counting legs. Arminian readers note that the description is observational. Both traditions agree that the rule establishes a boundary, with exceptions following.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the phrase probably refers to their way of moving and should not be pressed as a technical claim. Shared ground: the law describes creatures as they appeared to ancient observers, and its purpose is practical separation for Israel.

Leviticus 11:21

21Yet you may eat these: of all winged creeping things that go on all fours, which have long, jointed legs for hopping on the earth.

Arminian

Winged creatures with jointed legs for hopping may be eaten. Arminian readers note that God’s law includes permissions as well as prohibitions. Both traditions agree that the exception allows certain insects, which were an available food source in the region.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s law is not merely restrictive but provides for his people. Shared ground: the exception reflects the practical realities of life in the region, and the criterion is again based on observable features.

Leviticus 11:22

22Even of these you may eat: any kind of locust, any kind of katydid, any kind of cricket, and any kind of grasshopper.

Arminian

Locusts, katydids, crickets, and grasshoppers may be eaten. Translations vary for these insect names. Arminian readers note that John the Baptist later ate locusts (Mark 1:6). Both traditions agree the list shows that not all insects were forbidden.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the uncertainty of identifying the insects, and that locusts were a known food (Mark 1:6). Shared ground: the permitted insects show the law’s practical character and God’s provision within the boundaries he set.

Leviticus 11:23

23But all winged creeping things which have four feet are an abomination to you.

Arminian

All other winged creeping things with four feet are an abomination. Arminian readers note that the summary restates the general rule after the exception. Both traditions agree that the structure of rule, exception, and restatement reinforces clarity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the restatement as typical of Levitical law, closing the section clearly. Shared ground: the rule and its exceptions define Israel’s diet regarding insects, and both traditions read it as part of the ceremonial law.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
