Passage

Leviticus 11

Verses 24–40 · WEB

This section turns from eating to contact: touching or carrying the carcasses of unclean animals, and even of clean animals that die naturally, makes a person ceremonially unclean until evening, often with washing of clothes. Rules follow for vessels, food, ovens, springs, and seed touched by the carcasses of creeping animals. Ceremonial uncleanness here is not the same as moral sin; it is a temporary state that restricted access to the sanctuary and was removed by prescribed means.

Leviticus 11:24

24“ ‘By these you will become unclean: whoever touches their carcass shall be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Touching the carcasses of unclean animals makes a person unclean until evening. Arminian readers note that this uncleanness is temporary and not sinful. Both traditions agree that ritual uncleanness restricted access to holy things but could be removed.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the distinction between ritual impurity and moral guilt. Uncleanness ends at evening. Shared ground: contact with death or unclean things affected one’s ritual status, teaching that God’s presence calls for purity. The evening limit marks a clear end to the impurity.

Leviticus 11:25

25Whoever carries any part of their carcass shall wash his clothes, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Carrying a carcass requires washing clothes and remaining unclean until evening. Arminian readers note the greater requirement for greater contact. Both traditions agree that washing symbolized cleansing and restored ritual purity. The added washing shows that closer contact required a fuller response, yet the remedy was simple and within reach.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the graded response to different levels of contact. Shared ground: the law provides means of cleansing, showing that uncleanness is not a permanent condition and that God provides a way back to purity.

Leviticus 11:26

26“ ‘Every animal which has a split hoof that isn’t completely divided, or doesn’t chew the cud, is unclean to you. Everyone who touches them shall be unclean.

Arminian

Animals with a split hoof not completely divided, or that do not chew the cud, are unclean, and touching them brings uncleanness. Arminian readers note the restatement of the land-animal criteria in the context of contact. Both traditions agree that the rule extends purity concerns beyond eating.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the criteria from 11:3 are applied again here to touching. Shared ground: the law addresses both eating and contact, showing how thoroughly holiness shaped Israel’s daily life. Holiness touched what Israel handled as well as what it ate.

Leviticus 11:27

27Whatever goes on its paws, among all animals that go on all fours, they are unclean to you. Whoever touches their carcass shall be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Animals that walk on paws are unclean, and touching their carcasses brings uncleanness until evening. Arminian readers note that this category includes many predators. Both traditions agree that the rule defines another clear boundary for Israel’s practice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that walking on paws, rather than hooves, marks these animals as unclean. Shared ground: the categories are practical, and the law’s purpose is to distinguish clean from unclean in Israel’s covenant life.

Leviticus 11:28

28He who carries their carcass shall wash his clothes, and be unclean until the evening. They are unclean to you.

Arminian

Carrying their carcass requires washing clothes and uncleanness until evening. Arminian readers note the consistency with the earlier rule (11:25). Both traditions agree that the repetition reinforces a clear procedure for restoring purity. A clear procedure meant that ordinary Israelites could know how to return to purity.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the parallel with 11:25. Shared ground: the law repeats its procedures for clarity, and the provision of cleansing shows that ritual impurity could be removed in an orderly way. The law anticipates ordinary tasks like moving a dead animal.

Leviticus 11:29

29“ ‘These are they which are unclean to you among the creeping things that creep on the earth: the weasel, the rat, any kind of great lizard,

Arminian

Certain creeping animals are unclean: the weasel, rat, and great lizard. Translations vary in identifying these creatures. Arminian readers note that such animals commonly entered homes. Both traditions agree that the following rules address everyday contact.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the uncertainty of identifying the Hebrew names. These small animals were likely to come into contact with household items. Shared ground: the law addresses common situations, extending purity concerns into the home.

Leviticus 11:30

30the gecko, and the monitor lizard, the wall lizard, the skink, and the chameleon.

Arminian

The gecko, monitor lizard, wall lizard, skink, and chameleon are also unclean. Arminian readers note the variety of small creatures listed. Both traditions agree that identification of these animals is uncertain and that translations differ.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the list continues the category of small creeping creatures. Shared ground: the precise species matter less than the category, which governs household purity in the following verses. The names show how closely these laws touched ordinary household life.

Leviticus 11:31

31These are they which are unclean to you among all that creep. Whoever touches them when they are dead shall be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Whoever touches these creatures when dead is unclean until evening. Arminian readers note the consistent pattern of temporary uncleanness. Both traditions agree that the rule concerns contact with death. The repeated limit of evening reassures that impurity was not permanent and could be dealt with in a predictable way.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the uncleanness is linked to death, a theme that runs through the purity laws. Shared ground: contact with dead creatures affected ritual status, teaching Israel that death and God’s holiness do not mix.

Leviticus 11:32

32Anything they fall on when they are dead shall be unclean; whether it is any vessel of wood, or clothing, or skin, or sack, whatever vessel it is, with which any work is done, it must be put into water, and it shall be unclean until the evening. Then it will be clean.

Arminian

Items touched by these dead creatures become unclean and must be washed, becoming clean at evening. Arminian readers note the practical detail. Both traditions agree that the law provides a way to restore purity to household objects.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law covers wood, cloth, skin, and sacks. Shared ground: purity extends to possessions, and washing restores them, showing the law’s practical and comprehensive concern for holiness in daily life. Ordinary tools of work were included within the scope of holiness.

Leviticus 11:33

33Every earthen vessel into which any of them falls and all that is in it shall be unclean. You shall break it.

Arminian

Earthen vessels touched by these creatures, and their contents, become unclean and must be broken. Arminian readers note that porous pottery could not be cleansed by washing. Both traditions agree that the law addresses the nature of different materials.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the distinction between materials that can be washed and those that must be destroyed. Shared ground: the law reflects careful attention to practical realities, and its purpose remains holiness. Breaking the vessel removed what could not be cleansed.

Leviticus 11:34

34All food which may be eaten which is soaked in water shall be unclean. All drink that may be drunk in every such vessel shall be unclean.

Arminian

Food soaked in water and drink in such vessels become unclean. Arminian readers note that moisture affects the transmission of uncleanness. Both traditions agree that the rule is practical and detailed. Ordinary meals and drinks were included in the purity system, making holiness a matter of daily attention.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that liquids are treated as transmitting uncleanness. Shared ground: the law’s detailed provisions show how thoroughly holiness shaped Israel’s daily life, extending even to food preparation. Nothing in the household was too ordinary to be affected.

Leviticus 11:35

35Everything whereupon part of their carcass falls shall be unclean; whether oven, or range for pots, it shall be broken in pieces. They are unclean, and shall be unclean to you.

Arminian

Ovens and ranges touched by a carcass must be broken. Arminian readers note the cost of maintaining purity. Both traditions agree that the rule reflects the seriousness of ritual uncleanness in Israel’s covenant life. Breaking an oven was a real loss, which underlines how seriously Israel was to take these distinctions.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that even cooking equipment is subject to purity rules. Shared ground: the law’s demands show that holiness affected every area of life, and both traditions read these rules as part of the ceremonial law fulfilled in Christ.

Leviticus 11:36

36Nevertheless a spring or a cistern in which water is gathered shall be clean, but that which touches their carcass shall be unclean.

Arminian

Springs and cisterns remain clean, though whoever touches the carcass becomes unclean. Arminian readers note the practical exception that protected water sources. Both traditions agree that the law balanced purity with the needs of life.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that collected water remains clean, a sensible provision for community life. Shared ground: the law includes practical exceptions, showing that it was designed for real life, not abstract theory. Without such an exception, a community’s water supply could easily have become unusable.

Leviticus 11:37

37If part of their carcass falls on any sowing seed which is to be sown, it is clean.

Arminian

Seed for sowing remains clean if a carcass touches it. Arminian readers note another practical exception. Both traditions agree that dry seed was not considered susceptible to uncleanness. The exception protected the coming harvest, and it shows that the law took account of how uncleanness was understood to spread.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that dry seed is treated differently from moistened seed (11:38). Shared ground: the law’s distinctions reflect careful attention to practical circumstances, protecting Israel’s food supply. The exception keeps the law from threatening the next season’s crops.

Leviticus 11:38

38But if water is put on the seed, and part of their carcass falls on it, it is unclean to you.

Arminian

If water is put on seed and a carcass touches it, it becomes unclean. Arminian readers note that moisture again plays a role in transmission. Both traditions agree that the rule continues the detailed application of purity principles.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the parallel with 11:34, where moisture transmits uncleanness. Shared ground: the law’s detail shows its comprehensive concern for holiness and its practical design. Moisture changes the status of the seed, so that the same contact has a different result depending on its condition.

Leviticus 11:39

39“ ‘If any animal of which you may eat dies, he who touches its carcass shall be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

If a clean animal dies on its own, touching its carcass brings uncleanness until evening. Arminian readers note that death, not the animal’s category, causes uncleanness here. Both traditions agree that the rule distinguishes natural death from proper slaughter.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that even clean animals become sources of uncleanness when they die naturally. Shared ground: death is the defiling factor, and the law teaches that God’s holiness is associated with life. Slaughter in the prescribed way differs from natural death.

Leviticus 11:40

40He who eats of its carcass shall wash his clothes, and be unclean until the evening. He also who carries its carcass shall wash his clothes, and be unclean until the evening.

Arminian

Eating or carrying such a carcass requires washing clothes and uncleanness until evening. Arminian readers note that the remedy is provided even for eating it. Both traditions agree that the law addresses real situations in which people might come into contact with dead animals.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the provision for cleansing shows God’s mercy in dealing with impurity. Shared ground: the law provides means of restoration, and both traditions read it as part of the ceremonial order fulfilled in Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
