Passage

Leviticus 4

Verses 13–21 · WEB

The second case is unintentional sin by the whole congregation of Israel. The wrong was hidden from the assembly and later comes to light. The procedure follows the priest’s offering closely: a young bull, hand-laying by the elders on the people’s behalf, blood brought into the Tent and sprinkled before the veil, fat burned on the altar, and the carcass burned outside the camp. For the first time the chapter states the outcome: the priest makes atonement, and they are forgiven. Both traditions see here corporate responsibility before God and God’s provision for the community as a whole.

Leviticus 4:13

13“ ‘If the whole congregation of Israel sins, and the thing is hidden from the eyes of the assembly, and they have done any of the things which the LORD has commanded not to be done, and are guilty;

Arminian

The whole congregation may sin without realizing it, “hidden from the eyes of the assembly.” The verse treats the community as a moral agent that can incur guilt together.

Arminian readers see corporate responsibility alongside personal responsibility. A people can drift from God’s commands collectively and must return together.

Reformed

The sin is hidden and unintentional, yet the congregation is still “guilty.” The Hebrew term can mean either being guilty or realizing one’s guilt, and translations differ.

Reformed readers note the covenant community’s shared standing before God. Both traditions agree that ignorance does not cancel the need for atonement.

Leviticus 4:14

14when the sin in which they have sinned is known, then the assembly shall offer a young bull for a sin offering, and bring it before the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

Once the sin is known, the assembly must act by offering a young bull, the same costly animal required of the anointed priest.

Arminian readers see that awareness brings responsibility to respond. Knowledge of sin calls for a deliberate turning back to God.

Reformed

The offering matches the high priest’s, which marks the whole people’s sin as equally weighty.

Reformed readers see that God reveals sin in order to deal with it, not to condemn without remedy. Both traditions agree that discovery of sin leads to God’s appointed means of cleansing.

Leviticus 4:15

15The elders of the congregation shall lay their hands on the head of the bull before the LORD; and the bull shall be killed before the LORD.

Arminian

The elders lay hands on the bull on behalf of the congregation. They act as the people’s representatives.

Arminian readers see responsible leaders standing with the community in confession. The people’s response is real even when expressed through representatives.

Reformed

The elders represent the whole assembly in the rite. One act stands for many.

Reformed readers note the principle of representation found throughout Scripture. Both traditions agree that the elders’ role expresses the congregation’s collective accountability.

Leviticus 4:16

16The anointed priest shall bring some of the blood of the bull to the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

As in verse 5, the anointed priest brings the blood into the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian readers note the priest’s mediating role. The people do not enter the holy place themselves, yet the blood is brought there for them.

Reformed

The blood goes into the Tent because the people’s sin, like the priest’s, reaches the sanctuary.

Reformed readers stress the priest’s office as mediator. Both traditions read Hebrews as presenting Christ as the priest who brings his own blood into God’s presence (Heb 9:11–12).

Leviticus 4:17

17The priest shall dip his finger in the blood and sprinkle it seven times before the LORD, before the veil.

Arminian

The priest sprinkles the blood seven times before the veil, repeating verse 6 exactly.

Arminian readers see the same thorough cleansing for the people as for the priest. God’s remedy is not reduced for the many.

Reformed

The repetition confirms that the congregation’s offering follows the same pattern as the priest’s.

Reformed readers note that sevenfold sprinkling signals a complete rite before God. Both traditions agree that the people’s offering is equal in weight to the high priest’s.

Leviticus 4:18

18He shall put some of the blood on the horns of the altar which is before the LORD, that is in the Tent of Meeting; and the rest of the blood he shall pour out at the base of the altar of burnt offering, which is at the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

Blood goes on the horns of the incense altar, and the rest is poured at the base of the outer altar.

Arminian readers see both altars cleansed. Sin touches the whole sanctuary, and God’s provision reaches as far as the defilement.

Reformed

The double application, inside and outside the Tent, repeats verse 7.

Reformed readers see the comprehensive reach of the cleansing God provides. Both traditions agree that the rite deals with sin’s effect on the place of worship.

Leviticus 4:19

19All its fat he shall take from it, and burn it on the altar.

Arminian

All the fat is burned on the altar. The summary refers back to the detailed list in verses 8–10.

Arminian readers note that the offering for the people is no less complete. God receives his portion in full.

Reformed

The fat is given to the LORD as in the earlier case.

Reformed readers see the offering belonging wholly to God. Both traditions agree that the shortened instruction assumes the earlier detail.

Leviticus 4:20

20He shall do this with the bull; as he did with the bull of the sin offering, so he shall do with this; and the priest shall make atonement for them, and they shall be forgiven.

Arminian

This verse first states the result: the priest makes atonement, “and they shall be forgiven.” The Hebrew verb for making atonement (kipper) is debated. Proposals include “cover,” “wipe clean,” and “ransom.” The passive “be forgiven” implies that God is the one who forgives.

Arminian readers stress that the forgiveness is real and that God freely grants it. The rite does not force God’s hand; it is the means he gives.

Reformed

The priest makes atonement and the people are forgiven. Interpreters debate the precise sense of kipper, but the outcome is clear.

Reformed readers stress that forgiveness rests on God’s appointed provision. Both traditions agree that sacrifices did not earn pardon in themselves and that their effectiveness depended on God and pointed to Christ (Heb 10:1–4).

Leviticus 4:21

21He shall carry the bull outside the camp, and burn it as he burned the first bull. It is the sin offering for the assembly.

Arminian

The bull is burned outside the camp like the first. The verse names it “the sin offering for the assembly.”

Arminian readers see God’s care for the community as a whole. The people have a remedy for shared sin, not only individuals.

Reformed

The disposal matches the high priest’s offering, completing the parallel.

Reformed readers note again the link with Heb 13:11–12. Both traditions see the burning outside the camp fulfilled in Christ’s suffering outside Jerusalem.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
