Passage

Leviticus 4

Verses 27–35 · WEB

The final case in chapter 4 concerns any one of the common people (literally “the people of the land”). When his unwitting sin is made known, he brings a female goat or, alternatively, a female lamb. The ritual parallels the ruler’s offering, with blood on the horns of the outer altar and fat burned like that of the peace offering. The section repeats the promise that the priest makes atonement and the offerer is forgiven, and adds that the smoke is “a pleasant aroma to the LORD.” Both traditions see God’s provision extended to ordinary Israelites, not only to leaders.

Leviticus 4:27

27“ ‘If anyone of the common people sins unwittingly, in doing any of the things which the LORD has commanded not to be done, and is guilty,

Arminian

The case turns to any of the common people, literally “the people of the land,” who sin unwittingly.

Arminian readers see God’s provision open to every Israelite. Ordinary people have the same access to atonement as leaders.

Reformed

The phrase “the common people” covers ordinary Israelites without office.

Reformed readers see God’s covenant care reaching all his people. Both traditions agree that no one in Israel was outside the provision for unwitting sin.

Leviticus 4:28

28if his sin which he has sinned is made known to him, then he shall bring for his offering a goat, a female without defect, for his sin which he has sinned.

Arminian

The commoner brings a female goat without defect. A female was likely less costly than a male, though the reason for the distinction is not stated.

Arminian readers note the fit between the offering and the offerer’s means. God does not demand from the poor what he demands from leaders.

Reformed

The offering is graded to the commoner’s position. The text does not explain why a female is specified.

Reformed readers see God’s fairness in the law. Both traditions agree that the remedy is real and sufficient at every level.

Leviticus 4:29

29He shall lay his hand on the head of the sin offering, and kill the sin offering in the place of burnt offering.

Arminian

He lays his hand on the sin offering and kills it in the place of burnt offering.

Arminian readers note that the ordinary Israelite performs the same personal acts as the ruler. Each offerer stands before God himself.

Reformed

The ritual for the commoner follows the ruler’s exactly.

Reformed readers note that the same God-given pattern applies to all. Both traditions agree that the ordinary worshiper is treated with the same seriousness.

Leviticus 4:30

30The priest shall take some of its blood with his finger, and put it on the horns of the altar of burnt offering; and the rest of its blood he shall pour out at the base of the altar.

Arminian

The priest puts blood on the horns of the outer altar and pours out the rest at its base.

Arminian readers see the priest serving every class of worshiper alike.

Reformed

As with the ruler, the blood is applied at the outer altar.

Reformed readers note that the priest mediates for all Israel. Both traditions agree that the procedure matches the ruler’s.

Leviticus 4:31

31All its fat he shall take away, like the fat is taken away from the sacrifice of peace offerings; and the priest shall burn it on the altar for a pleasant aroma to the LORD; and the priest shall make atonement for him, and he will be forgiven.

Arminian

The fat is burned “for a pleasant aroma to the LORD,” a phrase used here alone among the sin offerings of this chapter. The priest makes atonement, and he is forgiven.

Arminian readers see God’s pleasure in receiving the repentant commoner. He is not merely tolerated.

Reformed

The pleasant-aroma phrase signals that God accepts the offering. Why it appears only here in chapter 4 is unclear.

Reformed readers recall Eph 5:2, where Christ’s self-offering is a fragrant aroma to God. Both traditions read that verse as fulfilling this imagery.

Leviticus 4:32

32“ ‘If he brings a lamb as his offering for a sin offering, he shall bring a female without defect.

Arminian

The commoner may bring a lamb instead of a goat, again a female without defect.

Arminian readers see flexibility in God’s provision. The offerer may bring what he has.

Reformed

The alternative of a lamb gives the worshiper a choice.

Reformed readers note that God’s law allows for differing circumstances. Both traditions often connect the lamb without defect with Christ, the Lamb without blemish (1 Pet 1:19).

Leviticus 4:33

33He shall lay his hand on the head of the sin offering, and kill it for a sin offering in the place where they kill the burnt offering.

Arminian

The lamb is treated like the goat: hand-laying and slaughter at the place of burnt offering.

Arminian readers see the same personal participation whatever animal is brought.

Reformed

The procedure is unchanged for the lamb.

Reformed readers stress that the appointed pattern, not the species, governs the rite. Both traditions agree that the lamb is an equal substitute for the goat.

Leviticus 4:34

34The priest shall take some of the blood of the sin offering with his finger, and put it on the horns of the altar of burnt offering; and all the rest of its blood he shall pour out at the base of the altar.

Arminian

The blood is applied to the horns of the outer altar and the rest poured out, as in verse 30.

Arminian readers see the consistency of God’s provision.

Reformed

The repetition stresses that the lamb offering is just as effective as the goat.

Reformed readers note the precision of the law. Both traditions agree that each permitted offering receives the same ritual.

Leviticus 4:35

35He shall remove all its fat, like the fat of the lamb is removed from the sacrifice of peace offerings. The priest shall burn them on the altar, on the offerings of the LORD made by fire. The priest shall make atonement for him concerning his sin that he has sinned, and he will be forgiven.

Arminian

The fat is burned on the offerings made by fire. The priest makes atonement for his sin, and he will be forgiven.

Arminian readers note that the chapter ends with forgiveness offered to the common person. God’s goal is restoration.

Reformed

The section closes with the same promise. Atonement and forgiveness frame all four cases.

Reformed readers stress that God provides and promises forgiveness. Both traditions agree that the sin offering points beyond itself to the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ (Heb 10:10–14).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
