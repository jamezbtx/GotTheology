Passage

Leviticus 4

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Leviticus 4 opens a new divine speech and introduces the sin offering (Hebrew chatta’t; many interpreters render it “purification offering”). It deals with sins committed unintentionally, that is, in error or inadvertence, rather than defiantly (compare Num 15:22–31). Cases are graded by the offender’s standing. Verses 1–12 treat the anointed priest, whose sin brings guilt on the people. His bull’s blood is brought into the Tent of Meeting, sprinkled before the veil, and put on the incense altar. Its fat is burned on the altar, and the rest of the carcass is burned outside the camp. Both traditions read the passage first as Israel’s worship law, and both see it fulfilled in Christ (Heb 9; 13:11–12). The exact sense of some ritual terms is debated.

Leviticus 4:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

A new speech begins with the familiar formula. The sacrificial law comes as the LORD’s word from the Tent, not as a human invention.

Arminian readers note that God himself provides the way to deal with sin before Israel asks for it. The covenant God speaks first and opens a way back for his people.

Reformed

The formula marks what follows as divine instruction given through Moses as mediator.

Reformed readers stress that God sets the terms of approach to himself. Worship is shaped by his revealed word. Both traditions agree that the means of atonement are God’s gift and not human devising.

Leviticus 4:2

2“Speak to the children of Israel, saying, ‘If anyone sins unintentionally, in any of the things which the LORD has commanded not to be done, and does any one of them,

Arminian

The law addresses one who “sins unintentionally” (Hebrew shegagah, “in error” or “inadvertently”). The scope of the term is debated. It clearly covers acts done without knowing they broke a command, and some interpreters extend it to sins of weakness as opposed to defiant, “high-handed” sin (Num 15:30).

Arminian readers take from this that sin is measured by God’s command and not only by the sinner’s awareness. Unknowing sin still needs atonement, and God has provided for it.

Reformed

The verse treats breaking the LORD’s commands as sin even when the offender did not realize it. The exact range of shegagah is debated, but its contrast with defiant sin in Num 15 is clear.

Reformed readers see here that God’s law, not the conscience alone, defines sin, a theme echoed in Ps 19:12. Both traditions agree that the sinner’s ignorance lessens guilt without removing it.

Leviticus 4:3

3if the anointed priest sins so as to bring guilt on the people, then let him offer for his sin which he has sinned a young bull without defect to the LORD for a sin offering.

Arminian

The first case is the anointed priest, most likely the high priest. Because he represents the people, his sin “brings guilt on the people.” He must offer the most costly animal, a young bull without defect.

Arminian readers note that leaders carry greater responsibility. Their failures affect the whole community, so the remedy required is greater.

Reformed

The high priest’s sin is treated first and most seriously because of his representative office. His guilt reaches the people he serves.

Reformed readers note the theme of representation that runs through Scripture. Both traditions see Heb 7:27 and 9:7 drawing on this law: Israel’s high priest had to offer for his own sins, while Christ did not.

Leviticus 4:4

4He shall bring the bull to the door of the Tent of Meeting before the LORD; and he shall lay his hand on the head of the bull, and kill the bull before the LORD.

Arminian

The priest brings the bull to the Tent’s entrance, lays his hand on its head, and kills it before the LORD. The meaning of the hand-laying is debated. Proposals include identifying the offerer with the animal, marking ownership, or transferring sin.

Arminian readers see the offerer personally involved in confession and sacrifice. The rite is not done for him at a distance.

Reformed

The ritual begins with hand-laying and slaughter “before the LORD.” Interpreters differ on whether the gesture signals identification, ownership, or transfer, and the text does not explain it.

Reformed readers often see a link between the offerer and the victim that points toward substitution. Both traditions find in the offering a pattern later fulfilled in Christ’s death for sinners.

Leviticus 4:5

5The anointed priest shall take some of the blood of the bull, and bring it to the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

The priest takes some of the blood into the Tent of Meeting. This is unusual: in most sacrifices the blood stays at the outer altar.

Arminian readers see the seriousness of the leader’s sin in how far into the sanctuary the blood must go. Sin by the priest touches the holy place where he ministers.

Reformed

Bringing the blood inside the Tent marks this as one of the weightiest sin offerings.

Reformed readers note that the place of cleansing matches the reach of the defilement. Both traditions agree that the high priest’s sin was treated as affecting the sanctuary itself.

Leviticus 4:6

6The priest shall dip his finger in the blood, and sprinkle some of the blood seven times before the LORD, before the veil of the sanctuary.

Arminian

The priest sprinkles the blood seven times before the veil of the sanctuary. Seven often signals completeness in the Old Testament.

Arminian readers see the thoroughness of the cleansing God requires. The remedy for sin is full, not partial.

Reformed

Sevenfold sprinkling before the veil brings the blood close to the LORD’s presence behind it.

Reformed readers note that access to God is barred by sin and must be opened by blood. Both traditions read Heb 9:7–12 and 10:19–20 as building on such imagery for Christ’s entry into God’s presence.

Leviticus 4:7

7The priest shall put some of the blood on the horns of the altar of sweet incense before the LORD, which is in the Tent of Meeting; and he shall pour out the rest of the blood of the bull at the base of the altar of burnt offering, which is at the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

Blood is put on the horns of the incense altar inside the Tent, and the rest is poured at the base of the bronze altar outside. Many interpreters understand the blood as purifying the sanctuary from the defilement sin causes, which is one reason some prefer the term “purification offering.”

Arminian readers see that sin pollutes not only the sinner but the place where God meets his people. God provides cleansing for both.

Reformed

The incense altar’s horns receive blood, and the remainder goes to the base of the altar of burnt offering. Whether the blood mainly cleanses the sanctuary, the offerer, or both is debated.

Reformed readers stress that atonement is God-appointed and blood-centered (Lev 17:11). Both traditions agree that the rite shows sin’s defiling reach and God’s provision to remove it.

Leviticus 4:8

8He shall take all the fat of the bull of the sin offering from it: the fat that covers the innards, and all the fat that is on the innards,

Arminian

All the fat is removed. The fat that covers and is on the innards is commonly understood as the choicest part and belonged to the LORD (3:16).

Arminian readers see the best given to God even in an offering for sin. Repentance does not hold back.

Reformed

The fat portions are set apart as in the peace offering. In Leviticus the fat is reserved for the LORD.

Reformed readers see God’s claim to the best of what is offered. Both traditions agree that the offering expresses honor to God as well as dealing with sin.

Leviticus 4:9

9and the two kidneys, and the fat that is on them, which is by the loins, and the cover on the liver, with the kidneys, he shall remove,

Arminian

The kidneys, their fat, and the covering of the liver are also removed. The list repeats the procedure of chapter 3 closely.

Arminian readers note the careful precision of the instructions. Approaching God is not left to improvisation.

Reformed

The detailed list matches 3:4. Repetition fixes the rite so priests can carry it out faithfully.

Reformed readers stress that worship follows God’s word in detail. Both traditions agree that the precision reflects God’s holiness rather than mere ceremony.

Leviticus 4:10

10as it is removed from the bull of the sacrifice of peace offerings. The priest shall burn them on the altar of burnt offering.

Arminian

The fat is handled as in the peace offering and burned on the altar of burnt offering.

Arminian readers see continuity between offerings. The sin offering restores the worshiper to the fellowship that the peace offering celebrates.

Reformed

The cross-reference to the peace offering shows that the sacrifices form one connected system.

Reformed readers see the offerings as complementary, each showing a side of reconciliation with God. Both traditions read the system as a unified pattern fulfilled in Christ.

Leviticus 4:11

11He shall carry the bull’s skin, all its meat, with its head, and with its legs, its innards, and its dung

Arminian

The rest of the bull, including skin, meat, head, legs, innards, and dung, is carried out. Unlike sin offerings whose blood stays at the outer altar (6:26, 30), nothing of this bull is eaten by the priest.

Arminian readers note that a priest could not benefit from an offering made for his own sin. Everything left must be removed.

Reformed

None of the bull is eaten, since the priest is himself the offender. The whole remainder is taken away.

Reformed readers see a fitting rule: no one profits from atonement for his own guilt. Both traditions agree that the rule underlines the seriousness of a leader’s sin.

Leviticus 4:12

12—all the rest of the bull—outside of the camp to a clean place where the ashes are poured out, and burn it on wood with fire. It shall be burned where the ashes are poured out.

Arminian

The remainder is burned outside the camp at a ritually clean place where the ashes are poured out. The place is clean even though it is outside the camp.

Arminian readers see sin carried away from the community. Heb 13:11–12 recalls this rite when it says Jesus suffered outside the gate to sanctify the people through his own blood.

Reformed

Disposal outside the camp separates the remains from the camp’s life while keeping them in a clean place.

Reformed readers, like Arminians, follow Heb 13:11–13, which applies this pattern to Christ’s suffering outside the gate and calls believers to go out to him. This typological reading is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
