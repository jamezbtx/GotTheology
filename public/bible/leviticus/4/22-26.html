Passage

Leviticus 4

Verses 22–26 · WEB

The third case is a ruler, a tribal or clan leader, who sins unwittingly. When his sin is made known to him, he brings a male goat. Its blood is applied only to the outer altar of burnt offering, not inside the Tent, which marks a lesser degree of defilement than the priest’s or the whole congregation’s sin. The fat is burned as with the peace offering, and the priest makes atonement, and he is forgiven. Both traditions read the gradation as a sign that responsibility matches one’s role in the community.

Leviticus 4:22

22“ ‘When a ruler sins, and unwittingly does any one of all the things which the LORD his God has commanded not to be done, and is guilty,

Arminian

A ruler unwittingly breaks one of the commands of the LORD his God. The Hebrew word (nasi’) probably refers to a tribal or clan leader, not the king.

Arminian readers note that leaders are personally accountable to God. Their rank does not exempt them from his commands.

Reformed

The ruler is under “the LORD his God,” a reminder that civil leaders are subject to God.

Reformed readers see magistrates accountable to God’s law. Both traditions agree that a leader’s unwitting sin still needs atonement.

Leviticus 4:23

23if his sin in which he has sinned is made known to him, he shall bring as his offering a goat, a male without defect.

Arminian

When his sin is made known to him, he brings a male goat without defect. The text does not say how it becomes known, whether through conscience, others, or discovering the law.

Arminian readers see an honest response to newly known sin. Awareness calls for action.

Reformed

The ruler’s offering is a male goat, less costly than a bull, matching his lesser representative role.

Reformed readers note that God measures responsibility by office. Both traditions agree that coming to know one’s sin brings the duty to deal with it before God.

Leviticus 4:24

24He shall lay his hand on the head of the goat, and kill it in the place where they kill the burnt offering before the LORD. It is a sin offering.

Arminian

The ruler lays his hand on the goat and kills it where the burnt offering is slain.

Arminian readers see the offerer’s personal involvement again. He takes part in the act rather than delegating it.

Reformed

The slaughter takes place at the usual place before the LORD, and the text names it “a sin offering.”

Reformed readers stress the God-appointed setting of sacrifice. Both traditions agree that hand-laying links the offerer to the animal, whatever its precise meaning.

Leviticus 4:25

25The priest shall take some of the blood of the sin offering with his finger, and put it on the horns of the altar of burnt offering. He shall pour out the rest of its blood at the base of the altar of burnt offering.

Arminian

The blood goes only on the horns of the outer altar. Unlike the earlier cases, it is not brought into the Tent.

Arminian readers see the gradation in Leviticus: the more representative the sinner, the deeper into the sanctuary the cleansing must reach.

Reformed

Many interpreters read the application at the outer altar as marking the ruler’s sin as less defiling than the high priest’s or the congregation’s.

Reformed readers note the careful proportion in God’s law. Both traditions agree that sins are not all equal in their effects even though all need atonement.

Leviticus 4:26

26All its fat he shall burn on the altar, like the fat of the sacrifice of peace offerings; and the priest shall make atonement for him concerning his sin, and he will be forgiven.

Arminian

The fat is burned, the priest makes atonement, and “he will be forgiven.”

Arminian readers note the personal promise. Forgiveness is offered to the individual who brings the appointed offering.

Reformed

The verse repeats the formula of atonement and forgiveness for the ruler.

Reformed readers stress that God attaches a sure promise to his appointed means. Both traditions agree that the promise of forgiveness is the goal of the whole rite.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
