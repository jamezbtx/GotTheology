Passage

Leviticus 16

Verses 29–34 · WEB

The chapter closes by making the day a permanent statute. On the tenth day of the seventh month, Israel, native-born and foreigner alike, must afflict their souls and rest from all work, because on that day atonement cleanses them from all their sins. The anointed high priest is to perform the rite each year.

Leviticus 16:29

29“It shall be a statute to you forever: in the seventh month, on the tenth day of the month, you shall afflict your souls, and shall do no kind of work, whether native-born or a stranger who lives as a foreigner among you;

Arminian

The day is fixed: the tenth day of the seventh month. “Afflict your souls” has long been understood in Jewish tradition as fasting and self-humbling; Acts 27:9 likely refers to this day as “the Fast.” The foreigner living among Israel is included. Arminian readers see the people’s humble response as essential to receiving the benefit.

Reformed

Reformed readers also treat the affliction of soul as repentance and humility. They stress that the people’s response does not earn the atonement; it is the fitting response to what God provides. The inclusion of foreigners is welcomed by both traditions.

Leviticus 16:30

30for on this day shall atonement be made for you, to cleanse you. You shall be clean from all your sins before the LORD.

Arminian

“On this day shall atonement be made for you, to cleanse you.” The promise is comprehensive: “clean from all your sins before the LORD.” Arminian readers emphasize God’s gracious offer of full cleansing to the whole people.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the cleansing is God’s work, accomplished through the appointed rite. Hebrews 10:1–4 notes that these yearly sacrifices could not take away sins once for all, pointing to Christ’s sacrifice. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 16:31

31It is a Sabbath of solemn rest to you, and you shall afflict your souls. It is a statute forever.

Arminian

The day is a Sabbath of solemn rest. Resting from work underlines that atonement is received, not achieved. Arminian readers see rest and repentance held together.

Reformed

Reformed readers often connect the Sabbath rest with resting in the finished work of atonement. Both traditions affirm that the people contribute no work to their cleansing.

Leviticus 16:32

32The priest, who is anointed and who is consecrated to be priest in his father’s place, shall make the atonement, and shall put on the linen garments, even the holy garments.

Arminian

The rite is to continue through each anointed successor of Aaron. The office passes on, but the work stays the same. Hebrews 7:23–24 contrasts these many priests, prevented by death from continuing, with Christ’s permanent priesthood.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the same contrast from Hebrews 7. The succession of priests highlights the need for a priest who lives forever. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 16:33

33Then he shall make atonement for the Holy Sanctuary; and he shall make atonement for the Tent of Meeting and for the altar; and he shall make atonement for the priests and for all the people of the assembly.

Arminian

The verse summarizes the scope of atonement: the sanctuary, the tent, the altar, the priests, and “all the people of the assembly.” Arminian readers note the breadth of the provision for the whole covenant community.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same comprehensive summary and its focus on the covenant people gathered before God. The point of the verse is completeness; any contrast over extent is better seen at verse 21.

Leviticus 16:34

34“This shall be an everlasting statute for you, to make atonement for the children of Israel once in the year because of all their sins.” It was done as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

The statute is “everlasting” and is performed “once in the year.” The yearly repetition shows its provisional nature, which Hebrews 9:25–26 contrasts with Christ’s single offering. The closing line notes Israel’s obedience. Arminian readers see the old rite fulfilled rather than abolished in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read “everlasting” as finding its fulfillment in Christ, whose once-for-all sacrifice replaces annual repetition (Hebrews 9:25–28; 10:12). They also note the people’s obedience to God’s command. This is shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
