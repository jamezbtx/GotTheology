Passage

Leviticus 16

Verses 11–19 · WEB

Aaron performs the blood rites. He kills the bull for himself, brings burning incense inside the veil so its cloud covers the mercy seat, and sprinkles the bull’s blood. Then he kills the people’s goat and does the same with its blood. In this way he atones for the Most Holy Place, the Tent of Meeting, and the altar, all of which have been defiled by Israel’s sins and uncleanness.

Leviticus 16:11

11“Aaron shall present the bull of the sin offering, which is for himself, and shall make atonement for himself and for his house, and shall kill the bull of the sin offering which is for himself.

Arminian

Aaron kills the bull for himself and his household. The priest must be cleansed before he can minister for the people. Arminian readers stress that this sequence displays God’s holiness and the seriousness of every person’s sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here, again, the imperfection of the Levitical priest, contrasted in Hebrews 7:27 with Christ, who offered himself once and needed no offering for his own sin.

Leviticus 16:12

12He shall take a censer full of coals of fire from off the altar before the LORD, and two handfuls of sweet incense beaten small, and bring it within the veil.

Arminian

Aaron takes coals from the altar and finely beaten incense inside the veil. The fire comes from the appointed altar, a quiet contrast with the unauthorized fire of Leviticus 10:1. Worship follows God’s instructions.

Reformed

Reformed readers often draw from this the principle that God is to be approached as he has appointed. Some see the incense as a picture of intercession, an association found elsewhere in Scripture (Psalm 141:2; Revelation 8:3–4). Arminians commonly note these links too.

Leviticus 16:13

13He shall put the incense on the fire before the LORD, that the cloud of the incense may cover the mercy seat that is on the covenant, so that he will not die.

Arminian

The cloud of incense covers the mercy seat “so that he will not die.” Even the appointed high priest is in danger in God’s presence without this covering. Arminian readers emphasize that God both requires and provides the protection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same tension of holiness and grace. God provides what is needed for the priest to survive his presence. Many see this pointing to the need for a mediator who can stand before God on his own merit.

Leviticus 16:14

14He shall take some of the blood of the bull, and sprinkle it with his finger on the mercy seat on the east; and before the mercy seat he shall sprinkle some of the blood with his finger seven times.

Arminian

Blood is sprinkled on the mercy seat and seven times before it. The number seven often signals completeness. The mercy seat is the place where God promised to meet his people (Exodus 25:22). Paul’s word in Romans 3:25, often rendered “propitiation” or “mercy seat,” is the same Greek word the Septuagint uses for the mercy seat.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters emphasize the Romans 3:25 connection: Christ is set forth as the true place and means of atonement, satisfying God’s justice. Classical Arminians also affirm that Christ’s death satisfied divine justice and draw the same link, though some Arminian theologians frame that satisfaction in governmental terms.

Leviticus 16:15

15“Then he shall kill the goat of the sin offering that is for the people, and bring his blood within the veil, and do with his blood as he did with the blood of the bull, and sprinkle it on the mercy seat and before the mercy seat.

Arminian

The people’s goat is killed and its blood brought inside the veil like the bull’s. The same rite is performed for priest and people alike. Arminian readers stress that God’s provision covers the whole covenant community.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the high priest acts for a defined people, Israel. Hebrews 9:12 contrasts this with Christ, who entered once with his own blood and obtained eternal redemption. Both traditions read the goat’s blood as a shadow of Christ’s.

Leviticus 16:16

16He shall make atonement for the Holy Place, because of the uncleanness of the children of Israel, and because of their transgressions, even all their sins; and so he shall do for the Tent of Meeting that dwells with them in the middle of their uncleanness.

Arminian

Atonement is made for the Holy Place itself because Israel’s uncleanness and sins defile the sanctuary where God dwells “in the middle of their uncleanness.” Sin affects not only people but the place of God’s presence. God’s willingness to dwell among an unclean people is remarkable grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the total reach of sin, which pollutes even the means of worship. The purging of the sanctuary allows God to keep dwelling with his people. Hebrews 9:23 speaks of the heavenly things being cleansed with better sacrifices. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 16:17

17No one shall be in the Tent of Meeting when he enters to make atonement in the Holy Place, until he comes out, and has made atonement for himself and for his household, and for all the assembly of Israel.

Arminian

No one else may be in the tent while Aaron atones. He acts alone, on behalf of himself, his household, and “all the assembly of Israel.” Arminian readers note that the people contribute nothing to the rite; the work is done for them.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the solitary work of the mediator. Many see a picture of Christ accomplishing atonement alone, without human contribution. Both traditions affirm that atonement is God’s provision, not human achievement.

Leviticus 16:18

18“He shall go out to the altar that is before the LORD and make atonement for it, and shall take some of the bull’s blood, and some of the goat’s blood, and put it around on the horns of the altar.

Arminian

Aaron then goes out to the altar and puts blood from both the bull and the goat on its horns. The altar, too, has been defiled and needs atonement. The horns were the most prominent parts of the altar.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that every part of the worship space needs cleansing. The combined blood shows the rite covering priest and people together. This verse is shared ground.

Leviticus 16:19

19He shall sprinkle some of the blood on it with his finger seven times, and cleanse it, and make it holy from the uncleanness of the children of Israel.

Arminian

Seven sprinklings cleanse the altar and make it holy “from the uncleanness of the children of Israel.” Holiness is restored so that worship can continue throughout the year.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the restoration of the altar as necessary for continued communion with God. The cleansing is complete, but it must be repeated each year (verse 34), which Hebrews 10:1–4 cites as evidence that these sacrifices could not perfect the worshipers.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
