Passage

Leviticus 16

Verses 20–28 · WEB

With the sanctuary cleansed, Aaron lays both hands on the live goat, confesses all Israel’s iniquities over it, and sends it into the wilderness carrying them. He then removes the linen garments, bathes, puts on his regular garments, and offers the burnt offerings. Those who handle the scapegoat and burn the remains outside the camp must wash before returning.

Leviticus 16:20

20“When he has finished atoning for the Holy Place, the Tent of Meeting, and the altar, he shall present the live goat.

Arminian

Only after the sanctuary, tent, and altar are cleansed is the live goat presented. The order matters: the blood rites inside come first, then the removal of sin outside.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same sequence and read the two parts as one atonement. The live goat is presented only after the Holy Place, the Tent of Meeting, and the altar have been atoned for, so the sending away follows the blood work rather than replacing it.

This is shared ground.

Leviticus 16:21

21Aaron shall lay both his hands on the head of the live goat, and confess over him all the iniquities of the children of Israel, and all their transgressions, even all their sins; and he shall put them on the head of the goat, and shall send him away into the wilderness by the hand of a man who is ready.

Arminian

Aaron lays “both his hands” on the goat and confesses “all” Israel’s iniquities, transgressions, and sins, putting them on its head. The laying on of hands signifies transfer; the threefold vocabulary for sin stresses completeness. Arminian readers note that the rite covers the whole people of Israel, and that Leviticus 23:29 still warns that anyone who would not humble himself that day would be cut off. They see this as a picture of an atonement provided for all and received by repentance and faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in this verse a vivid picture of substitution and imputation: the people’s guilt is laid on another. The high priest confesses the sins of a particular covenant people, whose tribes he represented before God (Exodus 28:29). Many Reformed interpreters read this as foreshadowing Christ’s atonement as accomplished for his people, while also affirming that his sacrifice is sufficient for all.

Key contrast Both traditions agree that this verse pictures substitution: the people’s sins are transferred to another who bears them away, a pattern fulfilled in Christ. The difference is about extent and application. Classical Arminians typically read the rite’s scope as pointing to an atonement provided for everyone and applied to those who repent and believe. Reformed interpreters typically read the high priest’s work for a defined covenant people as pointing to an atonement that effectively secures the salvation of those God has chosen.

Leviticus 16:22

22The goat shall carry all their iniquities on himself to a solitary land, and he shall release the goat in the wilderness.

Arminian

The goat carries “all their iniquities” to a solitary land. The picture is of sin removed beyond return. Arminian readers connect it with the promise that God removes our transgressions far from us (Psalm 103:12).

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a picture of complete removal of guilt. Many link it to Isaiah 53, where the Servant bears the iniquities of others. Both traditions find assurance here: the sins confessed are carried away, not merely covered over.

Leviticus 16:23

23“Aaron shall come into the Tent of Meeting, and shall take off the linen garments which he put on when he went into the Holy Place, and shall leave them there.

Arminian

Aaron takes off the linen garments in the tent and leaves them there. They were special to this day’s work in the Most Holy Place.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the garments of the atoning work are set apart for that work alone. Little theological difference is at stake in this verse.

Leviticus 16:24

24Then he shall bathe himself in water in a holy place, put on his garments, and come out and offer his burnt offering and the burnt offering of the people, and make atonement for himself and for the people.

Arminian

After bathing, Aaron puts on his regular garments and offers burnt offerings for himself and the people. The burnt offering expresses complete dedication to God, following the cleansing of sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers often stress the sequence: atonement first, then consecration. Forgiveness leads into whole-hearted worship. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 16:25

25The fat of the sin offering he shall burn on the altar.

Arminian

The fat of the sin offering is burned on the altar, as the law prescribes for sin offerings elsewhere in Leviticus (4:8–10, 4:19). It belongs to the LORD.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The detail shows that the Day of Atonement fits within the regular sacrificial system rather than replacing it.

Leviticus 16:26

26“He who lets the goat go as the scapegoat shall wash his clothes, and bathe his flesh in water, and afterward he shall come into the camp.

Arminian

The man who leads the scapegoat away must wash and bathe before reentering the camp. Contact with the sin-bearing goat makes him unclean. Arminian readers see the seriousness of sin reflected even in the handling of the goat.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same point. The goat really carries guilt in the symbolism of the rite, so those who touch it must be cleansed. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 16:27

27The bull for the sin offering, and the goat for the sin offering, whose blood was brought in to make atonement in the Holy Place, shall be carried outside the camp; and they shall burn their skins, their flesh, and their dung with fire.

Arminian

The bodies of the bull and goat whose blood entered the Holy Place are burned outside the camp. Hebrews 13:11–12 draws on this: Jesus suffered outside the city gate to sanctify the people by his own blood. Arminian readers see Christ bearing rejection and shame for others.

Reformed

Reformed readers draw the same link to Hebrews 13. Christ, like the sin offering, was taken outside the camp, bearing sin and shame. Both traditions also hear the call of Hebrews 13:13 to go out to him, bearing his reproach.

Leviticus 16:28

28He who burns them shall wash his clothes, and bathe his flesh in water, and afterward he shall come into the camp.

Arminian

The person who burns the remains must also wash before returning. The rite ends with the restoration of everyone involved to the clean community.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the closing washings protect the holiness of the camp. The one who burns the remains outside must wash his clothes and bathe before coming back in, so even the day’s last chores are brought under the same concern for purity.

Little divergence arises here.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
