Passage

Leviticus 16

Verses 1–10 · WEB

After the deaths of Nadab and Abihu (Leviticus 10:1–2), the LORD tells Moses how Aaron may safely approach his presence above the mercy seat. The high priest may not enter the Most Holy Place whenever he likes. He must come once a year, in plain linen, with a bull as a sin offering for himself and two goats for the people. Lots assign one goat to the LORD and one to be sent away alive into the wilderness.

Leviticus 16:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses after the death of the two sons of Aaron, when they came near before the LORD, and died;

Arminian

The chapter opens by recalling Leviticus 10, where two priests died after offering unauthorized fire. The setting shows that what follows is a mercy: God gives a way to come near without being destroyed. Arminian readers stress that the holiness that judged Nadab and Abihu is the same holiness that now provides access.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the setting in judgment. The God who struck down careless worship is the one who graciously appoints a mediator and a rite. Access to God is always on his terms, never on human invention, and that is itself grace.

Leviticus 16:2

2and the LORD said to Moses, “Tell Aaron your brother not to come at just any time into the Most Holy Place within the veil, before the mercy seat which is on the ark; lest he die; for I will appear in the cloud on the mercy seat.

Arminian

Even Aaron cannot come “at just any time.” The veil marks a real separation between a holy God and a sinful people. God promises to appear in the cloud over the mercy seat, so the restriction exists for the sake of meeting, not merely exclusion. Hebrews 9:7–8 reads this limited access as a sign that the way into the holy place was not yet opened.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters see the same point: the restriction teaches the seriousness of sin and the need for a qualified mediator. Hebrews 9 reads this as a shadow pointing to Christ, who opens access to God permanently. Both traditions share this typological reading.

Leviticus 16:3

3“Aaron shall come into the sanctuary with a young bull for a sin offering, and a ram for a burnt offering.

Arminian

The high priest must bring his own sin offering before he can act for others. Arminian readers note that the mediator under the old covenant was himself a sinner in need of atonement, which Hebrews 7:27 contrasts with Christ, who needs no offering for himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same contrast from Hebrews 7:26–27. Aaron’s need for a bull underlines the imperfection of the Levitical priesthood and points ahead to a sinless priest. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 16:4

4He shall put on the holy linen tunic. He shall have the linen trousers on his body, and shall put on the linen sash, and he shall be clothed with the linen turban. They are the holy garments. He shall bathe his body in water, and put them on.

Arminian

On this day Aaron sets aside his ornate high-priestly garments for simple linen and bathes first. Interpreters suggest the plain clothing expresses humility or purity before God, though the text does not explain it. The emphasis falls on readiness and holiness.

Reformed

Reformed commentators often read the linen as a picture of purity and humility. Some see in it a hint of Christ, who humbled himself to make atonement. The text itself gives no explanation, so such readings are offered as typology rather than as the passage’s stated meaning.

Leviticus 16:5

5He shall take from the congregation of the children of Israel two male goats for a sin offering, and one ram for a burnt offering.

Arminian

The people provide two goats as one sin offering, together with a ram. The two goats work as a pair: one deals with sin by death, the other by removal. Arminian readers see both sides of the gospel pictured here, forgiveness through sacrifice and sin carried away.

Reformed

Reformed readers also treat the two goats as one offering showing two aspects of a single atonement: satisfaction through death and the removal of guilt. Many see both fulfilled in Christ’s one work. This reading is common to both traditions.

Leviticus 16:6

6“Aaron shall offer the bull of the sin offering, which is for himself, and make atonement for himself and for his house.

Arminian

Aaron atones first for himself and his household. Atonement here means the removal of sin and impurity so that a holy God can dwell with his people. The order shows that no one, not even the high priest, stands before God apart from atonement.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The priest’s household needs the same cleansing as the people. Many Reformed interpreters stress that atonement in Leviticus involves both cleansing and the turning aside of God’s rightful judgment.

Leviticus 16:7

7He shall take the two goats, and set them before the LORD at the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

Both goats are set “before the LORD.” The choice between them is not left to Aaron’s preference. The whole rite takes place in God’s presence and under his direction.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same thing. The animals are presented to God, and God, not the priest, will determine their roles. The emphasis is on God’s appointment of the means of atonement.

Leviticus 16:8

8Aaron shall cast lots for the two goats: one lot for the LORD, and the other lot for the scapegoat.

Arminian

Lots decide which goat is “for the LORD” and which is for the scapegoat. The Hebrew word behind “scapegoat” is ‘azazel, whose meaning is debated: it may describe the goat that departs, a remote place, or a name for a wilderness being. Interpreters across traditions differ here, and certainty is not possible.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters hold the same range of views on ‘azazel. Whatever the exact meaning, both agree the goat is not a sacrifice offered to anyone other than the LORD. It carries Israel’s sin away from the camp. The use of lots shows the LORD makes the choice (compare Proverbs 16:33).

Leviticus 16:9

9Aaron shall present the goat on which the lot fell for the LORD, and offer him for a sin offering.

Arminian

The LORD’s goat is offered as a sin offering, and its blood will be brought into the Most Holy Place. Arminian readers see here the costliness of forgiveness: sin is dealt with through death.

Reformed

Reformed readers see substitution in the death of the animal in place of the people, a reading shared by classical Arminians. The blood of this goat will be applied to the mercy seat in verse 15.

Leviticus 16:10

10But the goat on which the lot fell for the scapegoat shall be presented alive before the LORD, to make atonement for him, to send him away as the scapegoat into the wilderness.

Arminian

The second goat stays alive and is used “to make atonement” by being sent away. This shows that atonement in Leviticus is broader than a single act of killing; it also includes the removal of sin from God’s presence. Psalm 103:12 uses similar imagery of sins removed far away.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that atonement here includes removal. Many see in the two goats a picture of Christ, who both died for sin and bore it away. Both traditions read the live goat as completing, not competing with, the slain goat.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
