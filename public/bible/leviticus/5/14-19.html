Passage

Leviticus 5

Verses 14–19 · WEB

Verses 14–19 begin a new speech introducing the trespass offering (Hebrew asham, also rendered “guilt offering” or “reparation offering”). It covers unwitting trespass against the LORD’s holy things, which requires a ram plus restitution with an added fifth, and sins committed without knowledge. The phrase “according to your estimation in silver” may mean the ram had to be of a set value or that a monetary equivalent could be paid; interpreters differ. Leviticus 6:1–7 (WEB numbering) continues this offering for wrongs against a neighbor. Both traditions agree on the shared ground: guilt, restitution, and forgiveness. Isaiah 53:10 uses the same word asham for the Servant’s offering, which both traditions read as fulfilled in Christ.

Leviticus 5:14

14The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

A new speech begins, introducing the trespass offering (asham, also called the guilt or reparation offering).

Arminian readers see a further aspect of sin: it does harm that must be repaired as well as defiling.

Reformed

The new formula marks a distinct offering.

Reformed readers see God’s law addressing sin as a debt or damage as well as an impurity. Both traditions agree that the asham highlights restitution.

Leviticus 5:15

15“If anyone commits a trespass, and sins unwittingly regarding the LORD’s holy things, then he shall bring his trespass offering to the LORD: a ram without defect from the flock, according to your estimation in silver by shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, for a trespass offering.

Arminian

The case is an unwitting trespass against the LORD’s holy things, perhaps failing to give what was due to the sanctuary. He brings a ram without defect, valued in sanctuary shekels. Whether the ram had to meet a set value or could be replaced by money is debated.

Arminian readers see that wrongs against God’s holy things are real wrongs, even when unintended.

Reformed

The word for “trespass” (ma‘al) suggests unfaithfulness or misuse of what belongs to God. The ram is assessed by the sanctuary standard.

Reformed readers see God’s claim on what is consecrated to him. Both traditions agree that holy things are not to be treated as common.

Leviticus 5:16

16He shall make restitution for that which he has done wrong regarding the holy thing, and shall add a fifth part to it, and give it to the priest; and the priest shall make atonement for him with the ram of the trespass offering, and he will be forgiven.

Arminian

He must repay what he took or withheld, add a fifth, and give it to the priest. The priest then makes atonement with the ram, and he is forgiven.

Arminian readers see repentance shown in restitution. Forgiveness comes with a real effort to repair the wrong.

Reformed

Restitution plus a fifth accompanies the sacrifice. Repair and atonement go together.

Reformed readers see the justice of God’s law, which requires wrongs to be set right. Both traditions agree that restitution is part of genuine repentance (compare Luke 19:8).

Leviticus 5:17

17“If anyone sins, doing any of the things which the LORD has commanded not to be done, though he didn’t know it, he is still guilty, and shall bear his iniquity.

Arminian

Someone may break a command without knowing it, yet he is still guilty and bears his iniquity. Some interpreters see here a case of suspected but unconfirmed wrongdoing; the text does not settle this.

Arminian readers see God’s standard as objective. Ignorance reduces culpability but does not erase guilt.

Reformed

The verse states plainly that guilt can exist without knowledge.

Reformed readers stress that God’s law, not human awareness, defines sin (compare Ps 19:12). Both traditions agree that this verse shows the need for atonement even for unknown sin.

Leviticus 5:18

18He shall bring a ram without defect from of the flock, according to your estimation, for a trespass offering, to the priest; and the priest shall make atonement for him concerning the thing in which he sinned and didn’t know it, and he will be forgiven.

Arminian

He brings a ram without defect, and the priest makes atonement for the sin he did not know about. He will be forgiven.

Arminian readers see God’s generous provision for sins one cannot identify. God forgives what the sinner could not even name.

Reformed

Atonement covers the unknown sin, and forgiveness is promised.

Reformed readers see comfort here: God provides for faults hidden even from the sinner. Both traditions agree that forgiveness rests on God’s provision rather than on the sinner’s complete knowledge.

Leviticus 5:19

19It is a trespass offering. He is certainly guilty before the LORD.”

Arminian

The section closes: “It is a trespass offering. He is certainly guilty before the LORD.”

Arminian readers see that guilt is real before God, even when unintended, and that God has provided a remedy for it.

Reformed

The final words stress the reality of guilt before the LORD.

Reformed readers see the seriousness of guilt and the necessity of atonement. Both traditions read Isa 53:10, where the Servant is made an asham, as fulfilled in Christ, who bears guilt for others.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
