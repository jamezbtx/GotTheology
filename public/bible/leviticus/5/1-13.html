Passage

Leviticus 5

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Leviticus 5:1–13 continues the sin offering with particular cases: failing to testify when under public oath, unknowingly touching what is unclean, and making a rash oath. When the offender realizes his guilt, he must confess and bring an offering. The offering is graded by what he can afford: a female lamb or goat, two birds, or, for the poorest, a tenth of an ephah of fine flour. How this section relates to chapter 4 and to the guilt offering that follows is debated. Its offering is called a sin offering, yet 5:6–7 also uses the word for trespass or guilt (asham), which may here mean “penalty.” The meaning of “hidden from him” and of being “guilty” in verses 2–4 is also discussed. Both traditions see confession, God’s mercy to the poor, and real forgiveness as shared ground.

Leviticus 5:1

1“ ‘If anyone sins, in that he hears a public adjuration to testify, he being a witness, whether he has seen or known, if he doesn’t report it, then he shall bear his iniquity.

Arminian

The first case is a witness who hears a public adjuration, a formal call to testify, and stays silent though he saw or knew something. He “shall bear his iniquity,” that is, he is answerable for the guilt.

Arminian readers see that sins of omission are real sins. Withholding the truth when justice requires it is a failure of responsibility before God and neighbor.

Reformed

The case concerns a deliberate failure to speak, which is unlike the unwitting sins of chapter 4. That suggests the section covers a broader range than inadvertence alone, though its exact scope is debated.

Reformed readers connect the verse with the ninth commandment and the duty to uphold justice. Both traditions agree that silence can be sin.

Leviticus 5:2

2“ ‘Or if anyone touches any unclean thing, whether it is the carcass of an unclean animal, or the carcass of unclean livestock, or the carcass of unclean creeping things, and it is hidden from him, and he is unclean, then he shall be guilty.

Arminian

Touching the carcass of an unclean animal makes one unclean. The phrase “it is hidden from him” probably means he did not notice or forgot, and his guilt follows once he realizes it. The exact sense is debated.

Arminian readers see that holiness in Israel covered daily life. Uncleanness was not always sin in itself, but neglecting the required cleansing was.

Reformed

Contact with an unclean carcass brings uncleanness. Interpreters debate whether the guilt arises from the contact or from failing to purify after it.

Reformed readers see God’s holiness touching every part of Israel’s life. Both traditions agree that the purity laws taught Israel to treat God’s presence seriously.

Leviticus 5:3

3“ ‘Or if he touches the uncleanness of man, whatever his uncleanness is with which he is unclean, and it is hidden from him; when he knows of it, then he shall be guilty.

Arminian

Human uncleanness, such as that from bodily discharges or disease described later in Leviticus, is also included. When the person learns of it, he is guilty.

Arminian readers see the moment of knowing as the point of responsibility. God holds people accountable for what they come to know.

Reformed

The phrase “when he knows of it” marks the time when guilt must be addressed.

Reformed readers stress that God’s standard does not depend on human awareness, though awareness brings the duty to respond. Both traditions agree that ritual impurity had to be dealt with before approaching God.

Leviticus 5:4

4“ ‘Or if anyone swears rashly with his lips to do evil or to do good—whatever it is that a man might utter rashly with an oath, and it is hidden from him—when he knows of it, then he will be guilty of one of these.

Arminian

A rash oath “to do evil or to do good” is a careless vow, perhaps one that was forgotten or was unwise. When he realizes it, he is guilty. The exact case is not fully clear.

Arminian readers see the weight of spoken words before God. Careless promises carry real responsibility.

Reformed

The rash oath is a thoughtless use of the LORD’s name or of solemn speech. Whether the guilt lies in the oath itself or in failing to keep it is debated.

Reformed readers connect it with the third commandment and Jesus’ teaching on oaths (Matt 5:33–37). Both traditions agree that speech is accountable to God.

Leviticus 5:5

5It shall be, when he is guilty of one of these, he shall confess that in which he has sinned;

Arminian

When guilty in any of these ways, the offender must confess the sin he has committed. Confession is required here explicitly.

Arminian readers see the necessary personal response. Ritual alone is not enough; the sinner must acknowledge the wrong.

Reformed

Confession comes before the offering. The sinner names his sin.

Reformed readers note that true repentance includes acknowledging sin (compare Ps 32:5; 1 John 1:9). Both traditions agree that confession and sacrifice belong together.

Leviticus 5:6

6and he shall bring his trespass offering to the LORD for his sin which he has sinned: a female from the flock, a lamb or a goat, for a sin offering; and the priest shall make atonement for him concerning his sin.

Arminian

He brings a female lamb or goat as his “trespass offering” for a sin offering, and the priest makes atonement. The Hebrew word here (asham) may mean “penalty” or “compensation” rather than naming the separate guilt offering of 5:14.

Arminian readers see confession joined to God’s provision. The offerer brings what God requires, and God accepts it.

Reformed

The WEB’s “trespass offering” renders asham. In this context many interpreters take it as the penalty brought for the sin, while the offering itself is called a sin offering.

Reformed readers stress that atonement comes through the priest and the appointed sacrifice. Both traditions agree on the shared ground: confessed sin is atoned for by God’s provision.

Leviticus 5:7

7“ ‘If he can’t afford a lamb, then he shall bring his trespass offering for that in which he has sinned, two turtledoves, or two young pigeons, to the LORD; one for a sin offering, and the other for a burnt offering.

Arminian

If he cannot afford a lamb, he may bring two turtledoves or two young pigeons, one for a sin offering and one for a burnt offering.

Arminian readers see God’s mercy to the poor. Poverty does not shut anyone out of atonement.

Reformed

The concession lets the poor take part fully. Mary and Joseph later bring the poor person’s offering of birds, though for purification after childbirth (Luke 2:24; Lev 12:8).

Reformed readers stress God’s gracious accommodation. Both traditions agree that access to God does not depend on wealth.

Leviticus 5:8

8He shall bring them to the priest, who shall first offer the one which is for the sin offering. He shall wring off its head from its neck, but shall not sever it completely.

Arminian

The priest offers the sin-offering bird first, wringing its head without severing it.

Arminian readers note that dealing with sin comes before the burnt offering of devotion. Reconciliation leads into worship.

Reformed

The order places the sin offering before the burnt offering.

Reformed readers see a theological order in which sin is dealt with before full dedication. Both traditions find in this sequence a pattern of cleansing before consecration.

Leviticus 5:9

9He shall sprinkle some of the blood of the sin offering on the side of the altar; and the rest of the blood shall be drained out at the base of the altar. It is a sin offering.

Arminian

Some blood is sprinkled on the side of the altar, and the rest drained at its base. A bird yields little blood, so the procedure is adapted.

Arminian readers see that the smaller offering is just as truly a sin offering.

Reformed

The text says plainly, “It is a sin offering.” The rite is complete despite its scale.

Reformed readers stress that effectiveness rests on God’s appointment, not the size of the gift. Both traditions agree that the poor person’s offering is fully valid.

Leviticus 5:10

10He shall offer the second for a burnt offering, according to the ordinance; and the priest shall make atonement for him concerning his sin which he has sinned, and he shall be forgiven.

Arminian

The second bird is offered as a burnt offering. The priest makes atonement, and he shall be forgiven.

Arminian readers see the same promise of forgiveness given to the poor as to the wealthy.

Reformed

Forgiveness is promised “according to the ordinance.”

Reformed readers stress that the promise rests on God’s word. Both traditions agree that God’s pardon is equal for every class of worshiper.

Leviticus 5:11

11“ ‘But if he can’t afford two turtledoves or two young pigeons, then he shall bring as his offering for that in which he has sinned, one tenth of an ephah of fine flour for a sin offering. He shall put no oil on it, and he shall not put any frankincense on it, for it is a sin offering.

Arminian

The poorest may bring a tenth of an ephah of fine flour, with no oil or frankincense, because it is a sin offering and not a joyful grain offering.

Arminian readers see how far God’s mercy reaches. Even those who cannot afford birds have a way to atonement.

Reformed

The flour offering is plain, with no oil or frankincense, which suits its purpose for sin. A bloodless sin offering is an exception in the system.

Reformed readers often note Hebrews 9:22, which says that under the law “nearly everything is cleansed with blood”, and see this exception as consistent with it. Both traditions agree that God provides for the poorest.

Leviticus 5:12

12He shall bring it to the priest, and the priest shall take his handful of it as the memorial portion, and burn it on the altar, on the offerings of the LORD made by fire. It is a sin offering.

Arminian

The priest takes a handful as the memorial portion and burns it on the altar with the offerings made by fire.

Arminian readers see the poor person’s gift placed on the same altar as the others. It is not a second-class offering.

Reformed

The flour is burned on top of the LORD’s fire offerings. The text again affirms, “It is a sin offering.”

Reformed readers stress that God’s acceptance rests on his own appointment. Both traditions agree that the poorest worshiper truly shares in the altar.

Leviticus 5:13

13The priest shall make atonement for him concerning his sin that he has sinned in any of these things, and he will be forgiven; and the rest shall be the priest’s, as the meal offering.’ ”

Arminian

The priest makes atonement, and he is forgiven. The rest belongs to the priest, as with the grain offering.

Arminian readers note that the section ends, as chapter 4 did, with forgiveness. God’s purpose is restoration for all.

Reformed

Atonement and forgiveness close the section. The priest’s portion supports his ministry.

Reformed readers stress the sure promise attached to God’s provision. Both traditions agree that Israel’s offerings anticipated the full atonement in Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
