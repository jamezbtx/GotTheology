Passage

Leviticus 17

Verses 1–9 · WEB

The LORD commands that any Israelite who slaughters a bull, lamb, or goat must bring it to the door of the Tent of Meeting as an offering, rather than killing it in the open field. Anyone who fails to do so incurs bloodguilt and is cut off. The aim is to bring sacrifice under the LORD’s altar and end sacrifice to goat idols. The rule against offering elsewhere applies to resident foreigners as well.

Leviticus 17:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

A new section begins with the familiar formula of divine speech. Leviticus 17 is often treated as the opening of the so-called Holiness Code (chapters 17–26), though scholars debate where that unit begins and how it was formed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the transition. Whatever the literary questions, both traditions receive the chapter as the LORD’s word to Moses for Israel’s life in his presence.

Leviticus 17:2

2“Speak to Aaron, and to his sons, and to all the children of Israel, and say to them, ‘This is the thing which the LORD has commanded:

Arminian

The instruction is addressed to priests and people alike: “This is the thing which the LORD has commanded.” Worship is not left to individual preference; the whole community is accountable.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same breadth. The law governs priests and laity together under one Lord. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 17:3

3Whatever man there is of the house of Israel who kills a bull, or lamb, or goat in the camp, or who kills it outside the camp,

Arminian

The law concerns killing a sacrificial animal, whether in or outside the camp. Interpreters differ on whether this means all slaughter of such animals in the wilderness period or only slaughter intended as sacrifice. Deuteronomy 12:15, 20–21 later permits ordinary slaughter away from the sanctuary once Israel is settled and the place is far off.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters hold the same range of views. Many read Deuteronomy 12 as adapting this wilderness law to life in the land. The interpretive question is about history and application, not about a dispute between the two traditions.

Leviticus 17:4

4and hasn’t brought it to the door of the Tent of Meeting to offer it as an offering to the LORD before the LORD’s tabernacle: blood shall be imputed to that man. He has shed blood. That man shall be cut off from among his people.

Arminian

Killing such an animal without bringing it to the LORD’s tabernacle counts as bloodguilt: “He has shed blood.” Life belongs to God, so taking it apart from his appointed altar is a serious offense. The penalty is being cut off from the people.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that life is God’s property and that worship must follow his appointment. Interpreters in both traditions discuss whether “cut off” means death, exclusion from the community, or divine judgment; the text leaves that open.

Leviticus 17:5

5This is to the end that the children of Israel may bring their sacrifices, which they sacrifice in the open field, that they may bring them to the LORD, to the door of the Tent of Meeting, to the priest, and sacrifice them for sacrifices of peace offerings to the LORD.

Arminian

The purpose is stated: sacrifices made in the open field are to be brought to the LORD at the sanctuary as peace offerings. Arminian readers see God drawing his people’s worship toward the place he has chosen, where fellowship with him is enjoyed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same concern for centralized, regulated worship. Peace offerings were shared meals expressing communion with God. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 17:6

6The priest shall sprinkle the blood on the LORD’s altar at the door of the Tent of Meeting, and burn the fat for a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian

The priest applies the blood to the LORD’s altar and burns the fat as a pleasant aroma. Proper worship includes giving God what belongs to him, especially the blood and the fat.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest’s role guards the integrity of worship. The language of a pleasing aroma appears throughout Leviticus and is echoed in Ephesians 5:2 of Christ’s offering. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 17:7

7They shall no more sacrifice their sacrifices to the goat idols, after which they play the prostitute. This shall be a statute forever to them throughout their generations.’

Arminian

The law aims to stop sacrifices to “goat idols,” which the text calls playing the prostitute. Unregulated sacrifice in the field risked drifting into worship of other powers. Arminian readers note that God’s commands protect his people from spiritual unfaithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see idolatry pictured as covenant unfaithfulness. The exact nature of these “goat idols” is uncertain, though the same Hebrew term appears in 2 Chronicles 11:15. Both traditions read the verse as a call to exclusive loyalty to the LORD.

Leviticus 17:8

8“You shall say to them, ‘Any man there is of the house of Israel, or of the strangers who live as foreigners among them, who offers a burnt offering or sacrifice,

Arminian

The rule extends to foreigners living among Israel who offer burnt offerings or sacrifices. Arminian readers see God’s concern that everyone who worships within Israel worship him rightly.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the resident foreigner comes under the same covenant requirements for worship. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 17:9

9and doesn’t bring it to the door of the Tent of Meeting to sacrifice it to the LORD, that man shall be cut off from his people.

Arminian

Failure to bring the offering to the Tent of Meeting results in being cut off. Worship offered elsewhere is not neutral; it is treated as a breach of covenant.

Reformed

Reformed readers draw from this the principle that God determines how he is to be worshiped. Arminians commonly affirm the same seriousness here, though the two traditions, and denominations within them, apply the principle to Christian worship in varied ways.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
