Passage

Leviticus 17

Verses 10–16 · WEB

The LORD forbids eating blood, for Israelites and foreigners alike, because the life of the flesh is in the blood and God has given it on the altar to make atonement. Hunters must pour out the blood of game and cover it with dust. Anyone who eats an animal that died naturally or was torn by beasts must wash and remains unclean until evening.

Leviticus 17:10

10“ ‘Any man of the house of Israel, or of the strangers who live as foreigners among them, who eats any kind of blood, I will set my face against that soul who eats blood, and will cut him off from among his people.

Arminian

Eating blood is forbidden to Israelites and resident foreigners, and God himself sets his face against anyone who does it. The prohibition goes back to Genesis 9:4, given to Noah and so to all humanity. Arminian readers note the solemn personal language: God takes direct action.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise trace the law to Genesis 9:4 and note its universal roots. The phrase “set my face against” stresses personal divine judgment. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 17:11

11For the life of the flesh is in the blood. I have given it to you on the altar to make atonement for your souls; for it is the blood that makes atonement by reason of the life.

Arminian

This is the key verse of the chapter: “the life of the flesh is in the blood,” and God has given it on the altar “to make atonement for your souls.” Blood atones because it represents life given up. God gives the means of atonement; Israel does not devise it. Hebrews 9:22 reflects the same principle that without the shedding of blood there is no remission.

Reformed

Reformed readers read this verse as a foundation for substitutionary atonement: life is given for life. Classical Arminians affirm the same. Both traditions see the verse fulfilled in Christ’s blood, through which believers have redemption (Ephesians 1:7). The verse is shared ground rather than a point of contrast.

Leviticus 17:12

12Therefore I have said to the children of Israel, “No person among you may eat blood, nor may any stranger who lives as a foreigner among you eat blood.”

Arminian

The prohibition is restated for all: no Israelite or foreigner may eat blood. Because blood is set apart for atonement, it must not be treated as ordinary food.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same logic: what God reserves for the altar must not be used for common purposes. This is shared ground.

Leviticus 17:13

13“ ‘Whatever man there is of the children of Israel, or of the strangers who live as foreigners among them, who takes in hunting any animal or bird that may be eaten, he shall pour out its blood, and cover it with dust.

Arminian

Even blood from hunted animals, which are never offered on the altar, must be poured out and covered with dust. Respect for life extends beyond the sanctuary into everyday life.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the law teaches reverence for life as God’s gift. Some see the covering of the blood as a burial-like act of respect. The text does not explain it further.

Leviticus 17:14

14For as to the life of all flesh, its blood is with its life. Therefore I said to the children of Israel, “You shall not eat the blood of any kind of flesh; for the life of all flesh is its blood. Whoever eats it shall be cut off.”

Arminian

The reason is repeated: the life of all flesh is its blood, and whoever eats it shall be cut off. The doubling of the reason and the stated penalty show that this is not treated as a minor dietary preference.

Arminian readers hear the repetition as underlining how serious the matter is for Israel’s life with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same emphasis. In Acts 15:20, 29 the Jerusalem council asks Gentile believers to abstain from blood. Interpreters in both traditions discuss how far that request was meant as lasting moral law or as a measure for fellowship between Jewish and Gentile Christians.

Leviticus 17:15

15“ ‘Every person that eats what dies of itself, or that which is torn by animals, whether he is native-born or a foreigner, shall wash his clothes, and bathe himself in water, and be unclean until the evening. Then he shall be clean.

Arminian

Anyone who eats an animal that died naturally or was killed by beasts must wash and is unclean until evening. Such meat would not have had its blood properly drained. The remedy is simple and available to native and foreigner alike.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this law deals with ritual uncleanness, which can be removed by washing, rather than with deliberate defiance. Both traditions see God’s provision of cleansing for everyday impurity.

Leviticus 17:16

16But if he doesn’t wash them, or bathe his flesh, then he shall bear his iniquity.’ ”

Arminian

Refusing to wash means bearing one’s iniquity. Neglecting God’s provision has consequences. Arminian readers stress personal responsibility to use the means of cleansing God gives.

Reformed

Reformed readers also stress responsibility, along with the seriousness of disregarding God’s ordinances. Both traditions read the chapter as teaching that holiness covers ordinary life, not only formal worship.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
