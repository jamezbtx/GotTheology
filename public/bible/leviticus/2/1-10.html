Passage

Leviticus 2

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Chapter 2 turns from the animal burnt offering to the meal offering of fine flour, oil, and frankincense. A memorial portion is burned as a pleasant aroma to the LORD; what is left is most holy and belongs to Aaron and his sons. The offering may be brought as raw flour or cooked in the oven, on a griddle, or in a pan, but it is unleavened and mixed or anointed with oil. Both traditions treat this bloodless tribute as a gift of the offerer’s labor that accompanies the animal sacrifices. They share a caution not to force a symbol onto every ingredient, and they see its fulfillment in Christ’s perfect offering without inventing an Arminian or Reformed fork over the typology.

Leviticus 2:1

1“ ‘When anyone offers an offering of a meal offering to the LORD, his offering shall be of fine flour. He shall pour oil on it, and put frankincense on it.

Arminian

When anyone offers a meal offering to the LORD, it is to be of fine flour, with oil poured on it and frankincense put on it.

Arminian readers see a willing gift from daily food and labor: the worshiper brings choice flour and costly fragrance, not a leftover handful.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that even this bloodless offering has a prescribed form: fine flour, oil, and frankincense. Approach to the LORD is still on his terms.

Both traditions read the meal offering as a tribute of devotion that belongs with the burnt offerings of chapter 1, not as a substitute for atoning blood.

Leviticus 2:2

2He shall bring it to Aaron’s sons, the priests. He shall take his handful of its fine flour, and of its oil, with all its frankincense, and the priest shall burn its memorial on the altar, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian

The offerer brings the gift to Aaron’s sons, the priests. A handful of the fine flour and oil, with all the frankincense, is burned as its memorial, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian readers see the “memorial” as the portion that brings the offerer before God: a part goes up, and the worshiper is remembered in the offering God has appointed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take “memorial” as the altar portion that represents the whole gift before the LORD. The frankincense is all burned, not kept back.

Both traditions see God’s acceptance in the pleasant-aroma language already used of the burnt offering, and they again find its fullness in Christ’s fragrant offering (Ephesians 5:2).

Leviticus 2:3

3That which is left of the meal offering shall be Aaron’s and his sons’. It is a most holy part of the offerings of the LORD made by fire.

Arminian

What remains of the meal offering belongs to Aaron and his sons. It is a most holy part of the offerings of the LORD made by fire.

Arminian readers see God providing for the priests from the people’s gifts, and they note the “most holy” charge: this food is not common and must be handled as belonging to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the priests receive a portion God assigns, not a wage they take for themselves. “Most holy” marks the leftover as still the LORD’s.

Both traditions agree that ministry at the tent is sustained by God’s provision, and that holiness attaches even to what the priests eat.

Leviticus 2:4

4“ ‘When you offer an offering of a meal offering baked in the oven, it shall be unleavened cakes of fine flour mixed with oil, or unleavened wafers anointed with oil.

Arminian

A meal offering baked in the oven is to be unleavened cakes of fine flour mixed with oil, or unleavened wafers anointed with oil.

Arminian readers see household labor brought into worship: baked food, carefully prepared without yeast and with oil, is an acceptable gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the two permitted forms and the repeated requirement that the cakes or wafers be unleavened. The oven does not give the offerer liberty to change the offering’s character.

Both traditions wait until verses 11–12 for the yeast rule’s fuller statement, and they do not divide over these baking details.

Leviticus 2:5

5If your offering is a meal offering made on a griddle, it shall be of unleavened fine flour, mixed with oil.

Arminian

If the meal offering is made on a griddle, it is to be of unleavened fine flour mixed with oil.

Arminian readers hear the same standards applied to a simpler cooking method. A more ordinary pan does not excuse a lesser gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the griddle offering bound by the same rules of fine flour, oil, and the absence of yeast.

Both traditions read the variety of cooking methods as making the offering possible in more than one household form, under one divine pattern.

Leviticus 2:6

6You shall cut it in pieces, and pour oil on it. It is a meal offering.

Arminian

The griddle offering is cut in pieces and oil is poured on it. It is a meal offering.

Arminian readers see the gift further prepared so that it can be presented and a memorial portion taken. Oil is not withheld.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the explicit identification, “It is a meal offering,” after the pieces are oiled. The preparation does not turn it into another kind of sacrifice.

Both traditions treat the verse as procedural, without forcing a separate symbolism onto the cutting or the oil.

Leviticus 2:7

7If your offering is a meal offering of the pan, it shall be made of fine flour with oil.

Arminian

If the offering is a meal offering of the pan, it shall be made of fine flour with oil.

Arminian readers again see the same ingredients required of a third cooking form. Devotion is measured by obedience to what God asks, not by elaborate invention.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the consistency: fine flour with oil, whether from the oven, the griddle, or the pan.

Both traditions find no contrast here, only the same tribute prescribed under different methods of preparation.

Leviticus 2:8

8You shall bring the meal offering that is made of these things to the LORD. It shall be presented to the priest, and he shall bring it to the altar.

Arminian

The prepared meal offering is brought to the LORD, presented to the priest, and carried by him to the altar.

Arminian readers see a personal act of bringing the gift, then entrusting it to the appointed priest so that it reaches the altar.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the path the offering must take: to the LORD, by means of the priest, to the altar. The worshiper does not place it on the fire himself.

Both traditions see mediated approach as God’s provision in this covenant setting, later fulfilled in the one mediator, Christ.

Leviticus 2:9

9The priest shall take from the meal offering its memorial, and shall burn it on the altar, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian

The priest takes the memorial from the meal offering and burns it on the altar, an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian readers see the cooked offerings reach the same end as the raw flour of verse 2: a memorial portion goes up, and God accepts the gift he appointed.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the repeated memorial-and-aroma formula. Acceptance rests on the LORD’s ordinance, not on the cooking method.

Both traditions again relate the pleasant aroma to God’s pleasure in the offering fulfilled in Christ (Ephesians 5:2).

Leviticus 2:10

10That which is left of the meal offering shall be Aaron’s and his sons’. It is a most holy part of the offerings of the LORD made by fire.

Arminian

As in verse 3, what is left belongs to Aaron and his sons and is a most holy part of the offerings made by fire.

Arminian readers hear the rule restated so that every form of the meal offering supports the priests and remains holy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repetition as closing the unit: whatever the preparation, the leftover is most holy and is the priests’ portion by God’s gift.

Both traditions find shared ground here and no doctrinal fork.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
