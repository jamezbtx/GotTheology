Passage

Leviticus 2

Verses 11–16 · WEB

The meal-offering law now adds what may not be burned on the altar and what must not be lacking. Yeast and honey are not to be offered as an offering made by fire to the LORD, though they may be brought as first-fruits offerings that do not rise as a pleasant aroma on the altar. Every meal offering is to be seasoned with the salt of the covenant of God. First-fruits grain, parched and crushed, with oil and frankincense, is also a meal offering whose memorial is burned. Both traditions read the salt as a sign of the covenant’s enduring claim on Israel’s gifts, and they do not divide over the exclusions or the first-fruits rite.

Leviticus 2:11

11“ ‘No meal offering which you shall offer to the LORD shall be made with yeast; for you shall burn no yeast, nor any honey, as an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

No meal offering is to be made with yeast, and neither yeast nor honey is to be burned as an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian readers take the prohibition as it stands: some ordinary good things are not for the altar fire. The text itself does not state the reason.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise start from the command rather than from a theory. Interpreters in both traditions have often associated yeast with fermentation and corruption in sacrificial settings, and honey with a sweetness that ferments or with an addition God did not ask, but that rationale is not given here.

Both traditions therefore hold the rule firmly and treat explanations as secondary.

Leviticus 2:12

12As an offering of first fruits you shall offer them to the LORD, but they shall not rise up as a pleasant aroma on the altar.

Arminian

Yeast and honey may still be offered to the LORD as first fruits, but they shall not rise up as a pleasant aroma on the altar.

Arminian readers see a distinction of use: the first of the produce may be given, yet it is not treated as the fire offering of verses 2 and 9.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the ban in verse 11 is specifically about the altar fire, not a ban on ever bringing these foods. First fruits belong to God in their own way.

Both traditions let the verse correct an over-reading of verse 11, and they wait for later first-fruits laws rather than merging them here.

Leviticus 2:13

13Every offering of your meal offering you shall season with salt. You shall not allow the salt of the covenant of your God to be lacking from your meal offering. With all your offerings you shall offer salt.

Arminian

Every meal offering is to be seasoned with salt. The salt of the covenant of God must not be lacking, and salt is to be offered with all the offerings.

Arminian readers hear a covenant claim on ordinary worship: the gift is not complete without the sign God names, and the offerer must not omit it.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the covenant language. Salt marks the offering as bound to God’s enduring covenant, a use also found when Numbers 18:19 speaks of a “covenant of salt forever.”

Both traditions read the verse as requiring a covenant token in worship, and they do not force a contrast here.

Leviticus 2:14

14“ ‘If you offer a meal offering of first fruits to the LORD, you shall offer for the meal offering of your first fruits fresh heads of grain parched with fire and crushed.

Arminian

If a meal offering of first fruits is offered to the LORD, it is to be fresh heads of grain, parched with fire and crushed.

Arminian readers see the first of the harvest brought in a prepared form: new grain, roasted and broken, as a grateful gift of what God has just given.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that even first-fruits grain has a prescribed preparation. The freshness of the crop does not exempt the offerer from the LORD’s pattern.

Both traditions treat this as a lawful form of the meal offering, alongside the fine-flour gifts earlier in the chapter.

Leviticus 2:15

15You shall put oil on it and lay frankincense on it. It is a meal offering.

Arminian

Oil is put on the grain and frankincense is laid on it. It is a meal offering.

Arminian readers see the same accompaniments as in verse 1, now applied to the first-fruits grain. The early harvest is offered in the same character of tribute.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the explicit identification: with oil and frankincense, the parched grain “is a meal offering.”

Both traditions find continuity, not a new kind of sacrifice, and no contrast between the traditions.

Leviticus 2:16

16The priest shall burn as its memorial part of its crushed grain and part of its oil, along with all its frankincense. It is an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

The priest burns a memorial portion of the crushed grain and of the oil, along with all the frankincense. It is an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian readers see the first-fruits gift completed in the same memorial pattern as the earlier meal offerings: part goes to the altar, and the worshiper is brought before God with the first of the field.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that all the frankincense is again burned, and that the verse calls the memorial an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Both traditions close the chapter on shared ground: the meal offering, in every form, is God’s appointed tribute, fulfilled in Christ’s perfect offering and not a site of Arminian or Reformed disagreement.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
