Passage

Leviticus 12

Verses 1–8 · WEB

After the food laws of chapter 11, Leviticus turns to ritual impurity connected with the human body, beginning with childbirth. A mother is unclean for a set period after giving birth, longer for a daughter than for a son, and then brings a burnt offering and a sin offering so that the priest can make atonement and declare her clean. The chapter does not treat childbirth or the mother as sinful; children are a blessing throughout Scripture (Genesis 1:28; Psalm 127:3). Ritual impurity in Leviticus is a matter of status before the holy sanctuary, here tied to the flow of blood, not a matter of moral guilt. Both traditions read the passage with that distinction in view. Verse 8 allows a poorer family to bring two birds, the offering Mary brought after Jesus’ birth (Luke 2:22–24).

Leviticus 12:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses, as throughout Leviticus. These purity laws come as God’s word, not human custom.

Arminian readers note that God gives his people clear instruction for living near his holy presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the regulations rest on God’s authority and serve his purpose of dwelling among Israel.

Both traditions read what follows as divine provision, not as arbitrary rules.

Leviticus 12:2

2“Speak to the children of Israel, saying, ‘If a woman conceives, and bears a male child, then she shall be unclean seven days; as in the days of her monthly period she shall be unclean.

Arminian

A mother who bears a son is unclean seven days, as in her monthly period. The comparison links this impurity to the flow of blood (compare Leviticus 15:19).

Arminian readers note that the impurity is ritual, not moral; childbirth is a blessing, not a sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish ritual uncleanness from moral guilt. Some older Christian readers linked the rite to inherited sin (compare Psalm 51:5), but the text itself ties it to blood.

Both traditions affirm the dignity of the mother and the goodness of new life.

Leviticus 12:3

3In the eighth day the flesh of his foreskin shall be circumcised.

Arminian

On the eighth day the boy is circumcised. The verse recalls God’s covenant sign given to Abraham (Genesis 17:12).

Arminian readers note that the child is brought into the covenant community from his earliest days.

Reformed

Reformed readers often connect circumcision with covenant signs for children of believers, a point on which Christians differ, including within each tradition.

Both traditions note that Jesus was circumcised on the eighth day (Luke 2:21).

Leviticus 12:4

4She shall continue in the blood of purification thirty-three days. She shall not touch any holy thing, nor come into the sanctuary, until the days of her purifying are completed.

Arminian

The mother continues thirty-three days in “the blood of purification” and may not touch holy things or enter the sanctuary. This restricts access to holy space, not ordinary life with her family.

Arminian readers note that the rule protects the sanctuary’s holiness while giving the mother a period of recovery.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the separation is from the holy, not from the community.

Both traditions avoid reading the restriction as shame or punishment.

Leviticus 12:5

5But if she bears a female child, then she shall be unclean two weeks, as in her period; and she shall continue in the blood of purification sixty-six days.

Arminian

For a daughter, the periods are doubled: two weeks and sixty-six days. The text does not explain why.

Arminian readers note that various explanations have been proposed, and none is stated in the passage.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise refrain from reading a lower value of daughters into the law; elsewhere Scripture affirms male and female as made in God’s image (Genesis 1:27).

Both traditions hold the reason for the difference as uncertain.

Leviticus 12:6

6“ ‘When the days of her purification are completed for a son or for a daughter, she shall bring to the priest at the door of the Tent of Meeting, a year old lamb for a burnt offering, and a young pigeon or a turtledove, for a sin offering.

Arminian

At the end of purification, she brings a year-old lamb for a burnt offering and a pigeon or dove for a sin offering. The same offerings apply for a son or daughter.

Arminian readers note that the offerings restore her to full access to worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that many interpreters understand the Hebrew term for “sin offering” here as a purification offering, addressing ritual impurity rather than a particular sin.

Both traditions read the offering as restoration, not as a penalty for giving birth.

Leviticus 12:7

7He shall offer it before the LORD, and make atonement for her; then she shall be cleansed from the fountain of her blood. “ ‘This is the law for her who bears, whether a male or a female.

Arminian

The priest makes atonement for her, and she is cleansed from the flow of her blood. Atonement in Leviticus can mean cleansing from impurity, not only forgiveness of sin.

Arminian readers note God’s provision for restoring the mother to the worshiping community.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest’s mediation points forward to Christ’s greater work of cleansing (Hebrews 9:13–14).

Both traditions agree that the verse focuses on purification and welcome.

Leviticus 12:8

8If she cannot afford a lamb, then she shall take two turtledoves or two young pigeons: the one for a burnt offering, and the other for a sin offering. The priest shall make atonement for her, and she shall be clean.’ ”

Arminian

If she cannot afford a lamb, she brings two turtledoves or two young pigeons. Mary brought this offering after Jesus’ birth (Luke 2:22–24).

Arminian readers note God’s care for the poor; no family is excluded from worship by poverty.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Jesus was born into a family that brought the offering of the poor, a sign of his humility (compare 2 Corinthians 8:9).

Both traditions treasure this link between Leviticus and the Gospel.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
