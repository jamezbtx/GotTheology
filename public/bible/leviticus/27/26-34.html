Passage

Leviticus 27

Verses 26–34 · WEB

The last unit names what cannot be vowed, or cannot be redeemed, and then treats the tithe. A firstborn animal already belongs to the LORD and may not be dedicated as if it were the vower’s to give; an unclean firstborn may be bought back with the added fifth, or sold according to the valuation. A thing permanently devoted to the LORD may not be sold or redeemed; it is most holy. A person devoted to destruction is not to be ransomed and is to be put to death. Tithes of seed, fruit, and flock are already the LORD’s and holy; redeeming a tithe adds a fifth, and animals of the tithe may not be picked over or exchanged. The book closes with the commandments the LORD commanded Moses for Israel on Mount Sinai. Both traditions read the section as constructive law, and they note uncertainty where the text does not explain the exact legal setting of the ban in verses 28–29, rather than treating a disputed meaning as settled.

Leviticus 27:26

26“ ‘However the firstborn among animals, which belongs to the LORD as a firstborn, no man may dedicate, whether an ox or a sheep. It is the LORD’s.

Arminian

The firstborn among animals, which already belongs to the LORD as a firstborn, may not be dedicated, whether an ox or a sheep. It is the LORD’s.

Arminian readers see a simple limit on vows: one cannot offer God what is already his. The firstborn law is assumed, not invented here.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress prior ownership. Exodus 13:2, 12 already claims the firstborn as the LORD’s; a vow cannot pretend to give it again.

Both traditions take the verse as a guard against empty dedication, and they find shared ground in it.

Leviticus 27:27

27If it is an unclean animal, then he shall buy it back according to your valuation, and shall add to it the fifth part of it; or if it isn’t redeemed, then it shall be sold according to your valuation.

Arminian

If the firstborn is an unclean animal, the owner may buy it back at the valuation plus a fifth, or if it is not redeemed it shall be sold according to the valuation.

Arminian readers hear a practical path for an animal that cannot be offered on the altar: redeem it at cost, or let it be sold at the stated value.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same alternatives, consistent with Exodus 13:13 on the donkey and with the added fifth already used in this chapter.

Both traditions read the verse as a regulated exception for unclean firstborn, still without a doctrinal contrast.

Leviticus 27:28

28“ ‘Notwithstanding, no devoted thing that a man devotes to the LORD of all that he has, whether of man or animal, or of the field of his possession, shall be sold or redeemed. Everything that is permanently devoted is most holy to the LORD.

Arminian

No devoted thing that a person devotes to the LORD, of all that he has, whether person, animal, or inherited field, shall be sold or redeemed. Everything permanently devoted is most holy to the LORD.

Arminian readers hear a stricter category than the ordinary vow: what is permanently devoted cannot be bought back. It is most holy, wholly the LORD’s.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish this “devoted” (ban) gift from the redeemable dedications earlier in the chapter. Sale and redemption are both barred.

Both traditions take the irreversibility seriously. They also note that the text does not spell out every case in which a person or field is placed under this ban, and they do not treat a single further explanation as agreed.

Leviticus 27:29

29“ ‘No one devoted to destruction, who shall be devoted from among men, shall be ransomed. He shall surely be put to death.

Arminian

No one devoted to destruction, who is devoted from among men, shall be ransomed. He shall surely be put to death.

Arminian readers let the hard sentence stand: a person placed under this ban may not be bought back and is to be put to death. The verse does not, by itself, explain every setting in which that devotion occurs.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise refuse a ransom here. Interpreters often relate the line to the ban already known in Israel’s laws of judgment and war, but the exact legal setting is discussed rather than settled by this verse alone.

Both traditions decline to read the text as authorizing a private vow of human sacrifice, and they do not turn the remaining uncertainty into an Arminian or Reformed contrast.

Leviticus 27:30

30“ ‘All the tithe of the land, whether of the seed of the land or of the fruit of the trees, is the LORD’s. It is holy to the LORD.

Arminian

All the tithe of the land, whether seed or fruit of the trees, is the LORD’s. It is holy to the LORD.

Arminian readers see the tithe as already God’s, not as a gift the worshiper invents. Holiness attaches to the tenth of the land’s yield.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same prior claim: “It is the LORD’s.” The tithe is holy before anyone decides to treat it so.

Both traditions read the verse as a statement of God’s ownership, distinct from the voluntary vows that opened the chapter.

Leviticus 27:31

31If a man redeems anything of his tithe, he shall add a fifth part to it.

Arminian

If a man redeems anything of his tithe, he shall add a fifth part to it.

Arminian readers hear that even the tithe may, in some cases, be redeemed, but only with the added fifth already familiar from vows. God’s portion is not reclaimed cheaply.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same surcharge. Redemption of a tithe, where it is allowed, still costs more than the stated value.

Both traditions take the verse as a consistent rule of costly redemption, on shared ground.

Leviticus 27:32

32All the tithe of the herds or the flocks, whatever passes under the rod, the tenth shall be holy to the LORD.

Arminian

All the tithe of the herds or flocks, whatever passes under the rod, the tenth shall be holy to the LORD.

Arminian readers see the flock tithe counted as the animals pass under the rod, with every tenth set apart as holy. The worshiper does not first choose which animal to give.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the counted tenth. “Whatever passes under the rod” describes an ordered taking, not a private selection of the least or the best.

Both traditions read the verse as God’s claim on a counted tenth of the flock, and they do not require a further agreed symbolism for the rod.

Leviticus 27:33

33He shall not examine whether it is good or bad, neither shall he exchange it. If he exchanges it at all, then both it and that for which it is exchanged shall be holy. It shall not be redeemed.’ ”

Arminian

The giver shall not examine whether the animal is good or bad, and shall not exchange it. If he exchanges it at all, both it and the substitute shall be holy, and it shall not be redeemed. The divine speech ends here.

Arminian readers hear the same integrity already required in verse 10, now applied to the tithe: no picking through the flock, and a forbidden exchange makes both animals holy and unredeemable.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the stricter end: unlike some vowed animals, this exchanged tithe “shall not be redeemed.” The LORD’s tenth is not to be managed for advantage.

Both traditions take the closing rule as a guard on the tithe’s holiness, and they see the final quotes as ending the speech that began in verse 2.

Leviticus 27:34

34These are the commandments which the LORD commanded Moses for the children of Israel on Mount Sinai.

Arminian

A final colophon closes the book: these are the commandments which the LORD commanded Moses for the children of Israel on Mount Sinai.

Arminian readers see the vow and tithe laws, with all that precedes them in Leviticus, placed under God’s command at Sinai and given through Moses for the whole people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the verse as the book’s closing heading. The commandments are the LORD’s, commanded at the mountain, for Israel.

Both traditions take the line as a historical and literary close, on shared ground, without a contrast over its meaning.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
