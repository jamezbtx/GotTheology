Passage

Leviticus 27

Verses 1–15 · WEB

After the covenant sanctions of chapter 26, the LORD gives Moses laws for voluntary vows. A person may be consecrated to the LORD by a vow and then released according to a stated valuation in sanctuary shekels, scaled by age and sex, with the priest assigning a lesser amount if the vower is too poor to meet the standard. Vowed animals that belong on the altar become holy and may not be casually exchanged; an unclean animal may be redeemed by adding a fifth, and a dedicated house may be redeemed in the same way. Both traditions read the passage as constructive Sinai legislation: vows are free, but once made they are serious, and poverty is not ignored. They treat the scale as a commutation price for a vow, not as a ranking of persons’ worth before God, and they find shared ground throughout.

Leviticus 27:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses, opening a new unit of instruction after the close of chapter 26.

Arminian readers see God again taking the initiative to guide a redeemed people’s worship, now in the matter of vows.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note a fresh divine speech. The rules that follow will be the LORD’s word, not human custom left unregulated.

Both traditions read the verse as the heading of the chapter, still set at Sinai (verse 34).

Leviticus 27:2

2“Speak to the children of Israel, and say to them, ‘When a man consecrates a person to the LORD in a vow, according to your valuation,

Arminian

Moses is to tell Israel: when a man consecrates a person to the LORD in a vow, it is to be according to the stated valuation.

Arminian readers hear a voluntary act (“when” a person makes this vow) that must then be honored. Deuteronomy 23:21–23 likewise treats a vow as free to make and binding once made.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a voluntary consecration brought under the LORD’s valuation, not left to private appraisal.

Both traditions take the opening case as the commutation of a vowed person into a payment, and they do not read the verse as a command to vow.

Leviticus 27:3

3your valuation of a male from twenty years old to sixty years old shall be fifty shekels of silver, according to the shekel of the sanctuary.

Arminian

The valuation of a male from twenty to sixty years old is fifty shekels of silver, according to the shekel of the sanctuary.

Arminian readers see a public, fixed standard for the person in the prime of working life. The sanctuary shekel keeps the vow from being priced by private interest.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the sanctuary standard. The amount is set by God’s house, not by the vower’s estimate.

Both traditions treat the figure as a commutation price. They do not take the scale, here or in the next verses, as a statement of a person’s worth before God.

Leviticus 27:4

4If she is a female, then your valuation shall be thirty shekels.

Arminian

If the vowed person is a female, the valuation is thirty shekels.

Arminian readers note the different figure without turning it into a judgment on a woman’s dignity before God. In this law the sum is the redemption price of a vow.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise keep the verse within the commutation table. The text gives the amount; it does not explain the difference, and explanations that go beyond the text should be held lightly.

Both traditions refuse to read the scale as ranking souls, and they find no doctrinal contrast here.

Leviticus 27:5

5If the person is from five years old to twenty years old, then your valuation shall be for a male twenty shekels, and for a female ten shekels.

Arminian

From five to twenty years old, the valuation is twenty shekels for a male and ten for a female.

Arminian readers see the same ordered table extended to the young. The vow is still serious, and the amount is still a set price, not a bargaining chip.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the continued use of a public scale by age and sex. The sanctuary’s standard covers every usual case.

Both traditions again read the numbers as vow valuations, not as a theology of human value, and they do not divide over them.

Leviticus 27:6

6If the person is from a month old to five years old, then your valuation shall be for a male five shekels of silver, and for a female your valuation shall be three shekels of silver.

Arminian

From a month old to five years old, the valuation is five shekels of silver for a male and three for a female.

Arminian readers see even a young child brought within the vow law at a modest stated sum. The small amounts still have to be paid if the vow is to be commuted.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the table begins after the first month. The law is precise, and the sanctuary’s valuation still governs.

Both traditions keep the verse in the same descriptive register as verses 3–5.

Leviticus 27:7

7If the person is from sixty years old and upward; if he is a male, then your valuation shall be fifteen shekels, and for a female ten shekels.

Arminian

From sixty years old and upward, the valuation is fifteen shekels for a male and ten for a female.

Arminian readers see the scale lowered for the aged, still as a redemption price for a vow, still without a comment in the text on why the figures differ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the reduced amounts as part of the same public table. The law covers the full span of life it intends to cover.

Both traditions remain on shared, descriptive ground.

Leviticus 27:8

8But if he is poorer than your valuation, then he shall be set before the priest, and the priest shall assign a value to him. The priest shall assign a value according to his ability to pay.

Arminian

If the vower is poorer than the valuation, the person is set before the priest, who assigns a value according to the ability to pay.

Arminian readers hear a merciful provision: poverty does not make a vow impossible to resolve, and the priest must consider what the person can actually pay.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the relief is itself regulated. The priest, not the vower, assigns the reduced amount, and he does so by ability to pay.

Both traditions see here a care for the poor built into vow law, and they do not contrast over it.

Leviticus 27:9

9“ ‘If it is an animal of which men offer an offering to the LORD, all that any man gives of such to the LORD becomes holy.

Arminian

If the vow concerns an animal of the kind offered to the LORD, whatever is given of such to the LORD becomes holy.

Arminian readers see a vowed offering-animal pass into God’s ownership. Holiness here means it is set apart and may not be treated as common again.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that the gift “becomes holy.” The LORD’s claim attaches to the animal itself.

Both traditions read the verse as the start of the animal rules, still in the constructive register of the chapter.

Leviticus 27:10

10He shall not alter it, nor exchange it, a good for a bad, or a bad for a good. If he shall at all exchange animal for animal, then both it and that for which it is exchanged shall be holy.

Arminian

The animal may not be altered or exchanged, good for bad or bad for good. If it is exchanged at all, both the original and the substitute become holy.

Arminian readers see a guard against second thoughts: one may not swap a poorer beast for the one vowed, or dress up a gift after the fact. If an exchange is made, God claims both.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same double holiness as a deterrent to manipulation. The sanctuary does not lose, and the vower does not improve his position by switching animals.

Both traditions take the rule as protecting the integrity of what has been declared holy.

Leviticus 27:11

11If it is any unclean animal, of which they do not offer as an offering to the LORD, then he shall set the animal before the priest;

Arminian

If the animal is unclean and so is not offered as an offering to the LORD, it is to be set before the priest.

Arminian readers see a different path for an animal that cannot go on the altar. It is still brought under priestly review, not left to private use without a word.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that uncleanness does not end the vow’s seriousness. The priest must see the animal.

Both traditions read the verse as introducing the evaluation and possible redemption that follow.

Leviticus 27:12

12and the priest shall evaluate it, whether it is good or bad. As the priest evaluates it, so it shall be.

Arminian

The priest evaluates the unclean animal, whether good or bad, and his evaluation stands.

Arminian readers hear an appointed judgment in place of the vower’s own estimate. The priest’s word settles the worth.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the finality of the priestly evaluation. “As the priest evaluates it, so it shall be.”

Both traditions see authorized appraisal as a protection for the sanctuary and for fairness, and they do not divide here.

Leviticus 27:13

13But if he will indeed redeem it, then he shall add the fifth part of it to its valuation.

Arminian

If the owner will indeed redeem it, he must add a fifth part to its valuation.

Arminian readers see redemption left open, but not cheap: a fifth is added, so that taking the animal back costs more than the stated value.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the added fifth as a regular feature of this chapter’s redeeming rules. What is holy is not reclaimed at no cost.

Both traditions read the surcharge as part of the law’s seriousness, not as a point of contrast.

Leviticus 27:14

14“ ‘When a man dedicates his house to be holy to the LORD, then the priest shall evaluate it, whether it is good or bad. As the priest evaluates it, so it shall stand.

Arminian

When a man dedicates his house to be holy to the LORD, the priest evaluates it, whether good or bad, and that evaluation stands.

Arminian readers see a house, like a person or an animal, able to be dedicated and then priced by the priest. The dedication is voluntary; the valuation is not.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress again that the priest’s evaluation stands. A dedicated house is holy to the LORD and is not priced by its owner.

Both traditions take the verse as extending the same constructive pattern to property.

Leviticus 27:15

15If he who dedicates it will redeem his house, then he shall add the fifth part of the money of your valuation to it, and it shall be his.

Arminian

If the one who dedicated the house redeems it, he adds a fifth of the valuation money, and the house is his again.

Arminian readers hear the same path as with the unclean animal: redemption is allowed, with a fifth added, and then the house returns to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the identical surcharge. The dedicated thing may come back to the giver only at the price the law sets.

Both traditions close this unit on shared ground: vows of persons, animals, and houses are serious, priced, and, where redemption is allowed, costly to reverse.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
