Passage

Leviticus 27

Verses 16–25 · WEB

The vow laws now turn to land. A field from a family’s inherited possession is valued by the seed required to sow it, at fifty shekels of silver for a homer of barley, and the amount is adjusted toward the Year of Jubilee. The field may be redeemed by adding a fifth; if it is not redeemed, or if it has been sold to another, it is not redeemed afterward, and at the Jubilee it becomes holy to the LORD as a devoted field, owned by the priests. A field that was merely purchased is valued only up to the Jubilee and then returns to the original owner. All valuations use the sanctuary shekel, twenty gerahs to the shekel. Both traditions read the unit as constructive description, tying vows to the Jubilee already set out in chapter 25, and they find no doctrinal fork here.

Leviticus 27:16

16“ ‘If a man dedicates to the LORD part of the field of his possession, then your valuation shall be according to the seed for it. The sowing of a homer of barley shall be valued at fifty shekels of silver.

Arminian

If a man dedicates to the LORD part of the field of his possession, the valuation is according to the seed for it: the sowing of a homer of barley is valued at fifty shekels of silver.

Arminian readers see inherited land brought under a vow by a public measure, the seed it takes to sow it, not by a private guess at the field’s worth.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the fixed rate tied to seed and to the sanctuary’s silver. The LORD’s house, not the market mood of the moment, sets the figure.

Both traditions take the “field of his possession” as family inheritance, which the following verses will distinguish from a purchased field.

Leviticus 27:17

17If he dedicates his field from the Year of Jubilee, according to your valuation it shall stand.

Arminian

If he dedicates the field from the Year of Jubilee, the valuation stands as stated.

Arminian readers hear that a vow made at the Jubilee starting point pays the full standard amount. Timing matters because the land’s use is timed by God’s year of release.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the full valuation applies when the dedication is made from the Jubilee. The calendar of chapter 25 governs the vow.

Both traditions read the verse as the baseline for the adjustments that follow.

Leviticus 27:18

18But if he dedicates his field after the Jubilee, then the priest shall reckon to him the money according to the years that remain to the Year of Jubilee; and an abatement shall be made from your valuation.

Arminian

If he dedicates the field after the Jubilee, the priest reckons the money according to the years remaining until the next Jubilee, and the valuation is reduced.

Arminian readers see a fair reckoning: one does not pay as though for a full cycle when fewer years of use remain. The priest does the counting.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the priest’s reckoning and the abatement. The law ties the vow to the remaining years God has already marked out.

Both traditions take the reduction as honest accounting under the Jubilee, not as a loosening of the vow.

Leviticus 27:19

19If he who dedicated the field will indeed redeem it, then he shall add the fifth part of the money of your valuation to it, and it shall remain his.

Arminian

If the one who dedicated the field will indeed redeem it, he adds a fifth of the valuation money, and the field remains his.

Arminian readers hear again the allowed but costly redemption already seen with animals and houses. The inheritance can return to him, with a fifth added.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same added fifth. Remaining his is possible, but only on the LORD’s terms.

Both traditions read the verse as the ordinary path of redemption for a dedicated hereditary field.

Leviticus 27:20

20If he will not redeem the field, or if he has sold the field to another man, it shall not be redeemed any more;

Arminian

If he will not redeem the field, or if he has sold the field to another man, it shall not be redeemed any more.

Arminian readers see a point of no return: neglect to redeem, or a sale to someone else, closes the door later. The vow and the sale cannot be treated casually after the fact.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the finality of the two cases. The field’s future is then taken out of the original owner’s hand, as the next verse will say.

Both traditions take the bar on later redemption as part of the law’s seriousness, still without a contrast between the traditions.

Leviticus 27:21

21but the field, when it goes out in the Jubilee, shall be holy to the LORD, as a devoted field. It shall be owned by the priests.

Arminian

When the field goes out in the Jubilee, it shall be holy to the LORD, as a devoted field, and shall be owned by the priests.

Arminian readers hear the inheritance pass, at the Jubilee, into the LORD’s holy portion and into priestly ownership. What was not redeemed does not revert to the family in the usual way.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the special outcome: “holy to the LORD, as a devoted field,” owned by the priests. Jubilee here confirms a transfer to God, rather than a return to the clan.

Both traditions read the verse as an exception to the ordinary Jubilee return of chapter 25, required by the unresolved dedication, and they do not force a typological debate onto the word “devoted.”

Leviticus 27:22

22“ ‘If he dedicates a field to the LORD which he has bought, which is not of the field of his possession,

Arminian

A different case is opened: a field dedicated to the LORD which the man has bought, and which is not his inherited possession.

Arminian readers see the law distinguishing purchased land from the family field of verse 16. One may dedicate what one has bought, but the land’s deeper title is not the buyer’s to give away forever.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the careful distinction. The next verses will keep the Jubilee rights of the original possessor intact.

Both traditions take the distinction as a protection of inherited possession under God’s land laws.

Leviticus 27:23

23then the priest shall reckon to him the worth of your valuation up to the Year of Jubilee; and he shall give your valuation on that day, as a holy thing to the LORD.

Arminian

The priest reckons the valuation up to the Year of Jubilee, and the man is to give that valuation on that day as a holy thing to the LORD.

Arminian readers hear a payment due at once, covering only the years until Jubilee. The dedicated purchase-price becomes holy to the LORD that day.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the immediate giving of the reckoned sum “as a holy thing to the LORD.” The holiness attaches to the payment for the remaining years.

Both traditions read the verse as a time-limited dedication of bought land, consistent with the Jubilee calendar.

Leviticus 27:24

24In the Year of Jubilee the field shall return to him from whom it was bought, even to him to whom the possession of the land belongs.

Arminian

In the Year of Jubilee the field returns to the one from whom it was bought, the one to whom the possession of the land belongs.

Arminian readers see the original family right restored. A buyer’s vow cannot cancel the possession God assigned to another house.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same return. Jubilee reasserts the deeper ownership; the vow has been settled in money up to that year.

Both traditions find this in harmony with chapter 25 and see no disagreement here.

Leviticus 27:25

25All your valuations shall be according to the shekel of the sanctuary: twenty gerahs to the shekel.

Arminian

All valuations are to be according to the shekel of the sanctuary: twenty gerahs to the shekel.

Arminian readers hear a single standard applied to every case in the chapter. The sanctuary, not private weights, defines the shekel.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same public measure: twenty gerahs to the shekel. The LORD’s house fixes the unit of all these payments.

Both traditions take the verse as a closing rule for the valuations, shared and constructive.
