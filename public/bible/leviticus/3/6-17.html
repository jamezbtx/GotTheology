Passage

Leviticus 3

Verses 6–17 · WEB

The peace-offering law continues with an animal from the flock, a lamb or a goat, again male or female without defect. The offerer lays a hand on its head and kills it before the Tent of Meeting; the priests sprinkle the blood; and the fat, including the lamb’s entire tail fat, is burned as “the food of the offering made by fire.” The chapter closes with a perpetual statute for all generations and all dwellings: Israel shall eat neither fat nor blood, for all the fat is the LORD’s. Both traditions hear in this offering fellowship through substitution, fulfilled in Christ, and they read the statute as God’s claim on the choicest fat and the life-blood. They do not invent a contrast over the typology.

Leviticus 3:6

6“ ‘If his offering for a sacrifice of peace offerings to the LORD is from the flock, either male or female, he shall offer it without defect.

Arminian

If the peace offering is from the flock, it may be male or female, but it must be without defect.

Arminian readers again see the offering made available to those who bring sheep or goats, under the same standard of wholeness before the LORD.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the parallel with verse 1. Whether from the herd or the flock, the peace offering is “without defect.”

Both traditions find the same fellowship-sacrifice here, not a lesser rite, and they again see its fulfillment in the unblemished Christ.

Leviticus 3:7

7If he offers a lamb for his offering, then he shall offer it before the LORD;

Arminian

If the offerer brings a lamb, it is to be offered before the LORD.

Arminian readers see a simple, direct requirement: the lamb is presented in God’s presence, not handled as an ordinary meal first.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the phrase “before the LORD,” which has marked these chapters from the burnt offering onward.

Both traditions read the verse as locating the gift in God’s sight, and as introducing the lamb-specific details that follow.

Leviticus 3:8

8and he shall lay his hand on the head of his offering, and kill it before the Tent of Meeting. Aaron’s sons shall sprinkle its blood around on the altar.

Arminian

The offerer lays a hand on the lamb’s head and kills it before the Tent of Meeting; Aaron’s sons sprinkle its blood around on the altar.

Arminian readers hear the same identification and the same blood rite as with the herd. Peace is still costly and still personal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the pattern repeated so that the flock offering is unmistakably the same sacrifice of peace offerings.

Both traditions again share a substitutionary reading and refer it to Christ, through whom those who were far off are made near in his blood (Ephesians 2:13–18).

Leviticus 3:9

9He shall offer from the sacrifice of peace offerings an offering made by fire to the LORD; its fat, the entire tail fat, he shall take away close to the backbone; and the fat that covers the entrails, and all the fat that is on the entrails,

Arminian

From the lamb the offerer presents a fire offering to the LORD: its fat, including the entire tail fat taken away close to the backbone, and the fat that covers the entrails.

Arminian readers notice the added instruction for the tail fat of the sheep, a portion reserved wholly for God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the species-specific detail without changing the principle already given: the fat is the LORD’s portion from the peace offering.

Both traditions treat the backbone instruction as practical ritual law, not as a typological fork.

Leviticus 3:10

10and the two kidneys, and the fat that is on them, which is by the loins, and the cover on the liver, with the kidneys, he shall take away.

Arminian

As in verse 4, the kidneys, their fat, and the cover on the liver are taken away.

Arminian readers see the same inner portions set apart from the lamb as from the herd animal.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the repeated inventory. The LORD’s share does not shrink when the animal is from the flock.

Both traditions again leave the list as instruction, without invented symbolism.

Leviticus 3:11

11The priest shall burn it on the altar: it is the food of the offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

The priest burns these portions on the altar. The text calls them “the food of the offering made by fire to the LORD.”

Arminian readers hear table language: God is honored with the chosen portions as his food, while the rest of the peace offering will become a meal of fellowship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the “food” metaphor as the altar’s share given to God, not as a suggestion that the LORD lacks anything.

Both traditions read the language as covenantal fellowship, later completed in the people’s eating (set out in the following chapters), and fulfilled in the peace Christ makes. They do not disagree over the image.

Leviticus 3:12

12“ ‘If his offering is a goat, then he shall offer it before the LORD.

Arminian

If the offering is a goat, it is offered before the LORD.

Arminian readers see the third and last form of the peace offering opened with the same requirement of presentation in God’s presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the goat is not an afterthought; it too is offered “before the LORD.”

Both traditions read verses 12–16 as repeating the rite for this animal, so that every permitted creature is clearly included.

Leviticus 3:13

13He shall lay his hand on its head, and kill it before the Tent of Meeting; and the sons of Aaron shall sprinkle its blood around on the altar.

Arminian

The offerer lays a hand on the goat’s head and kills it before the Tent of Meeting; the sons of Aaron sprinkle its blood around on the altar.

Arminian readers hear, once more, identification and atoning blood as the way into peace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same substitutionary pattern applied to the goat.

Both traditions refuse to treat any of these animals as a different gospel; all of them point to the one offering of Christ (Hebrews 9–10).

Leviticus 3:14

14He shall offer from it as his offering, an offering made by fire to the LORD; the fat that covers the innards, and all the fat that is on the innards,

Arminian

The offerer presents as a fire offering the fat that covers the innards and all the fat on the innards.

Arminian readers see the goat’s richest inner fat reserved for the LORD, matching the herd offering of verse 3.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the consistent assignment of the covering fat to the altar.

Both traditions find repetition with a purpose: each form of the peace offering yields the same portion to God.

Leviticus 3:15

15and the two kidneys, and the fat that is on them, which is by the loins, and the cover on the liver, with the kidneys, he shall take away.

Arminian

The kidneys, the fat on them, and the cover on the liver are taken away, as before.

Arminian readers see careful obedience in a repeated instruction rather than empty redundancy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the repeated list as underscoring that the LORD’s portion is fixed.

Both traditions still decline to allegorize the organs.

Leviticus 3:16

16The priest shall burn them on the altar: it is the food of the offering made by fire, for a pleasant aroma; all the fat is the LORD’s.

Arminian

The priest burns these portions; they are the food of the offering made by fire, for a pleasant aroma. The verse then states the principle: all the fat is the LORD’s.

Arminian readers hear both acceptance (a pleasant aroma) and a claim: the choicest fat is not for common use.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the climactic sentence, “all the fat is the LORD’s.” The ritual details of the chapter rest on this ownership.

Both traditions connect the pleasant aroma with God’s acceptance and read the claim on the fat as a call to honor him with the best, without turning the verse into a contrast over typology.

Leviticus 3:17

17“ ‘It shall be a perpetual statute throughout your generations in all your dwellings, that you shall eat neither fat nor blood.’ ”

Arminian

A perpetual statute, for all generations and in all dwellings, forbids eating fat or blood.

Arminian readers hear a rule that follows the people home from the altar: even ordinary meals are marked by reverence for what God has claimed, the fat and the blood.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the scope: “perpetual,” “throughout your generations,” “in all your dwellings.” The prohibition is not limited to the sanctuary court.

Both traditions read the blood ban as guarding the life that belongs to God, a theme later chapters will develop, and the fat ban as protecting the LORD’s portion named in verse 16. They see no Arminian or Reformed fork here, and they take the closing quotes as ending the speech that opened in 1:2, completing this first unit of offering laws.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
