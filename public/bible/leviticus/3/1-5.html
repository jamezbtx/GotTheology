Passage

Leviticus 3

Verses 1–5 · WEB

Chapter 3 turns to the sacrifice of peace offerings. From the herd the offerer may bring a male or a female, still without defect, and lay a hand on its head at the door of the Tent of Meeting. The priests sprinkle the blood around the altar, and the fat portions are burned on the burnt offering already on the wood, as a pleasant aroma to the LORD. Unlike the burnt offering, this sacrifice is not wholly consumed; later instruction assigns the rest as a shared meal. Both traditions read the peace offering as fellowship with God through a substitutionary sacrifice, fulfilled in the peace Christ makes by his blood (Romans 5:1; Colossians 1:20; Ephesians 2:13–18). They do not invent a fork over that typology or over the ritual details.

Leviticus 3:1

1“ ‘If his offering is a sacrifice of peace offerings, if he offers it from the herd, whether male or female, he shall offer it without defect before the LORD.

Arminian

If the offering is a sacrifice of peace offerings from the herd, it may be male or female, but it must be without defect and offered before the LORD.

Arminian readers see a wide, personal invitation to fellowship: either sex of the herd may be brought, yet the gift must still be whole and unblemished.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that freedom as to male or female does not relax the requirement “without defect” or the setting “before the LORD.” Fellowship is still on holy terms.

Both traditions distinguish this offering from the burnt offering of chapter 1, which required a male, without turning that difference into a doctrinal contrast between the traditions.

Leviticus 3:2

2He shall lay his hand on the head of his offering, and kill it at the door of the Tent of Meeting. Aaron’s sons, the priests, shall sprinkle the blood around on the altar.

Arminian

The offerer lays a hand on the head of the offering and kills it at the door of the Tent of Meeting; the priests sprinkle the blood around on the altar.

Arminian readers see the same personal identification as in the burnt offering. Peace with God is not casual; it comes through a life given in the offerer’s place.

Reformed

Reformed readers again read the hand-laying and the blood as substitutionary, though interpreters debate how far hand-laying in the peace offering carries the same sense as in the burnt offering. The peace offering is a fellowship sacrifice, yet it still begins with blood at the altar.

Both traditions share that reading and see it fulfilled in Christ, who made peace through the blood of his cross (Colossians 1:20) and through whom we have peace with God (Romans 5:1).

Leviticus 3:3

3He shall offer of the sacrifice of peace offerings an offering made by fire to the LORD. The fat that covers the innards, and all the fat that is on the innards,

Arminian

From the peace offering the worshiper offers as a fire offering to the LORD the fat that covers the innards and all the fat on the innards.

Arminian readers see the richest inner portions reserved for God even in a sacrifice that will also feed the priests and the offerer.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the LORD’s portion is named first. Fellowship does not begin with the human meal; it begins with what is given to God on the altar.

Both traditions wait for the chapter’s later word that “all the fat is the LORD’s,” and they do not allegorize each fat deposit.

Leviticus 3:4

4and the two kidneys, and the fat that is on them, which is by the loins, and the cover on the liver, with the kidneys, he shall take away.

Arminian

The two kidneys, the fat on them by the loins, and the cover on the liver are taken away with the kidneys.

Arminian readers see a careful removal of the portions that belong on the altar. The offerer does not decide which inner parts are God’s.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a precise inventory of the LORD’s share. The rite is governed by the word, down to these details.

Both traditions treat the list as ritual instruction and decline to assign an independent meaning to kidneys or liver that the text does not state.

Leviticus 3:5

5Aaron’s sons shall burn it on the altar on the burnt offering, which is on the wood that is on the fire: it is an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian

Aaron’s sons burn the fat on the altar, on the burnt offering that is already on the wood on the fire. It is an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian readers notice that the peace offering is laid on a burnt offering: fellowship rests on the offering already given wholly to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note the placement “on the burnt offering.” The pleasant aroma is again the language of acceptance.

Both traditions read the joined offerings as harmonious rather than competing, and they see their fulfillment in Christ’s one offering, which is both a complete self-giving and the making of peace with God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
