Passage

Leviticus 13

Verses 47–59 · WEB

The final section applies tsara’at to garments of wool or linen and to leather. The WEB calls the condition “mildew” or “a destructive mildew” in some verses and “the plague of leprosy” in others, translating the same family of Hebrew terms. The priest isolates the item, re-examines it, and orders it washed, torn out, or burned depending on whether the mark spreads or fades. Verse 59 closes the chapter with a summary. Both traditions read this section as part of the same concern for purity in the camp, and they avoid speculating about the exact nature of the marks.

Leviticus 13:47

47“The garment also that the plague of leprosy is in, whether it is a woolen garment, or a linen garment;

Arminian

The law now applies to garments of wool or linen. Tsara’at is not limited to the human body.

Arminian readers note that the concern for purity extends to everyday objects.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the same Hebrew term is applied to garments, which shows that it is broader than a single disease.

Both traditions avoid speculating on the exact nature of the marks.

Leviticus 13:48

48whether it is in warp or woof; of linen or of wool; whether in leather, or in anything made of leather;

Arminian

The mark may be in the warp or woof, in linen or wool, or in leather. The WEB footnote explains that warp and woof are the vertical and horizontal threads of cloth.

Arminian readers note the thoroughness of the law.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the precision of the description, which covers wool and linen cloth, its individual threads, and leather goods alike.

Both traditions read this as practical guidance for the household.

Leviticus 13:49

49if the plague is greenish or reddish in the garment, or in the leather, or in the warp, or in the woof, or in anything made of leather; it is the plague of leprosy, and shall be shown to the priest.

Arminian

If the mark is greenish or reddish, it is shown to the priest. The colors are signs for examination.

Arminian readers note the care taken with household items.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest’s role extends to objects as well as people.

Both traditions avoid identifying the mark with a specific modern cause.

Leviticus 13:50

50The priest shall examine the plague, and isolate the plague seven days.

Arminian

The priest examines the mark and isolates the item seven days. The same patience applies to objects.

Arminian readers note the consistency of the procedure.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the waiting period applies here as with people.

Both traditions read this as careful, practical guidance.

Leviticus 13:51

51He shall examine the plague on the seventh day. If the plague has spread in the garment, either in the warp, or in the woof, or in the skin, whatever use the skin is used for, the plague is a destructive mildew. It is unclean.

Arminian

If the mark has spread on the seventh day, it is a destructive mildew, and the item is unclean.

Arminian readers note that spreading is again decisive.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the WEB uses “mildew” here, translating a term related to tsara’at.

Both traditions avoid speculating on the exact nature of the mark.

Leviticus 13:52

52He shall burn the garment, whether the warp or the woof, in wool or in linen, or anything of leather, in which the plague is, for it is a destructive mildew. It shall be burned in the fire.

Arminian

The item is burned, because the mark is destructive.

Arminian readers note the seriousness with which uncleanness is treated.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the destruction of the item protects the camp.

Both traditions read this as a practical measure for purity, not as a moral judgment.

Leviticus 13:53

53“If the priest examines it, and behold, the plague hasn’t spread in the garment, either in the warp, or in the woof, or in anything of skin;

Arminian

If the mark has not spread in the warp, the woof, or anything of skin, the process continues rather than ending in fire. Destruction is not required at the first examination.

Arminian readers note that not every case leads to destruction; the priest must wait for evidence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law provides options based on the evidence.

Both traditions see care and patience in the procedure.

Leviticus 13:54

54then the priest shall command that they wash the thing that the plague is in, and he shall isolate it seven more days.

Arminian

The item is washed and isolated another seven days. Washing plus a second wait gives the garment another chance before any tearing or burning is ordered.

Arminian readers note the opportunity for the item to be restored, matching the patient pattern already used for people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that washing is part of the purification process, as it is for people declared clean in verses 6 and 34.

Both traditions read this as practical guidance.

Leviticus 13:55

55Then the priest shall examine it, after the plague is washed; and behold, if the plague hasn’t changed its color, and the plague hasn’t spread, it is unclean; you shall burn it in the fire. It is a mildewed spot, whether the bareness is inside or outside.

Arminian

If the mark has not changed color after washing, even if it has not spread, the item is unclean and burned.

Arminian readers note that the law requires real change, not just containment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the mark’s persistence is enough to require burning.

Both traditions read this as practical guidance for purity.

Leviticus 13:56

56If the priest looks, and behold, the plague has faded after it is washed, then he shall tear it out of the garment, or out of the skin, or out of the warp, or out of the woof;

Arminian

If the mark has faded after washing, the affected part is torn out.

Arminian readers note that partial restoration is possible.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law seeks to save what can be saved.

Both traditions see wisdom in this measured approach.

Leviticus 13:57

57and if it appears again in the garment, either in the warp, or in the woof, or in anything of skin, it is spreading. You shall burn what the plague is in with fire.

Arminian

If the mark appears again, it is spreading, and the item is burned.

Arminian readers note that recurrence settles the case.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the consistency of the law’s judgments: spreading or recurring marks lead to the same outcome.

Both traditions read this as practical, not moral.

Leviticus 13:58

58The garment, either the warp, or the woof, or whatever thing of skin it is, which you shall wash, if the plague has departed from them, then it shall be washed the second time, and it will be clean.”

Arminian

If the mark has gone after washing, the item is washed again and is clean.

Arminian readers note the restoration of the item.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law ends with the possibility of cleanness.

Both traditions see God’s concern for restoration as well as separation.

Leviticus 13:59

59This is the law of the plague of mildew in a garment of wool or linen, either in the warp, or the woof, or in anything of skin, to pronounce it clean, or to pronounce it unclean.

Arminian

This is the law for marks in garments of wool or linen, warp or woof, or leather, to pronounce them clean or unclean. The chapter closes with a summary.

Arminian readers note the clarity and completeness of the instructions.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the purpose is to distinguish clean from unclean, a central task of the priesthood (compare Leviticus 10:10).

Both traditions read the chapter as an expression of God’s holiness and his care for the purity of his people’s camp.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
