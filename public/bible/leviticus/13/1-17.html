Passage

Leviticus 13

Verses 1–17 · WEB

Chapter 13 gives the priests procedures for examining skin conditions. The WEB renders the Hebrew term tsara’at as “leprosy,” but the word covers a range of surface conditions, and in verses 47–59 and 14:33–53 it is applied even to garments and houses. It should not be identified simply with modern Hansen’s disease. The priest acts not as a physician but as the one who declares ritual status: clean or unclean, with periods of isolation and re-examination when the case is unclear. Both traditions read these laws as protecting the holiness of the camp where God dwells, not as judging the sufferer’s moral worth. They also note that the Gospels show Jesus touching and cleansing people with this condition (Mark 1:40–45).

Leviticus 13:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses and to Aaron, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses and Aaron together. Aaron is included because the priests will carry out these examinations.

Arminian readers note that the priests are given responsibility to care for the community’s purity.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priests act under God’s direction, not their own judgment alone.

Both traditions read the chapter as practical guidance entrusted to the priesthood.

Leviticus 13:2

2“When a man shall have a swelling in his body’s skin, or a scab, or a bright spot, and it becomes in the skin of his body the plague of leprosy, then he shall be brought to Aaron the priest or to one of his sons, the priests.

Arminian

A swelling, scab, or bright spot that may become tsara’at must be shown to a priest. The WEB uses “leprosy,” but the Hebrew term covers a range of surface conditions.

Arminian readers note that the person is brought to the priest for a careful judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest’s role is to declare ritual status, not to provide medical treatment.

Both traditions avoid identifying the condition with any single modern disease.

Leviticus 13:3

3The priest shall examine the plague in the skin of the body. If the hair in the plague has turned white, and the appearance of the plague is deeper than the body’s skin, it is the plague of leprosy; so the priest shall examine him and pronounce him unclean.

Arminian

If the hair has turned white and the sore is deeper than the skin, the priest pronounces the person unclean. These are the visible signs the priest is told to look for.

Arminian readers note the care taken to base judgment on clear criteria.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest’s pronouncement determines the person’s standing in the camp.

Both traditions agree that the verdict concerns ritual status, not moral worth.

Leviticus 13:4

4If the bright spot is white in the skin of his body, and its appearance isn’t deeper than the skin, and its hair hasn’t turned white, then the priest shall isolate the infected person for seven days.

Arminian

If the spot is white but not deeper and the hair is unchanged, the priest isolates the person for seven days. Uncertain cases are not decided hastily.

Arminian readers note the fairness of waiting before reaching a verdict.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law builds patience into the process.

Both traditions see wisdom in not declaring someone unclean on uncertain evidence.

Leviticus 13:5

5The priest shall examine him on the seventh day. Behold, if in his eyes the plague is arrested and the plague hasn’t spread in the skin, then the priest shall isolate him for seven more days.

Arminian

On the seventh day the priest examines again; if the condition has not spread, another seven days of isolation follow. The process is careful and repeated.

Arminian readers note the patience shown to the person under examination.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest’s judgment is based on observation over time.

Both traditions read this as protecting both the individual and the community.

Leviticus 13:6

6The priest shall examine him again on the seventh day. Behold, if the plague has faded and the plague hasn’t spread in the skin, then the priest shall pronounce him clean. It is a scab. He shall wash his clothes, and be clean.

Arminian

If the spot has faded and not spread, the person is clean; it is only a scab. He washes his clothes and is clean.

Arminian readers note the relief of a favorable verdict and the simple washing that follows.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law aims as much at declaring people clean as at declaring them unclean.

Both traditions see a pastoral concern for restoring people to the community.

Leviticus 13:7

7But if the scab spreads on the skin after he has shown himself to the priest for his cleansing, he shall show himself to the priest again.

Arminian

If the scab spreads after the person was declared clean, he must return to the priest. A verdict can be revisited.

Arminian readers note that the process remains attentive to change.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law expects ongoing care rather than a single inspection.

Both traditions read this as practical, not as a sign of distrust.

Leviticus 13:8

8The priest shall examine him; and behold, if the scab has spread on the skin, then the priest shall pronounce him unclean. It is leprosy.

Arminian

If the scab has spread, the priest pronounces the person unclean; it is tsara’at. Spreading is the decisive sign.

Arminian readers note the seriousness of the verdict for the person involved.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest declares what the signs show, according to God’s instruction.

Both traditions read the verdict as ritual, not moral.

Leviticus 13:9

9“When the plague of leprosy is in a man, then he shall be brought to the priest;

Arminian

When someone has tsara’at, he is brought to the priest. A new case is introduced.

Arminian readers note that the community brings the person forward rather than ignoring him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest remains the appointed judge of ritual status.

Both traditions see the community’s responsibility for care and order.

Leviticus 13:10

10and the priest shall examine him. Behold, if there is a white swelling in the skin, and it has turned the hair white, and there is raw flesh in the swelling,

Arminian

The priest looks for a white swelling, white hair, and raw flesh in the swelling. These signs indicate an established condition.

Arminian readers note the specific criteria given to the priest.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law relies on visible signs, not guesswork.

Both traditions avoid trying to identify the condition medically.

Leviticus 13:11

11it is a chronic leprosy in the skin of his body, and the priest shall pronounce him unclean. He shall not isolate him, for he is already unclean.

Arminian

This is a chronic case; the priest pronounces the person unclean without isolation, because he is already unclean. No waiting period is needed.

Arminian readers note the hardship of a lasting condition.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the verdict is clear when the signs are clear.

Both traditions read this with compassion for those who live with long-term conditions.

Leviticus 13:12

12“If the leprosy breaks out all over the skin, and the leprosy covers all the skin of the infected person from his head even to his feet, as far as it appears to the priest,

Arminian

If the condition covers the whole body from head to foot, the priest examines it. A surprising case follows.

Arminian readers note the thoroughness of the examination.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law addresses even unusual cases.

Both traditions read this verse together with the next, where the verdict may surprise modern readers.

Leviticus 13:13

13then the priest shall examine him. Behold, if the leprosy has covered all his flesh, he shall pronounce him clean of the plague. It has all turned white: he is clean.

Arminian

If the whole body has turned white, the priest pronounces the person clean. The reason is not stated, and interpreters offer different explanations.

Arminian readers note that the verdict depends on the signs God specified, not on appearance alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the puzzle and avoid speculation.

Both traditions hold the meaning of this rule with caution.

Leviticus 13:14

14But whenever raw flesh appears in him, he shall be unclean.

Arminian

But whenever raw flesh appears, the person is unclean. Raw flesh changes the verdict.

Arminian readers note that the status can change as the condition changes.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law gives the priest clear criteria for each stage.

Both traditions read this as part of a careful, ongoing process.

Leviticus 13:15

15The priest shall examine the raw flesh, and pronounce him unclean: the raw flesh is unclean. It is leprosy.

Arminian

The priest examines the raw flesh and pronounces the person unclean. Raw flesh is a sign of tsara’at.

Arminian readers note the consistency of the law’s criteria.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest declares status according to God’s instruction.

Both traditions avoid reading moral judgment into the verdict.

Leviticus 13:16

16Or if the raw flesh turns again, and is changed to white, then he shall come to the priest.

Arminian

If the raw flesh turns white again, the person returns to the priest. Recovery is possible.

Arminian readers note the hope built into the law: a person can be restored.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law expects change and provides for re-examination.

Both traditions see God’s care for restoration as well as separation.

Leviticus 13:17

17The priest shall examine him. Behold, if the plague has turned white, then the priest shall pronounce him clean of the plague. He is clean.

Arminian

If the sore has turned white, the priest pronounces the person clean. The section ends with restoration.

Arminian readers note the joy of a person returning to the community.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest’s declaration of cleanness is as important as his declaration of uncleanness.

Both traditions see in this movement toward restoration a pattern fulfilled in Christ’s cleansing of the unclean (Mark 1:40–42).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
