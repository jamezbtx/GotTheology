Passage

Leviticus 13

Verses 18–28 · WEB

This section applies the same examination procedures to two special cases: a healed boil (verses 18–23) and a burn (verses 24–28). In each case the priest looks for signs such as white hair, depth beneath the skin, and spreading. If the spot is only a scar, the person is clean; if the signs are present or the spot spreads after a week of isolation, the person is unclean. Both traditions see the careful, patient process as protecting the community and the individual from hasty judgment. The passage remains about ritual status, not about medical diagnosis or moral guilt.

Leviticus 13:18

18“When the body has a boil on its skin, and it has healed,

Arminian

A case concerning a healed boil is introduced. Healing does not always end the matter.

Arminian readers note the attention given to the aftermath of illness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law covers specific situations carefully.

Both traditions read this as part of the priest’s practical guidance.

Leviticus 13:19

19and in the place of the boil there is a white swelling, or a bright spot, reddish-white, then it shall be shown to the priest.

Arminian

If a white swelling or reddish-white spot appears where the boil was, it is shown to the priest. The site of an old wound is watched.

Arminian readers note the care for each person’s condition.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the law’s precision in describing signs, so that the priest judges by what God specified.

Both traditions avoid speculating medically on the condition.

Leviticus 13:20

20The priest shall examine it. Behold, if its appearance is deeper than the skin, and its hair has turned white, then the priest shall pronounce him unclean. It is the plague of leprosy. It has broken out in the boil.

Arminian

If the spot is deeper than the skin and the hair is white, the priest pronounces the person unclean. It is tsara’at that has broken out in the boil.

Arminian readers note the clear criteria applied again.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the same signs apply here as in verse 3.

Both traditions read the verdict as concerning ritual status.

Leviticus 13:21

21But if the priest examines it, and behold, there are no white hairs in it, and it isn’t deeper than the skin, but is dim, then the priest shall isolate him seven days.

Arminian

If there are no white hairs, and the spot is not deeper but dim, the priest isolates the person for seven days. Uncertainty leads to patience.

Arminian readers note the fairness of waiting.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law consistently avoids hasty judgment, even when a verdict would be simpler.

Both traditions see wisdom in the waiting period.

Leviticus 13:22

22If it spreads in the skin, then the priest shall pronounce him unclean. It is a plague.

Arminian

If the spot spreads, the priest pronounces the person unclean. Spreading is again decisive.

Arminian readers note the consistency of the law.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest’s verdict follows the evidence.

Both traditions read this as ritual, not moral, uncleanness.

Leviticus 13:23

23But if the bright spot stays in its place, and hasn’t spread, it is the scar from the boil; and the priest shall pronounce him clean.

Arminian

If the spot stays in place and does not spread, it is the scar of the boil, and the person is clean. A scar is not uncleanness.

Arminian readers note the relief of a clean verdict.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law distinguishes carefully between signs of tsara’at and ordinary scarring.

Both traditions see care for the person in this distinction.

Leviticus 13:24

24“Or when the body has a burn from fire on its skin, and the raw flesh of the burn becomes a bright spot, reddish-white, or white,

Arminian

A case concerning a burn is introduced: raw flesh becomes a reddish-white or white spot. Another kind of wound is addressed.

Arminian readers note the law’s attention to everyday injuries.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the parallel with the boil case just above. A burn from fire can produce a reddish-white or white spot that must be examined, so ordinary injuries are neither ignored nor automatically treated as tsara’at.

Both traditions read this as practical guidance for the priest, protecting the person from hasty judgment.

Leviticus 13:25

25then the priest shall examine it; and behold, if the hair in the bright spot has turned white, and its appearance is deeper than the skin, it is leprosy. It has broken out in the burning, and the priest shall pronounce him unclean. It is the plague of leprosy.

Arminian

If the hair is white and the spot is deeper than the skin, the priest pronounces the person unclean; it is tsara’at in the burn.

Arminian readers note the same criteria applied consistently.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law’s signs are repeated for clarity.

Both traditions avoid speculating on the condition’s nature.

Leviticus 13:26

26But if the priest examines it, and behold, there is no white hair in the bright spot, and it isn’t deeper than the skin, but has faded, then the priest shall isolate him seven days.

Arminian

If there is no white hair, the spot is not deeper, and it has faded, the priest isolates the person seven days.

Arminian readers note the patience shown again.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that isolation is a temporary measure while the priest waits for clarity.

Both traditions see the waiting period as fair to the person involved.

Leviticus 13:27

27The priest shall examine him on the seventh day. If it has spread in the skin, then the priest shall pronounce him unclean. It is the plague of leprosy.

Arminian

On the seventh day, if the spot has spread, the person is unclean; it is tsara’at.

Arminian readers note that the verdict depends on what the examination shows.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the consistency between this case and the boil case.

Both traditions read the verdict as ritual status.

Leviticus 13:28

28If the bright spot stays in its place, and hasn’t spread in the skin, but is faded, it is the swelling from the burn, and the priest shall pronounce him clean, for it is the scar from the burn.

Arminian

If the spot stays in place and has faded, it is the scar of the burn, and the person is clean.

Arminian readers note the relief of a clean verdict.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law again distinguishes scars from tsara’at.

Both traditions see the careful process as an expression of care for each person.
