Passage

Leviticus 13

Verses 29–46 · WEB

The procedures continue with conditions on the scalp or beard (verses 29–37), dull white spots that are harmless (verses 38–39), and baldness, which is clean unless a reddish-white sore appears (verses 40–44). The section closes with the life of the person declared unclean: torn clothes, loose hair, a covered lip, the cry “Unclean! Unclean!,” and dwelling outside the camp (verses 45–46). These verses describe real hardship and isolation. Both traditions read them with compassion, and both see the Gospels’ accounts of Jesus cleansing such people, even touching one (Mark 1:40–45; Luke 17:11–19), as a striking reversal. Ritual separation here is not a verdict of moral guilt.

Leviticus 13:29

29“When a man or woman has a plague on the head or on the beard,

Arminian

A case concerning a sore on the head or beard of a man or woman is introduced. Both men and women are included.

Arminian readers note that the law applies equally to men and women.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the regulation names “a man or woman,” so the same care and the same standards apply to both.

Both traditions read this as practical guidance for the priest.

Leviticus 13:30

30then the priest shall examine the plague; and behold, if its appearance is deeper than the skin, and the hair in it is yellow and thin, then the priest shall pronounce him unclean. It is an itch. It is leprosy of the head or of the beard.

Arminian

If the sore is deeper than the skin and the hair is yellow and thin, the priest pronounces the person unclean. The WEB calls it an “itch.”

Arminian readers note the specific signs for this area.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the exact condition is uncertain, and translations differ on the term rendered “itch.”

Both traditions avoid medical speculation.

Leviticus 13:31

31If the priest examines the plague of itching, and behold, its appearance isn’t deeper than the skin, and there is no black hair in it, then the priest shall isolate the person infected with itching seven days.

Arminian

If the sore is not deeper and there is no black hair, the priest isolates the person seven days.

Arminian readers note the patience of the process.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the consistent pattern of waiting when signs are unclear.

Both traditions see fairness in the waiting period.

Leviticus 13:32

32On the seventh day the priest shall examine the plague; and behold, if the itch hasn’t spread, and there is no yellow hair in it, and the appearance of the itch isn’t deeper than the skin,

Arminian

On the seventh day, if the sore has not spread, has no yellow hair, and is not deeper, the process continues.

Arminian readers note the care taken in each step.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law requires multiple signs to be checked.

Both traditions read this as part of a thorough examination.

Leviticus 13:33

33then he shall be shaved, but he shall not shave the itch. Then the priest shall isolate the one who has the itch seven more days.

Arminian

The person is shaved, except for the sore itself, and isolated another seven days. Shaving allows clearer observation.

Arminian readers note the practical wisdom of the procedure.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law provides for careful observation.

Both traditions avoid adding medical speculation to the reason for shaving.

Leviticus 13:34

34On the seventh day, the priest shall examine the itch; and behold, if the itch hasn’t spread in the skin, and its appearance isn’t deeper than the skin, then the priest shall pronounce him clean. He shall wash his clothes and be clean.

Arminian

If the sore has not spread and is not deeper, the person is clean. He washes his clothes and is clean.

Arminian readers note the restoration that follows.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that cleanness is declared and then marked by washing.

Both traditions see God’s care for restoring people to the community.

Leviticus 13:35

35But if the itch spreads in the skin after his cleansing,

Arminian

If the sore spreads after the person was declared clean, the matter is reopened.

Arminian readers note that the process remains open to change.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law expects ongoing attention, because a declaration of cleanness responds to the signs present at the time.

Both traditions read this as practical care, not suspicion.

Leviticus 13:36

36then the priest shall examine him; and behold, if the itch has spread in the skin, the priest shall not look for the yellow hair; he is unclean.

Arminian

If it has spread, the priest does not need to look for yellow hair; the person is unclean.

Arminian readers note that spreading alone settles the case.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law simplifies the judgment when the sign is clear.

Both traditions read the verdict as ritual status.

Leviticus 13:37

37But if in his eyes the itch is arrested and black hair has grown in it, then the itch is healed. He is clean. The priest shall pronounce him clean.

Arminian

If the sore has stopped and black hair has grown, it is healed, and the person is clean.

Arminian readers note the joy of healing and restoration.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest declares what has happened, not what he has caused.

Both traditions see healing as God’s gift, recognized by the priest.

Leviticus 13:38

38“When a man or a woman has bright spots in the skin of the body, even white bright spots,

Arminian

A case of white bright spots on a man or woman is introduced.

Arminian readers note that the law addresses common conditions as well as serious ones.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that both men and women are included again.

Both traditions read this as practical guidance that leads to a clean verdict in the next verse.

Leviticus 13:39

39then the priest shall examine them. Behold, if the bright spots on the skin of their body are a dull white, it is a harmless rash. It has broken out in the skin. He is clean.

Arminian

If the spots are dull white, it is a harmless rash, and the person is clean.

Arminian readers note that not every skin mark is cause for concern.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law protects people from being wrongly declared unclean.

Both traditions see care for the individual in this distinction.

Leviticus 13:40

40“If a man’s hair has fallen from his head, he is bald. He is clean.

Arminian

A man whose hair has fallen out is bald, and he is clean.

Arminian readers note that ordinary hair loss carries no ritual impurity.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the law prevents unnecessary exclusion.

Both traditions read this as reassurance for those with ordinary conditions.

Leviticus 13:41

41If his hair has fallen off from the front part of his head, his forehead is bald. He is clean.

Arminian

Baldness at the front of the head is also clean, as ordinary rear baldness is in verse 40. Only a reddish-white sore on the bald spot would change the ruling (verse 42).

Arminian readers note the law’s care to avoid false judgments: everyday baldness is not treated as disease.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the precision with which ordinary cases are distinguished from tsara’at.

Both traditions read this as practical and compassionate.

Leviticus 13:42

42But if a reddish-white plague is in the bald head or the bald forehead, it is leprosy breaking out in his bald head or his bald forehead.

Arminian

But if a reddish-white sore appears in the bald area, it is tsara’at breaking out.

Arminian readers note that the law distinguishes baldness from tsara’at.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the signs, not the baldness itself, determine the verdict.

Both traditions read this as part of the careful process.

Leviticus 13:43

43Then the priest shall examine him. Behold, if the swelling of the plague is reddish-white in his bald head, or in his bald forehead, like the appearance of leprosy in the skin of the body,

Arminian

The priest examines the sore; if it is reddish-white like tsara’at elsewhere, the case proceeds.

Arminian readers note the consistency of the criteria.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the same signs are used throughout the chapter.

Both traditions avoid speculating on the condition medically.

Leviticus 13:44

44he is a leprous man. He is unclean. The priest shall surely pronounce him unclean. His plague is on his head.

Arminian

He is a man with tsara’at; he is unclean, and the priest must pronounce him unclean.

Arminian readers note the seriousness of this verdict for the person.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest declares what the signs require.

Both traditions read this as ritual status, not a moral verdict.

Leviticus 13:45

45“The leper in whom the plague is shall wear torn clothes, and the hair of his head shall hang loose. He shall cover his upper lip, and shall cry, ‘Unclean! Unclean!’

Arminian

The person must wear torn clothes, let his hair hang loose, cover his upper lip, and cry “Unclean! Unclean!” These are signs associated with mourning elsewhere (compare Leviticus 10:6; Ezekiel 24:17).

Arminian readers note the deep sorrow and isolation involved.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the visible signs protect others from contact with what is unclean.

Both traditions read this with compassion and see Christ’s willingness to touch such a person as a striking act of mercy (Mark 1:41).

Leviticus 13:46

46All the days in which the plague is in him he shall be unclean. He is unclean. He shall dwell alone. His dwelling shall be outside of the camp.

Arminian

As long as the condition remains, the person is unclean, lives alone, and dwells outside the camp. The separation is real and painful (compare Numbers 5:2–3).

Arminian readers note that the separation lasts only while the condition remains; restoration is possible (Leviticus 14).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that dwelling “outside the camp” is later echoed in Hebrews 13:12–13, where Jesus suffers outside the gate.

Both traditions read the separation as protecting holiness, not as a judgment of the person’s worth.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
