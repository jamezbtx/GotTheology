Passage

Leviticus 7

Verses 11–21 · WEB

Leviticus 7:11–21 gives the law of the peace offering in its three kinds: thanksgiving, vow, and freewill offering. These were the offerings the worshiper shared in eating. Thanksgiving meat must be eaten the same day, and vow and freewill meat by the second day, with leftovers burned on the third. Eating in a state of uncleanness brings the penalty of being ‘cut off’. Both traditions see fellowship with God as a gift to be received with gratitude and reverence, and many readers connect it with the Lord’s Supper (1 Corinthians 11:27–29).

Leviticus 7:11

11“ ‘This is the law of the sacrifice of peace offerings, which one shall offer to the LORD:

Arminian

The ‘law of the sacrifice of peace offerings’ introduces the fellowship meal, in which God, priest, and worshiper all share. Arminians see restored communion with God as the heart of worship.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the peace offering expresses fellowship already established by God’s covenant grace. Both traditions connect this shared meal with the communion believers enjoy through Christ.

Leviticus 7:12

12If he offers it for a thanksgiving, then he shall offer with the sacrifice of thanksgiving unleavened cakes mixed with oil, and unleavened wafers anointed with oil, and cakes mixed with oil.

Arminian

Thanksgiving offerings include unleavened cakes and wafers with oil. Arminians see gratitude as a fitting, freely offered response to God’s goodness (compare Psalm 50:14).

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that thanksgiving is the believer’s fitting response to grace already received. Both traditions connect this with Hebrews 13:15, the ‘sacrifice of praise’ offered through Christ.

Leviticus 7:13

13He shall offer his offering with the sacrifice of his peace offerings for thanksgiving with cakes of leavened bread.

Arminian

Leavened bread is included with the thanksgiving offering. Arminians note that it was eaten rather than burned on the altar, which is consistent with 2:11–12.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters make the same observation. Both traditions see the leavened bread as part of the shared meal and avoid forcing a symbolic meaning onto the leaven that the text does not state.

Leviticus 7:14

14Of it he shall offer one out of each offering for a heave offering to the LORD. It shall be the priest’s who sprinkles the blood of the peace offerings.

Arminian

One of each kind of bread goes to the priest who sprinkles the blood, as a heave, or contribution, offering. Arminians see the worshiper’s gratitude shared with God’s servants.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same provision for the officiating priest. Both traditions read ‘heave offering’ as a portion lifted or set apart for God and given to the priest, and they agree on the verse.

Leviticus 7:15

15The flesh of the sacrifice of his peace offerings for thanksgiving shall be eaten on the day of his offering. He shall not leave any of it until the morning.

Arminian

Thanksgiving meat must be eaten the same day, with nothing left until morning. Arminians see the joyful, immediate celebration of God’s goodness, likely shared widely with family and others.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the time limit keeps the meal holy and probably encouraged generous sharing. Both traditions read the verse as reverent celebration.

Leviticus 7:16

16“ ‘But if the sacrifice of his offering is a vow, or a free will offering, it shall be eaten on the day that he offers his sacrifice. On the next day what remains of it shall be eaten,

Arminian

Vow and freewill offerings may be eaten on the day of offering or the next day. Arminians note that freewill offerings reflect the worshiper’s voluntary devotion, warmly welcomed by God.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the freewill offering expresses a willing heart. Both traditions see the different time limits as part of God’s ordered worship and agree on the verse.

Leviticus 7:17

17but what remains of the meat of the sacrifice on the third day shall be burned with fire.

Arminian

What remains on the third day must be burned. Arminians see the limit as protecting the sacred meal from decay and casual handling.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the rule guards the offering’s holiness. Both traditions note that practical concerns and holiness concerns may overlap, without claiming more than the text says.

Leviticus 7:18

18If any of the meat of the sacrifice of his peace offerings is eaten on the third day, it will not be accepted, and it shall not be credited to him who offers it. It will be an abomination, and the soul who eats any of it will bear his iniquity.

Arminian

Meat eaten on the third day is not accepted or ‘credited’, is an ‘abomination’, and the eater bears iniquity. Arminians note that disobedience can turn a gift of worship into guilt, so worshipers are accountable for how they come.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the reckoning language (‘credited’) and stress that worship must follow God’s word to be acceptable. Both traditions agree that the verse concerns acceptance of the offering, and neither reads it as a direct statement about justification.

Leviticus 7:19

19“ ‘The meat that touches any unclean thing shall not be eaten. It shall be burned with fire. As for the meat, everyone who is clean may eat it;

Arminian

Meat that touches anything unclean is burned, but anyone clean may eat. Arminians see God welcoming his people to the meal while requiring purity.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree on both points: a broad invitation to the clean and a guard against defilement. Both see an echo in the call to approach God’s table reverently.

Leviticus 7:20

20but the soul who eats of the meat of the sacrifice of peace offerings that belongs to the LORD, having his uncleanness on him, that soul shall be cut off from his people.

Arminian

Eating the LORD’s peace offering while unclean brings being ‘cut off from his people’. Arminians take the warning seriously as real accountability for covenant members who treat holy things with contempt, and many relate it to 1 Corinthians 11:27–29.

Reformed

Reformed readers also take the warning seriously and connect it to 1 Corinthians 11:27–29. Both traditions take the penalty seriously, though interpreters differ on whether ‘cut off’ meant exclusion from the community, premature death, or direct divine judgment.

Leviticus 7:21

21When anyone touches any unclean thing, the uncleanness of man, or an unclean animal, or any unclean abomination, and eats some of the meat of the sacrifice of peace offerings which belong to the LORD, that soul shall be cut off from his people.’ ”

Arminian

Touching uncleanness, whether human, animal, or any abomination, and then eating brings the same penalty. Arminians note that the law teaches personal responsibility to keep oneself fit for worship.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and stress that uncleanness here is ritual, not necessarily moral. Both see the verse fulfilled in Christ’s cleansing of his people (Hebrews 9:13–14).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
