Passage

Leviticus 7

Verses 22–27 · WEB

Leviticus 7:22–27 is addressed to all Israel. It forbids eating the fat of bull, sheep, or goat and eating any blood, on pain of being ‘cut off’. The fat belonged to the LORD on the altar, and the blood carried the life (17:11). Arminian and Reformed readers agree on the meaning of the prohibitions and on their fulfillment in Christ. Christians have differed over how far the blood command still binds believers in light of Genesis 9:4 and Acts 15:20, 29.

Leviticus 7:22

22The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

A new divine speech, this time for all Israel, not only priests. Arminians note that holiness in eating is the whole community’s responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that these food laws apply to all God’s people in Israel. Both traditions read the speech formula as marking the following commands as God’s revealed will.

Leviticus 7:23

23“Speak to the children of Israel, saying, ‘You shall eat no fat, of bull, or sheep, or goat.

Arminian

No fat of bull, sheep, or goat may be eaten. Arminians note that the fat of sacrificial animals was the LORD’s portion on the altar (3:16–17), so eating it would take what belongs to God.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the prohibition reserves the choicest part for God. Most readers in both traditions limit it to the fat around the organs offered on the altar, not all fat in the meat.

Leviticus 7:24

24The fat of that which dies of itself, and the fat of that which is torn of animals, may be used for any other service, but you shall in no way eat of it.

Arminian

Fat from an animal that died or was torn may be used for other purposes but not eaten. Arminians see a practical allowance that still honors the principle that this fat is not food.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree on the distinction between use and eating. Both traditions see God’s law allowing ordinary use while guarding holiness, and they agree on the verse.

Leviticus 7:25

25For whoever eats the fat of the animal which men offer as an offering made by fire to the LORD, even the soul who eats it shall be cut off from his people.

Arminian

Whoever eats the fat of an offering made by fire is cut off. Arminians note that the severity shows how seriously God treats taking what is his.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and connect the principle to honoring God with what belongs to him. Both traditions acknowledge the debate over what ‘cut off’ entails.

Leviticus 7:26

26You shall not eat any blood, whether it is of bird or of animal, in any of your dwellings.

Arminian

No blood of bird or animal may be eaten, ‘in any of your dwellings’. Arminians note that the command reaches into daily home life and rests on the principle that life belongs to God (17:11, 14; Genesis 9:4).

Reformed

Reformed readers agree on the life-is-God’s principle. Christians in both traditions have differed on whether Acts 15:20, 29 keeps a binding abstinence for believers or was a temporary measure for Jewish–Gentile fellowship, and that difference does not divide neatly along Arminian and Reformed lines.

Leviticus 7:27

27Whoever it is who eats any blood, that soul shall be cut off from his people.’ ”

Arminian

Whoever eats blood is cut off. Arminians see the gravity of the command as pointing to the sacredness of blood in atonement, fulfilled in Christ’s blood (Hebrews 9:22).

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and note the striking contrast with John 6:53–56, where Jesus speaks of drinking his blood. Both traditions read that saying in light of Christ’s sacrifice rather than as permission to eat literal blood.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
