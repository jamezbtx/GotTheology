Passage

Leviticus 7

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Leviticus 7:1–10 gives the priests’ law of the trespass offering (compare 5:14–6:7). It also sets out which portions belong to the officiating priest: the flesh of the sin and trespass offerings, the skin of the burnt offering, and the cooked meal offerings, while the uncooked meal offerings are shared among all the priests. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that these procedural verses show order in worship and God’s provision for his ministers, a theme the New Testament takes up in 1 Corinthians 9:13.

Leviticus 7:1

1“ ‘This is the law of the trespass offering: It is most holy.

Arminian

The ‘law of the trespass offering’ declares it ‘most holy’. Arminians note that the offering for wrongs done against God and neighbor carries the highest sanctity, showing how seriously God treats both.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree and connect the trespass offering with Isaiah 53:10, where the Servant’s life is made an asham. Both traditions read this as anticipating Christ.

Leviticus 7:2

2In the place where they kill the burnt offering, he shall kill the trespass offering; and its blood he shall sprinkle around on the altar.

Arminian

It is killed where the burnt offering is killed, and its blood is sprinkled around the altar. Arminians see blood as the God-given means of atonement (17:11), applied visibly at the altar.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that the blood marks a life given in place of the offender, a picture they connect with Christ’s substitution. Many Arminians share the substitutionary reading, so both traditions agree on the verse.

Leviticus 7:3

3He shall offer all of its fat: the fat tail, and the fat that covers the innards,

Arminian

All its fat is offered: the fat tail and the fat covering the innards. Arminians note that the best and richest parts go to God, a pattern of giving him the first and finest.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the fat, regarded as the choicest part, belongs to the LORD (3:16). Both traditions read this as honoring God with the best, and they agree on the verse.

Leviticus 7:4

4and he shall take away the two kidneys, and the fat that is on them, which is by the loins, and the cover on the liver, with the kidneys;

Arminian

The kidneys, their fat, and the covering of the liver are removed. Arminians accept that the specific parts follow the pattern of the peace offering (3:3–4) and avoid speculating beyond the text.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the parallel with 3:3–4 and the inner organs set apart for God. Both traditions see careful obedience to God’s instructions here and decline to build doctrine on anatomical detail.

Leviticus 7:5

5and the priest shall burn them on the altar for an offering made by fire to the LORD: it is a trespass offering.

Arminian

The priest burns them on the altar as an offering made by fire. Arminians note that the priest acts as a mediator between worshiper and God, a role fulfilled in Christ (1 Timothy 2:5).

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the priest’s mediating action foreshadows Christ’s priesthood. Both see the verse as completing the trespass offering’s altar procedure and agree on its meaning.

Leviticus 7:6

6Every male among the priests may eat of it. It shall be eaten in a holy place. It is most holy.

Arminian

Every male among the priests may eat it in a holy place, for ‘it is most holy’. Arminians see God’s generosity to his servants in letting them share in the offering.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree and note the same holy-place restriction as for the sin offering (6:26, 29). Both traditions read the verse as provision combined with reverence.

Leviticus 7:7

7“ ‘As is the sin offering, so is the trespass offering; there is one law for them. The priest who makes atonement with them shall have it.

Arminian

‘As is the sin offering, so is the trespass offering; there is one law for them.’ Arminians note how the two offerings are closely related, though they addressed different needs.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree on the close relation and note that the officiating priest receives the meat. Both traditions read the shared law as simplifying priestly practice and see both offerings fulfilled together in Christ.

Leviticus 7:8

8The priest who offers any man’s burnt offering shall have for himself the skin of the burnt offering which he has offered.

Arminian

The priest who offers a burnt offering keeps its skin. Arminians observe that even this offering, otherwise wholly burned, contributes something to the priest’s livelihood.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same provision. Some interpreters in both traditions recall Genesis 3:21, where God clothed Adam and Eve with skins, but most treat that as an association rather than the meaning of the verse.

Leviticus 7:9

9Every meal offering that is baked in the oven, and all that is prepared in the pan and on the griddle, shall be the priest’s who offers it.

Arminian

Cooked meal offerings, baked in oven, pan, or griddle, belong to the officiating priest. Arminians see God carefully providing for those who do the work.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters agree that the priest who serves receives the benefit, a principle echoed in 1 Corinthians 9:13. Both traditions agree on the meaning of the verse.

Leviticus 7:10

10Every meal offering, mixed with oil or dry, belongs to all the sons of Aaron, one as well as another.

Arminian

Uncooked meal offerings, with oil or dry, are shared equally among all Aaron’s sons. Arminians note the fairness built into God’s arrangements, so no priest is left without provision.

Reformed

Reformed readers also highlight the equal sharing (‘one as well as another’). Both traditions see God’s concern for justice even in the distribution of priestly portions.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
