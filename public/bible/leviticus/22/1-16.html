Passage

Leviticus 22

Verses 1–16 · WEB

God instructs Aaron and his sons to handle the holy offerings with care so they do not profane his name. Priests who are ritually unclean must wait and wash before eating holy food, and only the priest’s household may share it, with provision for a returning widowed or divorced daughter and restitution for unwitting misuse. Both traditions see privilege joined to responsibility before the LORD who sanctifies, and read these purity laws as concerning ritual status rather than moral worth.

Leviticus 22:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses, introducing further instruction for the priests. Arminian readers see God continuing to guide his covenant people through his word, giving them what they need to live near his presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s initiative in revealing how he is to be approached. Both traditions agree that the holiness laws that follow rest on God’s self-revelation, not on human religious invention.

Leviticus 22:2

2“Tell Aaron and his sons to separate themselves from the holy things of the children of Israel, which they make holy to me, and that they not profane my holy name. I am the LORD.

Arminian

Aaron and his sons must handle the holy things carefully so they do not profane God’s holy name. Arminian readers see priestly privilege joined to real responsibility before a holy God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s name and glory as the governing concern of priestly service. Both traditions agree that the closer one stands to holy things, the greater the care required.

Leviticus 22:3

3“Tell them, ‘If anyone of all your offspring throughout your generations approaches the holy things which the children of Israel make holy to the LORD, having his uncleanness on him, that soul shall be cut off from before me. I am the LORD.

Arminian

A priest who approaches the holy things while unclean is cut off from God’s presence. Arminian readers see the seriousness of drawing near carelessly, a warning that privilege does not remove accountability.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the holiness of God setting boundaries even for his appointed ministers. Both traditions read the penalty as underscoring that access to God is on his terms, not the priest’s convenience.

Leviticus 22:4

4“ ‘Whoever of the offspring of Aaron is a leper or has a discharge shall not eat of the holy things until he is clean. Whoever touches anything that is unclean by the dead, or a man who has a seminal emission,

Arminian

Priests with skin disease, discharge, or contact with uncleanness may not eat the holy food until clean. Arminian readers see ritual purity marking the difference between ordinary life and life near the sanctuary.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the purity laws teaching the distinction between holy and common. Both traditions agree these conditions concern ritual status rather than moral guilt, and that Leviticus 13–15 explains them more fully.

Leviticus 22:5

5or whoever touches any creeping thing by which he may be made unclean, or a man from whom he may become unclean, whatever uncleanness he has—

Arminian

Contact with creeping things or unclean people also renders a priest unclean. Arminian readers see how ordinary contact in daily life could affect a priest’s readiness to serve.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the thoroughness of the purity system in shaping Israel’s awareness of holiness. Both traditions agree the rules teach attentiveness to God’s presence rather than contempt for the people or creatures involved.

Leviticus 22:6

6the person that touches any such shall be unclean until the evening, and shall not eat of the holy things unless he bathes his body in water.

Arminian

The unclean priest remains so until evening and must bathe before eating holy things. Arminian readers see God providing a clear, accessible path back to purity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see cleansing appointed by God as the means of restoration. Both traditions agree that the uncleanness is temporary and remedied by obedience to the prescribed washing.

Leviticus 22:7

7When the sun is down, he shall be clean; and afterward he shall eat of the holy things, because it is his bread.

Arminian

After sunset the priest is clean and may eat, because it is his bread. Arminian readers see God’s generous provision for priests restored once the required steps are taken.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the holy food as God’s gift for those who serve at his altar. Both traditions note that the restriction is not punishment but a reminder that holy provision belongs to holy service.

Leviticus 22:8

8He shall not eat that which dies of itself or is torn by animals, defiling himself by it. I am the LORD.

Arminian

Priests must not eat animals that died naturally or were torn, defiling themselves. Arminian readers see a higher standard placed on those who stand closest to the sanctuary.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see priestly holiness expressed even in diet. Both traditions agree that the closing declaration ‘I am the LORD’ grounds the command in God’s character rather than in mere custom.

Leviticus 22:9

9“ ‘They shall therefore follow my commandment, lest they bear sin for it and die in it, if they profane it. I am the LORD who sanctifies them.

Arminian

The priests must keep God’s command lest they bear sin and die, for the LORD sanctifies them. Arminian readers see both God’s sanctifying work and the priests’ real responsibility to obey.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God as the one who sanctifies, with obedience as the fitting response. Both traditions agree the verse holds divine holiness-giving and human faithfulness together without setting them against each other.

Leviticus 22:10

10“ ‘No stranger shall eat of the holy thing: a foreigner living with the priests, or a hired servant, shall not eat of the holy thing.

Arminian

No outsider, foreign resident, or hired worker may eat the holy food. Arminian readers see the holy portions reserved for the priestly household as part of its consecrated life.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the boundaries of the priestly household defined by God. Both traditions agree the restriction concerns sacred food, not the worth of the people excluded.

Leviticus 22:11

11But if a priest buys a slave, purchased by his money, he shall eat of it; and those who are born in his house shall eat of his bread.

Arminian

A slave purchased by a priest or born in his house may eat the holy food. Arminian readers see household membership, rather than birth alone, determining access to the priest’s portion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see inclusion in the household extending its privileges. Readers in both traditions approach such regulations of ancient servitude with care and do not treat them as a model for today.

Leviticus 22:12

12If a priest’s daughter is married to an outsider, she shall not eat of the heave offering of the holy things.

Arminian

A priest’s daughter married to an outsider may not eat the holy contribution. Arminian readers see that marriage moves her into another household and changes her access to priestly food.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see household belonging determining participation in the holy portions. Both traditions agree this is a matter of sacred food regulation rather than a judgment on the daughter.

Leviticus 22:13

13But if a priest’s daughter is a widow, or divorced, and has no child, and has returned to her father’s house as in her youth, she may eat of her father’s bread; but no stranger shall eat any of it.

Arminian

A widowed or divorced daughter without children who returns home may eat again. Arminian readers see God’s provision for a vulnerable woman restored to her father’s care.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see compassion built into the law for women left without support. Both traditions agree that the law makes room for her return, while maintaining that no outsider may eat.

Leviticus 22:14

14“ ‘If a man eats something holy unwittingly, then he shall add the fifth part of its value to it, and shall give the holy thing to the priest.

Arminian

Someone who eats holy food unknowingly must repay it with an added fifth. Arminian readers see accountability for unintended wrongs, with a clear path to make restitution.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s holiness requiring restitution even for unwitting offenses. Both traditions agree the added fifth expresses the seriousness of misusing what belongs to God, as also in the guilt offering of Leviticus 5.

Leviticus 22:15

15The priests shall not profane the holy things of the children of Israel, which they offer to the LORD,

Arminian

The priests must not profane the holy gifts the people offer to the LORD. Arminian readers see the priests entrusted with guarding the people’s offerings as a sacred trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the priests responsible for maintaining the holiness of what is given to God. Both traditions agree that careless handling dishonors both the givers and the God who receives the gifts.

Leviticus 22:16

16and so cause them to bear the iniquity that brings guilt when they eat their holy things; for I am the LORD who sanctifies them.’ ”

Arminian

Mishandling the holy things would make the people bear guilt, for the LORD sanctifies them. Arminian readers see how leaders’ negligence can harm those they serve.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the communal effects of priestly failure under a holy God. Both traditions agree the repeated refrain that the LORD sanctifies grounds the whole section in his action and character.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
