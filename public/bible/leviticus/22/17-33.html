Passage

Leviticus 22

Verses 17–33 · WEB

Offerings brought by Israelites or foreigners must be without defect to be accepted, with gradations for freewill gifts and humane rules for newborn animals and their mothers. The chapter closes by grounding holiness in the LORD who sanctifies Israel and brought them out of Egypt. Both traditions share the conviction that acceptable worship follows God’s word, and many in both connect the unblemished offering with Christ.

Leviticus 22:17

17The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses again, turning to acceptable sacrifices. Arminian readers see God instructing the whole community on how to bring offerings that please him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God defining acceptable worship by his own word. Both traditions agree the instructions that follow concern what God himself is willing to receive.

Leviticus 22:18

18“Speak to Aaron, and to his sons, and to all the children of Israel, and say to them, ‘Whoever is of the house of Israel, or of the foreigners in Israel, who offers his offering, whether it is any of their vows or any of their free will offerings, which they offer to the LORD for a burnt offering:

Arminian

The law applies to Israelites and foreigners alike who bring vows or freewill burnt offerings. Arminian readers see openness to foreigners who worship the LORD, sharing the same standard as Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the inclusion of foreigners within Israel’s worship under one law. Both traditions agree that a single standard governs all offerings, regardless of the worshiper’s origin.

Leviticus 22:19

19that you may be accepted, you shall offer a male without defect, of the bulls, of the sheep, or of the goats.

Arminian

To be accepted, the offering must be a male without defect from the herd or flock. Arminian readers see the call to bring God the best, reflecting sincere devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see acceptance tied to the offering’s fitness as God prescribes. Both traditions often connect unblemished sacrifices with Christ, described in 1 Peter 1:19 as a lamb without blemish or spot.

Leviticus 22:20

20But you shall not offer whatever has a defect, for it shall not be acceptable for you.

Arminian

Anything with a defect will not be accepted. Arminian readers see that giving God what is damaged reveals a heart that does not value him rightly.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God refusing offerings that fall short of his standard. Both traditions note that Malachi 1 later rebukes Israel for offering blemished animals, applying this principle.

Leviticus 22:21

21Whoever offers a sacrifice of peace offerings to the LORD to accomplish a vow, or for a free will offering of the herd or of the flock, it shall be perfect to be accepted. It shall have no defect.

Arminian

A peace offering for a vow or freewill gift must be perfect and without defect. Arminian readers see voluntary worship held to the same high standard as required offerings.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see that even freely given offerings must conform to God’s word. Both traditions agree that generosity does not replace the need to offer what God accepts.

Leviticus 22:22

22You shall not offer what is blind, is injured, is maimed, has a wart, is festering, or has a running sore to the LORD, nor make an offering by fire of them on the altar to the LORD.

Arminian

Blind, injured, maimed, or diseased animals may not be offered on the altar. Arminian readers see concrete examples that prevent worshipers from disposing of unwanted animals as offerings.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God guarding the integrity of the altar. Both traditions agree that the list makes the standard practical and unmistakable for ordinary Israelites.

Leviticus 22:23

23Either a bull or a lamb that has any deformity or lacking in his parts, that you may offer for a free will offering; but for a vow it shall not be accepted.

Arminian

An animal with a deformity may be a freewill offering but not a vow offering. Arminian readers see a measured distinction that recognizes different kinds of gifts.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see careful gradations within God’s law. Both traditions agree that the stricter standard for vows reflects the seriousness of promises made to God.

Leviticus 22:24

24You must not offer to the LORD that which has its testicles bruised, crushed, broken, or cut. You must not do this in your land.

Arminian

Animals with damaged reproductive organs may not be offered to the LORD. Arminian readers see this as part of the broader requirement for wholeness in what is presented at the altar.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the emphasis on completeness in sacrificial animals. Both traditions agree the verse addresses the fitness of offerings and not the worth of any person.

Leviticus 22:25

25You must not offer any of these as the bread of your God from the hand of a foreigner, because their corruption is in them. There is a defect in them. They shall not be accepted for you.’ ”

Arminian

Defective animals from a foreigner’s hand also may not be offered. Arminian readers see that the standard for acceptability applies regardless of the source of the animal.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see no exception for offerings brought from outside Israel. Both traditions note that the rule concerns the animals’ defects, consistent with the inclusion of foreign worshipers in verse 18.

Leviticus 22:26

26The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses with further regulations about offerings. Arminian readers see God’s attention extending even to the timing and treatment of sacrificial animals.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the thoroughness of God’s instruction for worship. Both traditions agree the coming rules express care within the sacrificial system. Nothing in worship is too small for his concern.

Leviticus 22:27

27“When a bull, a sheep, or a goat is born, it shall remain seven days with its mother. From the eighth day on it shall be accepted for the offering of an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

A newborn animal must stay seven days with its mother before it can be offered. Arminian readers see humane concern woven into the law, respecting the bond between mother and young.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s care for creation reflected in sacrificial regulations. Both traditions agree that the eighth-day provision shows consideration rather than arbitrary rule.

Leviticus 22:28

28Whether it is a cow or ewe, you shall not kill it and its young both in one day.

Arminian

A mother and her young may not be slaughtered on the same day. Arminian readers see compassion toward animals as part of Israel’s holy calling.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a law that restrains cruelty. Both traditions often compare it with similar concern in Deuteronomy 22:6–7 regarding a mother bird and her young.

Leviticus 22:29

29“When you sacrifice a sacrifice of thanksgiving to the LORD, you shall sacrifice it so that you may be accepted.

Arminian

A thanksgiving sacrifice must be offered so that it is accepted. Arminian readers see gratitude expressed in worship that follows God’s instructions, not personal preference.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see thanksgiving shaped by God’s prescribed manner. Both traditions agree that grateful worship seeks God’s acceptance rather than merely the worshiper’s satisfaction.

Leviticus 22:30

30It shall be eaten on the same day; you shall leave none of it until the morning. I am the LORD.

Arminian

The thanksgiving offering must be eaten the same day, with nothing left until morning. Arminian readers see a practical rule that keeps the offering fresh and its celebration focused.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God regulating the meal that accompanies thanksgiving. Both traditions agree the closing I am the LORD roots the timing rule in God’s authority.

Leviticus 22:31

31“Therefore you shall keep my commandments, and do them. I am the LORD.

Arminian

Israel must keep and do God’s commandments, for he is the LORD. Arminian readers see a summons to wholehearted obedience as the response of a redeemed people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see obedience flowing from God’s covenant identity. Both traditions agree that the command summarizes the chapter: holiness is lived out in faithful practice.

Leviticus 22:32

32You shall not profane my holy name, but I will be made holy among the children of Israel. I am the LORD who makes you holy,

Arminian

Israel must not profane God’s holy name, for he will be sanctified among them, and he makes them holy. Arminian readers see God’s sanctifying work calling for Israel’s responsive faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God as the source of Israel’s holiness and the goal of his glory. Both traditions agree that holiness is first God’s gift and then Israel’s calling, and they do not divide over this verse.

Leviticus 22:33

33who brought you out of the land of Egypt, to be your God. I am the LORD.”

Arminian

The LORD who brought Israel out of Egypt is their God. Arminian readers see redemption as the foundation of the call to holiness, with obedience responding to grace already shown.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the exodus grounding God’s claim on his people. Both traditions agree that the law is given to a people already delivered, not as a means of earning deliverance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
