Passage

Philemon 1

Verses 22–25 · WEB

Paul closes with a practical request for hospitality, hope for release through the church’s prayers, and greetings from his companions. These ordinary details place reconciliation within the wider fellowship of believers. His final blessing returns the whole community to the grace of Jesus Christ on which their shared life depends.

Philemon 1:22

22Also, prepare a guest room for me, for I hope that through your prayers I will be restored to you.

Arminian

Paul asks for a room and hopes that their prayers will contribute to his return. Arminian readers see prayer as meaningful participation in God’s care, not a ceremonial addition to a fixed human plan. His hope remains humble rather than a promise of certain release.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God appointing prayer as a real means within providence. Trust in divine sovereignty encourages rather than cancels petition. Preparing hospitality expresses practical hope, while Paul’s wording leaves the outcome in God’s hands.

Philemon 1:23

23Epaphras, my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus, greets you,

Arminian

Epaphras’s greeting reminds the recipients that Paul’s appeal comes from within a wider fellowship. Arminian readers see shared suffering and solidarity in Christ. The brief notice does not explain the circumstances of his imprisonment, so further details should not be supplied.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see costly partnership in Christ extending beyond the main figures in the letter. A fellow prisoner’s greeting binds the household church to believers elsewhere. Christian reconciliation belongs within this shared life rather than an isolated private spirituality.

Philemon 1:24

24as do Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke, my fellow workers.

Arminian

The greetings from four fellow workers widen the circle of Christian concern. Arminian readers see ordinary relationships supporting gospel ministry across distance. The verse names their present partnership without offering an account of each person’s whole spiritual journey.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church’s work carried through a community of coworkers rather than a solitary apostle. These names make fellowship personal and concrete. Nothing in this greeting requires speculation about later events in order to understand its encouragement.

Philemon 1:25

25The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Amen.

Arminian

Paul ends by asking that Christ’s grace be with the community’s spirit. Arminian readers see continuing dependence on grace as essential to the willing love requested throughout the letter. Reconciliation is sustained by Christ’s presence, not simply by a burst of good intentions.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see grace surrounding the entire appeal, from the opening greeting to the final blessing. The community needs Christ’s sustaining gift to live as brothers and sisters. Both traditions close with dependence on Jesus rather than confidence in unaided moral effort.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
