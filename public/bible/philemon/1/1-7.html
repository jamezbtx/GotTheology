Passage

Philemon 1

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Paul writes as a prisoner to Philemon and the church meeting in his house. Thanksgiving for Philemon’s faith and refreshing love prepares an appeal concerning Onesimus. The opening makes a personal matter part of the community’s shared life in Christ. Arminian and Reformed readers largely agree that received grace should become practical fellowship.

Philemon 1:1

1Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy our brother, to Philemon, our beloved fellow worker,

Arminian

Paul introduces himself through imprisonment for Christ and calls Philemon a beloved coworker. Arminian readers see a relationship of shared service rather than mere rank. The appeal that follows will ask love to shape conduct within this fellowship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that Paul begins with fellowship and costly service rather than an assertion of apostolic office. Philemon is addressed as a partner in Christ’s work. This relational opening prepares a request grounded in their common belonging to Jesus.

Philemon 1:2

2to the beloved Apphia, to Archippus our fellow soldier, and to the assembly in your house:

Arminian

The greeting extends beyond Philemon to named believers and the church in his house. Arminian readers see that personal reconciliation has communal significance. The letter does not identify Apphia’s and Archippus’s precise family relationships, so those should not be assumed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the household gathering as a community that shares responsibility for living out the gospel. The request to Philemon is personal but not isolated from the church. Naming fellow believers places the appeal within accountable Christian fellowship.

Philemon 1:3

3Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Grace and peace come from the Father and Jesus Christ before Paul asks anything of Philemon. Arminian readers see divine generosity as the source of loving action. Reconciliation is a response to grace, not a way to purchase God’s favor.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the blessing grounding the letter’s ethical appeal in God’s gift. Peace with God should bear fruit in peace among believers. Both traditions understand Christian obedience as flowing from grace rather than creating it.

Philemon 1:4

4I thank my God always, making mention of you in my prayers,

Arminian

Paul thanks God for Philemon and remembers him in prayer. Arminian readers see gratitude for grace at work in another person, alongside continuing dependence on God. Prayer accompanies the appeal rather than functioning as a substitute for practical engagement.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul gives God credit for the good he sees in Philemon. Thanksgiving and petition belong together because grace both begins and sustains Christian life. The coming request rests on prayerful fellowship, not mere pressure.

Philemon 1:5

5hearing of your love and of the faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints,

Arminian

Philemon’s faith in the Lord and love for the saints are already known to Paul. Arminian readers see living trust expressing itself in care for others. Paul appeals to an established pattern of love rather than inventing an entirely new obligation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see love for believers as the fruit of faith in Christ. Paul’s praise does not make love the ground of acceptance with God. It identifies the gospel’s visible effect, which the appeal concerning Onesimus will bring into sharper focus.

Philemon 1:6

6that the fellowship of your faith may become effective in the knowledge of every good thing which is in us in Christ Jesus.

Arminian

Paul prays that shared faith will become effective through fuller recognition of the good found in Christ. Arminian readers see fellowship maturing through active participation. Faith is not merely private conviction; it opens believers to the gifts and needs of others.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see fellowship in Christ producing practical fruit as believers recognize their shared blessings. The prayer anticipates Paul’s later appeal to receive Onesimus as a partner. Understanding the good given in Christ should deepen generous participation in his people’s life.

Philemon 1:7

7For we have much joy and comfort in your love, because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed through you, brother.

Arminian

Philemon’s love has brought real refreshment to the saints. Arminian readers see encouragement that grace-enabled choices can strengthen an entire community. The appeal will ask him to extend this same refreshing care to a person whose reception may be difficult.

Reformed

Reformed readers see existing love as evidence of God’s work in Philemon and a fitting basis for confidence. Paul will later ask that his own heart be refreshed. The thanksgiving gently connects past kindness with the reconciliation now being requested.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
