Passage

Philemon 1

Verses 8–21 · WEB

Paul appeals for Onesimus, whom he now calls his child and a beloved brother. Rather than simply issuing a command, he asks Philemon to welcome Onesimus as he would welcome Paul and offers to assume any debt. The letter challenges relationships shaped by slavery through their new kinship in Christ, without spelling out every legal consequence.

Philemon 1:8

8Therefore though I have all boldness in Christ to command you that which is appropriate,

Arminian

Paul acknowledges authority to command appropriate conduct, yet does not immediately use it. Arminian readers see pastoral leadership aiming for willing love rather than mere external compliance. Authority is real, but its exercise can make room for persuasion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see no denial of apostolic authority here. Paul chooses an appeal because the desired act should express Christian love. Both traditions distinguish the rightness of the duty from the pastoral wisdom of how it is urged.

Philemon 1:9

9yet for love’s sake I rather appeal to you, being such a one as Paul, the aged, but also a prisoner of Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Paul appeals on the basis of love, speaking as an aged prisoner. Arminian readers see costly personal concern drawing Philemon toward a willing response. His vulnerable circumstances make the appeal concrete without changing the moral seriousness of the request.

Reformed

Reformed readers see love guiding the use of authority. Paul’s age and imprisonment underscore the personal weight of his request, but he seeks more than reluctant compliance. Grace-shaped obedience should arise from a heart engaged by love.

Philemon 1:10

10I appeal to you for my child Onesimus, whom I have become the father of in my chains,

Arminian

Paul calls Onesimus his child because their relationship has been formed through the gospel during his imprisonment. Arminian readers see God’s grace reaching someone through a suffering witness. New spiritual kinship now changes the terms on which Philemon should receive him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see spiritual fatherhood as Paul’s ministerial role in Onesimus’s conversion, not an independent power to regenerate. God works through gospel witness even in confinement. Onesimus is now presented as a person bound to Paul by affection in Christ.

Philemon 1:11

11who once was useless to you, but now is useful to you and to me.

Arminian

Paul contrasts Onesimus’s former uselessness with his present usefulness to both men. Arminian readers see changed life opening possibilities for restored service. The contrast should not reduce his dignity to productivity; the coming appeal identifies him as a beloved brother.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gospel bearing practical fruit in a transformed relationship. Onesimus can now serve both Paul and Philemon in a new way. His value is not exhausted by usefulness, since Paul’s central request concerns welcome and brotherhood.

Philemon 1:12

12I am sending him back. Therefore receive him, that is, my own heart,

Arminian

Sending Onesimus back costs Paul something personal, for he describes him as his own heart. Arminian readers see reconciliation involving affection and vulnerability rather than administrative tidiness. Philemon is asked to receive someone whom Paul deeply loves.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the depth of Paul’s identification with Onesimus. The return is not presented as a detached disposal of a problem. Paul places his own affection behind the request and asks Philemon to recognize the relationship Christ has created.

Philemon 1:13

13whom I desired to keep with me, that on your behalf he might serve me in my chains for the Good News.

Arminian

Paul wanted Onesimus to serve him in imprisonment on Philemon’s behalf. Arminian readers see a genuine opportunity for cooperative ministry, but one that must not bypass Philemon’s participation. The good of gospel service does not remove the need for relational integrity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s imprisonment becoming an occasion for shared gospel labor. Yet a useful outcome alone does not settle how others should be involved. The next verse explains why Paul refuses to turn his desire into a unilateral decision.

Philemon 1:14

14But I was willing to do nothing without your consent, that your goodness would not be as of necessity, but of free will.

Arminian

Paul wants Philemon’s good action to be willing rather than compelled. Arminian readers value this picture of responsible, grace-enabled participation. The immediate issue is consent to a practical request, so the verse should not be made to settle every debate about saving grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Christian goodness should be voluntary, not forced by external pressure. God’s work in a renewed will is compatible with heartfelt consent. Paul is discussing the character of Philemon’s response, not offering a technical account of conversion.

Philemon 1:15

15For perhaps he was therefore separated from you for a while that you would have him forever,

Arminian

Paul cautiously suggests that a temporary separation may lead to an enduring relationship. Arminian readers see God bringing redemptive good out of painful circumstances without needing to cause wrongdoing. The word perhaps preserves humility about the hidden purposes of providence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a possible providential purpose in the separation: God can order difficult events toward lasting good. Paul’s perhaps restrains confident claims about details he does not explain. Divine purpose never turns possible wrongdoing into something morally approved.

Philemon 1:16

16no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother—especially to me, but how much rather to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord.

Arminian

Onesimus is to be received as more than a slave: a beloved brother in ordinary life as well as in the Lord. Arminian readers see grace challenging treatment based on status. The verse requires concrete recognition of kinship, not merely a spiritual label that leaves conduct untouched.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see union with Christ transforming obligations between people of unequal social standing. Brotherhood must matter in bodily and social life, not only in worship. Paul does not specify every legal step, but the demand for loving reception cannot be reduced to approval of slavery.

Philemon 1:17

17If then you count me a partner, receive him as you would receive me.

Arminian

Paul asks Philemon to welcome Onesimus as he would welcome Paul himself. Arminian readers see fellowship tested by the reception of another believer, not merely affirmed in words. Love crosses the boundaries that would otherwise make Onesimus unwelcome.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul identifying himself with Onesimus on the basis of partnership in Christ. Their common fellowship must become visible hospitality and acceptance. The appeal does not depend on Onesimus having the same social position as the apostle.

Philemon 1:18

18But if he has wronged you at all or owes you anything, put that to my account.

Arminian

Paul offers to assume any wrong or debt rather than leaving it as a barrier to welcome. Arminian readers see reconciliation making room for material responsibility. The conditional wording does not establish exactly what happened, so a detailed story of theft should not be assumed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Paul removing a possible financial obstacle to reconciliation. His offer bears another’s cost without pretending wrong does not matter. It may illustrate costly mediation, but the verse itself concerns this concrete relationship rather than explaining the whole doctrine of atonement.

Philemon 1:19

19I, Paul, write this with my own hand: I will repay it (not to mention to you that you owe to me even your own self besides).

Arminian

Paul personally pledges repayment while reminding Philemon of the deeper debt of their relationship. Arminian readers see moral persuasion joined to tangible commitment. The reminder invites gratitude to become generosity, not a calculation that cancels Onesimus’s dignity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul putting his own resources and credibility behind the appeal. Philemon’s indebtedness points to the life-changing good received through Paul’s ministry. The gospel’s benefits should loosen resentment, though they do not make financial concerns unreal.

Philemon 1:20

20Yes, brother, let me have joy from you in the Lord. Refresh my heart in the Lord.

Arminian

Paul asks Philemon to refresh his heart in the Lord, echoing the earlier praise for refreshing the saints. Arminian readers see the requested reconciliation as the next expression of an existing love. The good of others becomes a source of shared Christian joy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect this request with Paul’s thanksgiving in verse 7. Welcoming Onesimus would refresh Paul because their lives are joined in Christ. Spiritual fellowship becomes concrete in the way one believer treats another.

Philemon 1:21

21Having confidence in your obedience, I write to you, knowing that you will do even beyond what I say.

Arminian

Paul expresses confidence that Philemon will respond generously, even beyond the stated request. Arminian readers see encouragement toward freely embraced obedience rather than a mechanically guaranteed response. The unspecified extra should not be turned into certainty about a particular legal action.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pastoral confidence that established grace will bear further fruit in Philemon’s conduct. This confidence does not tell readers exactly what he later did. Both traditions recognize that the appeal seeks generous love beyond minimal compliance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
