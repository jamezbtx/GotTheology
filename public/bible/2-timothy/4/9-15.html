Passage

2 Timothy 4

Verses 9–15 · ESV · Voices

Paul turns to personal need: come soon. Demas, in love with this present world, has deserted him; Crescens and Titus are away; Luke alone remains. Mark, once a departure story himself, is now useful—bring him. Tychicus has been sent to Ephesus. Bring the cloak, the books, and above all the parchments. Alexander the coppersmith did great harm and opposed the message; the Lord will repay him, and Timothy must beware.

2 Timothy 4:9

9Do your best to come to me soon.

Arminian

Come soon: the apostle who just spoke of a laid-up crown still wants a friend’s presence. Ministry at the end is not a solo performance.

Arminian pastoral: haste to help a suffering brother is a real duty under love. Grace that poured Paul out as an offering did not make him beyond need. Timothy’s coming is a means of comfort, not a lack of faith in the Lord who stands by.

Reformed

Do your best to come soon: even the man who has finished the race asks for company. God ordinarily comforts His servants through other servants.

Reformed comfort: providence includes friendships on the road to death. The request does not weaken 4:8; it shows that the God who lays up a crown also uses a timely visit. Timothy’s obedience here is part of fulfilling his ministry (v. 5).

2 Timothy 4:10

10For Demas, in love with this present world, has deserted me and gone to Thessalonica. Crescens has gone to Galatia, Titus to Dalmatia.

Arminian

Demas, once a fellow laborer (Col 4:14; Phlm 24), has deserted Paul, in love with this present world. Wesley: once my fellowlabourer, hath forsaken me. Crescens and Titus are gone on work; Demas is named for a different going.

Arminian soteriology: this is heard as real apostasy, or at least a real falling away of a true coworker. Love of the present world can pull a man who once stood in the work. The warning is pastoral, not theoretical: cling; the world is still able to win a former companion.

Reformed

Calvin: it was truly base to prefer the love of this world to Christ. Yet we must not suppose he altogether denied Christ; he preferred private convenience or safety to Paul’s life. Desertion is still called desertion.

Reformed soteriology: a final love of this present world reveals a heart not kept in persevering grace. Those who go out in that way show they were not truly of the persevering company, however useful they once appeared. Calvin’s caution against assuming total denial of Christ still leaves the fork: if the desertion holds to the end, it was not the faith God preserves.

Key contrast The live fork is Demas’s desertion. Arminian readers, noting he was a fellow laborer, typically hear a real falling away: love of this world can pull a true coworker from the work. Reformed readers typically hear a professor whose love of the present world showed he did not finally belong to the persevering; if the desertion is final, he never truly persevered, however near he once stood.

2 Timothy 4:11

11Luke alone is with me. Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is very useful to me for ministry.

Arminian

Luke alone is with me: the beloved physician stayed. Get Mark—Wesley: though he once departed from the work, he is now again profitable. Restoration is on the page beside Demas’s desertion.

Arminian hope: a past failure in ministry is not a sealed fate. Mark’s usefulness returned; grace received again made him profitable. The same gospel that warns about Demas also welcomes a recovered coworker. Bring him: the church should make room for the restored.

Reformed

Luke remained; Mark is to be brought because he is useful for ministry. God kept one companion near and returned another who had once left the work (Acts 13:13; 15:37–39).

Reformed comfort: restoration of Mark is the Lord’s doing in a man He had not abandoned. Usefulness is gift. The contrast with Demas is not that Paul plays favorites; it is that God grants perseverance and recovery according to His purpose, and the church is told to employ the useful man.

2 Timothy 4:12

12Tychicus I have sent to Ephesus.

Arminian

Tychicus sent to Ephesus: even in confinement Paul is still deploying servants. The network of the gospel does not freeze because one apostle is near death.

Both camps can sit here without a soteriology fight. Sending is stewardship. Ephesus still needs a faithful man; Timothy may be called away to Paul, and Tychicus fills a gap.

Reformed

Tychicus I have sent: the verb is Paul’s, the mission is the Lord’s. Workers are moved, not stored.

Reformed instinct: God staffs His churches through apostolic sending. The same providence that left Luke alone with Paul (v. 11) also sent Tychicus where he was needed. Ministry is assignment under Christ’s headship.

2 Timothy 4:13

13When you come, bring the cloak that I left with Carpus at Troas, also the books, and above all the parchments.

Arminian

A cloak at Troas, books, and above all parchments: the man awaiting a crown still feels cold and still wants texts. Scripture-saturated ministry does not despise a coat or a page.

Arminian pastoral: the apostle’s humanity is not an embarrassment. Bring what he needs. Love of the parchments matches 3:15–17: the sacred writings remain the servant’s equipment until departure.

Reformed

Cloak, books, parchments: Calvin’s Paul, who just called Scripture sufficient, still wants the copies in hand. Means matter. A winter cloak is not unbelief.

Reformed simplicity: God keeps His servant through ordinary mercies—fabric, friendship, and written Word. Above all the parchments tells the church what to prize when travel is costly and time is short.

2 Timothy 4:14

14Alexander the coppersmith did me great harm; the Lord will repay him according to his deeds.

Arminian

Alexander the coppersmith did great harm. Wesley, on the Lord’s repayment: this he spoke prophetically. Paul does not take the sword; he leaves vengeance with the righteous Judge of v. 8.

Arminian pastoral: naming harm is not spite. The Lord’s repayment according to deeds is a real moral order. Deeds matter; grace does not make opposition to the gospel weightless. Timothy is about to be told to beware (v. 15), so the prophecy is also protection.

Reformed

Calvin: Alexander’s evils consisted in this, that he opposed Paul’s doctrine. The harm is theological before it is merely personal. The Lord will repay according to his deeds.

Reformed reserve: vengeance is the Lord’s. Paul commits the coppersmith to the Judge rather than to a private feud. God will deal with those who withstand the gospel; the church’s part is to tell the truth about the harm and to stay out of Alexander’s path.

2 Timothy 4:15

15Beware of him yourself, for he strongly opposed our message.

Arminian

Beware of him yourself: the opposition was to our message, not only to Paul’s nerves. Strong resistance to the gospel is a pastoral fact Timothy must not naively re-engage.

Arminian watch: grace toward enemies (v. 16 will show a generous spirit) does not require unguarded proximity to a man still fighting the word. Love of the flock includes this caution. The message remains the issue.

Reformed

Beware: the same man who opposed the doctrine is a present danger to Timothy. Our message is the apostolic gospel, not a personality clash.

Reformed watch: God shepherds through warnings. Strong opposition to the Word is enough reason to keep distance. Charity will appear in v. 16 toward deserters; prudence appears here toward an active opponent of the message.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Demas’s desertion, Mark restored to usefulness, and Alexander the coppersmith—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside 2 Timothy 4:9–15.

Arminian Notable voices

“Demas—Once my fellowlabourer, hath forsaken me. Take Mark—Who, though he once departed from the work, is now again profitable to me. The Lord will reward him—This he spoke prophetically.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on 2 Tim 4:10–14)

Reformed Notable voices

“It was truly base in such a man to prefer the love of this world to Christ. And yet we must not suppose that he altogether denied Christ; but he merely preferred his private convenience, or his safety, to the life of Paul. Alexander the coppersmith hath done me many evil things: they consisted in this, that he opposed his doctrine.”
John Calvin · Commentary on 2 Timothy (on 2 Tim 4:10, 14)
