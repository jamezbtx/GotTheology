Passage

2 Timothy 4

Verses 1–8 · ESV · Voices

Paul charges Timothy before God and Christ Jesus, the coming Judge: preach the word in season and out, with patience and teaching, because people will not endure sound doctrine. Timothy must stay sober-minded, endure suffering, do an evangelist’s work, and fulfill his ministry. Paul is already poured out; he has fought the good fight, finished the race, and kept the faith—and a crown of righteousness awaits him and all who have loved the Lord’s appearing.

2 Timothy 4:1

1I charge you in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus, who is to judge the living and the dead, and by his appearing and his kingdom:

Arminian

The charge is sworn before God and Christ Jesus, who will judge the living and the dead, and by His appearing and kingdom. Ministry is done under a coming court, not under a private brand.

Arminian gravity: the Judge sincerely summons all, and Timothy must preach as a man who will give account. Appearing and kingdom are not decorations on an ordination certificate; they are the horizon that makes unfaithful silence terrifying and faithful speech worthwhile.

Reformed

Calvin: after Scripture’s usefulness, Paul infers not only that all ought to read it, but that teachers ought to administer it. The charge is located in God’s presence and in Christ’s future judgment, appearing, and kingdom.

Reformed gravity: Timothy’s pulpit stands before the Judge who already reigns and will appear. The same Christ who will judge has appointed the means. The charge is heavy because God is not absent from the administration of His Word.

2 Timothy 4:2

2preach the word; be ready in season and out of season; reprove, rebuke, and exhort, with complete patience and teaching.

Arminian

Preach the word: not your mood, not the room’s appetite. Wesley: be instant—insist, urge, in season and out of season, continually, at all times and places; when no fair occasion is given, one must be made. Reprove, rebuke, exhort—with complete patience and teaching.

Arminian ministry: the Word is a means of grace to hearers who can still receive or refuse. Readiness is cooperation with the Spirit, not waiting for ideal conditions. Patience and teaching keep urgency from becoming a harsh substitute for the gospel.

Reformed

Preach the word; be ready in season and out of season. Calvin made administration of Scripture the teacher’s duty, not an optional extra once private reading is commended.

Reformed ministry: God has spoken; the minister must serve that speech. Reproof, rebuke, and exhortation are how the sufficient Word (3:16–17) is applied. Patience and teaching are not softness instead of truth; they are the manner of a steward who trusts the Spirit to use what God breathed.

2 Timothy 4:3

3For the time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching, but having itching ears they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions,

Arminian

The time is coming—already on the way—when people will not endure sound teaching. Itching ears collect teachers to suit passions. The market creates the pulpit it wants.

Arminian pastoral: passions can outvote doctrine in a hearer who still sits in church. Grace that can be received can also be refused in favor of a custom teacher. Timothy must preach the word anyway; itching ears are the reason for the charge, not the excuse to soften it.

Reformed

They will not endure sound teaching: the failure is endurance, not lack of supply. Itching ears assemble a faculty of teachers fitted to desire.

Reformed reading: this is last-days unbelief wearing a Christian syllabus. God still sends sound teaching; the itch is the heart’s rebellion. The accumulation of agreeable voices is itself a judgment, and the faithful preacher is not authorized to join the pile.

2 Timothy 4:4

4and will turn away from listening to the truth and wander off into myths.

Arminian

Turning from the truth is an act; wandering into myths is the path that follows. The ear that will not listen will still fill itself—with stories.

Arminian seriousness: people can leave the truth they once heard. Myths are not harmless color; they are what the soul prefers when it will not have Paul’s gospel. The preacher’s job is to keep holding out the truth, knowing some will turn and some will still hear.

Reformed

Turning away from listening to the truth and wandering into myths is a single motion of unbelief. The myths may sound religious; they are still a leaving of God’s speech.

Reformed accent: those who finally prefer myths show what they were. The warning still functions as a means: the elect are kept, in part, by staying under the truth Timothy is charged to preach. Myths are not a second equally valid Christian dialect.

2 Timothy 4:5

5But as for you, always be sober-minded, endure suffering, do the work of an evangelist, fulfill your ministry.

Arminian

But as for you: four charges against the itch. Be sober-minded, endure suffering, do an evangelist’s work, fulfill your ministry. The whole office is to be filled, not sampled.

Arminian energy: these are real acts under assisting grace. Sobriety can be abandoned, suffering dodged, evangelism neglected, ministry left half-done. Fulfill is a word for a man who could still leave the work unfinished; take the grace and finish.

Reformed

Sober-minded, endure, evangelize, fulfill: the contrast with itching ears is a whole life, not a better slogan. Suffering is expected in the job description.

Reformed charge: God assigned the ministry Timothy must fulfill. The command is how the Lord keeps His servant at the work. Evangelist’s labor is not a personality type; it is part of administering the Word so that the gospel actually reaches people.

2 Timothy 4:6

6For I am already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time of my departure has come.

Arminian

Paul is already being poured out as a drink offering; departure has come. The charge to Timothy rides on an apostle near death, not on a man protecting a brand.

Arminian pastoral: a life can be spent as worship. Pouring out is voluntary sacrifice under grace, matching the drink offerings of the old altar. The approaching departure makes Timothy’s remaining work more urgent, not less.

Reformed

Already poured out, departure at hand: Paul interprets his death as offering, not as accident. The time has come—under God’s appointment.

Reformed comfort: the apostle’s end is in the Lord’s hands. Being poured out is priestly language for a life God has used. Timothy inherits the work because God is not poured out; the gospel remains when the messenger departs.

2 Timothy 4:7

7I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.

Arminian

Fought, finished, kept: three perfects from a man who did not treat the race as automatic. Wesley’s later line on the crown (v. 8) already leans this way—righteousness imputed and wrought, belonging to those who have actually loved the appearing.

Arminian soteriology: keeping the faith is a grace-enabled perseverance that must be done. Paul can say he has kept it at the end; Demas, soon named, did not. The fight is good, the race has a finish line, and faith is a trust that can be abandoned if it is not held.

Reformed

Calvin: I have fought the good fight—Paul boasts that the contest is excellent, whatever the world thinks of it. Finished and kept are a dying apostle’s accounting, not a self-made résumé waved at heaven.

Reformed soteriology: keeping the faith is evidence of God’s keeping. The same grace that justified Paul carried him through the fight and the race. He speaks of his own endurance because God granted it; the boast is in the excellence of the fight under Christ, not in a bare human hold that could equally have failed at the last.

Key contrast Both hear a real fight, race, and keeping. Arminian readers, with Wesley’s accent on a righteousness imputed and wrought, take “I have kept the faith” as grace-enabled perseverance a believer must actually complete—and might not. Reformed readers take Paul’s keeping as the fruit of the God who kept him; the elect finish because they are preserved, and the boast is of grace, not of a finally uncertain hold.

2 Timothy 4:8

8Henceforth there is laid up for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will award to me on that day, and not only to me but also to all who have loved his appearing.

Arminian

A crown of righteousness is laid up, to be awarded by the righteous Judge on that day—not only to Paul but to all who have loved His appearing. Wesley: the crown of that righteousness which God has imputed to me and wrought in me; He will render it to all who have loved His appearing, which only a real Christian can do.

Arminian hope: justification’s imputed righteousness and sanctification’s wrought righteousness meet in the crown. Loving the appearing is the mark of a real Christian, not a spectator. The Judge’s award takes a lived love seriously; it is not wages earned apart from grace, and it is not a crown for a faith that never became love.

Reformed

Calvin: justification by free grace through faith is not at variance with the rewarding of works. Once God has received us into favor, He accepts our works so as even to deign to give them a reward, though it is not due to them.

Reformed hope: the crown is laid up because the Judge is righteous and gracious. He crowns His own gifts. Works are rewarded as accepted fruits, never as the ground of the verdict. All who have loved His appearing are those God has made lovers of Christ; the award on that day confirms free grace, it does not reopen it as a wage.

Key contrast The fork is how the crown relates to grace and works. Wesley heard righteousness both imputed and wrought, awarded to those who have loved the appearing. Calvin heard free-grace justification not at variance with God rewarding works—works He accepts and deigns to crown though nothing is due. Arminians stress a real, grace-shaped life the Judge honors; Reformed readers stress that the Judge crowns His own gifts, not a merit that sits beside the gospel.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on preaching the word, fighting the good fight, and the crown of righteousness—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside 2 Timothy 4:1–8.

Arminian Notable voices

“Be instant—Insist on, urge these things in season, out of season—That is, continually, at all times and places. Not only when a fair occasion is given: even when there is none, one must be made. The crown of that righteousness—Which God has imputed to me and wrought in me. Will render to all that have loved his appearing—Which only a real Christian can do.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on 2 Tim 4:2, 8)

Reformed Notable voices

“Paul, after having spoken of the usefulness of Scripture, infers not only that all ought to read it, but that teachers ought to administer it. I have fought the good fight: he therefore boasts that it is excellent, whatever may be the light in which it is regarded by the world. Justification by free grace, which is bestowed on us through faith, is not at variance with the rewarding of works; for as soon as God has received us into favor, he likewise accepts our works, so as even to deign to give them a reward, though it is not due to them.”
John Calvin · Commentary on 2 Timothy (on 2 Tim 4:1, 7–8)
