Passage

Exodus 12

Verses 15–28 · WEB

The instructions widen from one night to a seven-day Feast of Unleavened Bread, then return to Moses’ commands to the elders about the lamb, hyssop, and blood. The ritual is designed to be repeated and explained to children. Both traditions see remembered redemption shaping worship, obedience, and teaching across generations.

Exodus 12:15

15“ ‘Seven days you shall eat unleavened bread; even the first day you shall put away yeast out of your houses, for whoever eats leavened bread from the first day until the seventh day, that soul shall be cut off from Israel.

Arminian

For seven days Israel must eat unleavened bread and remove yeast; anyone eating leaven will be cut off. Arminian readers see the seriousness of covenant obedience and the danger of disregarding God’s ordinances. Paul’s use of leaven imagery in 1 Corinthians 5:6–8 is shared Christian reading.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a call to holiness expressed in a concrete practice. Being cut off is a grave covenant penalty, whose exact form interpreters discuss. Both traditions read Paul’s application of unleavened bread to sincerity and truth as shared ground.

Exodus 12:16

16In the first day there shall be to you a holy convocation, and in the seventh day a holy convocation; no kind of work shall be done in them, except that which every man must eat, only that may be done by you.

Arminian

The first and seventh days are holy convocations with no work except preparing food. Arminian readers see rest and worship framing the week. The exception for food shows that the ordinance serves the people’s life rather than burdening them needlessly.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see holy time set apart for gathered worship. The provision for food preparation balances sacred rest with human need. Both traditions see these days as God ordering his people’s common life around remembrance.

Exodus 12:17

17You shall observe the feast of unleavened bread; for in this same day I have brought your armies out of the land of Egypt. Therefore you shall observe this day throughout your generations by an ordinance forever.

Arminian

The feast is kept because on this day the LORD brought Israel’s armies out of Egypt. Arminian readers see obedience grounded in what God has already done. The ordinance is a grateful response to deliverance, not a means of earning it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the indicative of God’s saving act grounding the imperative of observance. Grace precedes law here. Both traditions agree that Israel’s worship flows from redemption rather than securing it.

Exodus 12:18

18In the first month, on the fourteenth day of the month at evening, you shall eat unleavened bread, until the twenty first day of the month at evening.

Arminian

The dates run from the fourteenth evening to the twenty-first evening of the first month. Arminian readers see the feast anchored precisely in the calendar God established. Concrete dates make remembrance a shared, regular practice rather than a vague sentiment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God ordering time for his people. The detailed reckoning reflects the importance of keeping the feast together. Both traditions read the dates as practical instruction, not as a basis for speculative calculation.

Exodus 12:19

19There shall be no yeast found in your houses for seven days, for whoever eats that which is leavened, that soul shall be cut off from the congregation of Israel, whether he is a foreigner, or one who is born in the land.

Arminian

No yeast may be found in Israelite houses, and the penalty applies to both foreigner and native-born. Arminian readers note that the law includes sojourners within the community’s obligations. Holiness is expected of everyone who shares Israel’s life.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see one standard for all who dwell within the covenant community. The inclusion of foreigners anticipates verses 48–49. Both traditions read the verse as evidence that covenant membership was not defined by ancestry alone.

Exodus 12:20

20You shall eat nothing leavened. In all your habitations you shall eat unleavened bread.’ ”

Arminian

Israel must eat nothing leavened in all its dwellings. Arminian readers see the command applied throughout the land and life of the people. The repetition reinforces how thoroughly the feast should shape household practice.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a comprehensive ordinance reaching every home. The emphasis falls on faithful observance of what God commanded. Both traditions see no particular point of dispute in this summary command.

Exodus 12:21

21Then Moses called for all the elders of Israel, and said to them, “Draw out, and take lambs according to your families, and kill the Passover.

Arminian

Moses calls the elders and instructs them to select lambs and kill the Passover. Arminian readers see leaders passing God’s word to the people so that each family can obey. Paul’s statement in 1 Corinthians 5:7 that Christ our Passover has been sacrificed is shared Christian reading.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s word transmitted through appointed leaders. The typological connection to Christ as the Passover lamb belongs to both traditions equally. Neither tradition treats this typology as a point of division.

Exodus 12:22

22You shall take a bunch of hyssop, and dip it in the blood that is in the basin, and strike the lintel and the two door posts with the blood that is in the basin. None of you shall go out of the door of his house until the morning.

Arminian

Hyssop is dipped in the blood and applied to the doorframe, and no one may leave the house until morning. Arminian readers see trust expressed in staying under the sign God appointed. Safety is found in obeying his instruction, not in self-reliance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see protection found only within the place God marked. The command to remain inside underlines dependence on his provision. Both traditions see the household sheltered by blood as a picture of refuge in God’s saving appointment.

Exodus 12:23

23For the LORD will pass through to strike the Egyptians; and when he sees the blood on the lintel, and on the two door posts, the LORD will pass over the door, and will not allow the destroyer to come in to your houses to strike you.

Arminian

The LORD will pass through, see the blood, pass over, and not let the destroyer enter. Arminian readers see God’s promise honored for every household that applied the blood. Interpreters discuss the relation between the LORD and the destroyer; the text keeps judgment under God’s authority.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the LORD sovereignly sparing those under the sign. The destroyer acts only within God’s permission. Both traditions read the verse as shared testimony to deliverance through God’s appointed substitute, without a distinctively Arminian or Reformed fork.

Exodus 12:24

24You shall observe this thing for an ordinance to you and to your sons forever.

Arminian

The ordinance is to be kept by Israel and its children forever. Arminian readers see each generation called to embrace the memory personally. Faith is transmitted through practice but must be owned by those who receive it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s covenant extending to children through ordered worship. Christians in both traditions read the permanence of the ordinance in light of its fulfillment in Christ. The precise relation of Old Testament feasts to Christian practice is discussed within each tradition.

Exodus 12:25

25It shall happen when you have come to the land which the LORD will give you, as he has promised, that you shall keep this service.

Arminian

The service is to continue when Israel enters the promised land. Arminian readers see the Passover tied to God’s faithfulness in fulfilling his promise of land. Remembrance looks back to deliverance and forward to inheritance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s promise guaranteeing Israel’s future in the land. The ordinance links past redemption with expected fulfillment. Both traditions see memory and hope joined in Israel’s worship.

Exodus 12:26

26It will happen, when your children ask you, ‘What do you mean by this service?’

Arminian

Children will ask what the service means. Arminian readers see worship designed to provoke questions and invite understanding. Faith grows when the next generation is given reasons, not merely rituals.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see catechesis built into worship. The child’s question becomes an occasion to tell God’s saving acts. Both traditions value teaching children the meaning of what the people of God do.

Exodus 12:27

27that you shall say, ‘It is the sacrifice of the LORD’s Passover, who passed over the houses of the children of Israel in Egypt, when he struck the Egyptians, and spared our houses.’ ” The people bowed their heads and worshiped.

Arminian

Parents answer that it is the LORD’s Passover sacrifice, and the people bow and worship. Arminian readers see personal testimony to God’s deliverance prompting a worshiping response. The answer centers on what God did, not on Israel’s merit.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Passover described as sacrifice and the people responding with reverent worship. Both traditions see the answer as gospel-shaped: God passed over and spared his people when he struck Egypt. This is shared ground, not a point of contrast.

Exodus 12:28

28The children of Israel went and did so; as the LORD had commanded Moses and Aaron, so they did.

Arminian

Israel does exactly as the LORD commanded Moses and Aaron. Arminian readers see faith expressed in obedient action before the promised deliverance arrives. The people’s response matters within the story.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see obedience as the fitting fruit of trust in God’s word. The people act on what they have been told before seeing the outcome. Both traditions read the verse as an example of faith that obeys.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
