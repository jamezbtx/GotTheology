Passage

Exodus 12

Verses 43–51 · WEB

A closing ordinance defines who may eat the Passover. Foreigners and hired servants are excluded, but circumcised servants and sojourners may share it as one born in the land. The meal must be eaten in one house with no bone broken. Both traditions read these rules within Israel’s covenant life, and both share the Gospel use of the unbroken bones for Christ; views on covenant signs cut across them.

Exodus 12:43

43The LORD said to Moses and Aaron, “This is the ordinance of the Passover. No foreigner shall eat of it,

Arminian

The LORD gives the Passover ordinance: no foreigner may eat it. Arminian readers see the meal as belonging to those within the covenant community. The restriction protects the meaning of the feast rather than expressing ethnic hostility, since verse 48 provides a way in.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Passover as a covenant meal for those who belong to God’s people. The exclusion concerns covenant standing, not birth alone. Both traditions read this verse together with the inclusion provisions that follow.

Exodus 12:44

44but every man’s servant who is bought for money, when you have circumcised him, then shall he eat of it.

Arminian

A purchased servant may eat once circumcised. Arminian readers note that the household servant is brought into the covenant meal through the covenant sign. The ancient institution of slavery is described, not endorsed as an ideal, and should be read in its setting.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see household members included through circumcision, consistent with Genesis 17. Some Reformed readers draw analogies to household baptism; Baptist readers in both traditions see such analogies differently. The verse itself concerns Israel’s practice.

Exodus 12:45

45A foreigner and a hired servant shall not eat of it.

Arminian

A foreigner or hired servant may not eat. Arminian readers see a distinction between temporary residents and those who have joined the covenant community. The issue is commitment and belonging rather than worth.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see covenant membership, not mere presence, as the condition for sharing the meal. Both traditions read the rule within Israel’s legal setting and do not treat it as a statement about the value of outsiders.

Exodus 12:46

46It must be eaten in one house. You shall not carry any of the meat outside of the house. Do not break any of its bones.

Arminian

The Passover is eaten in one house, no meat carried outside, and no bone broken. Arminian readers see the unity and completeness of the meal. John 19:36 applies the unbroken bones to Jesus, a christological reading both traditions share.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see John’s application of this verse to Christ as the true Passover lamb. The typology is shared ground, not a point of division. In its own setting, the rule preserves the meal’s integrity within the household.

Exodus 12:47

47All the congregation of Israel shall keep it.

Arminian

The whole congregation of Israel shall keep it. Arminian readers see the Passover as a communal obligation that unites every family. No member of the community stands outside the call to remember.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the entire covenant people gathered in one act of worship. The verse summarizes the communal character of the feast. Both traditions see no point of contention here.

Exodus 12:48

48When a stranger lives as a foreigner with you, and would like to keep the Passover to the LORD, let all his males be circumcised, and then let him come near and keep it. He shall be as one who is born in the land; but no uncircumcised person shall eat of it.

Arminian

A sojourner who wishes to keep the Passover may do so once his males are circumcised, becoming as one born in the land. Arminian readers see a genuine invitation for outsiders to join God’s people by entering the covenant. Inclusion is open, though it carries the covenant sign.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God welcoming outsiders into covenant fellowship through the appointed sign. The full equality of the circumcised stranger is significant. Views on how covenant signs relate to faith and to children vary within both traditions, so they should not be assigned to one camp.

Exodus 12:49

49One law shall be to him who is born at home, and to the stranger who lives as a foreigner among you.”

Arminian

One law applies to the native-born and the stranger. Arminian readers see equal standing for all who share the covenant meal. The verse anticipates the wider welcome of the nations into God’s people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see equal treatment under God’s law for every covenant member. Christians in both traditions see this pointing toward the inclusion of Gentiles in Christ. This is shared ground.

Exodus 12:50

50All the children of Israel did so. As the LORD commanded Moses and Aaron, so they did.

Arminian

All Israel does as the LORD commanded Moses and Aaron. Arminian readers see the community responding in obedience. The repetition of this formula emphasizes faithful compliance with God’s word.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see obedience as the fitting response to God’s gracious instruction. The verse frames the narrative of deliverance with faithful practice. Both traditions read it without contrast.

Exodus 12:51

51That same day, the LORD brought the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt by their armies.

Arminian

That same day the LORD brings Israel out of Egypt by their armies. Arminian readers see the chapter closing with God’s promise fulfilled. Deliverance is credited entirely to the LORD.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the LORD as the sole agent of Israel’s deliverance. The ordered armies show a people redeemed and constituted by God. Both traditions celebrate the Exodus as God’s mighty act of salvation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
