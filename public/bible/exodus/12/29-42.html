Passage

Exodus 12

Verses 29–42 · WEB

At midnight the firstborn of Egypt die, Pharaoh finally sends Israel away, and the people leave in haste with unleavened dough, Egyptian goods, flocks, and a mixed multitude. The narrator marks the end of a long sojourn and calls the night one to be observed. Interpreters differ on historical questions such as dating and the size of the departure; the text’s focus is the LORD’s faithful deliverance.

Exodus 12:29

29At midnight, the LORD struck all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, from the firstborn of Pharaoh who sat on his throne to the firstborn of the captive who was in the dungeon, and all the firstborn of livestock.

Arminian

At midnight the LORD strikes every firstborn, from Pharaoh’s heir to the prisoner’s child and the livestock. Arminian readers see God fulfilling his warning exactly. The universal reach shows that no rank in Egypt stands outside his judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the LORD’s word fulfilled to the letter and his justice reaching the highest and lowest. Both traditions acknowledge the moral weight of this judgment and read it within the narrative’s account of oppression and repeated refusal.

Exodus 12:30

30Pharaoh rose up in the night, he, and all his servants, and all the Egyptians; and there was a great cry in Egypt, for there was not a house where there was not one dead.

Arminian

Pharaoh, his servants, and all Egypt rise in the night with a great cry, for every house has a death. Arminian readers see the cost of persistent resistance to God falling on an entire society. The narrative invites sober reflection rather than triumph.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fulfillment of 11:6 and the reversal of Israel’s earlier cries. Judgment is real and grievous. Both traditions read the scene as a solemn display of God’s justice against oppression.

Exodus 12:31

31He called for Moses and Aaron by night, and said, “Rise up, get out from among my people, both you and the children of Israel; and go, serve the LORD, as you have said!

Arminian

Pharaoh summons Moses and Aaron and orders Israel to go and serve the LORD. Arminian readers see Pharaoh conceding under judgment what he refused under warning. The demand to serve the LORD finally receives the answer God sought from the beginning.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s purpose accomplished: Pharaoh releases Israel exactly as the LORD foretold. The king’s words echo Moses’ original request. Both traditions see the reversal as evidence of the LORD’s supremacy over Egypt’s ruler.

Exodus 12:32

32Take both your flocks and your herds, as you have said, and be gone; and bless me also!”

Arminian

Pharaoh tells them to take flocks and herds and asks them to bless him. Arminian readers see a broken king seeking blessing from the people he oppressed. The request shows how completely the power relationship has been reversed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the irony of Pharaoh asking for blessing. The text does not describe his repentance, and later chapters show renewed pursuit. Both traditions avoid reading more into the request than the narrative supports.

Exodus 12:33

33The Egyptians were urgent with the people, to send them out of the land in haste, for they said, “We are all dead men.”

Arminian

The Egyptians urge Israel to leave quickly, fearing they will all die. Arminian readers see fear driving Egypt to release what it once clung to. God’s judgment accomplishes what negotiations could not.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God fulfilling his promise in 11:1 that Israel would be driven out. The Egyptians’ urgency serves God’s purpose. Both traditions read the haste as the fulfillment of divine prediction.

Exodus 12:34

34The people took their dough before it was leavened, their kneading troughs being bound up in their clothes on their shoulders.

Arminian

The people carry unleavened dough in kneading troughs bound in their clothing. Arminian readers see the historical root of the feast in the haste of departure. Ordinary details of daily life become the basis of lasting worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the memorial grounded in an actual event of hurried departure. The unleavened dough connects verses 15–20 with this night. Both traditions value the concreteness of the narrative.

Exodus 12:35

35The children of Israel did according to the word of Moses; and they asked of the Egyptians jewels of silver, and jewels of gold, and clothing.

Arminian

Israel asks the Egyptians for silver, gold, and clothing, as Moses instructed. Arminian readers see obedience to God’s earlier word in 11:2. The request fulfills promises given long before deliverance arrived.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God keeping his promise from Exodus 3:21–22. The people act on Moses’ word. Both traditions see faithfulness on God’s side and obedience on Israel’s side in this detail.

Exodus 12:36

36The LORD gave the people favor in the sight of the Egyptians, so that they let them have what they asked. They plundered the Egyptians.

Arminian

The LORD gives Israel favor, the Egyptians give what is asked, and Israel plunders Egypt. Arminian readers see the oppressed leaving with provision after long labor. Interpreters discuss the ethics and nature of the transfer, but the text attributes the favor to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s providence directing the Egyptians’ generosity. The language of plunder echoes a victory without battle. Both traditions read the verse as God’s vindication of his people, not as a general license for taking property.

Exodus 12:37

37The children of Israel traveled from Rameses to Succoth, about six hundred thousand on foot who were men, in addition to children.

Arminian

Israel travels from Rameses to Succoth, about six hundred thousand men on foot, besides children. Arminian readers see God fulfilling his promise to multiply Abraham’s descendants. Interpreters differ on how to understand the large number, including discussion of the Hebrew term often translated thousand.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fulfillment of God’s promise of numerous offspring. Readers in both traditions hold various views on the figure’s meaning and on historical reconstruction. The text’s emphasis is God bringing out a great people.

Exodus 12:38

38A mixed multitude went up also with them, with flocks, herds, and even very much livestock.

Arminian

A mixed multitude goes up with Israel, along with many flocks and herds. Arminian readers see non-Israelites joining the departure, a reminder that God’s people were never defined by ancestry alone. The text does not explain who they were or why they came.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the presence of outsiders among the departing people, which anticipates the inclusion laws in verses 43–49. The text gives few details, so speculation should be restrained. Both traditions see the verse as shared ground.

Exodus 12:39

39They baked unleavened cakes of the dough which they brought out of Egypt; for it wasn’t leavened, because they were thrust out of Egypt, and couldn’t wait, and they had not prepared any food for themselves.

Arminian

They bake unleavened cakes from the dough because they were thrust out and had no time to prepare. Arminian readers see the feast’s origin in a real experience of urgent departure. God’s deliverance came suddenly, before human plans were complete.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the explanation for unleavened bread rooted in history. The people’s lack of preparation highlights that deliverance was God’s work. Both traditions read the verse as linking the ordinance to the event.

Exodus 12:40

40Now the time that the children of Israel lived in Egypt was four hundred thirty years.

Arminian

Israel lived in Egypt four hundred thirty years. Arminian readers see the long sojourn recalling God’s word to Abraham in Genesis 15:13. Ancient versions differ here: the Greek Septuagint and Samaritan Pentateuch include Canaan in the period, and Galatians 3:17 counts from the promise; interpreters weigh these differently.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s faithfulness across a long period of waiting. The textual and chronological questions are real, and readers in both traditions hold different reconstructions. Neither tradition’s reading of salvation depends on resolving the dating.

Exodus 12:41

41At the end of four hundred thirty years, to the day, all of the LORD’s armies went out from the land of Egypt.

Arminian

At the end of four hundred thirty years, to the day, the LORD’s armies leave Egypt. Arminian readers see God’s timing honored precisely. The people are called the LORD’s armies, an ordered host belonging to him rather than a fleeing crowd.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the exact fulfillment of God’s appointed time. The precision underscores his control over history. Both traditions agree that the departure displays God’s faithfulness to his word.

Exodus 12:42

42It is a night to be much observed to the LORD for bringing them out from the land of Egypt. This is that night of the LORD, to be much observed by all the children of Israel throughout their generations.

Arminian

The night is to be observed to the LORD by all Israel throughout their generations. Arminian readers see a vigil answered by vigil: as the LORD watched over Israel, Israel keeps watch in remembrance. Each generation is invited into the memory.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a night belonging to the LORD and set apart for his people’s remembrance. Ordered memory is a means God uses to nurture faith. Both traditions see the verse as a call to grateful, continuing worship.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
