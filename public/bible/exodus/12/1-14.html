Passage

Exodus 12

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Before the final plague, the LORD gives instructions for the Passover lamb, the blood on the doorposts, and a meal eaten in haste. The night of judgment on Egypt becomes the beginning of Israel’s calendar and a lasting memorial. Both traditions read the Passover first in its own setting as deliverance by God’s word, and both share the New Testament’s reading of Christ as the Passover lamb.

Exodus 12:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses and Aaron in the land of Egypt, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses and Aaron while they are still in Egypt. Arminian readers note that the instructions come before deliverance: Israel is to prepare and obey in faith before the outcome is visible. Worship is shaped by God’s word, not invented after the fact.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God taking the initiative to order his people’s worship before redemption is complete. The setting in Egypt stresses grace given to a people still in bondage. Both traditions see deliverance and worship as joined from the start.

Exodus 12:2

2“This month shall be to you the beginning of months. It shall be the first month of the year to you.

Arminian

This month becomes the beginning of months for Israel. Arminian readers see redemption reshaping time and identity: a new calendar marks a new life under the LORD. The people’s year will begin with remembrance of what God has done.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s saving act becoming the foundation for Israel’s ordered life and worship. Interpreters discuss how this calendar relates to other ancient reckonings, but the theological point is clear. Both traditions agree that redemption begins a new story for God’s people.

Exodus 12:3

3Speak to all the congregation of Israel, saying, ‘On the tenth day of this month, they shall take to them every man a lamb, according to their fathers’ houses, a lamb for a household;

Arminian

Each household is to select a lamb on the tenth day. Arminian readers note that every family must participate by acting on God’s instruction. The household setting makes deliverance personal and communal rather than a distant ritual performed only by leaders.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the household as the setting for covenant worship and teaching. Provision is made for every family within the community. Both traditions see the lamb chosen in obedience to God’s word as preparation for the deliverance he alone will accomplish.

Exodus 12:4

4and if the household is too little for a lamb, then he and his neighbor next to his house shall take one according to the number of the souls. You shall make your count for the lamb according to what everyone can eat.

Arminian

Smaller households are to share a lamb with neighbors according to what each can eat. Arminian readers see a practical provision ensuring that no one is excluded because a family is small or poor. The meal is meant to be shared and fully used.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see care for every member of the covenant community. The neighbor’s inclusion shows that the Passover is a communal event, not a private privilege. Both traditions read the detail as evidence of God’s attention to the needs of each household.

Exodus 12:5

5Your lamb shall be without defect, a male a year old. You shall take it from the sheep or from the goats.

Arminian

The lamb must be without defect, a year-old male, from sheep or goats. Arminian readers note that the requirement reflects the worth of what is offered to God. The New Testament’s picture of Christ as the lamb without blemish, in 1 Peter 1:19, is shared Christian reading.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the unblemished lamb as pointing forward to Christ’s sinless sacrifice, a typological reading both traditions share. In its own setting, the requirement teaches that God’s provision must be offered as he prescribes. There is no Arminian/Reformed disagreement on this typology.

Exodus 12:6

6You shall keep it until the fourteenth day of the same month; and the whole assembly of the congregation of Israel shall kill it at evening.

Arminian

The lamb is kept until the fourteenth day and then killed at evening by the whole assembly. Arminian readers see corporate obedience: the entire community acts together at the appointed time. Preparation over several days gives space for anticipation and readiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the whole congregation participating at the time God appointed. The detail situates each household’s act within the larger people of God. Both traditions read the timing as part of the careful ordering of worship that God provides.

Exodus 12:7

7They shall take some of the blood, and put it on the two door posts and on the lintel, on the houses in which they shall eat it.

Arminian

Blood is placed on the doorposts and lintel of houses where the lamb is eaten. Arminian readers see a visible act of trust in God’s promised protection. The household that applies the blood acts on God’s word before the judgment comes.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the blood as God’s appointed sign of protection for those within the house. Its power lies in God’s promise, not in the material itself. Both traditions see substitution and protection here, read in Christian theology in light of Christ’s blood.

Exodus 12:8

8They shall eat the meat in that night, roasted with fire, with unleavened bread. They shall eat it with bitter herbs.

Arminian

The meat is eaten roasted, with unleavened bread and bitter herbs. Arminian readers see a meal that rehearses both haste and the bitterness of slavery. The ritual teaches through taste and action, inviting each participant into the memory of deliverance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the meal as embodied teaching about bondage and redemption. Bitter herbs remember suffering; unleavened bread recalls haste. Both traditions value the concrete, sensory character of worship that God commands for his people’s instruction.

Exodus 12:9

9Don’t eat it raw, nor boiled at all with water, but roasted with fire; with its head, its legs and its inner parts.

Arminian

The lamb must not be eaten raw or boiled but roasted whole. Arminian readers see the detail as part of a precisely ordered act of obedience. Interpreters have offered various explanations for the requirement, and the text itself does not state its reason.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise accept the instruction as God’s ordering of the meal, without requiring a single explanation of its symbolism. Some find meaning in the lamb’s wholeness. Both traditions should avoid presenting speculative explanations as settled fact.

Exodus 12:10

10You shall let nothing of it remain until the morning; but that which remains of it until the morning you shall burn with fire.

Arminian

Nothing may remain until morning; leftovers must be burned. Arminian readers see the meal belonging wholly to that night of deliverance. The rule guards against treating the sacred meal as ordinary food to be stored for later convenience.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the holiness of the meal and its tie to the one decisive night. Burning the remainder prevents misuse. Both traditions read the rule as protecting the integrity of the ordinance God gave.

Exodus 12:11

11This is how you shall eat it: with your belt on your waist, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand; and you shall eat it in haste: it is the LORD’s Passover.

Arminian

The meal is eaten dressed for travel, in haste, because it is the LORD’s Passover. Arminian readers see faith expressed in readiness to leave as soon as God acts. The posture embodies trust that deliverance is near, even before it is seen.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see expectant faith shaped by God’s promise. The Passover belongs to the LORD, not to Israel’s invention. Both traditions read the readiness as a model of believers living in hope of God’s promised deliverance.

Exodus 12:12

12For I will go through the land of Egypt in that night, and will strike all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, both man and animal. I will execute judgments against all the gods of Egypt. I am the LORD.

Arminian

The LORD will strike Egypt’s firstborn and judge all Egypt’s gods. Arminian readers see the plague as a confrontation with false powers that claimed authority over Egypt. The LORD’s self-identification asserts his sole right to be worshiped.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the LORD’s supremacy over every rival claim to divinity. The judgment on the gods exposes idolatry’s emptiness. Both traditions agree that the Exodus displays who the true God is, not merely Israel’s political liberation.

Exodus 12:13

13The blood shall be to you for a token on the houses where you are. When I see the blood, I will pass over you, and no plague will be on you to destroy you when I strike the land of Egypt.

Arminian

The blood will be a sign; when the LORD sees it, he will pass over. Arminian readers see protection given through God’s appointed means and received by obedient faith. Christians in both traditions read this in light of Christ, whose blood shelters believers from judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s promise attached to the sign and see the blood’s efficacy grounded in his word. The typological link to Christ’s atoning death is shared Christian reading. Both traditions see substitution and grace here rather than a contest over the meaning of the sign.

Exodus 12:14

14This day shall be a memorial for you. You shall keep it as a feast to the LORD. You shall keep it as a feast throughout your generations by an ordinance forever.

Arminian

The day becomes a perpetual memorial and feast. Arminian readers see memory as a means of sustaining faith across generations. Each new generation is invited to identify with the redeemed community and respond in worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God appointing remembrance as a means of grace for his covenant people. Christians in both traditions connect Passover memory with the Lord’s Supper, while differing among themselves about its precise sacramental meaning. That difference does not follow the Arminian/Reformed line.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
