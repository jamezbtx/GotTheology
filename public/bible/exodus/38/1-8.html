Passage

Exodus 38

Verses 1–8 · WEB

Moving from the holy place to the courtyard, Bezalel makes the bronze altar of burnt offering, square, with horns, a bronze grating, utensils, and poles, and then the bronze basin, made from the mirrors of the women who served at the door of the Tent of Meeting. These items follow the instructions of Exodus 27 and 30 and stand where worshipers first approached God: an altar for sacrifice and a basin for the priests’ washing. The detail about the mirrors shows ordinary possessions willingly given and transformed for God’s service. Arminian and Reformed readers largely agree in reading this section as a picture of the cost of approaching a holy God and of God’s provision of sacrifice and cleansing. Both commonly see these provisions as fulfilled in Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice (Hebrews 9:11–12), while recognizing that the text itself is descriptive.

Exodus 38:1

1He made the altar of burnt offering of acacia wood. It was square. Its length was five cubits, its width was five cubits, and its height was three cubits.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the altar of burnt offering as the first object a worshiper would meet, a reminder that approaching God involves dealing with sin. Its size suggests the seriousness and the ongoing need of sacrifice.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the altar as central to the way God provided for sinners to approach him. Both traditions commonly see in it a foreshadowing of Christ’s sacrifice, and the cubit measurements are approximate, as the WEB footnote notes.

Exodus 38:2

2He made its horns on its four corners. Its horns were of one piece with it, and he overlaid it with bronze.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice the horns of one piece with the altar, which later Scripture associates with refuge and atonement. The altar is a place where mercy can be sought.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the bronze overlay and horns as marking the altar’s strength and its role in atonement. Both traditions agree that the altar points to God’s provision of a way back to him.

Exodus 38:3

3He made all the vessels of the altar: the pots, the shovels, the basins, the forks, and the fire pans. He made all its vessels of bronze.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the practical vessels as signs of the real, daily work of sacrifice and service. Worship involved effort and attention from those who served.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that every utensil is listed, showing that the altar’s service was ordered in detail by God. Both traditions value this picture of faithful, careful ministry.

Exodus 38:4

4He made for the altar a grating of a network of bronze, under the ledge around it beneath, reaching halfway up.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the bronze grating as part of the altar’s sturdy design for continual use. God’s provision for worship is practical and enduring.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the grating as part of the pattern God revealed. Both traditions agree that the detail matters mainly as evidence of faithful obedience rather than as a code to decipher.

Exodus 38:5

5He cast four rings for the four corners of bronze grating, to be places for the poles.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that even the large bronze altar was made to move with the people. Sacrifice and worship accompany them on the journey.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the rings at the corners as fulfilling the instructions for transporting the altar. Both traditions read the portability of the tabernacle as a sign of God’s presence with a pilgrim people.

Exodus 38:6

6He made the poles of acacia wood, and overlaid them with bronze.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the bronze-covered poles as fitting the altar’s outer location, distinct from the gold of the inner sanctuary. The materials mark degrees of nearness to God’s presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the shift from gold to bronze as reflecting the ordered holiness of the tabernacle. Both traditions agree that the progression teaches reverence in approaching God.

Exodus 38:7

7He put the poles into the rings on the sides of the altar, with which to carry it. He made it hollow with planks.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the hollow altar, made of planks and carried on poles, as practical design for a people on the move. God meets them where they are.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the altar is made exactly according to the pattern given earlier. Both traditions see faithful obedience to God’s word expressed in these construction details.

Exodus 38:8

8He made the basin of bronze, and its base of bronze, out of the mirrors of the ministering women who ministered at the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

Arminian readers are moved by the women who gave their mirrors, ordinary and personal possessions, to make the basin for washing. Their willing gift shows how everyday things can be offered up for God’s service.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the basin as God’s provision for the priests’ cleansing before ministry, and the women’s gift as fruit of willing devotion. Both traditions honor these women who served at the door of the Tent of Meeting, though what their service involved is not described and should not be overstated.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
