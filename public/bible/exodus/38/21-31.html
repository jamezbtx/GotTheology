Passage

Exodus 38

Verses 21–31 · WEB

The chapter closes with an inventory of the metals used for the tabernacle, compiled under Ithamar for the Levites’ service, and a tribute to Bezalel of Judah and Oholiab of Dan, who carried out all that the LORD commanded Moses. The gold came from freewill offerings, while the silver came from the half-shekel paid by each person counted in the census, which Exodus 30:11–16 calls atonement money. Modern equivalents of these talents and shekels are approximate, as the WEB footnotes indicate, and readers differ over how to understand the large census figures. Arminian and Reformed readers share the main themes here: careful stewardship, accountability, willing generosity, and every member’s equal share in the sanctuary. They agree that the passage honors faithful obedience and the God-given skill of those who built his dwelling.

Exodus 38:21

21These are the amounts of materials used for the tabernacle, even the Tabernacle of the Testimony, as they were counted, according to the commandment of Moses, for the service of the Levites, by the hand of Ithamar, the son of Aaron the priest.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the careful counting of materials as an act of accountability among God’s people. Those entrusted with offerings are answerable for how they are used.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the inventory, compiled under Ithamar for the Levites’ service, as reflecting ordered stewardship in God’s house. Both traditions agree that integrity in handling gifts honors God.

Exodus 38:22

22Bezalel the son of Uri, the son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah, made all that the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

Arminian readers see Bezalel as a model of Spirit-given skill freely offered in faithful service. His work is summed up as doing all that the LORD commanded Moses.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Bezalel’s gifts came from God, who filled him with his Spirit for this work (Exodus 31:3). Both traditions agree that this verse honors obedience and God-given ability working together.

Exodus 38:23

23With him was Oholiab, the son of Ahisamach, of the tribe of Dan, an engraver, and a skillful workman, and an embroiderer in blue, in purple, in scarlet, and in fine linen.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that Oholiab of Dan works alongside Bezalel of Judah, showing God using people from different tribes in shared service. Many gifts contribute to one work.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Oholiab’s varied skills as further evidence of God’s gracious distribution of gifts to his people. Both traditions read this partnership as a picture of the body of God’s people working together.

Exodus 38:24

24All the gold that was used for the work in all the work of the sanctuary, even the gold of the offering, was twenty-nine talents and seven hundred thirty shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the gold of the offering as the visible result of the people’s willing generosity in Exodus 35. Their freely given gifts became part of God’s dwelling.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the large amount of gold as a testimony to God stirring his people’s hearts to give. The WEB footnotes give modern equivalents only approximately, and both traditions focus on the generosity rather than the precise weight.

Exodus 38:25

25The silver of those who were counted of the congregation was one hundred talents and one thousand seven hundred seventy-five shekels, according to the shekel of the sanctuary:

Arminian

Arminian readers see the silver as coming from every counted member of the congregation, so that each person had a share in the sanctuary. The whole people stand together before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this silver with the census offering of Exodus 30:11–16, which God appointed as atonement money. Both traditions see in it a reminder that each person’s life belongs to God and needs his provision.

Exodus 38:26

26a beka a head, that is, half a shekel, according to the shekel of the sanctuary, for everyone who passed over to those who were counted, from twenty years old and upward, for six hundred three thousand five hundred fifty men.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the same half shekel was required of everyone, rich and poor alike, according to Exodus 30:15. All stand on equal footing before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the equal payment as a sign that no one can buy greater standing with God. The census total matches Numbers 1:46, and while readers differ over how to understand such large numbers, both traditions agree on the lesson of equal need and equal access.

Exodus 38:27

27The one hundred talents of silver were for casting the sockets of the sanctuary and the sockets of the veil: one hundred sockets for the one hundred talents, one talent per socket.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the silver used for the sockets as forming the foundation of the sanctuary. The people’s shared gifts literally support God’s dwelling.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sockets cast from atonement silver as a striking picture of God’s house resting on his provision. Both traditions treat that symbolism cautiously, while agreeing that the people’s offerings were put to faithful use.

Exodus 38:28

28From the one thousand seven hundred seventy-five shekels he made hooks for the pillars, overlaid their capitals, and made fillets for them.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that even the leftover silver is accounted for and used for hooks and fillets. Nothing given to God is wasted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the careful accounting as further evidence of faithful stewardship. Both traditions agree that every gift, large or small, found its place in God’s house.

Exodus 38:29

29The bronze of the offering was seventy talents and two thousand four hundred shekels.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the bronze offering as another fruit of the people’s generosity. The willing gifts were more than enough, as Exodus 36:7 notes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the bronze as God’s provision through his people for the court and altar. The WEB footnote gives only an approximate modern weight, and both traditions focus on the abundance of the people’s giving.

Exodus 38:30

30With this he made the sockets to the door of the Tent of Meeting, the bronze altar, the bronze grating for it, all the vessels of the altar,

Arminian

Arminian readers see the bronze used for the entrance sockets and the altar as linking generosity with access to God. The people’s gifts serve the way of approach.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the listing of these items as confirming that the work was completed as God commanded. Both traditions agree that the altar and the entrance are central to worship in God’s presence.

Exodus 38:31

31the sockets around the court, the sockets of the gate of the court, all the pins of the tabernacle, and all the pins around the court.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the closing list of sockets and pins as bringing the inventory to completion. Faithful work is finished, down to the last detail.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the completed accounting as preparing for the tabernacle’s assembly and the LORD’s glory filling it (Exodus 40:34). Both traditions agree that the chapter ends by honoring faithful obedience to God’s pattern.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
