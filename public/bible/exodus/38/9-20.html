Passage

Exodus 38

Verses 9–20 · WEB

This section describes the court surrounding the tabernacle: linen hangings on every side, pillars with bronze sockets and silver hooks and bands, and an embroidered screen for the gate on the east. The measurements and materials closely follow Exodus 27:9–19, emphasizing that the whole enclosure was built according to the pattern God gave. The court marked a boundary between the camp and God’s dwelling while also providing a single entrance through which worshipers could come. Arminian and Reformed readers largely agree here, seeing both God’s holiness and his welcome in the design. Any differences concern how much symbolic meaning to draw from the colors, materials, and numbers, a question on which readers within each tradition differ.

Exodus 38:9

9He made the court: for the south side southward the hangings of the court were of fine twined linen, one hundred cubits;

Arminian

Arminian readers see the court’s linen hangings as marking a holy space while still allowing worshipers to come near. God sets boundaries out of holiness and opens a way out of grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the court as part of the ordered pattern of God’s dwelling, distinguishing the holy from the common. Both traditions agree that the enclosure teaches both reverence and welcome.

Exodus 38:10

10their pillars were twenty, and their sockets twenty, of bronze; the hooks of the pillars and their fillets were of silver.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice the combination of bronze sockets and silver hooks and fillets, which gives the court strength and beauty. God’s house is both sturdy and lovely.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the materials as carefully chosen according to God’s instructions. Both traditions focus on faithful obedience rather than trying to assign a fixed meaning to each metal.

Exodus 38:11

11For the north side one hundred cubits, their pillars twenty, and their sockets twenty, of bronze; the hooks of the pillars, and their fillets, of silver.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the north side matching the south, showing a balanced and complete enclosure. The order of God’s house reflects his own trustworthiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the symmetry as reflecting the order of God’s design. Both traditions agree that the repetition emphasizes completeness and care.

Exodus 38:12

12For the west side were hangings of fifty cubits, their pillars ten, and their sockets ten; the hooks of the pillars, and their fillets, of silver.

Arminian

Arminian readers note the shorter west side, which faced the back of the tabernacle where the holiest place stood. The layout guided worshipers toward God’s presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the west side as completing the boundary around God’s dwelling. Both traditions read the arrangement as part of a carefully planned approach to the holy.

Exodus 38:13

13For the east side eastward fifty cubits,

Arminian

Arminian readers see the east side, facing the sunrise, as the side where the entrance would be. There is a way in, and God himself designed it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the east side matches the instructions given in Exodus 27. Both traditions find meaning in the fact that the court has an entrance at all.

Exodus 38:14

14the hangings for the one side were fifteen cubits; their pillars three, and their sockets three;

Arminian

Arminian readers see the hangings on either side of the gate as framing the entrance. The design draws attention to the way of approach.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the measured hangings as part of the precise order God required. Both traditions agree that the detail serves the larger picture of access to God on his terms.

Exodus 38:15

15and so for the other side: on this hand and that hand by the gate of the court were hangings of fifteen cubits; their pillars three, and their sockets three.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the balance of the two sides by the gate as giving the entrance prominence. God’s invitation is clearly marked.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the symmetry around the gate as reflecting God’s ordered design. Both traditions value the picture of a single, clearly defined way into God’s presence.

Exodus 38:16

16All the hangings around the court were of fine twined linen.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the fine twined linen around the court as a sign of purity in God’s dwelling. Those who approach him are drawn into a holy space.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the linen hangings as marking the court as set apart for God. Both traditions agree that the whole enclosure speaks of holiness, without needing to press every material into a symbol.

Exodus 38:17

17The sockets for the pillars were of bronze. The hooks of the pillars and their fillets were of silver. Their capitals were overlaid with silver. All the pillars of the court had silver bands.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice the silver capitals and bands, giving beauty to the pillars that held up the court. Even structural pieces are made with honor.

Reformed

Reformed readers see these details as further evidence of faithful work done according to God’s pattern. Both traditions find in them a picture of the glory of God’s dwelling.

Exodus 38:18

18The screen for the gate of the court was the work of the embroiderer, of blue, purple, scarlet, and fine twined linen. Twenty cubits was the length, and the height along the width was five cubits, like the hangings of the court.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the embroidered screen of blue, purple, and scarlet as an inviting entrance, marked by beauty. The way into God’s presence is not hidden or grim.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gate as the one prescribed entrance, made with the same colors as other sacred hangings. Many in both traditions connect the single gate with Christ as the way to God, while recognizing that the text does not state that link.

Exodus 38:19

19Their pillars were four, and their sockets four, of bronze; their hooks of silver, and the overlaying of their capitals, and their fillets, of silver.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the four pillars of the gate as giving strength to the entrance. The way God provides is secure.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the bronze sockets and silver fittings as part of the consistent pattern. Both traditions agree that the verse reflects faithful obedience and the dignity of the entrance.

Exodus 38:20

20All the pins of the tabernacle, and around the court, were of bronze.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the bronze pins holding the whole structure together as reminders that small, unseen pieces matter. Faithful service often lies in humble tasks.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the pins as completing the faithful construction of the court according to God’s word. Both traditions find encouragement in the value God places on even the smallest part of his house.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
