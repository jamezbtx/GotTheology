Passage

Exodus 7

Verses 1–13 · WEB

The LORD prepares Moses and Aaron for their confrontation with Pharaoh, announcing both the hardening of Pharaoh’s heart and the signs that will make Egypt know him. At court, Aaron’s rod becomes a serpent and swallows the magicians’ imitations, but Pharaoh does not listen. The scene introduces the contest between the LORD and Egypt’s king, and the hardening language that both traditions read with care.

Exodus 7:1

1The LORD said to Moses, “Behold, I have made you as God to Pharaoh; and Aaron your brother shall be your prophet.

Arminian

The LORD makes Moses “as God to Pharaoh,” with Aaron as his prophet. Moses does not become divine; he carries God’s authority into a court that treats its king as more than human.

Arminian readers see a reluctant servant, who had pleaded his weak speech, now equipped rather than replaced. God answers human limitation with provision and a partner, and calls Moses to act on it.

Reformed

Moses will stand toward Pharaoh as God’s spokesman, and Aaron toward Moses as a prophet stands toward the one who sends him. The pattern of sender, messenger, and word frames everything that follows.

Reformed readers stress that the authority is entirely delegated. The contest is between the LORD and Pharaoh, and the servants carry weight only because God sends them.

Exodus 7:2

2You shall speak all that I command you; and Aaron your brother shall speak to Pharaoh, that he let the children of Israel go out of his land.

Arminian

Moses speaks what God commands, Aaron speaks to Pharaoh, and the demand stays the same: let Israel go. The mediators add nothing of their own.

Arminian readers note that the command is a real summons to Pharaoh, not a formality. He is addressed as someone who can and should respond, and the coming plagues answer his refusals.

Reformed

The chain of speech runs from the LORD through Moses and Aaron to Pharaoh. Faithfulness for the messengers means passing on the word whole.

Reformed readers likewise see a genuine command laid on Pharaoh. Both traditions agree that God’s purpose to free Israel is announced openly, and that the king is accountable for what he does with it.

Exodus 7:3

3I will harden Pharaoh’s heart, and multiply my signs and my wonders in the land of Egypt.

Arminian

Here the LORD says, “I will harden Pharaoh’s heart,” and joins it to multiplied signs and wonders. Both traditions take the statement seriously and keep it tied to God’s purpose to make himself known in Egypt.

Many Arminian readers see judicial hardening: God gives a proud, oppressive ruler over to the stubbornness he is already showing, as the narrative’s later “he hardened his heart” makes clear. The hardening serves a historical purpose of revelation and deliverance rather than a statement about Pharaoh’s eternal destiny, and Arminian accounts vary on how direct God’s action is.

Reformed

Reformed readers take “I will harden” as a real act of God, announced before the plagues begin and governing the whole cycle. Many connect it with Paul’s use of Pharaoh in Romans 9:17–18, where God shows mercy and hardens as he wills.

Pharaoh remains fully culpable, because he acts willingly and is never coerced against his own desires. Reformed accounts differ on whether hardening means withholding restraining grace or a more positive act, but they agree it serves God’s glory in Israel’s rescue.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm that God says “I will harden” and that Pharaoh is culpable. Arminian readers commonly see a judicial hardening that confirms Pharaoh’s own prior and ongoing choices, serving a historical purpose rather than settling his eternal destiny; Reformed readers commonly see a real, sovereign act announced in advance, often linked with Romans 9:17–18, without coercing Pharaoh’s will. Each tradition holds varied accounts of how God hardens.

Exodus 7:4

4But Pharaoh will not listen to you, so I will lay my hand on Egypt, and bring out my armies, my people the children of Israel, out of the land of Egypt by great judgments.

Arminian

God foretells Pharaoh’s refusal and his own response: his hand on Egypt, his people brought out by great judgments. Israel is called his armies, a people marching out under their Lord.

Arminian readers see God’s foreknowledge of the refusal and his justice in answering it. Judgment falls on a regime that has enslaved and killed, not arbitrarily on the innocent.

Reformed

The refusal is not a surprise to God; it is part of the plan he has announced. Great judgments will bring Israel out.

Reformed readers emphasise that God’s purpose cannot be frustrated by Pharaoh’s resistance. The king’s no becomes the very occasion through which the LORD displays his power and rescues his people.

Exodus 7:5

5The Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD when I stretch out my hand on Egypt, and bring the children of Israel out from among them.”

Arminian

The goal is stated plainly: the Egyptians shall know that “I am the LORD.” The plagues are revelation as well as judgment, aimed even at Israel’s oppressors.

Arminian readers hear a wide intention: God makes himself known to Egypt, not only to Israel. Some Egyptians will later heed his word, so the knowledge offered is more than bare defeat.

Reformed

God acts so that Egypt will know who he is when he stretches out his hand. Recognition of the LORD is the purpose running through the whole story.

Reformed readers stress that God’s glory is the deepest aim of the exodus. Both traditions share the point that the LORD, not Pharaoh or Egypt’s gods, is the living God.

Exodus 7:6

6Moses and Aaron did so. As the LORD commanded them, so they did.

Arminian

Moses and Aaron obey exactly. After objections in earlier chapters, the narrative now records simple compliance.

Arminian readers see restored trust expressed in action. Grace has met Moses’ fears, and his response is real obedience, which God has invited and enabled.

Reformed

The verse marks the servants’ obedience to God’s command. They do as the LORD commanded them.

Reformed readers see God’s word producing the obedience it calls for. Both traditions honour the plain point: faithful service follows God’s instruction rather than human strategy.

Exodus 7:7

7Moses was eighty years old, and Aaron eighty-three years old, when they spoke to Pharaoh.

Arminian

Moses is eighty and Aaron eighty-three when they face Pharaoh. The narrator pauses to give their ages at the start of the confrontation.

Arminian readers find encouragement that God calls and uses people late in life. Long years in Midian were not wasted, and age is no barrier to faithful service.

Reformed

The ages mark the solemn beginning of the mission. Two old men stand before the most powerful ruler they know.

Reformed readers see God choosing what looks weak to confront what looks strong. Both traditions agree the strength of the mission lies in the One who sends them.

Exodus 7:8

8The LORD spoke to Moses and to Aaron, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to both Moses and Aaron, preparing them for Pharaoh’s demand for a sign. Instruction comes before the encounter.

Arminian readers note God’s care to equip his servants in advance. They are not left to improvise in the court of a hostile king.

Reformed

God addresses both brothers and directs the confrontation. Each step in the contest is set by his word.

Reformed readers stress that the LORD leads the action from start to finish. Both traditions see his servants acting under orders, not on their own initiative.

Exodus 7:9

9“When Pharaoh speaks to you, saying, ‘Perform a miracle!’ then you shall tell Aaron, ‘Take your rod, and cast it down before Pharaoh, and it will become a serpent.’ ”

Arminian

Pharaoh is expected to ask for a wonder, and Aaron’s rod will become a serpent. The sign answers a request for credentials.

Arminian readers see God accommodating Pharaoh with evidence. The king is given reasons to listen, so his later refusal is not for want of signs.

Reformed

God anticipates Pharaoh’s demand and supplies the response. The serpent sign confronts royal power on its own ground.

Reformed readers note that signs authenticate the messenger without themselves producing faith. Both traditions agree Pharaoh receives a clear witness to the LORD’s authority.

Exodus 7:10

10Moses and Aaron went in to Pharaoh, and they did so, as the LORD had commanded. Aaron cast down his rod before Pharaoh and before his servants, and it became a serpent.

Arminian

Aaron throws down his rod before Pharaoh and his officials, and it becomes a serpent. The first wonder takes place in full view of the court.

Arminian readers see a public, unmistakable sign that leaves Pharaoh responsible. Evidence is placed before him and his advisers alike.

Reformed

The servants do as commanded and the sign follows. Pharaoh’s court witnesses the LORD’s power through a simple act of obedience.

Reformed readers see the word and the wonder working together. Both traditions see the scene as God’s challenge to a ruler who claims sacred authority.

Exodus 7:11

11Then Pharaoh also called for the wise men and the sorcerers. They also, the magicians of Egypt, did the same thing with their enchantments.

Arminian

Pharaoh summons wise men, sorcerers, and magicians, and they do the same with their enchantments. Egypt answers with its own religious expertise.

Interpreters in both traditions differ over whether the magicians used trickery or real occult power. Arminian readers note that imitation gives Pharaoh an excuse he chooses to take; the counterfeit lets him avoid facing the sign honestly.

Reformed

Egypt’s experts match the first sign, at least outwardly. The narrative does not explain how, and readers within each tradition differ between sleight of hand and real spiritual power permitted by God.

Reformed readers note that apparent duplication cannot equal the original. Both traditions see how counterfeit wonders can harden unbelief rather than open the eyes.

Exodus 7:12

12For they each cast down their rods, and they became serpents; but Aaron’s rod swallowed up their rods.

Arminian

Every magician’s rod becomes a serpent, but Aaron’s rod swallows them all. The imitation is real enough to impress, yet it is overpowered.

Arminian readers see a clear signal of superiority given to Pharaoh. The evidence points to the LORD even when Egypt can copy the surface of the wonder.

Reformed

Aaron’s rod swallowing the others is a pointed display that the LORD prevails over Egypt’s powers. Many see a challenge to royal and religious symbols of Egypt, though precise symbolism is debated.

Reformed readers stress that God’s power is not one force among equals. Both traditions read the swallowing as a foretaste of the outcome of the contest.

Exodus 7:13

13Pharaoh’s heart was hardened, and he didn’t listen to them, as the LORD had spoken.

Arminian

The WEB says “Pharaoh’s heart was hardened,” without naming an agent, and adds “as the LORD had spoken.” The outcome fulfils what God foretold.

Arminian readers often stress that the passive leaves room for Pharaoh’s own choice, set against signs he could have heeded. God’s prediction describes what will happen without making the king a puppet.

Reformed

The hardening comes about exactly as the LORD said. The passive form names the result, while verse 3 has already named God as the one who hardens.

Reformed readers see God’s announced purpose being carried out through Pharaoh’s real resistance. Both traditions agree Pharaoh does not listen and is responsible for not listening.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
