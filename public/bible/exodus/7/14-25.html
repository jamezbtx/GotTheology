Passage

Exodus 7

Verses 14–25 · WEB

The first plague turns the Nile and Egypt’s waters to blood after Moses warns Pharaoh at the river. The magicians imitate the sign, Pharaoh’s heart is hardened, and ordinary Egyptians dig for water. The narrative stresses God’s word fulfilled, the public nature of the sign, and the LORD’s rule over the river on which Egypt depended.

Exodus 7:14

14The LORD said to Moses, “Pharaoh’s heart is stubborn. He refuses to let the people go.

Arminian

The LORD describes Pharaoh’s heart as stubborn and his refusal as ongoing. The verdict comes from God, who sees the king clearly.

Arminian readers note that here the stubbornness is a settled trait in Pharaoh himself. God names what Pharaoh is doing and responds with the first plague.

Reformed

God reports the condition of Pharaoh’s heart: stubborn and refusing. The diagnosis sets up the first of the plagues.

Reformed readers note that God’s purpose does not cancel the king’s real refusal; both are true at once. Shared ground is plain: Pharaoh will not let Israel go.

Exodus 7:15

15Go to Pharaoh in the morning. Behold, he is going out to the water. You shall stand by the river’s bank to meet him. You shall take the rod which was turned to a serpent in your hand.

Arminian

Moses is to meet Pharaoh by the river in the morning, carrying the rod that became a serpent. The confrontation moves to the Nile itself.

Arminian readers see God going to meet Pharaoh where he is, giving another opportunity to hear. The rod reminds him of the sign already given.

Reformed

God directs the time, the place, and the instrument. The river, central to Egypt’s life, becomes the setting of the next sign.

Reformed readers see the LORD choosing to strike at Egypt’s source of security. Both traditions note how the plagues engage the world Pharaoh trusts.

Exodus 7:16

16You shall tell him, ‘The LORD, the God of the Hebrews, has sent me to you, saying, “Let my people go, that they may serve me in the wilderness. Behold, until now you haven’t listened.”

Arminian

Moses repeats the message from “the LORD, the God of the Hebrews”: let my people go to serve me. He adds that Pharaoh has not listened until now.

Arminian readers stress the repeated invitation. Each plague is preceded by a word, so Pharaoh is warned before he is struck.

Reformed

The demand is worship: Israel must be free to serve the LORD. The note “until now you haven’t listened” keeps Pharaoh’s accountability in view.

Reformed readers see the conflict as a question of whom Israel will serve. Both traditions agree that the exodus is about freedom for worship, not only escape from labour.

Exodus 7:17

17The LORD says, “In this you shall know that I am the LORD. Behold: I will strike with the rod that is in my hand on the waters which are in the river, and they shall be turned to blood.

Arminian

God announces that striking the river will turn it to blood, so that Pharaoh will know “I am the LORD.” Revelation remains the stated aim.

Arminian readers note that knowledge of God is offered to Pharaoh through the sign. The plague is meant to teach, not only to punish.

Reformed

The rod in Moses’ hand is called the instrument by which the LORD will strike. God speaks of his servant’s action as his own.

Reformed readers see divine agency working through human means. The Nile is often proposed as a target of Egypt’s religious devotion, though how directly the plague addresses specific deities remains debated.

Exodus 7:18

18The fish that are in the river will die and the river will become foul. The Egyptians will loathe to drink water from the river.” ’ ”

Arminian

The fish will die, the river will stink, and Egyptians will loathe its water. The plague strikes daily life and the economy.

Arminian readers see judgment proportionate to oppression: a nation that threw Hebrew boys into the Nile now finds the river turned against it. Interpreters differ on how far to press that connection.

Reformed

God foretells the consequences in concrete terms. Egypt’s life-giving river becomes a source of revulsion.

Reformed readers see the Creator’s rule over nature on display. Both traditions agree the sign shows that the LORD governs the waters Egypt depends on.

Exodus 7:19

19The LORD said to Moses, “Tell Aaron, ‘Take your rod, and stretch out your hand over the waters of Egypt, over their rivers, over their streams, and over their pools, and over all their ponds of water, that they may become blood. There will be blood throughout all the land of Egypt, both in vessels of wood and in vessels of stone.’ ”

Arminian

Aaron is to stretch his hand over all Egypt’s waters, so that blood fills even vessels of wood and stone. The plague’s reach is total.

Arminian readers see the comprehensive scope as a call to the whole land to recognise the LORD. No part of Egypt can treat the sign as merely local.

Reformed

The command extends the plague to every pool and container. Its breadth shows that no sphere lies outside God’s power.

Reformed readers likewise stress the LORD’s complete dominion. Both traditions read the detail as part of the narrative’s portrait of God judging a whole system, not only its ruler.

Exodus 7:20

20Moses and Aaron did so, as the LORD commanded; and he lifted up the rod, and struck the waters that were in the river, in the sight of Pharaoh, and in the sight of his servants; and all the waters that were in the river were turned to blood.

Arminian

Moses and Aaron obey, and the rod strikes the river in front of Pharaoh and his servants. The waters turn to blood.

Arminian readers note the public character of the sign. Pharaoh sees it with his own eyes, so he cannot plead ignorance.

Reformed

Obedience is followed by fulfilment exactly as God spoke. The narrative stresses that everything happens “as the LORD commanded.”

Reformed readers see God’s word accomplishing its purpose. Both traditions agree the plague is a deliberate act of the LORD carried out through his servants.

Exodus 7:21

21The fish that were in the river died. The river became foul. The Egyptians couldn’t drink water from the river. The blood was throughout all the land of Egypt.

Arminian

The fish die, the river stinks, and blood is everywhere in Egypt. The narrator confirms each element of God’s warning.

Arminian readers see real hardship falling on Egypt because of its ruler’s defiance. The suffering of ordinary people under a stubborn king is part of the tragedy.

Reformed

The plague comes to pass completely. God’s announced judgment is not partial or symbolic only.

Reformed readers see the faithfulness of God’s word in judgment as in promise. Both traditions acknowledge the gravity of the scene without making it a lesson detached from Israel’s oppression.

Exodus 7:22

22The magicians of Egypt did the same thing with their enchantments. So Pharaoh’s heart was hardened, and he didn’t listen to them, as the LORD had spoken.

Arminian

The magicians do the same with their enchantments, and Pharaoh’s heart was hardened, as the LORD had spoken. The imitation gives the king an excuse.

Arminian readers note that copying a plague only adds more blood; it does nothing to relieve Egypt. Pharaoh seizes on a counterfeit rather than heeding the original.

Reformed

Again the passive is used: Pharaoh’s heart was hardened, fulfilling God’s word. The magicians’ imitation feeds his resistance.

Reformed readers see the LORD’s prediction coming true through ordinary causes, including Pharaoh’s own preference for his advisers. Both traditions agree the king is responsible for not listening.

Exodus 7:23

23Pharaoh turned and went into his house, and he didn’t even take this to heart.

Arminian

Pharaoh turns away into his house and does not take even this to heart. Indifference is part of his response.

Arminian readers see a pattern of deliberate disregard. He chooses not to consider what he has seen, which is the very attitude the hardening language describes.

Reformed

Pharaoh simply walks away. The narrator presents his refusal as inward as well as outward.

Reformed readers see the hardened heart displayed in quiet dismissal. Both traditions agree the verse describes Pharaoh’s own disposition, whatever further account is given of God’s role.

Exodus 7:24

24All the Egyptians dug around the river for water to drink; for they couldn’t drink the river water.

Arminian

Ordinary Egyptians dig near the river for water. They bear the cost of their king’s choice.

Arminian readers note the human suffering here and the injustice of a ruler who will not yield. Pharaoh’s stubbornness affects people with no voice in his decision.

Reformed

The people search desperately for drinkable water. The plague touches every household.

Reformed readers see how judgment on a nation reaches its members. Both traditions can acknowledge the hardship without treating every sufferer as individually guilty.

Exodus 7:25

25Seven days were fulfilled, after the LORD had struck the river.

Arminian

Seven days pass after the LORD struck the river. The narrator marks time before the next warning.

Arminian readers see an interval in which Pharaoh could reconsider. God does not rush from plague to plague without space for response.

Reformed

The verse closes the first plague by naming the LORD as the one who struck the river. Time passes under his control.

Reformed readers stress that God sets the pace of the contest. Both traditions read the pause as part of a measured sequence leading toward Israel’s release.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
