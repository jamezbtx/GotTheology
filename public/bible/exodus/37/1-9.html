Passage

Exodus 37

Verses 1–9 · WEB

After the people’s willing offerings and the call of Bezalel and Oholiab (Exodus 35–36), the narrative records the making of the tabernacle furnishings, closely echoing the instructions of Exodus 25. Bezalel begins with the ark of acacia wood overlaid with gold, carried on poles, and the mercy seat of pure gold with two cherubim of one piece with it. The repetition shows that the work matches the pattern God gave on the mountain, and it centers on the place where the LORD had promised to meet with Moses above the mercy seat (Exodus 25:22). Arminian and Reformed readers largely agree in reading these verses as a description of God’s gracious presence and the way he provides for sinful people to draw near. Both commonly see the mercy seat as foreshadowing Christ (Hebrews 9:5, 11–12), though readers in each tradition differ over how far to press the symbolism of individual details.

Exodus 37:1

1Bezalel made the ark of acacia wood. Its length was two and a half cubits, and its width a cubit and a half, and a cubit and a half its height.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the passage opens with Bezalel, the craftsman God filled with his Spirit for this work (Exodus 31:3), personally making the ark. Skilled human labor, freely offered, becomes the means by which God’s dwelling is built.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Bezalel’s skill as a gift of God’s Spirit, so that the work is God’s provision as much as human effort. Both traditions read the ark’s modest dimensions as part of the pattern God gave; converting cubits into modern units is only approximate, as the WEB footnote shows.

Exodus 37:2

2He overlaid it with pure gold inside and outside, and made a molding of gold for it around it.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the pure gold inside and out as fitting the holiness of the God who would meet his people there. Nothing about this object is careless or second-rate.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the overlay of gold as underscoring the glory and purity of God’s presence among a sinful people. Both traditions agree that the beauty of the ark points beyond itself to the worth of the God it represents.

Exodus 37:3

3He cast four rings of gold for it in its four feet—two rings on its one side, and two rings on its other side.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice the practical detail of the rings, which let the ark travel with a people on the move. God’s presence is not fixed to one place but goes with his people on their journey.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the rings as part of God’s careful design, so that even the carrying of the ark follows his instructions. Both traditions find comfort in a God who accompanies his people through the wilderness.

Exodus 37:4

4He made poles of acacia wood and overlaid them with gold.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the gold-covered poles as a sign that even the instruments of carrying share in the holiness of what they bear. Those who serve God’s presence are called to treat it with reverence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the poles match the earlier command exactly, illustrating obedience to God’s pattern. Both traditions agree that the detail reflects worship shaped by God’s word rather than human invention.

Exodus 37:5

5He put the poles into the rings on the sides of the ark, to bear the ark.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the poles placed in the rings so the ark can be borne, preparing for a journey of faith toward the promised land. The God who dwells with Israel also leads them forward.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the ark carried by God’s appointed means and in his appointed way. Both traditions read this as a reminder that God’s presence is to be handled on his terms, not ours.

Exodus 37:6

6He made a mercy seat of pure gold. Its length was two and a half cubits, and a cubit and a half its width.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the mercy seat as the place where God provides for sin to be covered and fellowship restored. Many connect it with Christ’s atoning work, which they understand as offered for all and received by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the mercy seat as the place of atonement and commonly connect it with Christ, as Hebrews 9:5 names it and Romans 3:25 speaks of his atoning sacrifice. Both traditions affirm that God himself provides the way of reconciliation, though they differ elsewhere over the intended scope of the atonement, a question this verse does not raise.

Exodus 37:7

7He made two cherubim of gold. He made them of beaten work, at the two ends of the mercy seat:

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the cherubim are hammered of beaten gold, not attached afterward. The careful craftsmanship expresses wholehearted devotion in the service of God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the cherubim as heavenly attendants, recalling the guardians of God’s holiness in Genesis 3. Both traditions read their presence as a sign that this place reflects the throne room of God.

Exodus 37:8

8one cherub at the one end, and one cherub at the other end. He made the cherubim of one piece with the mercy seat at its two ends.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the unity of cherubim and mercy seat as a picture of holiness and mercy belonging together. God’s nearness to sinners does not lessen his holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the cherubim are of one piece with the mercy seat, reflecting the integrity of God’s design. Both traditions agree that holiness and mercy meet in the same place.

Exodus 37:9

9The cherubim spread out their wings above, covering the mercy seat with their wings, with their faces toward one another. The faces of the cherubim were toward the mercy seat.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that the cherubim face the mercy seat, as though attending to the place where mercy is shown. Worship gathers around God’s provision for sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the overshadowing wings as a picture of God’s holy presence guarding and enthroning the place of atonement. Both traditions treasure this image of God dwelling with his people above the mercy seat, as promised in Exodus 25:22.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
