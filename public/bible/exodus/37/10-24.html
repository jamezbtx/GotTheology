Passage

Exodus 37

Verses 10–24 · WEB

Bezalel next makes the furnishings of the Holy Place: the gold-covered table with its vessels and the lampstand of pure gold, hammered from a single talent with almond-blossom cups. These items match the instructions of Exodus 25 and portray a house where the LORD dwells among his people, with bread set before him and light that does not go out. Hebrews 9:2 names the lampstand and the table as part of the first room of the tabernacle. Arminian and Reformed readers largely agree here, seeing God’s generous provision, worship ordered by his word, and patterns that many in both traditions connect with Christ as the light and life of his people. They differ mainly over how much detail to read typologically, and that difference runs within each tradition rather than between them.

Exodus 37:10

10He made the table of acacia wood. Its length was two cubits, and its width was a cubit, and its height was a cubit and a half.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the table as part of God’s hospitality, a place prepared for bread set before him. God’s dwelling is a home, not only a sanctuary of distance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the table as made according to the divine pattern, an ordered part of the worship God prescribes. Both traditions read its presence as a sign of fellowship between God and his people.

Exodus 37:11

11He overlaid it with pure gold, and made a gold molding around it.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the table, like the ark, is overlaid with pure gold and bordered with a molding. Every item in God’s house is made with honor.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gold overlay as a reminder that what is set apart for God’s presence must reflect his glory. Both traditions agree that the gold signals holiness rather than mere decoration.

Exodus 37:12

12He made a border of a hand’s width around it, and made a golden molding on its border around it.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the border of a hand’s width as a practical and protective detail, showing care for what is placed on the table. Faithful service attends to small things.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that even this border follows the instructions given on the mountain. Both traditions see in such details a God who values careful, obedient worship.

Exodus 37:13

13He cast four rings of gold for it, and put the rings in the four corners that were on its four feet.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the rings on the table’s feet as another sign that worship moves with a pilgrim people. God provides for his presence to travel with them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repeated pattern of rings and poles as part of the unity of God’s design for the whole tabernacle. Both traditions read the detail as preparing the furnishings for a journey under God’s guidance.

Exodus 37:14

14The rings were close by the border, the places for the poles to carry the table.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice the careful placement of the rings close to the border. Even practical arrangements in God’s house are thoughtfully made.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the placement as part of faithful execution of God’s instructions. Both traditions agree that the verse’s significance lies in obedience to the pattern more than in hidden symbolism.

Exodus 37:15

15He made the poles of acacia wood, and overlaid them with gold, to carry the table.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the gold-covered poles for the table as one more sign that holy things are carried with reverence. The people’s worship is portable but never casual.

Reformed

Reformed readers note how closely the verse echoes the earlier command, showing the work done as God directed. Both traditions read it as a picture of worship ordered by God’s word.

Exodus 37:16

16He made the vessels which were on the table, its dishes, its spoons, its bowls, and its pitchers with which to pour out, of pure gold.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the vessels of pure gold as tools for real service before God, including offerings poured out. Worship involves concrete acts of devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the vessels as part of the worship God himself appointed, made of the finest material. Both traditions agree that these items served the regular presentation of offerings in God’s presence.

Exodus 37:17

17He made the lamp stand of pure gold. He made the lamp stand of beaten work. Its base, its shaft, its cups, its buds, and its flowers were of one piece with it.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the lampstand, hammered from pure gold in one piece, as a sign of light God provides in his dwelling. Many connect it with Christ, who said he is the light of the world (John 8:12).

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the lampstand as God’s provision of light in the holy place and often read it as pointing to Christ. Both traditions use such typology with care, since the text itself describes craftsmanship rather than spelling out symbolic meanings.

Exodus 37:18

18There were six branches going out of its sides: three branches of the lamp stand out of its one side, and three branches of the lamp stand out of its other side:

Arminian

Arminian readers notice the six branches spreading from the central shaft, giving the lampstand a tree-like form. The design suggests life flowing from a single source.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the symmetry of the branches as reflecting the order and beauty of God’s design. Both traditions read the lampstand as a picture of light and life in God’s presence, while holding particular symbolic readings loosely.

Exodus 37:19

19three cups made like almond blossoms in one branch, a bud and a flower, and three cups made like almond blossoms in the other branch, a bud and a flower; so for the six branches going out of the lamp stand.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the almond-blossom cups as images of life and growth in the midst of the wilderness. God’s house speaks of flourishing.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the almond blossoms recall the life-giving character of God’s presence, and some connect them with Aaron’s rod that budded. Both traditions agree that the imagery evokes life, without needing to fix every detail’s meaning.

Exodus 37:20

20In the lamp stand were four cups made like almond blossoms, its buds and its flowers;

Arminian

Arminian readers see the extra cups on the central shaft as part of the lampstand’s rich, garden-like beauty. Worship in God’s house is not drab but full of life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the central shaft’s ornamentation as part of the same unified pattern. Both traditions agree that the craftsmanship reflects the glory of the God who dwells there.

Exodus 37:21

21and a bud under two branches of one piece with it, and a bud under two branches of one piece with it, and a bud under two branches of one piece with it, for the six branches going out of it.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice the repetition in describing the buds beneath each pair of branches, which slows the reader to appreciate the work. Patient attention is itself a form of reverence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the threefold repetition as stressing exact conformity to God’s pattern. Both traditions find in the careful wording a call to take God’s instructions seriously.

Exodus 37:22

22Their buds and their branches were of one piece with it. The whole thing was one beaten work of pure gold.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the phrase “one beaten work of pure gold” as highlighting the costly, unified effort behind the lampstand. Devotion gives its best to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the unity of the piece as reflecting the integrity of God’s design. Both traditions agree that the lampstand’s wholeness fits the holiness of its place.

Exodus 37:23

23He made its seven lamps, and its snuffers, and its snuff dishes, of pure gold.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the seven lamps and their tools as provision for keeping the light burning in God’s house. Faithful worship includes the humble work of tending.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that even the snuffers and dishes are of pure gold, showing that no part of God’s service is common. Both traditions see value in ordinary tasks done in God’s presence.

Exodus 37:24

24He made it of a talent of pure gold, with all its vessels.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the talent of gold as a sign of the generosity behind the people’s offerings. The willing gifts of Exodus 35 now take visible form.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the costly material as fitting the glory of God’s dwelling. A talent was a large weight, but modern equivalents are approximate, as the WEB footnote indicates, and both traditions focus on the generosity rather than the exact amount.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
