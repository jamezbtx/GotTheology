Passage

Exodus 37

Verses 25–29 · WEB

The chapter closes with the golden altar of incense, square with horns of one piece with it and poles for carrying, and with the holy anointing oil and pure incense prepared after the art of the perfumer. These items carry out the instructions of Exodus 30, which tie the altar to the daily burning of incense before the LORD. The section shows that prayer, consecration, and fragrant worship belong to life in God’s presence. Arminian and Reformed readers share this reading, commonly linking incense with the prayers of God’s people and anointing with being set apart for God’s service. Any differences concern how far to press the symbolism, not the main point of the passage.

Exodus 37:25

25He made the altar of incense of acacia wood. It was square: its length was a cubit, and its width a cubit. Its height was two cubits. Its horns were of one piece with it.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the altar of incense as the place where fragrant offering rose before God, often linked with the prayers of his people. Prayer is a central part of life in God’s presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect the altar of incense with prayer and intercession, some seeing in it a picture of Christ’s ongoing intercession. Both traditions agree that the altar points to the privilege of approaching God in worship.

Exodus 37:26

26He overlaid it with pure gold: its top, its sides around it, and its horns. He made a gold molding around it.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the gold overlay on the incense altar as matching the holiness of the inner sanctuary. Prayer is offered in a holy place before a holy God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gold and the molding as marking the altar’s close association with God’s presence. Both traditions read the detail as underscoring reverence in prayer.

Exodus 37:27

27He made two golden rings for it under its molding crown, on its two ribs, on its two sides, for places for poles with which to carry it.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the rings and places for poles as preparing the altar to travel with the people. Prayer goes with God’s people wherever he leads them.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the faithful execution of the pattern, down to the rings beneath the molding. Both traditions agree that prayer and worship belong to the whole journey, not one fixed place.

Exodus 37:28

28He made the poles of acacia wood, and overlaid them with gold.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the gold-covered poles as another sign of reverence for holy things in transit. Even carrying the altar is part of service to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repetition of poles and gold throughout the chapter as reflecting the coherence of God’s design. Both traditions find in this unity a picture of worship ordered by God.

Exodus 37:29

29He made the holy anointing oil and the pure incense of sweet spices, after the art of the perfumer.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the anointing oil and incense as making both people and objects set apart for God’s service. Consecration involves willing dedication of life to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the holy oil and pure incense, prepared with skill, as provisions God himself appointed for consecration and worship. Both traditions commonly connect anointing with the Spirit’s work and incense with prayer, while holding those connections as interpretive rather than stated in this verse.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
