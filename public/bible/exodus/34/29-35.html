Passage

Exodus 34

Verses 29–35 · WEB

Moses comes down from Sinai with the new tablets, not knowing his face is shining because he has been speaking with God. The people are afraid at first, but he calls them near and gives them the commandments. Afterward he wears a veil, taking it off when he goes in before the LORD and when he speaks God’s word to the people.

Exodus 34:29

29When Moses came down from Mount Sinai with the two tablets of the covenant in Moses’ hand, when he came down from the mountain, Moses didn’t know that the skin of his face shone by reason of his speaking with him.

Arminian

Moses’ face shines without his knowing, a reflection of God’s glory from speaking with him. The Hebrew verb is related to the word for “horn” (as in rays), which is why the Latin Vulgate rendered it “horned.”

Arminians see the transforming effect of communion with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see reflected glory, a picture Paul develops of believers being transformed from glory to glory (2 Corinthians 3:18).

Shared ground: being with God leaves a mark.

Exodus 34:30

30When Aaron and all the children of Israel saw Moses, behold, the skin of his face shone; and they were afraid to come near him.

Arminian

Aaron and the people fear to approach. The glory on Moses’ face echoes the fear of chapter 19.

Arminians see the natural response of sinners to holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the awe that God’s glory produces, even reflected.

Both note the fear is fitting but not final.

Exodus 34:31

31Moses called to them, and Aaron and all the rulers of the congregation returned to him; and Moses spoke to them.

Arminian

Moses calls them, and the leaders come back. The mediator draws the people near.

Arminians see invitation overcoming fear.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the mediator bringing God’s people near.

Shared ground: Moses bridges the distance.

Exodus 34:32

32Afterward all the children of Israel came near, and he gave them all the commandments that the LORD had spoken with him on Mount Sinai.

Arminian

All the people come near and receive the commandments. God’s word is delivered to the whole community.

Arminians see the people welcomed back into covenant.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the word of God shaping the covenant people.

Both agree the commandments are given to all.

Exodus 34:33

33When Moses was done speaking with them, he put a veil on his face.

Arminian

After speaking, Moses veils his face. Paul interprets the veil in 2 Corinthians 3:13 as keeping Israel from seeing the end of a fading glory.

Arminians and Reformed readers both draw on Paul’s use of the passage.

Reformed

Reformed readers follow Paul in contrasting the old covenant’s glory with the greater, lasting glory of the new (2 Corinthians 3:7–11).

Shared ground: the veil points beyond itself.

Exodus 34:34

34But when Moses went in before the LORD to speak with him, he took the veil off, until he came out; and he came out, and spoke to the children of Israel that which he was commanded.

Arminian

Before the LORD, Moses removes the veil; then he speaks God’s word to the people. Communion with God comes first, then ministry.

Arminians see the pattern of personal communion fueling service.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Paul’s application: when one turns to the Lord, the veil is removed (2 Corinthians 3:16).

Both agree unveiled access to God is the goal.

Exodus 34:35

35The children of Israel saw Moses’ face, that the skin of Moses’ face shone; so Moses put the veil on his face again, until he went in to speak with him.

Arminian

The pattern repeats: the shining face, the veil, the return to God. Moses’ ministry is a rhythm of meeting God and speaking for him.

Arminians see faithful, continuing ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses’ veiled glory pointing to the unveiled glory of God in the face of Christ (2 Corinthians 4:6).

Shared ground: God’s glory is reflected in those who meet with him.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
