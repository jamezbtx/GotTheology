Passage

Exodus 34

Verses 10–28 · WEB

God answers Moses by renewing the covenant. He promises marvels and the driving out of the nations, and calls Israel to make no covenant with the land’s inhabitants, to tear down their altars, and to worship no other god, for the LORD’s name is Jealous. He restates key laws: no cast idols, the feasts, the firstborn, the Sabbath, the pilgrimages, and the firstfruits. Moses stays forty days and nights and the words of the covenant, the ten commandments, are written on the tablets.

Exodus 34:10

10He said, “Behold, I make a covenant: before all your people I will do marvels, such as have not been worked in all the earth, nor in any nation; and all the people among whom you are shall see the work of the LORD; for it is an awesome thing that I do with you.

Arminian

“Behold, I make a covenant.” After the calf, God renews his covenant and promises marvels before all the nations.

Arminian readers see God’s faithfulness to his people renewed through Moses’ intercession.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sovereign grace: God makes the covenant, does the marvels, and gets the glory.

Shared ground: the covenant is renewed by God’s initiative.

Exodus 34:11

11Observe that which I command you today. Behold, I will drive out before you the Amorite, the Canaanite, the Hittite, the Perizzite, the Hivite, and the Jebusite.

Arminian

Israel must observe God’s commands, and God will drive out the nations. Obedience and promise go together.

Arminians emphasize covenant faithfulness required of the people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience as the fitting response to grace, not the ground of it.

Both agree God’s commands and promises belong together.

Exodus 34:12

12Be careful, lest you make a covenant with the inhabitants of the land where you are going, lest it be for a snare among you;

Arminian

No covenant with the inhabitants of the land, lest it become a snare. The danger is spiritual compromise.

Arminians see real danger of falling into idolatry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God guarding his people from influences that would draw them away.

Shared ground: alliances with idolatry endanger faithfulness.

Exodus 34:13

13but you shall break down their altars, and dash in pieces their pillars, and you shall cut down their Asherah poles;

Arminian

Altars, pillars and Asherah poles must be torn down. Canaanite worship is to be removed, not accommodated.

The command concerns objects of false worship in the land Israel will inherit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see exclusive worship requiring removal of rivals.

Both agree idolatry must not be tolerated among God’s people.

Exodus 34:14

14for you shall worship no other god; for the LORD, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God.

Arminian

“The LORD, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God.” His jealousy is covenant love that will not share his people with rivals.

Arminians see God’s passionate commitment to his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s jealousy as zeal for his own glory and his people’s good.

Shared ground: God’s jealousy is holy, not petty.

Exodus 34:15

15“Don’t make a covenant with the inhabitants of the land, lest they play the prostitute after their gods, and sacrifice to their gods, and one call you and you eat of his sacrifice;

Arminian

Covenants with the inhabitants lead to joining their sacrifices. The language of prostitution describes spiritual unfaithfulness.

Arminians see the real possibility of turning away from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the marriage imagery of the covenant: idolatry is adultery against God.

Both agree the warning is serious.

Exodus 34:16

16and you take of their daughters to your sons, and their daughters play the prostitute after their gods, and make your sons play the prostitute after their gods.

Arminian

Intermarriage with idolaters would lead sons into idolatry. The concern is religious, not ethnic (cf. Rahab and Ruth).

Arminians see the danger of drifting through close ties to unbelief.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom in guarding the household, echoed in 2 Corinthians 6:14.

Shared ground: the issue is faithfulness to God.

Exodus 34:17

17“You shall make no cast idols for yourselves.

Arminian

“No cast idols.” A direct reminder of the calf (32:4).

Arminians and Reformed readers alike see the second commandment restated after Israel broke it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the prohibition of images in worship as central to right worship.

Shared ground: God will not be represented by idols.

Exodus 34:18

18“You shall keep the feast of unleavened bread. Seven days you shall eat unleavened bread, as I commanded you, at the time appointed in the month Abib; for in the month Abib you came out of Egypt.

Arminian

The Feast of Unleavened Bread recalls the exodus in the month Abib. Israel must remember their redemption.

Arminians see memory as fuel for faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the feasts as covenant signs pointing to redemption, fulfilled in Christ (cf. 1 Corinthians 5:7–8).

Both agree the feast is about remembering God’s deliverance.

Exodus 34:19

19“All that opens the womb is mine; and all your livestock that is male, the firstborn of cow and sheep.

Arminian

Every firstborn belongs to God, recalling the Passover when he spared Israel’s firstborn (13:2, 12–15).

The claim is rooted in redemption.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s ownership of his people grounded in his saving act.

Shared ground: God’s claim rests on redemption.

Exodus 34:20

20You shall redeem the firstborn of a donkey with a lamb. If you will not redeem it, then you shall break its neck. You shall redeem all the firstborn of your sons. No one shall appear before me empty.

Arminian

Firstborn donkeys are redeemed with a lamb, and firstborn sons are redeemed. No one comes before God empty-handed.

Arminians see a picture of redemption by substitution.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the principle of substitutionary redemption that runs to Christ.

Both agree redemption is costly and God-given.

Exodus 34:21

21“Six days you shall work, but on the seventh day you shall rest: in plowing time and in harvest you shall rest.

Arminian

The Sabbath must be kept even in plowing and harvest, when work seems most urgent.

Arminians see trust in God’s provision.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Sabbath as a creation ordinance and covenant sign (31:13–17), with diversity within the tradition over its continuing application.

Shared ground: rest expresses dependence on God.

Exodus 34:22

22“You shall observe the feast of weeks with the first fruits of wheat harvest, and the feast of harvest at the year’s end.

Arminian

The Feast of Weeks and the feast of harvest mark the agricultural year with worship.

The calendar is ordered around God’s gifts.

Reformed

Reformed readers see all of life sanctified by worship.

Both note the rhythm of gratitude.

Exodus 34:23

23Three times in the year all your males shall appear before the Lord GOD, the God of Israel.

Arminian

Three times a year the males appear before the Lord GOD. Worship gathers the covenant community.

Arminians see regular communal worship as vital.

Reformed

Reformed readers see corporate worship as commanded by God.

Shared ground: God calls his people together.

Exodus 34:24

24For I will drive out nations before you and enlarge your borders; neither shall any man desire your land when you go up to appear before the LORD, your God, three times in the year.

Arminian

God promises to protect Israel’s land while they go up to worship. Obedience is backed by his care.

Arminians see God’s faithfulness to those who obey.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence guarding his people as they worship.

Both agree God provides for those who honor him.

Exodus 34:25

25“You shall not offer the blood of my sacrifice with leavened bread. The sacrifice of the feast of the Passover shall not be left to the morning.

Arminian

No leaven with sacrificial blood, and no Passover leftovers by morning. Worship follows God’s instructions.

The details preserve the meaning of Passover.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God regulating his own worship.

Shared ground: worship is offered God’s way.

Exodus 34:26

26“You shall bring the first of the first fruits of your ground to the house of the LORD your God. “You shall not boil a young goat in its mother’s milk.”

Arminian

The first of the firstfruits go to God. The prohibition of boiling a young goat in its mother’s milk is given without explanation, and interpreters offer different proposed reasons.

Arminians see honoring God with the first and best.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the firstfruits principle of giving God the first share.

Both note that the reason for the goat prohibition is not stated in the text.

Exodus 34:27

27The LORD said to Moses, “Write these words; for in accordance with these words I have made a covenant with you and with Israel.”

Arminian

Moses is to write these words, the basis of the renewed covenant with Moses and Israel.

Arminians see the covenant clearly set down.

Reformed

Reformed readers see written Scripture as the instrument of covenant.

Shared ground: God’s covenant is put in writing.

Exodus 34:28

28He was there with the LORD forty days and forty nights; he neither ate bread, nor drank water. He wrote on the tablets the words of the covenant, the ten commandments.

Arminian

Forty days and nights without food or water: a miraculous sustaining. “He wrote on the tablets” is grammatically ambiguous; 34:1 says God would write the words, and Deuteronomy 10:2–4 says the LORD wrote them, so many read God as the subject here, while others take Moses as writing the words of vv. 10–27.

The “ten commandments” (literally “ten words”) are named here for the first time.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same ambiguity and generally follow 34:1 and Deuteronomy 10:4 in reading God as the writer of the tablets.

Shared ground: the covenant is restored on tablets as before.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
