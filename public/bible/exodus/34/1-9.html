Passage

Exodus 34

Verses 1–9 · WEB

God tells Moses to cut two new stone tablets to replace the ones he broke, and to climb Sinai alone. There the LORD comes down in the cloud, passes before Moses, and proclaims his name: merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in loving kindness and truth, forgiving sin yet not clearing the guilty. Moses bows in worship and asks God to go with his stiff-necked people and take them as his inheritance.

Exodus 34:1

1The LORD said to Moses, “Chisel two stone tablets like the first. I will write on the tablets the words that were on the first tablets, which you broke.

Arminian

The broken tablets are replaced. God renews the covenant Israel shattered. Moses cuts the stone, but God writes the words.

Arminians and Reformed readers alike see grace in a second set of tablets.

Reformed

Reformed readers see covenant renewal resting on God’s initiative: the same words, freshly given, to a people who broke them.

Shared ground: God restores what sin destroyed.

Exodus 34:2

2Be ready by the morning, and come up in the morning to Mount Sinai, and present yourself there to me on the top of the mountain.

Arminian

Moses must be ready in the morning and present himself on the summit. The meeting is formal and holy.

Preparation reflects reverence for God’s presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God setting the terms of approach, as at the first giving of the law (19:10–15).

Both note the solemnity.

Exodus 34:3

3No one shall come up with you or be seen anywhere on the mountain. Do not let the flocks or herds graze in front of that mountain.”

Arminian

No one else may come up, and even the animals must keep away. The holiness boundaries of chapter 19 are restored.

Arminians see God’s holiness respected even in renewal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the mediator alone approaching God on behalf of the people.

Shared ground: grace does not lower holiness.

Exodus 34:4

4He chiseled two tablets of stone like the first; then Moses rose up early in the morning, and went up to Mount Sinai, as the LORD had commanded him, and took in his hand two stone tablets.

Arminian

Moses obeys exactly, rising early and carrying the tablets up the mountain.

His obedience models readiness to meet God on God’s terms.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faithful obedience from the mediator God has chosen.

Both note the careful obedience.

Exodus 34:5

5The LORD descended in the cloud, and stood with him there, and proclaimed the LORD’s name.

Arminian

The LORD descends in the cloud, stands with Moses, and proclaims his name, fulfilling the promise of 33:19.

Arminians see God keeping his word to his friend.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God revealing himself as he chooses, on his initiative.

Shared ground: the proclamation of the name is the heart of the revelation.

Exodus 34:6

6The LORD passed by before him, and proclaimed, “The LORD! The LORD, a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness and truth,

Arminian

God’s self-description, “merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abundant in loving kindness and truth,” is echoed again and again in Scripture (Numbers 14:18; Psalm 86:15; 103:8; Joel 2:13; Jonah 4:2). Arminian readers emphasize that God’s mercy and patience reveal his heart toward sinners, a sincere goodwill that seeks their return.

That God reveals this after the golden calf makes it all the more striking.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this as one of Scripture’s central revelations of God’s character. His mercy and faithfulness to his covenant are steadfast. Many also hear its echo in John 1:14–17, where glory is revealed “full of grace and truth.”

Both traditions treasure this verse as a foundation for prayer and worship.

Exodus 34:7

7keeping loving kindness for thousands, forgiving iniquity and disobedience and sin; and who will by no means clear the guilty, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children, and on the children’s children, on the third and on the fourth generation.”

Arminian

God keeps loving kindness for thousands and forgives iniquity, yet he “will by no means clear the guilty.” Mercy and justice stand together. The generational clause describes sin’s consequences spreading through families; Exodus 20:5 ties this to “those who hate me,” and Deuteronomy 24:16 and Ezekiel 18 make clear each person answers for his own sin.

Arminians stress that mercy is the larger reality: “thousands” versus “third and fourth generation.”

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s justice and mercy as perfectly united, a tension resolved at the cross, where God forgives sin without clearing guilt unjustly (cf. Romans 3:25–26).

Shared ground: God is both forgiving and just, and his mercy far outweighs his judgment.

Exodus 34:8

8Moses hurried and bowed his head toward the earth, and worshiped.

Arminian

Moses bows quickly and worships. The right response to God’s self-revelation is worship.

Arminians and Reformed readers alike see worship as the first answer to God’s name.

Reformed

Reformed readers see worship arising from the knowledge of God’s character.

Shared ground: Moses responds with immediate reverence.

Exodus 34:9

9He said, “If now I have found favor in your sight, Lord, please let the Lord go among us, even though this is a stiff-necked people; pardon our iniquity and our sin, and take us for your inheritance.”

Arminian

Moses pleads on the basis of what God has just revealed: go with us, pardon our sin, take us as your inheritance. He includes himself in “our iniquity.”

Arminian readers see intercession that appeals to God’s declared mercy and expects a real response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses praying God’s own words back to him, the pattern of faithful prayer.

Both agree that God’s revealed character is the ground of confident prayer.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
