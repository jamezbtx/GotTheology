Passage

Exodus 21

Verses 28–36 · WEB

The chapter closes with liability for animals and property. A bull that kills a person is stoned and its meat may not be eaten. Its owner is innocent unless he had been warned that the bull gored and failed to pen it. Then his life is forfeit, though a ransom may be set. A slain servant is valued at thirty shekels. Open pits and fights between oxen are settled by restitution. Similar goring-ox laws appear in older Mesopotamian codes, such as those of Eshnunna and Hammurabi, but Israel’s version treats human life as sacred, avoids punishing children for their fathers, and holds negligent owners accountable.

Exodus 21:28

28“If a bull gores a man or a woman to death, the bull shall surely be stoned, and its meat shall not be eaten; but the owner of the bull shall not be held responsible.

Arminian

A bull that kills a person is stoned, and its meat may not be eaten. Behind this lies Gen 9:5, where God requires an accounting for human blood even from an animal. The death is treated as bloodguilt, not simply property loss. If the owner had no warning, he is not held responsible.

Reformed

A bull that kills a person is stoned, and its meat may not be eaten. Reformed readers connect this with Gen 9:5, where God requires an accounting for human blood even from a beast. The sanctity of human life is upheld, and an owner who had no warning is not held guilty.

Exodus 21:29

29But if the bull had a habit of goring in the past, and this has been testified to its owner, and he has not kept it in, but it has killed a man or a woman, the bull shall be stoned, and its owner shall also be put to death.

Arminian

If the bull was known to gore and the owner had been warned but did not pen it, the owner is also liable to death. Negligence that leads to death is treated as a grave wrong. Arminian readers see the law holding people responsible for the foreseeable results of their choices.

Reformed

If the bull was known to gore and the owner had been warned but did not pen it, the owner is also liable to death. Knowing about a danger and doing nothing makes the owner guilty. Reformed readers see the sixth commandment requiring care to preserve the lives of others, not only refraining from murder.

Exodus 21:30

30If a ransom is imposed on him, then he shall give for the redemption of his life whatever is imposed.

Arminian

A ransom may be accepted instead of the owner’s life. This sets culpable negligence apart from murder, for which no ransom is allowed (Num 35:31). Arminian readers see justice tempered with mercy where the guilt is real but not deliberate.

Reformed

A ransom may be accepted instead of the owner’s life, which sets culpable negligence apart from murder, where ransom is forbidden (Num 35:31). Reformed readers note the law’s care to grade guilt fairly, with judgment proportioned to intent and responsibility.

Exodus 21:31

31Whether it has gored a son or has gored a daughter, according to this judgment it shall be done to him.

Arminian

The same rule applies if the victim is a son or daughter. Many scholars contrast this with older Mesopotamian laws that could punish an offender’s child for a child killed. Israel’s law keeps punishment on the guilty party (compare Deut 24:16).

Reformed

The same rule applies if the victim is a son or daughter. Many scholars note the contrast with some ancient laws that punished an offender’s child in kind. Reformed readers see the principle of Deut 24:16 here: each person is punished for his own wrong, and children are not punished for their parents’ crimes.

Exodus 21:32

32If the bull gores a male servant or a female servant, thirty shekels of silver shall be given to their master, and the ox shall be stoned.

Arminian

If a servant is killed, thirty shekels of silver go to the master and the bull is stoned. The payment reflects the servant’s legal status, yet the stoning of the bull treats the servant’s death as bloodguilt too. Christian readers note that Judas was paid thirty pieces of silver for Jesus (Matt 26:15; Zech 11:12–13), the price of a servant.

Reformed

If a servant is killed, thirty shekels of silver go to the master and the bull is stoned. The animal is put to death just as when a free person is killed, which shows that a servant’s blood also counts before God. Reformed and Arminian readers alike note the echo in the thirty pieces of silver paid to Judas (Matt 26:15; Zech 11:12–13).

Exodus 21:33

33“If a man opens a pit, or if a man digs a pit and doesn’t cover it, and a bull or a donkey falls into it,

Arminian

The law turns to property damage. A man who opens or digs a pit and leaves it uncovered is responsible if an animal falls in. Neglect of a hazard to others is treated as a real wrong.

Reformed

The law turns to property damage. A man who opens or digs a pit and leaves it uncovered is responsible if an animal falls in. Reformed readers see the eighth commandment requiring care for a neighbor’s goods, not merely refraining from theft.

Exodus 21:34

34the owner of the pit shall make it good. He shall give money to its owner, and the dead animal shall be his.

Arminian

The pit’s owner pays the animal’s value and keeps the carcass. Restitution restores what was lost without enriching anyone. Arminian readers see neighbor-love worked out in ordinary responsibility.

Reformed

The pit’s owner pays the animal’s value and keeps the carcass. Reformed readers see the general equity of restitution here: liability rests on the one whose negligence caused the loss, and the neighbor is made whole.

Exodus 21:35

35“If one man’s bull injures another’s, so that it dies, then they shall sell the live bull, and divide its price; and they shall also divide the dead animal.

Arminian

If one man’s bull kills another’s with no prior warning, both owners share the loss. They sell the live bull and split its price and the carcass. It is a fair solution when neither party is clearly at fault.

Reformed

If one man’s bull kills another’s with no prior warning, the owners split the loss by selling the living bull and dividing its price and the carcass. Reformed readers note the law’s practical wisdom where fault is unclear, sharing an unavoidable loss evenly.

Exodus 21:36

36Or if it is known that the bull was in the habit of goring in the past, and its owner has not kept it in, he shall surely pay bull for bull, and the dead animal shall be his own.

Arminian

If the bull was known to gore and the owner did not pen it, he must pay a full bull for the bull it killed, and he keeps the dead animal. Responsibility follows knowledge and negligence, and that principle is still widely recognized.

Reformed

If the bull was known to gore and the owner did not pen it, he must replace the dead bull and keep the carcass. Liability rests on prior knowledge and neglect. Reformed readers see the general equity of such laws as still useful for Christian thinking about justice and responsibility.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition.
