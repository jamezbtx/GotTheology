Passage

Exodus 21

Verses 12–27 · WEB

Verses 12–27 move from servants to bodily harm. Deliberate murder is a capital crime, while an accidental killer is promised a place of refuge, later the cities of refuge (Num 35; Deut 19). Striking or cursing parents and kidnapping are also capital offenses. Lesser injuries bring compensation for lost time and medical care. A master who kills a servant is punished, and one who maims a servant must set the servant free. At the center stands the formula “eye for eye, tooth for tooth,” which sets the judge’s penalty in proportion to the harm. In 21:22–25 the meaning of the harm to a pregnant woman and her child is debated.

Exodus 21:12

12“One who strikes a man so that he dies shall surely be put to death,

Arminian

Deliberate homicide is a capital crime, grounded in Gen 9:6: humans bear God’s image, so taking a life is an offense against God. Arminian readers share the common Christian reading that this civil penalty belonged to Israel’s judicial law. The underlying moral principle, the sanctity of human life, is permanent (compare Rom 13:4).

Reformed

Deliberate homicide is a capital crime, grounded in Gen 9:6, where the image of God makes murder an attack on God’s honor. Reformed readers treat the specific penalty as part of Israel’s judicial law. The moral principle behind it, the sanctity of life and the magistrate’s duty to punish murder, continues (Rom 13:4).

Exodus 21:13

13but not if it is unintentional, but God allows it to happen; then I will appoint you a place where he shall flee.

Arminian

An accidental killing is distinguished from murder, and the killer may flee to a place God will appoint, later the cities of refuge (Num 35; Deut 19:4–5). The WEB’s “God allows it to happen” renders a Hebrew phrase that can be read “God brought it to his hand.” Arminian readers take the wording as God’s permission and governance of events that he does not directly cause. The law treats the death as a true accident and holds the killer not guilty of murder.

Reformed

An accidental killing is distinguished from murder, and the killer may flee to a place God will appoint, later the cities of refuge (Num 35; Deut 19:4–5). The WEB’s “God allows it to happen” renders a Hebrew phrase that can be read “God brought it to his hand.” Reformed readers, whose confessions cite this verse when teaching providence, take it to show that events which are accidents to us still fall under God’s ordering. Because the man did not intend the death, he is not guilty of murder.

Key contrast Both traditions read the statute the same way: the man did not intend the death, is not guilty of murder, and may flee to the appointed place of refuge. They differ on how God’s involvement in the accident is described. Arminian readers typically take “God allows it to happen” as God’s permission and governance of an event he does not directly cause, working through a world of real contingency that he foreknows. Reformed readers typically take it as God’s providence ordaining and governing even events that are accidental from a human point of view, while the man remains a true second cause who acted without intent.

Exodus 21:14

14If a man schemes and comes presumptuously on his neighbor to kill him, you shall take him from my altar, that he may die.

Arminian

Premeditated murder gets no protection, not even at God’s altar. Holy places cannot be used to escape justice; compare Joab at the altar (1 Kgs 2:28–34). Arminian readers note that the law weighs intention: the same outward act is judged differently according to the heart and purpose behind it.

Reformed

Premeditated murder gets no protection, not even at God’s altar. Holy places may not be used to escape justice (compare 1 Kgs 2:28–34). Reformed readers note that God’s worship and God’s justice are not set against each other, and that the law weighs intention, scheming “presumptuously,” in judging guilt.

Exodus 21:15

15“Anyone who attacks his father or his mother shall be surely put to death.

Arminian

Striking a father or mother is a capital offense. It attacks the household order the fifth commandment protects (Exod 20:12). The severity shows how central the family was to covenant life. Arminian readers apply the moral principle, honor toward parents, while treating the penalty as part of Israel’s civil law.

Reformed

Striking a father or mother is a capital offense. It strikes at the authority the fifth commandment upholds (Exod 20:12). Reformed readers hold the moral duty of honoring parents to be permanent and regard the penalty as belonging to Israel’s judicial law, though its severity shows how seriously God regards the family.

Exodus 21:16

16“Anyone who kidnaps someone and sells him, or if he is found in his hand, he shall surely be put to death.

Arminian

Kidnapping a person, whether he is sold or still in the kidnapper’s possession, is punishable by death (compare Deut 24:7). This law cuts against manstealing, the trade at the root of later chattel slavery. Paul names “enslavers” among the lawless in 1 Tim 1:10. Christian abolitionists later appealed to this law against the slave trade.

Reformed

Kidnapping a person, whether he is sold or still in the kidnapper’s possession, is punishable by death (compare Deut 24:7). Reformed readers note that manstealing violates the eighth commandment at its most serious, and that 1 Tim 1:10 names “enslavers” among the lawless. This law condemns the kidnapping on which later chattel slavery depended.

Exodus 21:17

17“Anyone who curses his father or his mother shall surely be put to death.

Arminian

Cursing a parent, calling down harm or treating them with contempt, carries the same penalty as striking them. Jesus cites this verse (Matt 15:4; Mark 7:10) to rebuke those who used religious technicalities to avoid caring for their parents. Arminian readers see the inner attitude of honor as the lasting point.

Reformed

Cursing a parent carries the same penalty as striking them, because contempt in words also breaks the fifth commandment. Jesus cites this verse (Matt 15:4; Mark 7:10) against traditions that let people neglect their parents. Reformed readers hold that the moral law reaches words and attitudes as well as outward acts.

Exodus 21:18

18“If men quarrel and one strikes the other with a stone, or with his fist, and he doesn’t die, but is confined to bed;

Arminian

Now the law turns to injuries short of death. In a quarrel, a man hits another with a stone or his fist, and the injured man is laid up. The case is about restoring what was lost through a real but non-fatal wrong, not about vengeance.

Reformed

Now the law turns to injuries short of death. In a quarrel, a man hits another with a stone or his fist, and the injured man is laid up. Reformed readers note that the sixth commandment covers injury as well as killing, and that the civil law sets out remedies in proportion to the harm done.

Exodus 21:19

19if he rises again and walks around with his staff, then he who struck him shall be cleared; only he shall pay for the loss of his time, and shall provide for his healing until he is thoroughly healed.

Arminian

Once the injured man can walk again with a staff, the attacker is cleared of a capital charge, but he must pay for lost time and cover the cost of healing. Arminian readers see a sensible justice that repairs the harm done and does not demand more than that.

Reformed

Once the injured man can walk again with a staff, the attacker is cleared of a capital charge, but he must pay for lost time and medical care until full recovery. Reformed readers see the general equity of restitution here: the wrongdoer bears the cost of the harm he caused.

Exodus 21:20

20“If a man strikes his servant or his maid with a rod, and he dies under his hand, the man shall surely be punished.

Arminian

A master who beats a servant to death “shall surely be punished”; the Hebrew speaks of the death being avenged. The servant’s life is legally protected against the master himself, which is notable in the ancient Near East. Arminian readers see the law insisting that the servant is a person whose death demands justice.

Reformed

A master who beats a servant to death “shall surely be punished”; the Hebrew speaks of the death being avenged. Reformed readers note that the master’s authority does not reach to life and death: the servant bears God’s image, and his killing is a crime the community must answer.

Exodus 21:21

21Notwithstanding, if his servant gets up after a day or two, he shall not be punished, for the servant is his property.

Arminian

If the servant survives a day or two, the master is not punished, “for the servant is his property” (literally “his money”). The phrasing reflects the economic reality of servitude in Israel: the master’s own loss counts as part of the penalty, and the delay suggests no clear intent to kill. Christians read this verse honestly as a limit set within a hierarchy that the New Testament does not hold up as the ideal (Gal 3:28; Phlm 16).

Reformed

If the servant survives a day or two, the master is not punished, “for the servant is his property” (literally “his money”). The civil law regulated servitude as it existed: the master bears the financial loss, and the delay weakens any proof of intent to kill. Reformed readers see accommodation in Israel’s judicial law, which the fuller light of Christ’s kingdom moves beyond (Gal 3:28; Phlm 16).

Exodus 21:22

22“If men fight and hurt a pregnant woman so that she gives birth prematurely, and yet no harm follows, he shall be surely fined as much as the woman’s husband demands and the judges allow.

Arminian

Men fighting strike a pregnant woman, and “she gives birth prematurely”; the Hebrew says literally “her children come out.” If no further harm follows, a fine is set by the husband and confirmed by judges. Interpreters disagree whether the verse describes a premature live birth or a miscarriage, and whether “harm” refers to the mother, the child, or both. That disagreement runs through Arminian and Reformed commentators alike.

Reformed

Men fighting strike a pregnant woman, and “she gives birth prematurely”; the Hebrew says literally “her children come out.” If no further harm follows, a fine is set by the husband and confirmed by judges. Reformed interpreters, like others, are divided on whether this describes a premature live birth or a miscarriage, and on whether “harm” refers to the mother, the child, or both. Readers should hold conclusions about this verse with care.

Exodus 21:23

23But if any harm follows, then you must take life for life,

Arminian

If harm does follow, the penalty corresponds to it, up to “life for life.” Those who read v22 as premature birth often apply this to harm to the mother or the child; those who read miscarriage usually apply it to the mother. On either reading, the law treats violence that endangers a pregnant woman as a serious crime.

Reformed

If harm does follow, the penalty corresponds to it, up to “life for life.” Those who take v22 as a live birth apply this harm to either the mother or the child; those who read miscarriage usually apply it to the mother. On either reading, the case shows God’s concern for the vulnerable woman and the child she carries.

Exodus 21:24

24eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot,

Arminian

Lex talionis, “eye for eye,” is a limiting principle for judges, not permission for personal revenge. The penalty must match the injury, no more and no less, and it must be the same for rich and poor. Num 35:31 forbids ransom only for murder, and many infer that other injuries could be settled by equivalent payment. Jesus forbids using the principle for personal retaliation (Matt 5:38–39).

Reformed

Lex talionis, “eye for eye,” governs judges, not private vengeance. It requires proportion: the penalty fits the wrong, without excess or leniency for status. Many conclude from Num 35:31, which bars ransom only for murder, that bodily injuries could be settled with equivalent compensation. Jesus rejects using this rule for personal retaliation (Matt 5:38–39), while just proportion remains part of the general equity behind civil punishment.

Exodus 21:25

25burning for burning, wound for wound, and bruise for bruise.

Arminian

The list closes with burns, wounds, and bruises, covering every kind of bodily harm. The repetition stresses that justice must be measured and impartial.

Reformed

The list closes with burns, wounds, and bruises. The thoroughness of the list stresses that every injury comes before a just judge and that no wrong is too small to answer for.

Exodus 21:26

26“If a man strikes his servant’s eye, or his maid’s eye, and destroys it, he shall let him go free for his eye’s sake.

Arminian

If a master destroys a servant’s eye, the servant goes free. The talionis principle is applied to servants by release, not by maiming the master. The law gives the servant a real remedy against abuse by the master himself.

Reformed

If a master destroys a servant’s eye, the servant goes free. Reformed readers note that God limits a master’s power over the servant’s body: maiming ends the master’s claim entirely. Within Israel’s civil law, the servant is a person under God’s protection.

Exodus 21:27

27If he strikes out his male servant’s tooth, or his female servant’s tooth, he shall let the servant go free for his tooth’s sake.

Arminian

Even a knocked-out tooth sets the servant free. Making a relatively small injury cost the master his whole claim to the servant’s labor discourages violence strongly. Arminian readers see the law steadily guarding the dignity of the weaker party.

Reformed

Even a knocked-out tooth sets the servant free. Making a small injury cost the master the servant’s whole remaining service strongly discourages abuse. Reformed readers see the general equity: those under authority must be protected from those who hold it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
