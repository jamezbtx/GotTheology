Passage

Exodus 21

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Exodus 21 opens the Book of the Covenant (Exod 20:22–23:33), the case laws Moses is to set before Israel right after the Ten Words. Verses 1–11 begin, perhaps surprisingly, with servants. A Hebrew who sold himself, usually for debt, served six years and went free in the seventh. One who chose to stay was marked at the door with a pierced ear. A daughter sold as a servant-wife kept fixed rights to food, clothing, and marriage, and she could go free if those rights were denied. A nation just freed from Egypt is told first how to limit the power of masters.

Exodus 21:1

1“Now these are the ordinances which you shall set before them:

Arminian

“Ordinances” (mishpatim) are rulings for judges: case laws that apply the Ten Words to the disputes of a farming people. Arminian readers in the Wesleyan line, with the Anglican and Methodist articles of religion, share the classic threefold distinction of moral, ceremonial, and civil law. On that reading these statutes were Israel’s civil law. They do not bind Christian nations as a code, but their general equity and the moral principles under them still teach.

Reformed

“Ordinances” (mishpatim) are rulings for judges: case laws that apply the Ten Words to the disputes of a farming people. Reformed readers, with the Westminster Confession, hold the classic threefold distinction of moral, ceremonial, and civil law. These judicial laws expired with Israel’s state and do not bind other nations beyond the general equity they contain. Their moral substance, rooted in the Ten Words, still guides Christian justice.

Exodus 21:2

2“If you buy a Hebrew servant, he shall serve six years, and in the seventh he shall go out free without paying anything.

Arminian

This is debt servitude, not chattel slavery. A Hebrew sold, or selling himself, because of poverty serves six years and then goes free “without paying anything.” The seventh-year release echoes the Sabbath and Israel’s own release from Egypt (compare Deut 15:12–15, which adds generous provision at release). Arminian readers see a law that lets a poor brother pay off his debt with work while capping how long his labor can be held.

Reformed

This is debt servitude, not chattel slavery. A Hebrew sold, or selling himself, because of poverty serves six years and then goes free “without paying anything.” The seventh-year release echoes the Sabbath and the exodus (compare Deut 15:12–15). Reformed readers note that God sets a limit on a master’s claim at the very start of Israel’s civil law: a redeemed people may not hold a brother in permanent bondage against his will.

Exodus 21:3

3If he comes in by himself, he shall go out by himself. If he is married, then his wife shall go out with him.

Arminian

The servant’s household status is protected. If he came in married, his wife leaves with him, so his debt does not break up a family he already had. The law looks after the dependents of the poor, not only the poor man himself.

Reformed

The servant’s household status is protected. If he came in married, his wife leaves with him, so servitude does not dissolve a marriage that existed before it. Reformed readers note how the civil law guards marriage as a creation ordinance even in hard economic circumstances.

Exodus 21:4

4If his master gives him a wife and she bears him sons or daughters, the wife and her children shall be her master’s, and he shall go out by himself.

Arminian

This case is hard for modern readers. A wife provided by the master, likely a servant herself with her own term or obligation, stays with the master’s household, as do her children. The man goes free, but his family does not automatically go with him. The law reflects a world in which servitude was a legal status. Arminian readers acknowledge the hardship plainly. Verses 5–6 give the man a lawful way to keep his family.

Reformed

This case is hard for modern readers. A wife provided by the master, likely a servant herself still under obligation, remains in the master’s household with her children. The man’s release does not cancel her separate obligation. Reformed readers recognize that Israel’s civil law regulated the economic order of its day rather than abolishing it at once, and they read this verse honestly as such. Verses 5–6 provide a lawful way for the family to stay together.

Exodus 21:5

5But if the servant shall plainly say, ‘I love my master, my wife, and my children. I will not go out free;’

Arminian

The servant may choose to stay. His declaration names love for his master, his wife, and his children. Permanent service must be his own free and public choice, never something imposed on him. Arminian readers note that the law values a freely given commitment.

Reformed

The servant may choose to stay. His declaration names love for his master, his wife, and his children, and it shows that the household could be a place of good treatment and security. The change in his status depends on his own clear, spoken consent. Reformed readers note that the law guards against permanent bondage being forced on anyone.

Exodus 21:6

6then his master shall bring him to God, and shall bring him to the door or to the doorpost, and his master shall bore his ear through with an awl, and he shall serve him forever.

Arminian

The ceremony is solemn and public. “To God” may mean to the sanctuary or before the judges acting in God’s name; interpreters differ, and older English versions render it “judges.” Piercing the ear at the doorpost joins the man to the household for life. Some Christian writers have drawn a devotional picture of willing, lifelong service to God from this verse, but they offer it as application, not as the statute’s meaning.

Reformed

The ceremony is solemn and public. “To God” may mean to the sanctuary or before the judges as God’s representatives; interpreters differ, and older English versions render it “judges.” The pierced ear at the doorpost marks the man as part of the household for life. Reformed writers sometimes apply the scene to willing, lifelong devotion to God (compare Ps 40:6), while keeping that distinct from the verse’s civil meaning.

Exodus 21:7

7“If a man sells his daughter to be a female servant, she shall not go out as the male servants do.

Arminian

A poor father might “sell” a daughter into a household, usually with marriage to the master or his son in view. Hers is a different case from the male debt servant, and she does not simply leave after six years. The verses that follow give her protections suited to her vulnerable position. Deuteronomy 15:12 later speaks of Hebrew women also being released, so readers weigh how the two laws relate.

Reformed

A poor father might “sell” a daughter into a household, usually with marriage to the master or his son in view. Hers is a different case from the male debt servant, so the six-year rule is not applied to her in the same way. The following verses secure her rights as a wife. Deuteronomy 15:12 later treats Hebrew women in servitude alongside men, and commentators discuss how the two laws relate.

Exodus 21:8

8If she doesn’t please her master, who has married her to himself, then he shall let her be redeemed. He shall have no right to sell her to a foreign people, since he has dealt deceitfully with her.

Arminian

If the master who intended her for himself is displeased, he must allow her family to buy her back. He may not sell her to foreigners, since he has “dealt deceitfully” with her by breaking the arrangement. The law limits the stronger party and keeps a broken promise from turning into exploitation.

Reformed

If the master who intended her for himself is displeased, he must allow her to be redeemed. He may not sell her to foreigners, because he has broken faith with her. Reformed readers see here God’s concern that the powerful keep covenant commitments to the vulnerable, a concern that also runs through the prophets.

Exodus 21:9

9If he marries her to his son, he shall deal with her as a daughter.

Arminian

If she is given to the master’s son, she is to be treated as a daughter, with the full standing of a family member. She is not a lesser member of the household.

Reformed

If she is given to the master’s son, she must be treated as a daughter. The law raises her from purchased servant to a family member with recognized rights.

Exodus 21:10

10If he takes another wife to himself, he shall not diminish her food, her clothing, and her marital rights.

Arminian

If he takes another wife, the first woman’s food, clothing, and marital rights may not be reduced. The third term is rare, and some suggest “lodging” or “oil” instead of conjugal rights. The law regulates an ancient practice of multiple wives without endorsing it. Genesis 2:24 and Jesus’ teaching (Matt 19:4–8) show God’s creation design, while laws like this one restrain harm within Israel’s social reality.

Reformed

If he takes another wife, the first woman’s food, clothing, and marital rights may not be reduced. The third term is rare, and some suggest “lodging” or “oil.” Reformed readers note that the law here regulates a practice it does not commend. Creation’s pattern of one man and one woman (Gen 2:24; Matt 19:4–8) remains the standard, and this verse limits the damage.

Exodus 21:11

11If he doesn’t do these three things for her, she may go free without paying any money.

Arminian

If he will not provide these three things, she goes free with no price paid. Her rights can be enforced, and the master forfeits his investment if he neglects her. For its time, this is a significant protection for a woman with little social power.

Reformed

If he will not provide these three things, she goes free with no price paid. The law makes her rights enforceable and makes the master’s neglect cost him. Reformed readers note how often Israel’s civil law lowers the cost of justice for the weaker party.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
