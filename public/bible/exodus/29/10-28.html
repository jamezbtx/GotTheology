Passage

Exodus 29

Verses 10–28 · WEB

Three animals are offered in sequence. The bull is a sin offering, the first ram a whole burnt offering, and the second ram a ram of consecration, whose blood marks the priests’ right ear, thumb, and big toe. Portions are waved before the LORD, burned on the altar, or given to the priests as their share. Both traditions see here atonement, total dedication, and fellowship with God, and both read the details as preparing for the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ described in Hebrews. Interpreters differ on how far to press individual details as types, and the notes avoid treating such readings as settled.

Exodus 29:10

10“You shall bring the bull before the Tent of Meeting; and Aaron and his sons shall lay their hands on the head of the bull.

Arminian

Aaron and his sons lay their hands on the head of the bull. The gesture identifies them with the animal about to be offered.

Arminian readers see in the laying on of hands an act of identification and confession. The priests acknowledge that they need atonement.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see the gesture as signifying substitution or transfer, with the animal standing in the place of the offerers.

Both traditions agree that the priests who will offer for others must first be offered for, a point Hebrews makes about Christ’s superiority (Hebrews 7:27).

Exodus 29:11

11You shall kill the bull before the LORD at the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

The bull is killed before the LORD at the door of the Tent of Meeting. The death takes place in God’s presence.

Arminian readers note the gravity of sin. Approaching God requires a costly sacrifice that God has provided.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the sacrifice is offered before the LORD, to God. Atonement deals first with God’s holy response to sin.

Both traditions affirm that without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness (Hebrews 9:22), and that the animal sacrifices could not themselves take away sin (Hebrews 10:4).

Exodus 29:12

12You shall take of the blood of the bull, and put it on the horns of the altar with your finger; and you shall pour out all the blood at the base of the altar.

Arminian

Blood is placed on the altar’s horns and the rest poured at its base. The altar itself is involved in the cleansing.

Arminian readers note that the altar, the place of meeting, is purified by blood. Sin defiles even the means of worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that blood applied to the altar shows how fully sin affects the relationship between God and his people.

Both traditions see the use of blood as pointing to the cleansing that Hebrews 9:13–14 ascribes to the blood of Christ.

Exodus 29:13

13You shall take all the fat that covers the innards, the cover of the liver, the two kidneys, and the fat that is on them, and burn them on the altar.

Arminian

The fat covering the innards, the liver covering, and the kidneys with their fat are burned on the altar. These portions belong to God.

Arminian readers note that the choicest parts are given to God. Worship gives him what is best.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s portion is specified, not left to the offerer’s choice. The rite reflects God’s claim on the whole offering.

Both traditions avoid speculating on why these organs were chosen, beyond the general principle that the fat belongs to the LORD (compare Leviticus 3:16).

Exodus 29:14

14But the meat of the bull, and its skin, and its dung, you shall burn with fire outside of the camp. It is a sin offering.

Arminian

The bull’s meat, skin, and dung are burned outside the camp. It is a sin offering.

Arminian readers note that the remains of the sin offering are removed from the camp. Sin is not allowed to stay among God’s people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Hebrews 13:11–12 connects bodies burned outside the camp with Jesus suffering outside the city gate.

Both traditions share this New Testament reading, while recognizing that Exodus itself presents the rule as part of the sin offering.

Exodus 29:15

15“You shall also take the one ram, and Aaron and his sons shall lay their hands on the head of the ram.

Arminian

The first ram is presented, and Aaron and his sons lay their hands on its head. The sequence moves from sin offering to burnt offering.

Arminian readers note the progression: after atonement comes dedication. The priests give themselves wholly to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the order of offerings matters. Sin is dealt with before total dedication is offered.

Both traditions recognize that consecration rests on atonement and is not a substitute for it.

Exodus 29:16

16You shall kill the ram, and you shall take its blood, and sprinkle it around on the altar.

Arminian

The ram is killed and its blood sprinkled around the altar. The altar is surrounded with the sign of life given up.

Arminian readers see repeated blood as emphasizing the seriousness of approaching God. Each offering carries this solemn mark.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the repetition of blood in each offering. Every stage of approach rests on sacrifice.

Both traditions read the blood rites as preparing for the single sacrifice of Christ (Hebrews 10:12).

Exodus 29:17

17You shall cut the ram into its pieces, and wash its innards, and its legs, and put them with its pieces, and with its head.

Arminian

The ram is cut up, its innards and legs washed, and all the pieces arranged with the head. Nothing is to be offered unclean.

Arminian readers note the care for purity in what is offered. God is honored by attention to his instructions.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the whole animal is prepared for the altar. Nothing is held back from God.

Both traditions read the washing as part of the purity required in worship, without building further symbolism on it.

Exodus 29:18

18You shall burn the whole ram on the altar: it is a burnt offering to the LORD; it is a pleasant aroma, an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

The whole ram is burned on the altar, a burnt offering and a pleasant aroma to the LORD. The entire animal goes up to God.

Arminian readers see the burnt offering as total consecration. The priests give themselves wholly to God’s service.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the burnt offering is pleasing to God because he appointed it. Ephesians 5:2 speaks of Christ’s self-giving as a fragrant offering to God.

Both traditions affirm that Christ’s wholehearted obedience fulfills what the burnt offering pictured.

Exodus 29:19

19“You shall take the other ram, and Aaron and his sons shall lay their hands on the head of the ram.

Arminian

The second ram is presented with the same laying on of hands. This ram will be the ram of consecration.

Arminian readers note that the priests again identify with the offering. Consecration is personal.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the repetition of the gesture with each animal. The priests must be identified with every stage of the rite.

Both traditions see the whole sequence as one act of consecration in three parts.

Exodus 29:20

20Then you shall kill the ram, and take some of its blood, and put it on the tip of the right ear of Aaron, and on the tip of the right ear of his sons, and on the thumb of their right hand, and on the big toe of their right foot; and sprinkle the blood around on the altar.

Arminian

Blood from the second ram is placed on the right ear, right thumb, and right big toe of Aaron and his sons. The same pattern appears in the cleansing of a leper (Leviticus 14:14).

Arminian readers often read the marks as consecrating the priests’ hearing, doing, and walking to God. They hold this as a reasonable application rather than a stated meaning.

Reformed

Reformed readers also commonly see the whole person set apart, from ear to hand to foot. They likewise recognize that the text does not explain the symbolism.

Both traditions treat this interpretation as widely held but not stated in the passage.

Exodus 29:21

21You shall take of the blood that is on the altar, and of the anointing oil, and sprinkle it on Aaron, and on his garments, and on his sons, and on the garments of his sons with him: and he shall be made holy, and his garments, and his sons, and his sons’ garments with him.

Arminian

Blood from the altar and anointing oil are sprinkled on Aaron, his sons, and their garments. They and their garments are made holy.

Arminian readers note that blood and oil together consecrate the priests. Atonement and empowering belong together.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priests are made holy by something applied to them. Their holiness comes from outside themselves.

Both traditions see in the joining of blood and oil a pattern often connected with Christ’s sacrifice and the gift of the Spirit, while holding that connection as an inference.

Exodus 29:22

22Also you shall take some of the ram’s fat, the fat tail, the fat that covers the innards, the cover of the liver, the two kidneys, the fat that is on them, and the right thigh (for it is a ram of consecration),

Arminian

Fat portions and the right thigh are taken from the ram of consecration. The text explains that this is the ram of consecration.

Arminian readers note that specific portions are set apart for a specific purpose. Consecration involves careful distinction.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the right thigh is singled out here, as elsewhere in priestly regulations (compare Leviticus 7:32–34).

Both traditions read this as part of the detailed ritual order and avoid forcing a doctrinal contrast on it.

Exodus 29:23

23and one loaf of bread, one cake of oiled bread, and one wafer out of the basket of unleavened bread that is before the LORD.

Arminian

One loaf, one oiled cake, and one wafer are taken from the basket. The bread joins the meat in the offering.

Arminian readers note that bread and meat are offered together. Thanksgiving and consecration belong side by side.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the bread is “before the LORD.” Even ordinary food is set apart when it is offered to God.

Both traditions see the inclusion of bread as part of the rite’s completeness.

Exodus 29:24

24You shall put all of this in Aaron’s hands, and in his sons’ hands, and shall wave them for a wave offering before the LORD.

Arminian

The portions are placed in the priests’ hands and waved before the LORD. The wave offering presents the gift to God.

Arminian readers note that the priests themselves hold the offering. Their hands, now consecrated, are filled with what is offered to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that many interpreters connect this with the Hebrew idiom of “filling the hands” for ordination. They hold that connection with appropriate caution.

Both traditions read the waving as a public presentation to God.

Exodus 29:25

25You shall take them from their hands, and burn them on the altar on the burnt offering, for a pleasant aroma before the LORD: it is an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

The portions are taken back and burned on the altar with the burnt offering, as a pleasant aroma to the LORD.

Arminian readers note that what the priests held is given wholly to God. Consecration ends in surrender.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the offering is accepted because it follows God’s command. Its pleasing aroma rests on his appointment.

Both traditions see the language of pleasant aroma as pointing to God’s acceptance of the offering he prescribed.

Exodus 29:26

26“You shall take the breast of Aaron’s ram of consecration, and wave it for a wave offering before the LORD. It shall be your portion.

Arminian

The breast of the ram is waved before the LORD and becomes Moses’ portion. Moses acts as the officiant during the ordination.

Arminian readers note that the one who serves receives a share. God provides for those who minister.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Moses functions in a priestly role during this unique occasion. The ordination is a founding act.

Both traditions recognize Moses’ special role in establishing the priesthood.

Exodus 29:27

27You shall sanctify the breast of the wave offering and the thigh of the wave offering, which is waved, and which is raised up, of the ram of consecration, even of that which is for Aaron, and of that which is for his sons.

Arminian

The breast and thigh of the wave offering are sanctified. These are set apart for Aaron and his sons.

Arminian readers note that God provides for the priests through the offerings. Their needs are met as they serve.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priests’ portion is itself holy. What they eat has been dedicated to God.

Both traditions see here a principle later applied to those who serve in ministry (1 Corinthians 9:13).

Exodus 29:28

28It shall be for Aaron and his sons as their portion forever from the children of Israel; for it is a wave offering. It shall be a wave offering from the children of Israel of the sacrifices of their peace offerings, even their wave offering to the LORD.

Arminian

The breast and thigh become the priests’ perpetual portion from Israel’s peace offerings. The arrangement is lasting within the covenant.

Arminian readers note that the people’s offerings support the priests. Worship includes care for those who serve.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priests’ portion is assigned by statute. Their provision is God’s ordinance, not a matter of the people’s goodwill alone.

Both traditions read the peace offering as expressing fellowship between God, priests, and people.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
