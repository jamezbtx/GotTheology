Passage

Exodus 29

Verses 38–46 · WEB

The LORD appoints a continual burnt offering of two lambs, one in the morning and one at evening, with flour, oil, and wine. At this place of offering the LORD promises to meet with Israel, to sanctify the tent, altar, and priests by his glory, and to dwell among his people as their God. Verses 45–46 state the purpose of the exodus itself: God brought Israel out of Egypt so that he might dwell among them. This is broad shared ground for both traditions, which see the promise carried forward in Christ (John 1:14) and in the final dwelling of God with his people (Revelation 21:3).

Exodus 29:38

38“Now this is that which you shall offer on the altar: two lambs a year old day by day continually.

Arminian

Two year-old lambs are to be offered every day continually. Worship is ongoing, not occasional.

Arminian readers note that continual offering shows a continual relationship. God’s people are always before him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the continual offering is prescribed by God, not left to the people’s initiative. It is repeated in Numbers 28:3–8.

Both traditions see the daily rhythm as shaping Israel’s life around God’s presence.

Exodus 29:39

39The one lamb you shall offer in the morning; and the other lamb you shall offer at evening;

Arminian

One lamb is offered in the morning and the other at evening. The day is framed by sacrifice.

Arminian readers note that each day begins and ends with an offering. The whole day belongs to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the steady order that God gives to worship. Israel’s day rests on his appointed provision.

Both traditions draw pastoral application from the morning and evening rhythm for daily devotion.

Exodus 29:40

40and with the one lamb a tenth part of an ephah of fine flour mixed with the fourth part of a hin of beaten oil, and the fourth part of a hin of wine for a drink offering.

Arminian

With the morning lamb come a tenth of an ephah of fine flour mixed with a fourth of a hin of beaten oil, and a fourth of a hin of wine. The WEB footnotes give approximate modern equivalents.

Arminian readers note that the offering includes ordinary staples. Daily life is brought into worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the precision of the measures, which reflects God’s ordering of worship. The exact modern equivalents of these ancient measures are approximate.

Both traditions read the grain and drink offerings as accompanying the burnt offering.

Exodus 29:41

41The other lamb you shall offer at evening, and shall do to it according to the meal offering of the morning and according to its drink offering, for a pleasant aroma, an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian

The evening lamb is offered in the same way as the morning lamb, a pleasant aroma and an offering made by fire to the LORD.

Arminian readers note the consistency of morning and evening worship. God is honored with the same care at the end of the day as at the beginning.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the pattern of repetition, with the same offering twice daily. God receives it as he appointed it.

Both traditions see the language of pleasant aroma as expressing God’s acceptance of the worship he commanded.

Exodus 29:42

42It shall be a continual burnt offering throughout your generations at the door of the Tent of Meeting before the LORD, where I will meet with you, to speak there to you.

Arminian

This continual burnt offering is to be made throughout Israel’s generations at the door of the Tent of Meeting, where God will meet and speak with them.

Arminian readers note that the place of sacrifice is also the place of meeting. God’s purpose is relationship and communication.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God meets his people at the place of sacrifice. Access to him comes by the way he provides.

Both traditions agree that sacrifice serves the deeper goal of fellowship with God.

Exodus 29:43

43There I will meet with the children of Israel; and the place shall be sanctified by my glory.

Arminian

There God will meet with Israel, and the place will be sanctified by his glory. The place is holy because God is present.

Arminian readers note that God’s glory makes the place holy. His presence is a gift to his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that holiness comes from God’s glory, not from the materials or the rites themselves.

Both traditions see the glory of God dwelling with his people as the heart of the tabernacle, fulfilled in Christ (John 1:14).

Exodus 29:44

44I will sanctify the Tent of Meeting and the altar. I will also sanctify Aaron and his sons to minister to me in the priest’s office.

Arminian

God will sanctify the Tent, the altar, and Aaron and his sons for priestly service. He is the one who makes them holy.

Arminian readers note that the consecration rites are God’s own work. Human obedience responds to what God does.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the emphasis on “I will sanctify.” The rites carried out by Moses are means through which God himself acts.

Both traditions agree that holiness is God’s gift and work, received through his appointed means.

Exodus 29:45

45I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will be their God.

Arminian

God will dwell among the children of Israel and be their God. This is the goal of the tabernacle (compare 25:8).

Arminian readers rejoice in God’s desire to live among his people. He is not distant but near.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s presence among his people fulfills his covenant promise. The words echo Leviticus 26:11–12 and anticipate Revelation 21:3.

Both traditions treat this as one of the central promises of Scripture.

Exodus 29:46

46They shall know that I am the LORD their God, who brought them out of the land of Egypt, that I might dwell among them: I am the LORD their God.

Arminian

They will know that he is the LORD their God, who brought them out of Egypt so that he might dwell among them. The exodus served this purpose.

Arminian readers note that deliverance leads to relationship. God saves his people in order to live with them.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s purpose in redemption is his own presence among his people. The LORD’s name frames the verse at both ends.

Both traditions agree that redemption aims at communion with God, a theme fulfilled in Christ and the gift of the Spirit.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
