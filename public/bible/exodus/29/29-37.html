Passage

Exodus 29

Verses 29–37 · WEB

The holy garments pass to Aaron’s successors, and the consecration meal is eaten by the priests in a holy place. The ordination lasts seven days, with a daily sin offering that also makes atonement for the altar, so that the altar becomes most holy. Both traditions read this as showing that both people and place must be made holy by God’s appointed means before ministry begins. They share the conviction that these repeated rites point beyond themselves to a priesthood and sacrifice that would not need repeating (Hebrews 7:27; 10:11–12).

Exodus 29:29

29“The holy garments of Aaron shall be for his sons after him, to be anointed in them, and to be consecrated in them.

Arminian

Aaron’s holy garments pass to his sons after him for their anointing and consecration. The office continues beyond one man.

Arminian readers note that the priesthood is passed on through generations. God provides continuity for his people’s worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the need for successors reveals the limits of the Aaronic priesthood, a point Hebrews 7:23–24 makes when contrasting it with Christ’s permanent priesthood.

Both traditions see Christ as the high priest who needs no successor.

Exodus 29:30

30Seven days shall the son who is priest in his place put them on, when he comes into the Tent of Meeting to minister in the holy place.

Arminian

The son who succeeds as priest wears the garments for seven days when he enters to minister. Succession is marked by a solemn period.

Arminian readers note that each new priest is consecrated with care. Ministry is never taken lightly.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the seven days mirror the original ordination. Each successor receives the office in the same God-given way.

Both traditions read the seven days as a full and complete period of consecration.

Exodus 29:31

31“You shall take the ram of consecration and boil its meat in a holy place.

Arminian

The meat of the ram of consecration is boiled in a holy place. The meal is prepared with holiness in view.

Arminian readers note that even the cooking of the meal happens in a holy place. Fellowship with God is not casual.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the location is specified. The meal belongs to the sphere of what is set apart for God.

Both traditions see the consecration ending in a shared meal before the LORD.

Exodus 29:32

32Aaron and his sons shall eat the meat of the ram, and the bread that is in the basket, at the door of the Tent of Meeting.

Arminian

Aaron and his sons eat the meat and the bread at the door of the Tent of Meeting. The rite ends in a meal.

Arminian readers note the fellowship expressed in eating. Those who are consecrated enjoy communion with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priests eat what was offered for their consecration. They partake of what God provided.

Both traditions see in this meal a picture of fellowship with God that many Christians connect with the Lord’s Supper, though Exodus does not draw that link.

Exodus 29:33

33They shall eat those things with which atonement was made, to consecrate and sanctify them; but a stranger shall not eat of it, because they are holy.

Arminian

They eat what was used to make atonement, to consecrate and sanctify them, and no stranger may eat it because it is holy.

Arminian readers note that the meal is limited to those consecrated for this office. The restriction protects what God has made holy.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that atonement here is linked to consecration. The priests are set apart through what was offered for them.

Both traditions read “stranger” here as anyone outside the priestly family, not as a judgment on foreigners as such.

Exodus 29:34

34If anything of the meat of the consecration, or of the bread, remains to the morning, then you shall burn the remainder with fire. It shall not be eaten, because it is holy.

Arminian

Any leftover meat or bread must be burned in the morning. It cannot be eaten because it is holy.

Arminian readers note that holy things must be handled as God directs. Even leftovers are treated with reverence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that holiness places limits on ordinary use. What has been offered is not returned to common use.

Both traditions see here the seriousness of the distinction between holy and common.

Exodus 29:35

35“You shall do so to Aaron and to his sons, according to all that I have commanded you. You shall consecrate them seven days.

Arminian

Moses must do all this to Aaron and his sons for seven days, according to all God commanded. Obedience must be complete.

Arminian readers note that God’s instructions are to be followed fully. Partial obedience is not enough.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the rite is defined entirely by God’s command. Nothing is to be added or omitted.

Both traditions see the seven days as a complete consecration, carried out in Leviticus 8:33–35.

Exodus 29:36

36Every day you shall offer the bull of sin offering for atonement. You shall cleanse the altar when you make atonement for it. You shall anoint it, to sanctify it.

Arminian

A bull of sin offering is offered daily for atonement, and the altar is cleansed and anointed. Even the altar needs atonement.

Arminian readers note that the altar must be purified because it stands among a sinful people. The provision of atonement is renewed daily.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that daily repetition shows that these offerings could not finally remove sin (Hebrews 10:1–4, 11). They point to a sacrifice offered once for all.

Both traditions agree that the repetition of animal sacrifice prepares for the sufficiency of Christ’s single offering.

Exodus 29:37

37Seven days you shall make atonement for the altar, and sanctify it; and the altar shall be most holy. Whatever touches the altar shall be holy.

Arminian

For seven days the altar is atoned for and sanctified, and it becomes most holy. Whatever touches the altar becomes holy.

Arminian readers note that the altar becomes a place of intense holiness. Approaching it is serious.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the phrase “whatever touches” is debated: some read it as conferring holiness, others as meaning that whatever touches it must already be holy. Haggai 2:11–13 is often discussed alongside it.

Both traditions hold the meaning of this clause with some caution.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
