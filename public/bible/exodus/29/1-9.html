Passage

Exodus 29

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Having described the priestly garments in chapter 28, the LORD now gives Moses the rite for consecrating Aaron and his sons. Moses is to gather a bull, two rams, and unleavened bread, then wash, clothe, and anoint the priests at the door of the Tent of Meeting. The rite is carried out in Leviticus 8. Both traditions read the passage as God himself providing the way for sinful people to serve near his holy presence, and both see its fulfillment in Christ, the great high priest of Hebrews 7–10.

Exodus 29:1

1“This is the thing that you shall do to them to make them holy, to minister to me in the priest’s office: take one young bull and two rams without defect,

Arminian

The rite of consecration aims “to make them holy, to minister to me.” The first requirement is a young bull and two rams without defect.

Arminian readers note that God himself provides the way for priests to serve him. Holiness is a gift received through God’s appointed means, and it calls for a life of willing service.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the priests cannot consecrate themselves. God prescribes every element, and the animals without defect point to the need for a perfect offering.

Both traditions see Christ as the unblemished high priest and sacrifice in whom this pattern is fulfilled (Hebrews 7:26–27).

Exodus 29:2

2unleavened bread, unleavened cakes mixed with oil, and unleavened wafers anointed with oil. You shall make them of fine wheat flour.

Arminian

Unleavened bread, cakes mixed with oil, and wafers anointed with oil are prepared from fine wheat flour. These will be part of the offerings and the priests’ meal.

Arminian readers note the care and quality required. What is offered to God should be the best, not leftovers.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the bread will be shared before the LORD and by the priests, joining consecration and fellowship.

Both traditions avoid pressing every ingredient into a symbol, while recognizing that the use of unleavened bread recalls the exodus meal.

Exodus 29:3

3You shall put them into one basket, and bring them in the basket, with the bull and the two rams.

Arminian

Everything is placed in one basket and brought with the bull and the rams. The offerings are presented together as a single act of consecration.

Arminian readers see the orderly preparation as an act of obedience. Approaching God involves following his instructions rather than inventing one’s own worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the regulating word of God governs worship in detail. The priests come with what God commanded, not with what seemed fitting to them.

Both traditions agree that the order and seriousness of the rite reflect the holiness of the God being approached.

Exodus 29:4

4You shall bring Aaron and his sons to the door of the Tent of Meeting, and shall wash them with water.

Arminian

Aaron and his sons are brought to the door of the Tent of Meeting and washed with water. Cleansing comes before clothing and anointing.

Arminian readers note that the priests must be cleansed before they can serve. God provides purification for those he calls.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priests are washed by Moses, not by themselves. Their cleansing is received rather than achieved.

Both traditions recognize the priority of cleansing in drawing near to God, a theme the New Testament takes up in speaking of hearts and bodies washed (Hebrews 10:22).

Exodus 29:5

5You shall take the garments, and put on Aaron the tunic, the robe of the ephod, the ephod, and the breastplate, and clothe him with the skillfully woven band of the ephod.

Arminian

Aaron is clothed in the tunic, robe, ephod, breastplate, and woven band described in chapter 28. These garments mark him as Israel’s high priest.

Arminian readers note that Aaron serves as a representative before God. The garments show that his role is given to him, not seized.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the garments are made by God’s design and put on Aaron by Moses. His office is conferred, not self-appointed (compare Hebrews 5:4).

Both traditions see the high priest’s representative role fulfilled in Christ, who intercedes for his people.

Exodus 29:6

6You shall set the turban on his head, and put the holy crown on the turban.

Arminian

The turban is placed on Aaron’s head, and on it the holy crown. Chapter 28 describes a gold plate engraved “HOLY TO THE LORD” fixed to the turban (28:36–37).

Arminian readers note that holiness is displayed on the high priest’s head as he represents the people. He bears their need before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the high priest’s holiness is symbolic and borrowed. It points to a holiness he does not possess in himself.

Both traditions see in Christ the one who truly is holy and who bears his people before God.

Exodus 29:7

7Then you shall take the anointing oil, and pour it on his head, and anoint him.

Arminian

The anointing oil is poured on Aaron’s head. Anointing sets him apart for his office.

Arminian readers see the anointing as God’s empowering for service. Those whom God calls, he also equips.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that “Messiah” and “Christ” both mean “anointed one.” The anointing of priests helps shape the expectation that the New Testament sees fulfilled in Jesus.

Both traditions agree that the oil marks Aaron as set apart by God rather than by human choice.

Exodus 29:8

8You shall bring his sons, and put tunics on them.

Arminian

Aaron’s sons are brought and clothed in tunics. They share in the priestly service under Aaron.

Arminian readers note that ministry is shared. God calls a household of priests, not a single individual working alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the sons serve under the high priest and in dependence on his office. Their role derives from his.

Both traditions read this as part of an ordered priesthood that the New Testament fulfills in Christ and applies to his people (1 Peter 2:9).

Exodus 29:9

9You shall clothe them with belts, Aaron and his sons, and bind headbands on them. They shall have the priesthood by a perpetual statute. You shall consecrate Aaron and his sons.

Arminian

The sons receive belts and headbands. The priesthood is theirs “by a perpetual statute,” and Moses is to consecrate them.

Arminian readers note that the priesthood is given as an enduring arrangement within Israel’s covenant. Hebrews later explains that a new priesthood has come in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that “perpetual” must be read in light of the covenant it belongs to. Hebrews 7:11–12 teaches that a change of priesthood has taken place in Christ.

Both traditions agree that this permanence is fulfilled, not cancelled, in Christ’s permanent priesthood (Hebrews 7:23–25).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
