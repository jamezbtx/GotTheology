Passage

Exodus 27

Verses 9–21 · WEB

The instructions continue with the courtyard that enclosed the tabernacle: linen hangings on bronze-socketed pillars with silver hooks and bands, forming a rectangle of one hundred by fifty cubits. A single embroidered screen on the east side provided the entrance. The chapter closes with the command to bring pure olive oil so that a lamp burns continually before the LORD, tended by Aaron and his sons.

Exodus 27:9

9“You shall make the court of the tabernacle: for the south side southward there shall be hangings for the court of fine twined linen one hundred cubits long for one side.

Arminian

The court is enclosed by linen hangings one hundred cubits long on the south side. Arminian readers see a boundary that both separates the holy from the common and marks a place where Israel may come to worship. Both traditions agree the court defines sacred space around God’s dwelling.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the court creates graded holiness: court, holy place, and most holy place. The enclosure teaches that God is approachable but not casually. Shared ground: the fine twined linen and careful measurements mark this space as set apart for the LORD.

Exodus 27:10

10Its pillars shall be twenty, and their sockets twenty, of bronze. The hooks of the pillars and their fillets shall be of silver.

Arminian

Twenty pillars stand in bronze sockets, with silver hooks and fillets. Arminian readers see the materials as honoring God’s dwelling even at its outer edge. Both traditions agree that the combination of metals reflects the overall design, with more precious materials generally used closer to the center of the sanctuary.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the orderly structure of the court, built from the offerings of the people (25:2–7). The materials grade toward greater value nearer God’s presence. Both traditions agree that interpreters should be cautious about assigning fixed symbolic meanings to each metal, since the text does not explain them.

Exodus 27:11

11Likewise for the length of the north side, there shall be hangings one hundred cubits long, and its pillars twenty, and their sockets twenty, of bronze; the hooks of the pillars, and their fillets, of silver.

Arminian

The north side mirrors the south: one hundred cubits of hangings, twenty pillars, bronze sockets, silver hooks and fillets. Arminian readers note the symmetry of the design. Both traditions agree that the repetition reinforces the order and completeness of the sanctuary God commands.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the symmetry a reflection of God’s orderliness; his house is not haphazard. The repetition is typical of the tabernacle chapters. Shared ground: careful correspondence of the sides underlines that the court is built exactly as God directs.

Exodus 27:12

12For the width of the court on the west side shall be hangings of fifty cubits; their pillars ten, and their sockets ten.

Arminian

The west side is fifty cubits wide, with ten pillars and ten sockets. This is the back of the court, behind the tent. Arminian readers note that the court is twice as long as it is wide. Both traditions agree that the dimensions define a substantial but bounded space for worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the west end stands behind the most holy place, since the entrance faces east. The proportions reflect careful design. Both traditions agree that the layout guides the worshiper from the entrance toward the presence of God in an ordered progression.

Exodus 27:13

13The width of the court on the east side eastward shall be fifty cubits.

Arminian

The east side is also fifty cubits, but it contains the entrance. Arminian readers see significance in the single way in, open to Israel through God’s appointed means. Both traditions note that entering from the east echoes other biblical patterns of approach to God’s presence, though the text itself does not comment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the entrance on the east establishes a single path of approach. Some see a connection to Eden, where the cherubim guarded the east entrance (Genesis 3:24), but this is an inference, not stated here. Shared ground: there is one God-appointed way into the court.

Exodus 27:14

14The hangings for the one side of the gate shall be fifteen cubits; their pillars three, and their sockets three.

Arminian

On one side of the gate are hangings of fifteen cubits with three pillars and sockets. Arminian readers note that the walls frame the opening, guiding worshipers toward the entrance. Both traditions agree that these measurements form part of the overall plan for an ordered, accessible sanctuary.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the flanking hangings define the gateway and its proportions. Each detail fits the whole. Both traditions agree that the eastern façade combines enclosure and invitation, and that the precise figures emphasize obedience rather than carrying hidden meaning.

Exodus 27:15

15For the other side shall be hangings of fifteen cubits; their pillars three, and their sockets three.

Arminian

The other side of the gate mirrors the first with fifteen cubits, three pillars, and three sockets. Arminian readers note the balance of the entrance. Both traditions agree that the symmetry reflects careful design, leaving a twenty-cubit opening for the gate itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the balanced frame around the gate as consistent with the orderly design of the whole sanctuary. Shared ground: the fifteen-cubit sections on each side leave room for the central screen, so that the court’s single entrance is clearly marked.

Exodus 27:16

16For the gate of the court shall be a screen of twenty cubits, of blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine twined linen, the work of the embroiderer; their pillars four, and their sockets four.

Arminian

The gate is a twenty-cubit embroidered screen of blue, purple, scarlet, and fine linen, matching the colors of the tent’s inner curtains. Arminian readers see beauty at the entrance as an invitation to approach. Both traditions agree that the gate’s richness distinguishes it from the plain linen walls.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the gate shares the colors used in the holy place, linking the entrance with the inner sanctuary. Many in both traditions see the one gate as foreshadowing Christ as the way to God (compare John 10:9; 14:6), though this is a typological reading, not stated in Exodus.

Exodus 27:17

17All the pillars of the court around shall be filleted with silver; their hooks of silver, and their sockets of bronze.

Arminian

All the court’s pillars are banded with silver, with silver hooks and bronze sockets. Arminian readers see a consistent pattern of honor throughout the enclosure. Both traditions agree that the uniformity of the pillars reinforces the unity and order of God’s dwelling place.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the summary verse confirms the materials used all around the court. The pillars form a single unified enclosure. Shared ground: these details show the craftsmanship and generosity invested in the place where God chose to dwell among Israel.

Exodus 27:18

18The length of the court shall be one hundred cubits, and the width fifty throughout, and the height five cubits, of fine twined linen, and their sockets of bronze.

Arminian

The court’s overall dimensions are summarized: one hundred by fifty cubits, five cubits high, with linen hangings and bronze sockets. Arminian readers note that the walls were low enough to suggest separation without hiding the tent entirely. Both traditions agree that the summary emphasizes completeness and precision.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the summary as reinforcing the boundaries God set for his dwelling. The court is spacious yet clearly defined. Both traditions agree that the hangings mark holy space, and that the measurements, while precise, should not be pressed for hidden numerical meanings the text does not provide.

Exodus 27:19

19All the instruments of the tabernacle in all its service, and all its pins, and all the pins of the court, shall be of bronze.

Arminian

All the tabernacle’s tools and pins are to be bronze. Arminian readers note that even tent pegs belong to God’s service. Both traditions agree that nothing used in the sanctuary is too small to be included in God’s instructions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the inclusion of pins and tools as evidence that God’s ordering of worship extends to every detail. Shared ground: the bronze implements of the court belong to the sanctuary’s service, distinguishing the outer area, with its bronze, from the gold of the inner rooms.

Exodus 27:20

20“You shall command the children of Israel, that they bring to you pure olive oil beaten for the light, to cause a lamp to burn continually.

Arminian

Israel must bring pure beaten olive oil for a lamp that burns continually. Arminian readers stress the people’s participation: their offerings sustain the light of worship. Both traditions agree that the continual lamp signifies ongoing service before God and the people’s shared responsibility to maintain it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God commands the offering and ordains its purpose, while the people truly bring it. The pure beaten oil reflects the quality God requires. Both traditions agree that the perpetual light symbolizes God’s continuing presence and Israel’s continual worship.

Exodus 27:21

21In the Tent of Meeting, outside the veil which is before the covenant, Aaron and his sons shall keep it in order from evening to morning before the LORD: it shall be a statute forever throughout their generations on the behalf of the children of Israel.

Arminian

Aaron and his sons tend the lamp in the Tent of Meeting outside the veil, from evening to morning, as a perpetual statute for Israel. Arminian readers see priestly service done on behalf of the whole people. Both traditions agree that the verse introduces the priesthood described in chapter 28.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priests serve “on the behalf of the children of Israel,” a representative role developed in chapter 28. The statute is lasting for the Mosaic order. Both traditions agree that Hebrews reads the Levitical priesthood as fulfilled in Christ, though Hebrews mentions the lampstand only briefly (9:2).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
