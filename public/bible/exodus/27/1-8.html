Passage

Exodus 27

Verses 1–8 · WEB

Within the long instructions God gives Moses on the mountain (Exodus 25–31), chapter 27 moves from the tent itself to the courtyard, beginning with the altar of burnt offering. The altar is a portable, bronze-overlaid box of acacia wood with horns at its corners, a grating, and carrying poles. It stood in the court, the first object a worshiper approached, and it was to be made according to the pattern shown on the mountain.

Exodus 27:1

1“You shall make the altar of acacia wood, five cubits long, and five cubits wide. The altar shall be square. Its height shall be three cubits.

Arminian

The altar of burnt offering is the largest object in the court, about seven and a half feet square according to the WEB footnotes on the cubit. Arminian readers see here God’s provision of a way for sinful people to draw near through sacrifice. Both traditions agree that access to God is on his terms, not human invention.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the altar stands at the entrance: no one approaches God’s dwelling except by way of sacrifice. The measurements, explained in the WEB footnotes, stress careful obedience to God’s design. Both traditions read the altar as pointing forward, in light of Hebrews, to the once-for-all sacrifice of Christ.

Exodus 27:2

2You shall make its horns on its four corners. Its horns shall be of one piece with it. You shall overlay it with bronze.

Arminian

The horns at the corners are part of the altar itself and are overlaid with bronze. In later texts the horns receive sacrificial blood and serve as a place of refuge (Leviticus 4:25; 1 Kings 1:50). Arminian readers see a picture of mercy offered to those who flee to God. Both traditions treat the horns as signs of the altar’s power and holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the horns are later smeared with atoning blood (Leviticus 4:25), linking strength and refuge to sacrifice. Bronze, a durable metal, fits an altar exposed to fire. Both traditions agree that the significance of the horns comes from God’s appointment, not from any power in the object itself.

Exodus 27:3

3You shall make its pots to take away its ashes; and its shovels, its basins, its meat hooks, and its fire pans. You shall make all its vessels of bronze.

Arminian

The altar’s tools are listed: pots for ashes, shovels, basins, meat hooks, and fire pans, all of bronze. Arminian readers see the daily, practical labor of worship that sustained Israel’s relationship with God. Both traditions agree that even humble implements share in the holiness of the altar’s service.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that worship is ordered down to its humblest utensils; nothing is left to improvisation. The basins for blood point to the central place of atonement in the system. Shared ground: God cares about how he is approached, and service at his altar involves real, sometimes unglamorous work.

Exodus 27:4

4You shall make a grating for it of network of bronze. On the net you shall make four bronze rings in its four corners.

Arminian

A bronze grating with rings at its corners forms part of the altar’s structure. Its exact placement and function are not fully clear from the text, and interpreters differ on the details. Arminian readers, like Reformed readers, focus less on reconstruction than on the care with which God’s dwelling is built.

Reformed

Reformed readers acknowledge that the grating’s precise design is uncertain. What is clear is that the altar is a carefully built, functional structure for fire and sacrifice. Both traditions agree that details of construction serve the larger point: God provides a sanctioned place where sacrifice can be offered.

Exodus 27:5

5You shall put it under the ledge around the altar beneath, that the net may reach halfway up the altar.

Arminian

The grating sits under the ledge, reaching halfway up the altar. The technical details remain somewhat obscure. Arminian readers note that obedience to God’s pattern extends even to measurements that later readers struggle to visualize. Both traditions avoid building theology on uncertain reconstructions of the ledge and net.

Reformed

Reformed readers similarly treat the ledge and grating as construction details whose exact form is debated. The emphasis is on faithful execution of God’s instructions. Shared ground: the text values precise obedience, and neither tradition needs to settle the engineering to grasp the altar’s role in worship.

Exodus 27:6

6You shall make poles for the altar, poles of acacia wood, and overlay them with bronze.

Arminian

Poles of acacia overlaid with bronze make the altar portable. Arminian readers see a God who travels with his people in the wilderness rather than staying fixed in one place. Both traditions note that the same pattern of poles appears for the ark and the table, marking all these objects as belonging to a moving sanctuary.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the altar, like the other furnishings, is designed for a pilgrim people. God’s provision of sacrifice accompanies Israel on the journey. Both traditions agree that the portability of the tabernacle reflects Israel’s wilderness situation and God’s presence among them as they travel.

Exodus 27:7

7Its poles shall be put into the rings, and the poles shall be on the two sides of the altar when carrying it.

Arminian

The poles go through the rings so the altar can be carried by its sides. Arminian readers see a reminder that holy objects were handled in prescribed ways. Both traditions agree that the instructions guard reverence, since the furnishings were to be moved without casual handling.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the carrying arrangement reflects the holiness of the altar and the order God requires in worship. The narrative later assigns this work to the Levites. Shared ground: the way sacred things are moved is itself a matter of obedience to God’s word.

Exodus 27:8

8You shall make it hollow with planks. They shall make it as it has been shown you on the mountain.

Arminian

The altar is hollow, built of planks, and must follow the pattern shown on the mountain. Arminian readers stress that worship must follow God’s revelation, not human preference. Both traditions highlight the phrase about the pattern, which Hebrews 8:5 cites to describe the tabernacle as a copy and shadow of heavenly realities.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in “as it has been shown you” a principle that God determines how he is worshiped, a theme important in Reformed thinking about worship. Both traditions agree that Hebrews 8:5 reads the tabernacle pattern as a shadow of heavenly things fulfilled in Christ, though they apply that principle with some variation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
