Passage

Exodus 17

Verses 1–7 · WEB

At Rephidim the people find no water, quarrel with Moses, and put the LORD to the test. God has Moses strike the rock at Horeb before the elders, and water flows; the place is named Massah and Meribah because Israel asked whether the LORD was among them. Arminian and Reformed readers share the main lessons here, God’s patient provision and the seriousness of unbelief, and differ mainly in how they apply the later warnings drawn from this scene (Ps 95; 1 Cor 10; Heb 3).

Exodus 17:1

1All the congregation of the children of Israel traveled from the wilderness of Sin, starting according to the LORD’s commandment, and encamped in Rephidim; but there was no water for the people to drink.

Arminian

Israel moves by the LORD’s commandment and still arrives at a place with no water. Arminian readers stress that obedience does not exempt God’s people from hardship; the dry camp is a real trial of trust, not a sign that God has failed.

The same route that leads to thirst is the route God chose. Faith here means taking the next step without seeing how provision will come, as with the bitter water at Marah and the manna (15:22–25; 16:4).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s leading brings Israel straight to need. Providence directs the journey and the trial alike, so the lack of water is part of how God teaches his people to depend on him (Deut 8:2–3).

The stop at Rephidim follows the LORD’s command, so it is not an accident. Faith learns that God’s leading and our discomfort can belong to the same plan.

Exodus 17:2

2Therefore the people quarreled with Moses, and said, “Give us water to drink.” Moses said to them, “Why do you quarrel with me? Why do you test the LORD?”

Arminian

The people turn their need into a quarrel and make a demand: Give us water. Moses names it as testing the LORD, which means pressing God to prove himself on their terms. Arminian readers see a real choice between asking in trust and demanding in distrust.

Moses separates himself from God: Why quarrel with me? Why test the LORD? Complaint against God’s servant is finally complaint against God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that grumbling against Moses is really unbelief toward God, who sent him. Testing the LORD treats past mercies as if they did not count and requires new proof before trusting (Ps 78:18–20).

The heart’s restlessness shows even in a redeemed people. Grace has brought them out of Egypt, yet it has not yet removed every root of distrust. That is why God’s patience here deserves notice.

Exodus 17:3

3The people were thirsty for water there; so the people murmured against Moses, and said, “Why have you brought us up out of Egypt, to kill us, our children, and our livestock with thirst?”

Arminian

Thirst is real, and the text does not mock it. The sin lies in the conclusion: Moses brought us out to kill us. Arminian readers note how quickly grief can become a hostile story about God’s intentions, one the people are responsible for telling.

The complaint repeats the pattern of 14:11 and 16:3. Habits of distrust are formed by repeated choices, and they can be unlearned by remembering God’s works.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear a familiar charge: the exodus was a death march. Fallen hearts rewrite redemption as harm when present comfort is threatened (Num 14:2–3).

The children and livestock are mentioned because the people fear for their families. The answer is not to scold their need but to show that the God who redeemed them will keep them.

Exodus 17:4

4Moses cried to the LORD, saying, “What shall I do with these people? They are almost ready to stone me.”

Arminian

Moses does not answer the people with anger; he cries to the LORD. Arminian readers see a model of leadership under pressure: bring the crisis to God honestly, even the fear of being stoned.

Moses’ question, What shall I do? admits his limits. Prayer here is not resignation but a request for direction from the God who called him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the mediator turns to God when the people turn against him. Moses’ prayer previews the greater Mediator who bears the hostility of sinners and still intercedes (Heb 12:3; 7:25).

God has appointed prayer as the way his servants receive help. Moses’ honesty, including his fear, is welcome before God.

Exodus 17:5

5The LORD said to Moses, “Walk on before the people, and take the elders of Israel with you, and take the rod in your hand with which you struck the Nile, and go.

Arminian

God tells Moses to go ahead of the people with the elders and the rod that struck the Nile. Arminian readers note that the elders act as witnesses, so the provision is public and accountable rather than private.

The rod that brought judgment on Egypt (7:20) now becomes the instrument of provision for Israel. The same God who judged Pharaoh now meets his people’s thirst.

Reformed

Reformed readers notice that God answers complaint with provision, not destruction. The rod of judgment is taken up for mercy, showing that God’s power serves his covenant purposes.

The elders are included so that leaders will see and testify. God orders the means of his work as carefully as the work itself.

Exodus 17:6

6Behold, I will stand before you there on the rock in Horeb. You shall strike the rock, and water will come out of it, that the people may drink.” Moses did so in the sight of the elders of Israel.

Arminian

God promises to stand before Moses on the rock at Horeb, and water flows when Moses strikes it. Arminian readers, with the wider church, follow Paul in seeing Christ present with Israel: they drank from the spiritual rock that followed them, and the rock was Christ (1 Cor 10:4).

The gift is offered to the whole quarrelsome camp. Grace meets people before they have stopped grumbling, and it calls them to drink and trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear 1 Cor 10:4 here and see the struck rock as a picture of Christ, who is struck so that living water may flow (John 7:37–39). God says he will stand there on the rock, and many readers find in that detail a hint that the blow falls where God has placed himself.

The water comes before any repentance is recorded. Mercy goes first, and gratitude follows from it.

Exodus 17:7

7He called the name of the place Massah, and Meribah, because the children of Israel quarreled, and because they tested the LORD, saying, “Is the LORD among us, or not?”

Arminian

The names Massah (testing) and Meribah (quarreling) keep the sin in Israel’s memory. The question, Is the LORD among us, or not? doubts God’s presence right after the cloud, the sea, and the manna. Arminian readers take later warnings from this place as real appeals to people who could harden their hearts and fall away (Ps 95:7–11; Heb 3:7–12).

Scripture uses this place to teach, not to shame. Do not test the LORD as at Massah (Deut 6:16), a line Jesus quotes in the wilderness (Matt 4:7).

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear the warnings in Ps 95 and Heb 3–4, but they read them as means God uses to keep his people persevering. The wilderness generation included many who did not join the word to faith (Heb 4:2), and Paul calls these events examples for us (1 Cor 10:6, 11).

The place names are a mercy, a memorial that teaches later generations to trust instead of testing. God’s presence was never in doubt; Israel’s faith was.

Key contrast Both read Massah as a solemn warning against unbelief and see Christ as the rock (1 Cor 10:4). Arminian readers take the later warnings from this scene (Heb 3:12) to describe a real possibility that genuine believers could fall away. Reformed readers take the same warnings as God’s appointed means for keeping the elect, and they see those who fell in the wilderness as people who never had true faith (Heb 4:2).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
