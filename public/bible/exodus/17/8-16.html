Passage

Exodus 17

Verses 8–16 · WEB

Amalek attacks Israel at Rephidim; Joshua leads the fighting while Moses stands on the hill with God’s rod, and Israel prevails while his hands are raised, held steady by Aaron and Hur until sunset. God has Moses write a memorial of Amalek’s coming judgment, and Moses builds an altar called “The LORD our Banner.” Both traditions see dependence on God and shared intercession, and they differ mainly in how they relate prayer and means to God’s purpose and how they understand the judgment on Amalek.

Exodus 17:8

8Then Amalek came and fought with Israel in Rephidim.

Arminian

Amalek comes and fights Israel right after the water from the rock. Deuteronomy says Amalek struck the weary stragglers at the rear and did not fear God (Deut 25:17–18). Arminian readers note that the text treats this as Amalek’s own guilty choice.

Provision and attack come close together. Being cared for by God does not mean being free from enemies.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the first armed enemy of the redeemed people. Amalek, descended from Esau (Gen 36:12), attacks the vulnerable, and Deuteronomy calls it a deed of one who did not fear God (Deut 25:18).

God has just shown that he sustains Israel. Now he shows that he also defends them. The Christian life likewise holds provision and conflict together.

Exodus 17:9

9Moses said to Joshua, “Choose men for us, and go out to fight with Amalek. Tomorrow I will stand on the top of the hill with God’s rod in my hand.”

Arminian

Joshua appears here for the first time, chosen to lead the fighting men. Moses will stand on the hilltop with God’s rod. Arminian readers see different callings at work: some fight in the valley, and some stand before God on the hill.

Moses does not choose between prayer and action. Both are commanded, and both are real participation in what God is doing.

Reformed

Reformed readers notice that God gives his people both a commander and an intercessor. Joshua, whose name anticipates Jesus (the same name in Greek), fights; Moses holds up the rod of God.

The rod is called God’s rod because the power is his. The means are real, yet the victory is not credited to human strength.

Exodus 17:10

10So Joshua did as Moses had told him, and fought with Amalek; and Moses, Aaron, and Hur went up to the top of the hill.

Arminian

Joshua obeys and fights, while Moses, Aaron, and Hur go up the hill. Arminian readers note the plain obedience of each person: Joshua does what Moses told him.

Hur appears here and again in 24:14. Leaders on the hill need companions, a theme the next verses make clear.

Reformed

Reformed readers see order in the people: commander, intercessor, and helpers each in place. God works through ordered roles rather than random zeal.

Three men on the hill and an army in the valley form one action of God’s people. Neither group wins without the other.

Exodus 17:11

11When Moses held up his hand, Israel prevailed. When he let down his hand, Amalek prevailed.

Arminian

When Moses’ hand is raised, Israel prevails; when it drops, Amalek prevails. Arminian readers take this as a vivid sign that God responds to his people’s dependence and intercession. Prayer genuinely matters in how events unfold (Jas 5:16).

The raised hand is not magic. It signals reliance on God, and the swings of the battle teach Israel that their strength comes from him.

Reformed

Reformed readers also deny any magic in Moses’ arm. God has appointed intercession as a real means through which he brings about the victory he intends. Ends and means are ordained together, so prayer is never pointless.

The swinging battle shows that the outcome is God’s. Israel learns to look up the hill, beyond the swords, to the God who acts through Moses’ raised hands.

Key contrast Both affirm that God really uses Moses’ raised hands and that the power is God’s, not the gesture’s. Arminian readers stress that God genuinely responds to intercession as events unfold, so the outcome is truly tied to whether his people keep looking to him. Reformed readers stress that God ordained both the victory and the intercession as its means, so prayer is real and effective within his fixed purpose.

Exodus 17:12

12But Moses’ hands were heavy; so they took a stone, and put it under him, and he sat on it. Aaron and Hur held up his hands, the one on the one side, and the other on the other side. His hands were steady until sunset.

Arminian

Moses’ hands grow heavy. Aaron and Hur set a stone under him and hold up his hands, one on each side, until sunset. Arminian readers see a picture of mutual support: even a great intercessor needs others to sustain him (Gal 6:2).

The steadiness comes from shared effort over a long day. Faithfulness often looks like simply staying with it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that even Moses fails in strength, so the victory cannot rest on him. God provides helpers so that his appointed means continue until the battle ends.

The church holds up the hands of its leaders through prayer and practical help. God’s strength is shown when human strength is not enough (2 Cor 12:9).

Exodus 17:13

13Joshua defeated Amalek and his people with the edge of the sword.

Arminian

Joshua defeats Amalek with the sword. Arminian readers note that the victory still takes real effort in battle; God’s help does not replace Joshua’s action.

The fighting and the praying belong together. Both are honored in the outcome.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s victory carried out by Joshua’s sword. Secondary causes are real, and God’s hand is behind them.

The result fits what the raised hands signaled. God keeps his promise to defend his people through the ordinary means of battle.

Exodus 17:14

14The LORD said to Moses, “Write this for a memorial in a book, and rehearse it in the ears of Joshua: that I will utterly blot out the memory of Amalek from under the sky.”

Arminian

God has Moses write a memorial and say it in Joshua’s hearing: he will blot out the memory of Amalek. Arminian readers stress that the judgment answers Amalek’s actual cruelty to the weak and its refusal to fear God (Deut 25:17–19). This concerns a nation’s history under God’s justice, not a statement about any individual’s eternal destiny.

This is the first command in Exodus to write something down. God’s people are to remember his justice and his protection.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the judgment is grounded in Amalek’s sin (Deut 25:18) and is just. They also stress God’s sovereign resolve: he declares beforehand what he will do, and later Scripture follows the thread (Num 24:20; 1 Sam 15:2–3; the Agagite Haman in Esth 3:1).

The written record shows that God’s judgments are not impulsive. He remembers the wrong done to his people and will deal with it in his time.

Key contrast Both read this chiefly as just judgment on a nation for real cruelty, not as a statement about individual salvation. Arminian readers emphasize that the sentence responds to Amalek’s chosen hostility. Reformed readers, while affirming that ground, also emphasize that God declares his settled purpose in advance and carries it out across generations.

Exodus 17:15

15Moses built an altar, and called its name “The LORD our Banner”.

Arminian

Moses builds an altar and names it The LORD our Banner. Arminian readers see worship as the right response to victory: the credit goes to God, and the altar marks the place where Israel learned it.

A banner is a rallying point. Israel’s identity in battle is found in the LORD himself, not in Moses’ rod or Joshua’s skill.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the altar gives God the glory for what the means accomplished. The rod was raised like a standard, but the true banner is the LORD.

Naming the altar turns a battlefield into a place of worship. God’s people remember victories by confessing who gave them (Ps 20:5–7).

Exodus 17:16

16He said, “The LORD has sworn: ‘The LORD will have war with Amalek from generation to generation.’ ”

Arminian

The LORD has sworn to have war with Amalek from generation to generation. Arminian readers take this as a solemn statement of God’s ongoing opposition to Amalek’s hostility, which later generations would face (1 Sam 15).

The Hebrew of the first line is difficult, and translations differ. The main point is clear: Israel’s conflict with Amalek is the LORD’s own.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God binding himself by oath to oppose the enemy of his people. The conflict belongs to the LORD, and his purpose extends beyond one battle.

The wording of the first line is uncertain, so readers should hold any detailed interpretation loosely. The confession that the LORD fights for his people stands firm.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
