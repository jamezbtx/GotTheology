Passage

Exodus 20

Verses 18–26 · WEB

Overwhelmed by the thunder and smoke, the people ask Moses to speak to them in God’s place. Moses explains that God has come to test them and instill reverent fear, then approaches the thick darkness. God reaffirms the ban on idols and gives instructions for simple altars where he will come and bless. Both traditions see mediation, reverent fear, and God-ordered worship as shared ground.

Exodus 20:18

18All the people perceived the thunderings, the lightnings, the sound of the trumpet, and the mountain smoking. When the people saw it, they trembled, and stayed at a distance.

Arminian

The people perceive the thunder, lightning, trumpet, and smoke, and they tremble and stand far off. Arminian readers see fear prompting distance after hearing God’s voice. The experience is overwhelming, and the people recognize their need for a mediator.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sinful people unable to bear the direct presence of God’s holiness. Their withdrawal underscores the need for mediation. Both traditions see the people’s fear as a natural response to the revelation they have witnessed.

Exodus 20:19

19They said to Moses, “Speak with us yourself, and we will listen; but don’t let God speak with us, lest we die.”

Arminian

The people ask Moses to speak to them instead of God, lest they die. Arminian readers see a request for mediation arising from awe and fear. They want to hear God’s word but through a human intermediary they can bear.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the request highlighting the need for a mediator between God and sinners. The people recognize the danger of direct encounter. Both traditions see Moses’ role confirmed by the people’s own appeal.

Exodus 20:20

20Moses said to the people, “Don’t be afraid, for God has come to test you, and that his fear may be before you, that you won’t sin.”

Arminian

Moses tells them not to fear, for God has come to test them and to place his fear before them so they will not sin. Arminian readers see a distinction between terror and reverent awe that leads to obedience. The testing invites a faithful response, aiming at holiness rather than destruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God using the revelation to instill reverent fear that restrains sin. The test reveals and shapes the people’s hearts. Both traditions see a contrast between paralyzing dread and the healthy fear of God that leads to faithful living.

Exodus 20:21

21The people stayed at a distance, and Moses came near to the thick darkness where God was.

Arminian

The people remain far off while Moses approaches the thick darkness where God is. Arminian readers see Moses entering where others cannot, acting on the people’s behalf. The darkness conveys God’s mysterious and holy presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses as mediator drawing near to God while the people remain at a distance. The thick darkness shows God’s presence veiled in mystery. Both traditions see Moses’ unique access serving the people who cannot approach.

Exodus 20:22

22The LORD said to Moses, “This is what you shall tell the children of Israel: ‘You yourselves have seen that I have talked with you from heaven.

Arminian

The LORD tells Moses to remind Israel that they have seen him speak from heaven. Arminian readers see revelation grounded in the people’s own experience. They are witnesses of God’s speaking, which forms the basis for the instructions that follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God appealing to what Israel has witnessed as the ground for his commands. Revelation from heaven carries unique authority. Both traditions see the people’s experience of God’s voice shaping their worship and obedience.

Exodus 20:23

23You shall most certainly not make gods of silver or gods of gold for yourselves to be alongside me.

Arminian

Israel must not make gods of silver or gold alongside the LORD. Arminian readers see the prohibition of idolatry restated immediately after the revelation. Having heard God speak, they must not replace him with crafted images or place rivals beside him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the second commandment applied directly to Israel’s worship. Precious metals do not make idols acceptable. Both traditions see God’s exclusive claim reaffirmed at the beginning of the covenant instructions.

Exodus 20:24

24You shall make an altar of earth for me, and shall sacrifice on it your burnt offerings and your peace offerings, your sheep and your cattle. In every place where I record my name I will come to you and I will bless you.

Arminian

Israel is to make an altar of earth for burnt and peace offerings, and God promises to come and bless wherever he causes his name to be remembered. Arminian readers see worship ordered by God and accompanied by his promise of presence. Blessing comes as God meets his people in the worship he has appointed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God regulating worship and promising his presence where he records his name. Simple altars keep attention on God rather than human craftsmanship. Both traditions see God’s gracious promise to meet and bless his people in worship he establishes.

Exodus 20:25

25If you make me an altar of stone, you shall not build it of cut stones; for if you lift up your tool on it, you have polluted it.

Arminian

If Israel builds a stone altar, it must not be of cut stones, since using tools would profane it. Arminian readers see worship kept simple and free from human embellishment. The instruction guards against treating the altar as a showcase of human skill.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God setting the terms for acceptable worship, excluding human additions that might obscure his glory. The uncut stones emphasize divine rather than human workmanship. Both traditions read the command within the Sinai setting of early altar worship.

Exodus 20:26

26You shall not go up by steps to my altar, that your nakedness may not be exposed to it.’

Arminian

Israel must not ascend the altar by steps, so that nakedness is not exposed. Arminian readers see concern for modesty and reverence in worship. The detail shows God’s care for the dignity of those who approach him. The WEB speech here continues into the next chapter.

Reformed

Reformed readers see reverence extending to physical details of worship. Modesty guards against shame in God’s presence. Both traditions see this practical instruction reflecting the holiness that marks the entire Sinai revelation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
