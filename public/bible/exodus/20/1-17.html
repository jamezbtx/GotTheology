Passage

Exodus 20

Verses 1–17 · WEB

God speaks the Ten Commandments directly to Israel, beginning with his identity as the LORD who brought them out of Egypt. The commands call for exclusive worship, reverence for his name, Sabbath rest, honor for parents, and protection of life, marriage, property, truth, and the neighbor’s goods. Both traditions read the Decalogue as given to a redeemed people within the Sinai covenant, without turning each command into a debate about justification.

Exodus 20:1

1God spoke all these words, saying,

Arminian

God speaks all these words directly. Arminian readers see the commandments coming from God himself, not from Moses’ invention. The scene underscores that the law is divine address to a redeemed people, inviting them to hear and respond to the God who has spoken.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Decalogue introduced as God’s own speech, giving it unique authority. These words carry the weight of the God who has just appeared in fire and cloud. Both traditions treat the Ten Commandments as a special summary of God’s will spoken directly to Israel.

Exodus 20:2

2“I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.

Arminian

God identifies himself as the LORD who brought Israel out of Egypt and slavery. Arminian readers see the commandments grounded in grace already given; obedience is a grateful response to deliverance. The relationship comes first, then the instructions for living within it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the preface as the foundation of the whole Decalogue: God redeems before he commands. The law is given to a people already rescued, not as a means of earning rescue. Both traditions emphasize this order of grace and obligation as central to reading the commandments.

Exodus 20:3

3“You shall have no other gods before me.

Arminian

Israel shall have no other gods before the LORD. Arminian readers see exclusive loyalty as the heart of the covenant relationship. Having been delivered by the LORD, Israel owes him undivided allegiance, which shapes all other obligations that follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the first commandment establishing God’s exclusive claim on his people’s worship and trust. Every other command flows from honoring him alone. Both traditions affirm that undivided devotion is the fitting response to the God who saves.

Exodus 20:4

4“You shall not make for yourselves an idol, nor any image of anything that is in the heavens above, or that is on the earth beneath, or that is in the water under the earth:

Arminian

Israel must not make idols or images of anything in heaven, on earth, or in the waters. Arminian readers see a warning against reducing God to something humans can shape or control. Worship must honor God as he has revealed himself, not as people imagine him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a prohibition on fashioning images for worship, guarding the distinction between the Creator and creation. Reformed churches have historically applied this command strictly to worship practices. Both traditions see it protecting the true knowledge of God against idolatrous substitutes.

Exodus 20:5

5you shall not bow yourself down to them, nor serve them, for I, the LORD your God, am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children, on the third and on the fourth generation of those who hate me,

Arminian

Israel must not bow to idols, for the LORD is a jealous God, visiting iniquity on children to the third and fourth generation of those who hate him. Arminian readers see the effects of idolatry spreading through families that continue in rebellion, with the phrase those who hate me qualifying the judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s jealousy as his rightful zeal for exclusive worship. The generational language shows sin’s far-reaching consequences in families that persist in hatred of God. Both traditions note the qualifier those who hate me and read the verse alongside the abundant mercy promised in the next verse.

Exodus 20:6

6and showing loving kindness to thousands of those who love me and keep my commandments.

Arminian

God shows loving kindness to thousands of those who love him and keep his commandments. Arminian readers see mercy vastly outweighing judgment and extending to all who respond in love and obedience. The contrast with the third and fourth generation highlights the generosity of God’s character.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s steadfast love far exceeding his judgment, reaching countless generations of those who love him. Love and obedience mark those who belong to his covenant. Both traditions see this contrast as revealing God’s merciful character and his delight in faithful love.

Exodus 20:7

7“You shall not misuse the name of the LORD your God, for the LORD will not hold him guiltless who misuses his name.

Arminian

Israel must not misuse the LORD’s name, for he will not hold guiltless those who do. Arminian readers see reverence for God’s name expressed in speech and conduct. The WEB notes an alternative rendering, take the name in vain. Invoking God carelessly or falsely treats his holiness lightly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command guarding the honor of God’s revealed name in oaths, worship, and daily speech. The warning of guilt underlines its seriousness. Both traditions see this command calling for reverence wherever God’s name is spoken or claimed.

Exodus 20:8

8“Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy.

Arminian

Israel must remember the Sabbath day and keep it holy. Arminian readers see a call to set apart time for God as part of covenant life. Remembering implies intentional observance, marking the rhythm of the week with rest and devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Sabbath as a holy day set apart by God for rest and worship. The command to remember shows it as an ordinance to be actively kept. Both traditions contain varied views on how the Sabbath applies after Christ, so its later application should not be framed as an Arminian and Reformed divide.

Exodus 20:9

9You shall labor six days, and do all your work,

Arminian

Six days are for labor and all work. Arminian readers see work honored as part of God’s design for human life. The Sabbath command assumes and blesses ordinary labor, giving it a rhythm rather than diminishing its value.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the dignity of work affirmed within the pattern God established. Labor and rest together form the rhythm of covenant life. Both traditions see this verse as framing the Sabbath within a week of faithful work.

Exodus 20:10

10but the seventh day is a Sabbath to the LORD your God. You shall not do any work in it, you, nor your son, nor your daughter, your male servant, nor your female servant, nor your livestock, nor your stranger who is within your gates;

Arminian

The seventh day belongs to the LORD, and no one in the household, including servants, livestock, and resident foreigners, may work. Arminian readers see rest extended to all, including the vulnerable. The Sabbath protects servants and strangers from endless labor, showing God’s care for everyone in the community.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Sabbath belonging to God and embracing the entire household. The inclusion of servants and foreigners shows the command’s humane breadth. Both traditions see God’s concern for all who live within Israel’s gates expressed in this shared rest.

Exodus 20:11

11for in six days the LORD made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that is in them, and rested the seventh day; therefore the LORD blessed the Sabbath day, and made it holy.

Arminian

The Sabbath is grounded in creation: God made all things in six days and rested on the seventh, then blessed and hallowed it. Arminian readers see Israel’s rhythm patterned on God’s own work and rest. Keeping the Sabbath honors the Creator and his design.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the creation pattern providing the theological basis for the Sabbath. God’s blessing makes the day holy. Both traditions see the link to creation as giving the command significance beyond mere convenience, rooted in God’s own example.

Exodus 20:12

12“Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the land which the LORD your God gives you.

Arminian

Israel must honor father and mother, with the promise of long life in the land. Arminian readers see family respect as part of covenant faithfulness, tied to blessing in the land God gives. The promise encourages obedience while showing God’s care for ordered communities.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this command beginning the instructions concerning neighbors and establishing honor for God-given authority within the family. The promise relates to Israel’s life in the land. Both traditions see honoring parents as a foundational social duty within the covenant.

Exodus 20:13

13“You shall not murder.

Arminian

Israel shall not murder. Arminian readers see the sanctity of human life protected by God’s command. The prohibition guards the neighbor against unjust killing and reflects the value God places on each person.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command protecting human life as bearing God’s image. Reformed teaching has often extended it to hatred and the preservation of life. Both traditions see this command as foundational to justice and the protection of neighbors within the covenant community.

Exodus 20:14

14“You shall not commit adultery.

Arminian

Israel shall not commit adultery. Arminian readers see faithfulness in marriage protected as part of covenant holiness. The command guards the marriage bond and the trust it requires, reflecting God’s own faithfulness to his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command protecting the sanctity of marriage and the integrity of the family. Reformed catechesis often applies it to purity in thought and conduct more broadly. Both traditions see marital faithfulness as a reflection of covenant loyalty.

Exodus 20:15

15“You shall not steal.

Arminian

Israel shall not steal. Arminian readers see respect for others’ property as part of loving the neighbor. The command protects the means of life God has given each household and promotes trust within the community.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command guarding property and calling for honesty in dealings. It assumes God’s provision to each household and forbids unjust taking. Both traditions see it as essential to a just and trustworthy community.

Exodus 20:16

16“You shall not give false testimony against your neighbor.

Arminian

Israel shall not give false testimony against a neighbor. Arminian readers see truthfulness protected, especially in legal settings where a neighbor’s life or livelihood may depend on honest witness. Justice requires integrity in speech.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command protecting a neighbor’s reputation and the justice of the courts. Reformed teaching often applies it to truthfulness more generally. Both traditions see honest witness as vital to justice and love of neighbor.

Exodus 20:17

17“You shall not covet your neighbor’s house. You shall not covet your neighbor’s wife, nor his male servant, nor his female servant, nor his ox, nor his donkey, nor anything that is your neighbor’s.”

Arminian

Israel shall not covet a neighbor’s house, wife, servants, animals, or anything that is theirs. Arminian readers see the law reaching beyond outward acts to the desires of the heart. Coveting is where many outward sins begin, so the command calls for contentment and inward integrity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the final commandment exposing the inner life, showing that God’s law addresses desire as well as action. It reveals the depth of sin and the need for a renewed heart. Both traditions see the command teaching contentment and love that respects what belongs to others.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
