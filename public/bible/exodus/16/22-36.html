Passage

Exodus 16

Verses 22–36 · WEB

A double portion on the sixth day makes room for a holy Sabbath, given before the formal law at Sinai. Some still go out on the seventh day, and God rebukes their refusal to keep his commands. An omer of manna is preserved as a memorial, and the narrator notes that Israel ate manna for forty years. Both traditions see rest as a gift grounded in provision and remembrance as essential to faith; traditions vary internally on how the Sabbath applies to Christians.

Exodus 16:22

22On the sixth day, they gathered twice as much bread, two omers for each one; and all the rulers of the congregation came and told Moses.

Arminian

On the sixth day the people gather twice as much, and the leaders report it to Moses. Arminian readers see the people following God’s earlier word, though the leaders’ report suggests they did not yet understand it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s word from 16:5 being fulfilled. Both note that obedience and understanding develop gradually as Moses explains the Sabbath to the leaders and the people.

Exodus 16:23

23He said to them, “This is that which the LORD has spoken, ‘Tomorrow is a solemn rest, a holy Sabbath to the LORD. Bake that which you want to bake, and boil that which you want to boil; and all that remains over lay up for yourselves to be kept until the morning.’ ”

Arminian

Moses explains that tomorrow is a holy Sabbath, so the extra may be prepared and kept. Arminian readers see rest offered as a gift from God, freeing the people from ceaseless gathering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Sabbath given before Sinai’s formal law, rooted in God’s pattern and provision. Traditions vary internally on how Sabbath applies to Christians, which this verse does not settle.

Exodus 16:24

24They laid it up until the morning, as Moses ordered, and it didn’t become foul, and there were no worms in it.

Arminian

The kept portion does not spoil this time. Arminian readers see that the difference lies in obedience to God’s instruction, not in the manna itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s word governing the outcome, so that the same act brings decay on one day and preservation on another. Both see the miracle confirming the Sabbath command.

Exodus 16:25

25Moses said, “Eat that today, for today is a Sabbath to the LORD. Today you shall not find it in the field.

Arminian

Moses tells them to eat what was kept, for none will be found in the field. Arminian readers see God inviting the people to trust his prior provision and rest.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God both commanding rest and supplying what makes it possible. Both traditions see Sabbath rest as an act of trust rather than idleness.

Exodus 16:26

26Six days you shall gather it, but on the seventh day is the Sabbath. In it there shall be none.”

Arminian

Six days for gathering and none on the seventh. Arminian readers see a pattern of work and rest established by God for his people’s good.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a creation-shaped rhythm of six days and a seventh. Both agree the verse joins labor and rest under God’s ordering, while traditions differ on later application.

Exodus 16:27

27On the seventh day, some of the people went out to gather, and they found none.

Arminian

Some go out on the seventh day and find nothing. Arminian readers see the persistence of distrust and disobedience even after clear instruction and evident provision.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see remaining unbelief among God’s people. Both note that the disobedient gain nothing by ignoring God’s word, since the provision follows his command.

Exodus 16:28

28The LORD said to Moses, “How long do you refuse to keep my commandments and my laws?

Arminian

God asks how long they will refuse to keep his commandments. Arminian readers see a real rebuke of a pattern of disobedience, warning that continued refusal is serious for those God has redeemed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s rebuke addressed to persistent covenant disobedience. Both traditions agree the question exposes ongoing distrust and calls the people back to obedience.

Exodus 16:29

29Behold, because the LORD has given you the Sabbath, therefore he gives you on the sixth day the bread of two days. Everyone stay in his place. Let no one go out of his place on the seventh day.”

Arminian

God reminds them that he has given the Sabbath and provided two days’ bread; they must stay in place. Arminian readers see the command grounded in prior gift, making obedience a response to grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Sabbath described as a gift with provision attached. Both agree that God’s generosity precedes his command and makes obedience reasonable rather than burdensome for his people.

Exodus 16:30

30So the people rested on the seventh day.

Arminian

The people rest on the seventh day. Arminian readers see correction bearing fruit as the people respond to God’s rebuke with obedience, showing that his warnings aim at restoration rather than rejection.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s word achieving its purpose in bringing the people to rest. Both traditions see Sabbath rest as a shared act of trust in God’s sufficiency.

Exodus 16:31

31The house of Israel called its name “Manna”, and it was like coriander seed, white; and its taste was like wafers with honey.

Arminian

Israel names it manna and describes it as white like coriander seed and sweet like honey wafers. Arminian readers see the name preserving the people’s initial wonder at an unfamiliar gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the description emphasizing that God’s provision was real, distinct, and good. Both note the WEB footnote that manna means “What is it?” connecting the name to 16:15.

Exodus 16:32

32Moses said, “This is the thing which the LORD has commanded, ‘Let an omer-full of it be kept throughout your generations, that they may see the bread with which I fed you in the wilderness, when I brought you out of the land of Egypt.’ ”

Arminian

God commands that an omer be kept for future generations to see the bread he gave. Arminian readers see memory preserved to strengthen the faith of later generations.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God appointing a memorial of his provision so that his works would be remembered. Both agree that remembering what God has done is essential to continued trust.

Exodus 16:33

33Moses said to Aaron, “Take a pot, and put an omer-full of manna in it, and lay it up before the LORD, to be kept throughout your generations.”

Arminian

Aaron is to place an omer in a pot before the LORD. Arminian readers see a tangible witness kept in God’s presence for the benefit of future worshipers.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the memorial set before God as testimony to his faithfulness. Both traditions see the pot as a sign of grace, not an object to be venerated in itself.

Exodus 16:34

34As the LORD commanded Moses, so Aaron laid it up before the Testimony, to be kept.

Arminian

Aaron lays it up before the Testimony as commanded. Arminian readers note obedience completing the instruction and the narrative looking ahead to the covenant documents given later.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the reference to the Testimony anticipates later events, indicating the narrator’s retrospective perspective. Both read it as a memorial preserved by obedient worship.

Exodus 16:35

35The children of Israel ate the manna forty years, until they came to an inhabited land. They ate the manna until they came to the borders of the land of Canaan.

Arminian

Israel eats manna forty years until reaching the borders of Canaan. Arminian readers see God’s patient, faithful provision sustaining the people through a long wilderness period, including years marked by their unbelief.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s unwavering faithfulness feeding his people throughout the journey. Both traditions see the forty years as testimony to provision that did not depend on Israel’s consistency.

Exodus 16:36

36Now an omer is one tenth of an ephah.

Arminian

An explanatory note defines the omer as a tenth of an ephah. Arminian readers see a narrator helping later readers understand the measure used throughout the chapter.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see an explanatory comment aiding later readers. Both note that WEB footnotes give modern equivalents for the measures, which are approximate rather than precise historical claims.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
