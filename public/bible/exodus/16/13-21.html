Passage

Exodus 16

Verses 13–21 · WEB

Quail arrive in the evening and manna in the morning. Moses identifies it as bread the LORD has given and commands daily gathering according to need without storing. Some disobey and their surplus spoils. Both traditions read the account as training in daily dependence on God’s word and provision; Paul later applies the equal measure of 16:18 to generosity among believers.

Exodus 16:13

13In the evening, quail came up and covered the camp; and in the morning the dew lay around the camp.

Arminian

Quail cover the camp in the evening and dew surrounds it in the morning. Arminian readers see God keeping his promise precisely, giving the people concrete grounds for trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s faithfulness fulfilling his word exactly as spoken. Both traditions read the provision as God’s act, whatever natural means may have been involved.

Exodus 16:14

14When the dew that lay had gone, behold, on the surface of the wilderness was a small round thing, small as the frost on the ground.

Arminian

After the dew lifts, a small round thing appears on the ground. Arminian readers note the humble, unfamiliar form of God’s provision, which required the people to receive what he gave.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that God provides in a way the people did not expect or design. Both see daily bread arriving as gift rather than as product of Israel’s labor.

Exodus 16:15

15When the children of Israel saw it, they said to one another, “What is it?” For they didn’t know what it was. Moses said to them, “It is the bread which the LORD has given you to eat.

Arminian

The people ask what it is, and Moses identifies it as bread the LORD has given. Arminian readers see God’s word interpreting his gift so that it can be received with understanding and gratitude.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the necessity of God’s word to explain his works. Both traditions connect this with later reflection, such as Jesus’ teaching in John 6 about the true bread from heaven.

Exodus 16:16

16This is the thing which the LORD has commanded: ‘Gather of it everyone according to his eating; an omer a head, according to the number of your persons, you shall take it, every man for those who are in his tent.’ ”

Arminian

Each person is to gather according to need, an omer per head. Arminian readers see a command calling for responsible participation in receiving God’s gift, neither hoarding nor neglecting it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God regulating the use of his provision for the good of the whole community. Both agree that the measure reflects need and fairness rather than individual ambition.

Exodus 16:17

17The children of Israel did so, and some gathered more, some less.

Arminian

Some gather more and some less. Arminian readers see real variety in human effort and response even under the same command and provision, a variety the next verse shows God graciously overruling.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the differences in gathering as part of the narrative’s setup for what follows. Both traditions avoid reading this verse alone as praise or blame, since the next verse explains the outcome.

Exodus 16:18

18When they measured it with an omer, he who gathered much had nothing over, and he who gathered little had no lack. They each gathered according to his eating.

Arminian

When measured, no one has too much or too little. Arminian readers see God’s provision equalizing need, a pattern Paul applies to generosity in 2 Corinthians 8:15.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s hand ensuring sufficiency for each, and they also note Paul’s use of the verse for sharing among believers. Both see daily dependence rather than accumulation as the lesson.

Exodus 16:19

19Moses said to them, “Let no one leave of it until the morning.”

Arminian

Moses forbids leaving any until morning. Arminian readers see a command that trains daily trust, testing whether the people will rely on tomorrow’s provision from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God teaching dependence by limiting storage, so that each morning becomes a fresh reminder of his care. Both traditions agree the instruction concerns trust in God’s faithfulness rather than arbitrary restriction.

Exodus 16:20

20Notwithstanding they didn’t listen to Moses, but some of them left of it until the morning, so it bred worms and became foul; and Moses was angry with them.

Arminian

Some disobey, and the stored manna breeds worms and stinks; Moses is angry. Arminian readers see real disobedience with real consequences, showing that God’s gifts can be misused through distrust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the folly of distrust exposed and corrected. Both agree that the failure belongs to the disobedient, while God’s provision remains good and sufficient.

Exodus 16:21

21They gathered it morning by morning, everyone according to his eating. When the sun grew hot, it melted.

Arminian

They gather each morning according to need, and the remainder melts in the sun. Arminian readers see a rhythm of daily responsive faith built into the pattern of provision.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s ordering of daily dependence. Both traditions see the morning rhythm as a training in trust that later Scripture echoes in the prayer for daily bread.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
