Passage

Exodus 16

Verses 1–12 · WEB

In the wilderness of Sin the whole congregation murmurs, wishing they had died in Egypt. God answers with a promise of bread from heaven and meat, designed to test whether they will walk in his law, and his glory appears in the cloud. Both traditions see undeserved patience toward a complaining people and provision aimed at knowing the LORD; they explain the nature of divine testing somewhat differently.

Exodus 16:1

1They took their journey from Elim, and all the congregation of the children of Israel came to the wilderness of Sin, which is between Elim and Sinai, on the fifteenth day of the second month after their departing out of the land of Egypt.

Arminian

The congregation moves from Elim into the wilderness of Sin, a month after leaving Egypt. Arminian readers note that the people pass from a place of rest into renewed need, testing continued trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the dated setting and the movement from provision into scarcity under God’s leading. Both read the itinerary as part of a real journey shaped by God’s guidance.

Exodus 16:2

2The whole congregation of the children of Israel murmured against Moses and against Aaron in the wilderness;

Arminian

The whole congregation murmurs against Moses and Aaron. Arminian readers see collective distrust spreading through the community and the danger of forgetting God’s faithfulness so soon after deliverance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see persistent ingratitude in God’s people even after great mercies. Both traditions see a warning about complaint that targets God’s appointed leaders while really questioning God himself.

Exodus 16:3

3and the children of Israel said to them, “We wish that we had died by the LORD’s hand in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the meat pots, when we ate our fill of bread, for you have brought us out into this wilderness to kill this whole assembly with hunger.”

Arminian

The people romanticize Egypt and accuse their leaders of bringing them out to die. Arminian readers see memory distorted by fear, preferring the security of slavery to the uncertainty of trusting God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the heart’s tendency to misremember bondage as comfort. Both note the bitter irony of wishing for death by the LORD’s hand in Egypt instead of trusting the LORD who rescued them.

Exodus 16:4

4Then the LORD said to Moses, “Behold, I will rain bread from the sky for you, and the people shall go out and gather a day’s portion every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law or not.

Arminian

God promises bread from heaven and daily gathering to test whether they will walk in his law. Arminian readers see a genuine test with real responsibility, in which the people’s response to God’s provision matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God using daily provision to prove and train his people, exposing hearts and teaching dependence. Both agree the provision is gracious and the instruction about gathering is meant to cultivate trust.

Key contrast Both affirm that God tests his people through his gracious provision. Classical Arminian readers, who affirm God’s foreknowledge, see a genuine test whose outcome depends on the people’s free response; Reformed readers see the test as God’s appointed means of exposing hearts and training his people within his sovereign purpose.

Exodus 16:5

5It shall come to pass on the sixth day, that they shall prepare that which they bring in, and it shall be twice as much as they gather daily.”

Arminian

A double portion on the sixth day anticipates the Sabbath instructions. Arminian readers see God’s provision arranged so that obedience and rest are possible rather than burdensome.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God ordering provision to support the rest he will command. Both traditions note that God supplies in advance what his commands require.

Exodus 16:6

6Moses and Aaron said to all the children of Israel, “At evening, you shall know that the LORD has brought you out from the land of Egypt.

Arminian

Moses and Aaron promise that evening will show the LORD brought them out of Egypt. Arminian readers see God answering complaint with evidence meant to restore trust in his saving action.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God reaffirming that the exodus was his work, not Moses’ scheme. Both traditions see the coming provision as revelation of God’s identity, not only as food.

Exodus 16:7

7In the morning, you shall see the LORD’s glory; because he hears your murmurings against the LORD. Who are we, that you murmur against us?”

Arminian

In the morning they will see the LORD’s glory, because he has heard their murmurings. Arminian readers see patience in God’s response to a complaining people, giving them reason to repent and trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God displaying his glory despite the people’s unbelief. Both note Moses redirecting the complaint: murmuring against God’s servants is finally murmuring against God.

Exodus 16:8

8Moses said, “Now the LORD will give you meat to eat in the evening, and in the morning bread to satisfy you, because the LORD hears your murmurings which you murmur against him. And who are we? Your murmurings are not against us, but against the LORD.”

Arminian

Moses explains that meat and bread will come because the LORD hears their complaints against him. Arminian readers see grace extended to a grumbling people, inviting them to recognize who they are really addressing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s undeserved kindness toward complainers and his sovereignty over their leaders. Both agree the verse identifies the people’s grumbling as directed against God himself.

Exodus 16:9

9Moses said to Aaron, “Tell all the congregation of the children of Israel, ‘Come close to the LORD, for he has heard your murmurings.’ ”

Arminian

Aaron is to summon the congregation to come near to the LORD who has heard them. Arminian readers see an invitation to approach God in spite of sin, a gracious opening for renewed trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God calling his people near in mercy rather than in immediate judgment. Both traditions note that being heard by God is both comfort and solemn accountability.

Exodus 16:10

10As Aaron spoke to the whole congregation of the children of Israel, they looked toward the wilderness, and behold, the LORD’s glory appeared in the cloud.

Arminian

The LORD’s glory appears in the cloud as Aaron speaks. Arminian readers see God confirming his presence and authority before the people, inviting reverent response.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a visible manifestation of the God who leads them, rebuking unbelief and confirming his word. Both see the cloud as a sign of God’s continuing guidance in the wilderness.

Exodus 16:11

11The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses. Arminian readers see God personally answering through his chosen mediator, keeping the people’s relationship with him grounded in his own word.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s initiative in revelation and his appointment of Moses as mediator. Both traditions agree that the provision that follows comes by God’s declared will, not by chance.

Exodus 16:12

12“I have heard the murmurings of the children of Israel. Speak to them, saying, ‘At evening you shall eat meat, and in the morning you shall be filled with bread. Then you will know that I am the LORD your God.’ ”

Arminian

God promises meat and bread so that the people will know he is the LORD their God. Arminian readers see provision aimed at relational knowledge and trust, not mere physical satisfaction.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s purpose to make himself known through his works. Both agree that the goal of provision is knowledge of God as Israel’s covenant Lord.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
