Passage

Exodus 11

Verses 1–10 · WEB

After nine plagues, the LORD announces one final blow: the death of Egypt’s firstborn. Moses warns Pharaoh that the night will distinguish Egypt from Israel, then leaves in anger. The chapter ends by summarizing the plague cycle and recalling that the LORD hardened Pharaoh’s heart. Both traditions read the hardening within the wider narrative of Exodus 4–14, but they relate divine and human agency differently.

Exodus 11:1

1The LORD said to Moses, “I will bring yet one more plague on Pharaoh, and on Egypt; afterwards he will let you go. When he lets you go, he will surely thrust you out altogether.

Arminian

The LORD announces a final plague and promises that Pharaoh will not merely permit departure but drive Israel out. Arminian readers see God’s patient warnings reaching a decisive end after repeated refusals. The announcement shows that deliverance rests on the LORD’s power, not Israel’s bargaining strength.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the LORD directing events toward the outcome he announced from the beginning of Moses’ mission. Pharaoh’s release will come on God’s terms and timing. Both traditions agree that Israel’s exit is a gift of divine deliverance, not a negotiated concession.

Exodus 11:2

2Speak now in the ears of the people, and let every man ask of his neighbor, and every woman of her neighbor, jewels of silver, and jewels of gold.”

Arminian

Israel is told to ask the Egyptians for silver and gold. Arminian readers note that the request fulfills the promise to Moses in Exodus 3 that Israel would not leave empty-handed. The detail points toward a reversal of the oppression Israel had suffered for so long.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect the request with God’s earlier promise and see his faithfulness reaching even practical details of the departure. Interpreters discuss whether to view the goods as gifts, wages, or spoil. The text’s emphasis is God providing for a people once enslaved.

Exodus 11:3

3The LORD gave the people favor in the sight of the Egyptians. Moreover, the man Moses was very great in the land of Egypt, in the sight of Pharaoh’s servants, and in the sight of the people.

Arminian

The LORD gives Israel favor with the Egyptians, and Moses is held in high regard. Arminian readers see God working through human attitudes without overriding the responsibility of those who respond. Even within a hostile land, ordinary Egyptians and officials can recognize what God is doing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divine providence shaping hearts and public opinion to accomplish his purpose. The favor is the LORD’s gift, not Israel’s achievement or Moses’ skill. Both traditions see the contrast between Egyptians who show favor and a king who remains unyielding.

Exodus 11:4

4Moses said, “This is what the LORD says: ‘About midnight I will go out into the middle of Egypt,

Arminian

Moses announces that the LORD himself will go through Egypt at midnight. Arminian readers stress the personal character of the coming judgment: it is not random disaster but the act of the God who has warned repeatedly. Responsibility rests on the ruler who refused those warnings.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the LORD acting directly as judge and deliverer. The precise time underlines his control over the event. Both traditions read the warning as part of the long conflict between the LORD and Pharaoh, not as an isolated threat.

Exodus 11:5

5and all the firstborn in the land of Egypt shall die, from the firstborn of Pharaoh who sits on his throne, even to the firstborn of the female servant who is behind the mill, and all the firstborn of livestock.

Arminian

The death of the firstborn will reach from the palace to the mill and even to livestock. Arminian readers feel the moral weight of a judgment that falls across Egyptian society, including those who did not rule. The text does not soften its severity, and readers should not either.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the judgment answering Pharaoh’s oppression of Israel, the LORD’s firstborn son, as announced in Exodus 4:22–23. The universal reach exposes the whole nation’s involvement in the system of bondage. Both traditions acknowledge the difficulty and gravity of this act of judgment.

Exodus 11:6

6There will be a great cry throughout all the land of Egypt, such as there has not been, nor will be any more.

Arminian

An unprecedented cry will rise throughout Egypt. Arminian readers see grief answering the earlier cries of oppressed Israel in Exodus 2–3. The reversal is sobering; it calls for reverence before God’s justice rather than celebration of others’ suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the reversal of cries between Israel and Egypt. God hears the oppressed and brings judgment on the oppressor. Both traditions read the verse with awe, not triumphalism, recognizing that the plague’s sorrow is real.

Exodus 11:7

7But against any of the children of Israel a dog won’t even bark or move its tongue, against man or animal, that you may know that the LORD makes a distinction between the Egyptians and Israel.

Arminian

Not even a dog will bark at Israel, so that all may know the LORD distinguishes between the two peoples. Arminian readers see the distinction as covenant protection tied to God’s promises and later to the blood on the doors. Knowledge of the LORD is the stated purpose of the sign.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the distinction as an expression of God’s covenant purpose for his people. The same night brings judgment on one and protection to the other. Both traditions agree that the distinction is God’s act and that its goal is recognition of who the LORD is.

Exodus 11:8

8All these servants of yours will come down to me, and bow down themselves to me, saying, “Get out, with all the people who follow you;” and after that I will go out.’ ” He went out from Pharaoh in hot anger.

Arminian

Moses predicts that Pharaoh’s officials will bow and urge Israel to leave, then departs in hot anger. Arminian readers see righteous indignation at Pharaoh’s repeated refusals. The prophet’s anger reflects the seriousness of resisting God’s word, not personal vindictiveness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Moses’ anger as fitting the gravity of the moment. The prediction that Pharaoh’s servants will bow reverses the power relation between Egypt and Israel. Both traditions note that Moses speaks with confidence because the outcome rests on God’s word.

Exodus 11:9

9The LORD said to Moses, “Pharaoh won’t listen to you, that my wonders may be multiplied in the land of Egypt.”

Arminian

The LORD tells Moses that Pharaoh will not listen, so that his wonders may be multiplied. Arminian readers see God turning Pharaoh’s persistent refusal into an occasion for displaying his power. The statement explains the long plague sequence without making Pharaoh a mere puppet in the narrative.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s purpose governing even Pharaoh’s refusal: the multiplied wonders serve to make the LORD known. Divine purpose and Pharaoh’s real unwillingness stand together. Both traditions read the verse as an explanation of why deliverance came through many signs.

Exodus 11:10

10Moses and Aaron did all these wonders before Pharaoh, but the LORD hardened Pharaoh’s heart, and he didn’t let the children of Israel go out of his land.

Arminian

The plague cycle closes with Moses and Aaron performing wonders, the LORD hardening Pharaoh’s heart, and Pharaoh refusing release. Arminian readers note that Exodus also says Pharaoh hardened his own heart, as in 8:15, 8:32, and 9:34. They commonly read God’s hardening as judicial: confirming a resistance Pharaoh had chosen rather than creating it unconditionally.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign action in the hardening, announced in advance in 4:21 and 7:3, and connect it with Paul’s use of Pharaoh in Romans 9:17–18. They also affirm that Pharaoh acted willingly and was responsible. Some Reformed interpreters stress God giving Pharaoh over to his own sin; others stress the decree. Neither view makes God the author of sin.

Key contrast Both affirm that God hardened Pharaoh and that Pharaoh was responsible. Arminian readers usually see a judicial hardening that follows and confirms Pharaoh’s own self-hardening. Reformed readers see hardening grounded in God’s sovereign purpose, as Paul applies it in Romans 9, without denying Pharaoh’s willing guilt.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
