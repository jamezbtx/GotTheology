Passage

Exodus 28

Verses 15–30 · WEB

The breastplate of judgment, a square folded pouch fastened to the ephod, carries twelve stones in four rows, each engraved with a tribal name. Detailed instructions for rings and chains ensure that it stays fixed over Aaron’s heart. The section closes with the Urim and Thummim placed in the breastplate, so that Aaron bears the names and the judgment of Israel before the LORD continually.

Exodus 28:15

15“You shall make a breastplate of judgment, the work of the skillful workman; like the work of the ephod you shall make it; of gold, of blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine twined linen, you shall make it.

Arminian

The breastplate of judgment is made like the ephod, of gold, blue, purple, scarlet, and fine linen. Arminian readers note that its name links it to discerning God’s will for Israel. Both traditions agree that the breastplate is the centerpiece of the high priest’s garments.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the name “breastplate of judgment” connects it to the Urim and Thummim (28:30) and the seeking of God’s decisions. Both traditions agree that the breastplate unites representation and guidance in the high priest’s ministry.

Exodus 28:16

16It shall be square and folded double; a span shall be its length, and a span its width.

Arminian

The breastplate is square, folded double, a span in length and width. The WEB footnote explains a span as about half a cubit, roughly nine inches. Arminian readers note that folding forms a pouch. Both traditions agree this pouch likely held the Urim and Thummim.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the precise measurement given in the WEB footnote and the double folding, which creates a pocket. Shared ground: the size and form fit the breastplate’s function of holding the stones and the Urim and Thummim over Aaron’s heart.

Exodus 28:17

17You shall set in it settings of stones, four rows of stones: a row of ruby, topaz, and beryl shall be the first row;

Arminian

Four rows of stones are set in the breastplate, the first being ruby, topaz, and beryl. The precise identification of these ancient gem names is uncertain, and translations vary. Arminian readers note the value of the stones. Both traditions agree that the stones honor the tribes they represent.

Reformed

Reformed readers acknowledge that the identification of ancient gemstones is uncertain, as differences among translations show. The preciousness of the stones expresses the value of God’s people. Both traditions agree that interpretation should rest on function and not on speculative stone symbolism.

Exodus 28:18

18and the second row a turquoise, a sapphire, and an emerald;

Arminian

The second row is turquoise, sapphire, and emerald. The WEB footnote notes that “sapphire” may be lapis lazuli. Arminian readers note this as one example of uncertainty in the gem names. Both traditions agree that the translation of the stones does not affect the passage’s meaning.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the WEB footnote’s alternative, lapis lazuli, and the general difficulty of identifying ancient stones. Shared ground: the stones are precious and distinct, each representing a tribe, and certainty about exact minerals is not required to grasp the point.

Exodus 28:19

19and the third row a jacinth, an agate, and an amethyst;

Arminian

The third row is jacinth, agate, and amethyst. Arminian readers note that each stone is distinct, as each tribe is distinct. Both traditions agree that the precise mineral identities remain uncertain, but the variety reflects the diversity of Israel’s tribes.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the continued listing of precious stones whose exact identification is debated. The variety suggests individuality within a unified people. Both traditions agree that the diversity of stones within one breastplate pictures many tribes as one people before God.

Exodus 28:20

20and the fourth row a chrysolite, an onyx, and a jasper. They shall be enclosed in gold in their settings.

Arminian

The fourth row is chrysolite, onyx, and jasper, all set in gold. Arminian readers note the completion of the twelve stones. Both traditions agree that the gold settings secure the stones and that some readers connect the stones with Revelation 21:19–20, where precious stones adorn the new Jerusalem.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the twelve stones are completed and enclosed in gold. The connection with the foundations of the new Jerusalem (Revelation 21:19–20) is a common typological observation, though the lists do not match exactly. Shared ground: the stones honor God’s people.

Exodus 28:21

21The stones shall be according to the names of the children of Israel, twelve, according to their names; like the engravings of a signet, everyone according to his name, they shall be for the twelve tribes.

Arminian

The twelve stones bear the names of the tribes, each engraved like a signet. Arminian readers see every tribe individually remembered before God. Both traditions agree that the names make the breastplate a sign of representation, with all twelve tribes carried by the high priest.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that each tribe is named, emphasizing the particular people for whom the high priest ministers. Both traditions agree that the engraving conveys permanence and that the twelve stones correspond to the twelve tribes of the covenant people.

Exodus 28:22

22You shall make on the breastplate chains like cords, of braided work of pure gold.

Arminian

Braided chains of pure gold are made for the breastplate. Arminian readers note the craftsmanship ensuring the breastplate stays in place. Both traditions agree this begins a series of detailed fastening instructions. The breastplate must not shift as Aaron moves about his duties.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the meticulous construction details, which reflect God’s concern that the breastplate remain fixed over the priest’s heart. Shared ground: the chains are practical, holding the breastplate securely to the ephod. Pure gold again signals that these fastenings belong to the holiest garments.

Exodus 28:23

23You shall make on the breastplate two rings of gold, and shall put the two rings on the two ends of the breastplate.

Arminian

Two gold rings are attached to the ends of the breastplate. Arminian readers note the practical engineering. Both traditions agree that these rings connect the breastplate to the gold chains described in the following verse.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the rings as part of the careful design that binds the breastplate to the shoulder pieces. Shared ground: these fastening details serve to keep the names of Israel securely on Aaron. Each ring has a specific place and purpose in the whole design.

Exodus 28:24

24You shall put the two braided chains of gold in the two rings at the ends of the breastplate.

Arminian

The braided gold chains pass through the rings at the ends of the breastplate. Arminian readers note the precision of the instructions. Both traditions agree that this detail links the breastplate to the ephod’s shoulder stones.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the chains physically unite the names on the shoulders with the names on the breastplate. Shared ground: the construction ensures that the high priest’s garments function as a unified whole. The two places where Israel’s names are borne are physically joined.

Exodus 28:25

25The other two ends of the two braided chains you shall put on the two settings, and put them on the shoulder straps of the ephod in its forepart.

Arminian

The other ends of the chains attach to the settings on the shoulder straps. Arminian readers note the completion of the upper connection. Both traditions agree that the breastplate hangs from the shoulder stones, joining the two places where the tribes’ names are borne.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the breastplate is suspended from the shoulder pieces, linking memorial on the shoulders with memorial on the heart. Both traditions agree that this connection is practical, though some readers see in it a picture of complete representation.

Exodus 28:26

26You shall make two rings of gold, and you shall put them on the two ends of the breastplate, on its edge, which is toward the side of the ephod inward.

Arminian

Two more gold rings are placed on the inner edges of the breastplate. Arminian readers note the attention to securing the lower corners. Both traditions agree that these rings anchor the breastplate to the ephod below.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the continuing precision of the design, ensuring stability. Shared ground: the breastplate is fixed at both top and bottom so that it remains in place during the priest’s ministry. Practical details like these show how seriously the text takes faithful execution.

Exodus 28:27

27You shall make two rings of gold, and shall put them on the two shoulder straps of the ephod underneath, in its forepart, close by its coupling, above the skillfully woven band of the ephod.

Arminian

Two gold rings are attached to the ephod’s shoulder straps near the woven band. Arminian readers note the completion of the fastening system. Both traditions agree that this detail prepares for the blue lace that binds the breastplate to the ephod.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the rings on the ephod correspond to those on the breastplate. The design is coherent and deliberate. Shared ground: the garments are made to function reliably in the holy place. Rings on both garments allow them to be fastened together securely.

Exodus 28:28

28They shall bind the breastplate by its rings to the rings of the ephod with a lace of blue, that it may be on the skillfully woven band of the ephod, and that the breastplate may not swing out from the ephod.

Arminian

A blue lace binds the breastplate’s rings to the ephod’s rings so that it does not swing loose. Arminian readers note that the names stay securely over Aaron’s heart. Both traditions agree that the stability of the breastplate symbolizes the constancy of the priest’s representative role.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the security of the breastplate as reflecting the steadfastness of the high priest’s representation before God. Both traditions agree that the blue lace completes the fastening and that the breastplate must not swing out from the ephod.

Exodus 28:29

29Aaron shall bear the names of the children of Israel in the breastplate of judgment on his heart, when he goes in to the holy place, for a memorial before the LORD continually.

Arminian

Aaron bears the names of Israel on his heart in the breastplate of judgment as a continual memorial. Arminian readers see a picture of loving representation for the whole community, often applied to Christ’s intercession, which they hold is grounded in an atonement offered for all and applied to those who believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Aaron bearing specific names, the tribes of the covenant people, on his heart before God. Many Reformed readers apply this to Christ’s intercession for those the Father has given him (compare John 17:9), seeing in the breastplate a picture of particular, effectual representation.

Key contrast Both agree the text concerns Aaron representing all twelve tribes of Israel, and both see the breastplate as a picture of Christ’s intercession. The difference lies in how that typology is applied: Arminian readers typically connect Christ’s priestly work with an atonement for all, applied to believers; Reformed readers often stress particular names as picturing Christ’s representation of the elect.

Exodus 28:30

30You shall put in the breastplate of judgment the Urim and the Thummim; and they shall be on Aaron’s heart, when he goes in before the LORD. Aaron shall bear the judgment of the children of Israel on his heart before the LORD continually.

Arminian

The Urim and Thummim are placed in the breastplate, on Aaron’s heart before the LORD. Their exact nature and use are uncertain; they were apparently a means of seeking God’s decisions. Arminian readers note God’s willingness to guide his people. Both traditions agree that guidance came from God, not from the objects.

Reformed

Reformed readers acknowledge that the Urim and Thummim are mysterious and that the text does not describe how they worked. They served as a means of divine guidance for Israel. Readers in both traditions generally hold that in the new covenant God guides his people through his word and Spirit rather than through such objects.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
