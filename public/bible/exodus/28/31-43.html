Passage

Exodus 28

Verses 31–43 · WEB

The remaining garments are described: the blue robe of the ephod with alternating pomegranates and golden bells, the gold plate engraved “HOLY TO THE LORD” worn on Aaron’s forehead, the tunic, turban, and sash, and the simpler garments of Aaron’s sons. The chapter ends with linen undergarments for modesty and a warning that priests must be properly clothed when they approach, lest they bear iniquity and die.

Exodus 28:31

31“You shall make the robe of the ephod all of blue.

Arminian

The robe of the ephod is all blue. Arminian readers note its distinctiveness among the garments. Both traditions agree that blue appears throughout the tabernacle’s materials, and they avoid assigning fixed symbolic meanings that the text does not provide.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the robe’s single color sets it apart. Interpreters have proposed meanings for the blue, but the text does not explain it. Shared ground: the robe is part of the holy garments made for glory and for beauty.

Exodus 28:32

32It shall have a hole for the head in the middle of it. It shall have a binding of woven work around its hole, as it were the hole of a coat of mail, that it not be torn.

Arminian

The robe has a head opening reinforced with woven binding so it will not tear. Arminian readers note the care for durability. Both traditions agree that the reinforced opening shows concern for preserving the holy garments.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the prohibition against tearing, which some connect with Leviticus 21:10, where the high priest must not tear his clothes. Both traditions agree that the robe’s integrity matters, and some readers see a link with the seamless garment of Jesus (John 19:23–24), though this is not stated explicitly.

Exodus 28:33

33On its hem you shall make pomegranates of blue, and of purple, and of scarlet, all around its hem; with bells of gold between and around them:

Arminian

Pomegranates of blue, purple, and scarlet alternate with golden bells around the hem. Arminian readers see beauty and fruitfulness in the design. Both traditions agree that pomegranates may evoke the abundance of the promised land (compare Deuteronomy 8:8), though the text itself does not explain the symbolism.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the pomegranates and bells combine beauty with function. The meaning of the pomegranates is not stated, and interpreters differ. Shared ground: the hem is decorated as part of garments made for glory and for beauty.

Exodus 28:34

34a golden bell and a pomegranate, a golden bell and a pomegranate, around the hem of the robe.

Arminian

The pattern alternates: a golden bell and a pomegranate around the robe’s hem. Arminian readers note the rhythmic design. Both traditions agree that this repetition prepares for the explanation of the bells in the next verse.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the deliberate alternation, reflecting the order evident throughout the tabernacle instructions. Shared ground: the design is purposeful and leads into the function of the bells. Bells and pomegranates each appear all around the hem, so the robe is complete in its design. The bells will be explained in the next verse.

Exodus 28:35

35It shall be on Aaron to minister: and its sound shall be heard when he goes in to the holy place before the LORD, and when he comes out, that he not die.

Arminian

The bells sound as Aaron enters and leaves the holy place, so that he does not die. Arminian readers see a reminder that approaching God is serious and must follow his instructions. Both traditions agree that the verse underscores the holiness of God and the danger of approaching him improperly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the warning the gravity of entering God’s presence and the necessity of the appointed mediator. The sound signals the priest’s ministry to those outside. Both traditions agree the main point is reverent, prescribed approach to the holy God.

Exodus 28:36

36“You shall make a plate of pure gold, and engrave on it, like the engravings of a signet, ‘HOLY TO THE LORD.’

Arminian

A plate of pure gold is engraved “HOLY TO THE LORD.” Arminian readers see a declaration that the priest and his ministry belong to God. Both traditions agree that the inscription states the purpose of the entire priesthood and of Israel’s worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the inscription declares consecration: Aaron is set apart for God. The holiness is conferred by God’s appointment. Both traditions agree that the words summarize the meaning of the priestly garments and point to the holiness God requires.

Exodus 28:37

37You shall put it on a lace of blue, and it shall be on the sash. It shall be on the front of the sash.

Arminian

The gold plate is fastened with a blue lace. The WEB reads that it is on the front of the “sash,” though the Hebrew word here is the one the WEB renders “turban” in 28:4 and 28:39. Arminian readers, like Reformed readers, understand the plate to be worn on Aaron’s forehead (28:38).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the plate is attached at the front of the headdress, as the next verse confirms by placing it on Aaron’s forehead. The WEB’s wording “sash” here differs from its “turban” elsewhere for the same Hebrew term. Both traditions agree on the plate’s placement and purpose.

Exodus 28:38

38It shall be on Aaron’s forehead, and Aaron shall bear the iniquity of the holy things, which the children of Israel shall make holy in all their holy gifts; and it shall be always on his forehead, that they may be accepted before the LORD.

Arminian

Aaron bears the iniquity of the holy things so that Israel’s gifts may be accepted before the LORD. Arminian readers see representative mediation: the priest covers imperfections in the people’s offerings. Both traditions agree that this anticipates Christ’s priestly work, by which believers’ worship is accepted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a clear picture of substitutionary mediation: the priest bears iniquity on behalf of the people, and their offerings are accepted. Many connect this with Christ bearing sin and making his people’s worship acceptable (compare 1 Peter 2:5). Both traditions agree on the mediatorial principle.

Exodus 28:39

39You shall weave the tunic with fine linen. You shall make a turban of fine linen. You shall make a sash, the work of the embroiderer.

Arminian

The tunic and turban are woven of fine linen, and the sash is embroidered. Arminian readers note the completion of the high priest’s garments. Both traditions agree that the linen garments, simpler than the ephod, still form part of the holy attire.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the tunic, turban, and sash complete the high priest’s clothing. Shared ground: these garments are holy because they are made by God’s command for priestly service. Fine linen, used throughout the sanctuary, clothes the priest from head to waist.

Exodus 28:40

40“You shall make tunics for Aaron’s sons. You shall make sashes for them. You shall make headbands for them, for glory and for beauty.

Arminian

Aaron’s sons receive tunics, sashes, and headbands, also for glory and for beauty. Arminian readers see dignity extended to all who serve, not just the high priest. Both traditions agree that the ordinary priests share in the honor of the office, though with simpler garments.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the distinction between the high priest’s garments and those of his sons, reflecting differing roles within one priesthood. Both traditions agree that the repeated phrase “for glory and for beauty” shows that all priestly service is honored by God.

Exodus 28:41

41You shall put them on Aaron your brother, and on his sons with him, and shall anoint them, and consecrate them, and sanctify them, that they may minister to me in the priest’s office.

Arminian

Moses is to clothe, anoint, consecrate, and sanctify Aaron and his sons. Arminian readers see God setting apart people for service through a public act. Both traditions agree that the ordination ceremony, described in chapter 29, marks their entry into priestly office.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that consecration comes from God’s command, carried out by Moses. The verbs stress being set apart for God. Both traditions agree that the anointing anticipates the detailed ordination in chapter 29, and many see it as pointing to Christ, the Anointed One.

Exodus 28:42

42You shall make them linen pants to cover their naked flesh. They shall reach from the waist even to the thighs.

Arminian

Linen undergarments cover the priests’ nakedness from waist to thighs. Arminian readers note God’s concern for modesty in worship. Both traditions agree that this connects with the earlier command in 20:26 that nakedness not be exposed at the altar.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the concern for decency and holiness in approaching God, echoing 20:26. Shared ground: worship must be ordered and modest, and the priests’ clothing protects the dignity of their service. Even the least visible garment is prescribed, showing that holiness extends to the whole person.

Exodus 28:43

43They shall be on Aaron and on his sons, when they go in to the Tent of Meeting, or when they come near to the altar to minister in the holy place, that they don’t bear iniquity, and die. This shall be a statute forever to him and to his offspring after him.

Arminian

The garments must be worn when the priests approach the Tent of Meeting or the altar, lest they bear iniquity and die. This is a perpetual statute for Aaron’s line. Arminian readers see the seriousness of obedience in worship. Both traditions agree that holiness and obedience go together in approaching God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the warning the gravity of God’s holiness and the necessity of approaching him in the way he commands. The statute applies to the Levitical order. Both traditions agree that Hebrews presents Christ as the fulfillment of this priesthood, bringing a new way of access to God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
