Passage

Exodus 28

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Chapter 28 turns from the sanctuary to the priesthood. God sets apart Aaron and his sons to serve as priests and commands holy garments for glory and for beauty, to be made by craftsmen God has filled with wisdom. The section describes the ephod and its two onyx shoulder stones engraved with the names of Israel’s sons, so that Aaron bears the tribes before the LORD as a memorial.

Exodus 28:1

1“Bring Aaron your brother, and his sons with him, near to you from among the children of Israel, that he may minister to me in the priest’s office: Aaron, with Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar, Aaron’s sons.

Arminian

God calls Aaron and his sons from among the Israelites to serve as priests. Arminian readers see God appointing representatives for the whole people. Both traditions agree the priesthood is a calling from God, not self-appointed, a point Hebrews 5:4 draws out when it says no one takes this honor for himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize God’s sovereign choice of Aaron’s line for priestly service, a vocational election within Israel. Hebrews 5:4 confirms that the office is received by God’s call. Shared ground: the priesthood exists for the people’s access to God, and its authority comes from God’s appointment.

Exodus 28:2

2You shall make holy garments for Aaron your brother, for glory and for beauty.

Arminian

The holy garments are for glory and for beauty. Arminian readers see the dignity God gives to those who serve him on behalf of others. Both traditions agree the garments express the honor of the office and the holiness of the God being served, rather than Aaron’s personal merit.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the glory belongs to the office God establishes, not to Aaron as an individual. The garments make visible what the priesthood signifies. Both traditions agree that beauty in worship, when God-ordained, honors him, and many in both traditions see the garments as foreshadowing Christ’s priestly glory.

Exodus 28:3

3You shall speak to all who are wise-hearted, whom I have filled with the spirit of wisdom, that they make Aaron’s garments to sanctify him, that he may minister to me in the priest’s office.

Arminian

The garments are made by the wise-hearted, whom God has filled with the spirit of wisdom. Arminian readers see God empowering human skill for his service. Both traditions agree that craftsmanship can be a gift of God’s Spirit, a theme repeated with Bezalel in chapter 31.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign gifting of artisans, so that even practical skill comes from him. The garments sanctify Aaron for ministry. Shared ground: human creativity and divine gifting work together, and both traditions honor craft as a legitimate calling in God’s service.

Exodus 28:4

4These are the garments which they shall make: a breastplate, an ephod, a robe, a fitted tunic, a turban, and a sash. They shall make holy garments for Aaron your brother and his sons, that he may minister to me in the priest’s office.

Arminian

The garments are listed: breastplate, ephod, robe, fitted tunic, turban, and sash. Arminian readers note the completeness of the priestly attire. Both traditions agree that each piece is described in the following verses and that together they set Aaron apart for ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the list provides the outline for the rest of the chapter. The repeated purpose clause stresses that the garments exist so Aaron can minister to God. Both traditions agree the clothing is functional and symbolic, marking the office rather than decorating the man.

Exodus 28:5

5They shall use the gold, and the blue, and the purple, and the scarlet, and the fine linen.

Arminian

The materials are gold, blue, purple, scarlet, and fine linen, matching those used in the tabernacle. Arminian readers see the priest visually tied to the sanctuary. Both traditions agree that the shared materials link the priest’s garments and God’s dwelling as parts of one system of worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priest is clothed in the same materials as the holy place, signifying that he belongs to God’s house. Both traditions agree on the connection, and both are cautious about assigning fixed meanings to each color, since the text itself does not explain them.

Exodus 28:6

6“They shall make the ephod of gold, blue, purple, scarlet, and fine twined linen, the work of the skillful workman.

Arminian

The ephod is made of gold, blue, purple, scarlet, and fine twined linen by skilled workmen. Its exact form is uncertain, but it appears to be an apron-like garment. Arminian readers note the costly materials. Both traditions agree the ephod is central to the high priest’s attire.

Reformed

Reformed readers acknowledge uncertainty about the ephod’s precise shape while noting its prominence as the base for the shoulder stones and breastplate. Both traditions agree that its richness reflects the dignity of approaching God on Israel’s behalf.

Exodus 28:7

7It shall have two shoulder straps joined to the two ends of it, that it may be joined together.

Arminian

The ephod has two shoulder straps joined at its ends. Arminian readers note that the shoulder straps will carry the onyx stones bearing Israel’s names. Both traditions agree this practical detail prepares for the symbolic meaning given in verse 12.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the straps the structural basis for the high priest’s bearing of the tribes on his shoulders. Shared ground: the construction serves the representative function described in the following verses. The straps make it possible for the stones to rest where a burden is carried.

Exodus 28:8

8The skillfully woven band, which is on it, shall be like its work and of the same piece; of gold, blue, purple, scarlet, and fine twined linen.

Arminian

The skillfully woven band is made of the same materials and as one piece with the ephod. Arminian readers note the unity and quality of the garment. Both traditions agree that the band secures the ephod and later anchors the breastplate (28:28).

Reformed

Reformed readers note the integrity of the garment: band and ephod are of one piece. This detail fits the chapter’s concern for completeness and order. Shared ground: the band is functional, holding the priestly garments together for service.

Exodus 28:9

9You shall take two onyx stones, and engrave on them the names of the children of Israel.

Arminian

Two onyx stones are engraved with the names of the children of Israel. Arminian readers see God’s concern that every tribe is remembered before him. Both traditions agree that the names make the high priest a representative of the whole people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the engraving as signifying that the high priest acts on behalf of the covenant people specifically named. Both traditions agree the stones express representation and memorial, and that the onyx’s precise identification is less important than its function.

Exodus 28:10

10Six of their names on the one stone, and the names of the six that remain on the other stone, in the order of their birth.

Arminian

Six names go on each stone in order of birth. Arminian readers note the equal inclusion of all the tribes. Both traditions agree that birth order preserves the family history of Jacob’s sons, rooting the priesthood in the story of Genesis.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the orderly arrangement as reflecting God’s covenant with Jacob’s family. Every tribe is included. Shared ground: the names tie the priestly ministry to God’s promises to the patriarchs. Listing the names in birth order keeps the high priest’s ministry anchored in the history God has already directed.

Exodus 28:11

11With the work of an engraver in stone, like the engravings of a signet, you shall engrave the two stones, according to the names of the children of Israel. You shall make them to be enclosed in settings of gold.

Arminian

The stones are engraved like a signet and set in gold. Arminian readers see permanence: the names are not written lightly but cut into stone. Both traditions agree that signet engraving suggests authority and lasting identity.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that signet engraving conveys ownership and permanence, fitting the idea of a people belonging to God. Both traditions agree that the craftsmanship underscores the importance of the names borne before the LORD.

Exodus 28:12

12You shall put the two stones on the shoulder straps of the ephod, to be stones of memorial for the children of Israel. Aaron shall bear their names before the LORD on his two shoulders for a memorial.

Arminian

Aaron bears the names on his shoulders before the LORD as a memorial. Arminian readers see a picture of priestly representation for the whole community. Both traditions agree that the shoulders suggest bearing responsibility, and many in both see this as foreshadowing Christ’s priestly ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Aaron bears the covenant people before God as their representative. Many Reformed readers connect this with Christ bearing his people. Both traditions agree on the memorial function: Israel is brought to God’s remembrance through the priest.

Exodus 28:13

13You shall make settings of gold,

Arminian

Gold settings are made for the stones and chains. Arminian readers note the careful attention to fastening. Both traditions agree this verse transitions to the attachments that join the shoulder pieces to the breastplate. Gold, the most precious metal in the sanctuary, holds what represents the people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this brief verse continues the meticulous instructions for the garments. Shared ground: the settings hold the stones securely, so that the names remain on the priest as he serves. The repeated use of gold marks these pieces as belonging to the holiest service.

Exodus 28:14

14and two chains of pure gold; you shall make them like cords of braided work. You shall put the braided chains on the settings.

Arminian

Two chains of pure gold, braided like cords, are attached to the settings. Arminian readers note the craftsmanship. Both traditions agree that the chains connect the shoulder stones to the breastplate, uniting the two places where Israel’s names are borne.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the chains the unity of the priestly garments: shoulders and breastplate are joined. Shared ground: the precise instructions ensure that the garments function together for the high priest’s representative ministry. Nothing about the high priest’s attire is left to chance or improvisation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
