Passage

Exodus 31

Verses 1–11 · WEB

God names Bezalel of Judah and Oholiab of Dan to build the tabernacle, filling Bezalel with the Spirit of God for wisdom and skilled workmanship. The list of furnishings, garments, oil, and incense recalls the instructions given in earlier chapters. Both traditions largely share this passage’s affirmation of craft as Spirit-given service and of worship shaped by God’s command.

Exodus 31:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses again on the mountain, closing the long instructions for the tabernacle. What follows names the people who will build it.

Arminian and Reformed readers alike see God not only designing his dwelling place but also providing the workers. The plan does not stay on paper.

Reformed

God’s speech moves from the pattern of the sanctuary to the people who will carry it out. Instruction and provision come from the same source.

Reformed readers see the LORD directing every part of his worship. Both traditions read the verse as a transition, not a point of dispute.

Exodus 31:2

2“Behold, I have called by name Bezalel the son of Uri, the son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah.

Arminian

God says he has called Bezalel by name, from the tribe of Judah. A craftsman receives the language of personal calling.

Arminian readers see God’s personal attention to an ordinary worker and his gifts. Bezalel is chosen for a task, and he must take it up willingly.

Reformed

Being called by name marks Bezalel as appointed by God for this work. The calling is to service, not to salvation.

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign choice of his servants for particular tasks. Both traditions agree this is a vocational call, and that skilled labour can be holy service.

Exodus 31:3

3I have filled him with the Spirit of God, in wisdom, and in understanding, and in knowledge, and in all kinds of workmanship,

Arminian

God has filled Bezalel with the Spirit of God for wisdom, understanding, knowledge, and craftsmanship. Skill is described as a gift of the Spirit.

Arminian readers see the Spirit empowering a person for practical service. Artistic and technical ability can be God-given and used for worship.

Reformed

This is one of the earliest explicit statements of the Spirit filling a person in Scripture. The gift equips Bezalel for skilled work.

Reformed readers stress that every good ability comes from God. Both traditions share the conviction that the Spirit’s work is not limited to prophecy or worship leadership.

Exodus 31:4

4to devise skillful works, to work in gold, and in silver, and in bronze,

Arminian

Bezalel will design artistic works in gold, silver, and bronze. Creativity itself is part of the gift.

Arminian readers value the dignity of craft and design. God’s Spirit inspires invention as well as obedience to a pattern.

Reformed

The gift covers both planning and execution. Bezalel is to devise as well as to make.

Reformed readers see human creativity as a reflection of the Creator, used here for his house. Both traditions agree this honours skilled work done for God.

Exodus 31:5

5and in cutting of stones for setting, and in carving of wood, to work in all kinds of workmanship.

Arminian

The list extends to cutting stones and carving wood. Every kind of workmanship is in view.

Arminian readers see God valuing many kinds of skill. No necessary trade is too humble to serve in his house.

Reformed

The breadth of crafts shows the scope of the gift. The Spirit equips for every task the tabernacle needs.

Reformed readers likewise see God’s provision matching his commands. Both traditions read the verse as practical and affirming of craftsmanship.

Exodus 31:6

6Behold, I myself have appointed with him Oholiab, the son of Ahisamach, of the tribe of Dan; and in the heart of all who are wise-hearted I have put wisdom, that they may make all that I have commanded you:

Arminian

God appoints Oholiab of Dan with Bezalel, and puts wisdom in the hearts of all the skilled workers. The project is shared.

Arminian readers note that the wise-hearted receive further wisdom from God. Existing aptitude and divine gift work together.

Reformed

The LORD gives Bezalel a partner from another tribe and gifts the whole team. Judah and Dan work side by side.

Reformed readers see God equipping a community, not a lone genius. Both traditions agree that the work belongs to many hands, all enabled by God.

Exodus 31:7

7the Tent of Meeting, the ark of the covenant, the mercy seat that is on it, all the furniture of the Tent,

Arminian

The list begins with the Tent of Meeting, the ark of the covenant, and the mercy seat. The most holy objects come first.

Arminian readers see the craftsmen entrusted with the centre of Israel’s worship. Their skill serves God’s presence among his people.

Reformed

The inventory moves from the inner sanctuary outward. The mercy seat stands at the heart of the covenant.

Reformed readers see the dwelling of God shaped around atonement and presence. Both traditions often see the tabernacle as pointing ahead to Christ, without needing to press every detail.

Exodus 31:8

8the table and its vessels, the pure lamp stand with all its vessels, the altar of incense,

Arminian

The table, the lamp stand, and the altar of incense are named. Each object has a role in daily worship.

Arminian readers note the care given to every furnishing. Worship involves ordered, faithful attention to what God commands.

Reformed

The holy place’s furnishings are listed in turn. Nothing is left to improvisation.

Reformed readers see worship regulated by God’s command. Both traditions read the list as part of God’s detailed provision for his dwelling.

Exodus 31:9

9the altar of burnt offering with all its vessels, the basin and its base,

Arminian

The altar of burnt offering and the basin complete the outer furnishings. Sacrifice and washing are provided for.

Arminian readers see God supplying the means of approach for a sinful people. The craftsmen build what makes worship possible.

Reformed

The courtyard objects concern sacrifice and cleansing. Access to God is costly and ordered.

Reformed readers likewise see the sacrificial system as God’s gracious provision. Both traditions agree that these objects frame Israel’s approach to a holy God.

Exodus 31:10

10the finely worked garments—the holy garments for Aaron the priest, the garments of his sons to minister in the priest’s office—

Arminian

The priestly garments for Aaron and his sons are also crafted by the gifted workers. Holiness extends to clothing.

Arminian readers see the priests’ calling made visible. The garments mark a ministry received from God.

Reformed

The garments set the priests apart for service. Their beauty serves the holiness of the office.

Reformed readers see the priesthood ordered entirely by divine command. Both traditions agree the verse concerns the dignity of priestly service, not personal merit.

Exodus 31:11

11the anointing oil, and the incense of sweet spices for the holy place: according to all that I have commanded you they shall do.”

Arminian

The anointing oil and incense complete the list, all to be made as God commanded. Obedience is the measure of success.

Arminian readers see gifted people faithfully following a divine pattern. Freedom in craft operates within God’s instructions.

Reformed

The list ends with the reminder that everything must follow what God has commanded. The gift serves obedience.

Reformed readers see a clear principle: God determines how he is worshipped. Both traditions agree on that point in this passage.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
