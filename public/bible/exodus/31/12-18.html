Passage

Exodus 31

Verses 12–18 · WEB

The tabernacle instructions close with the Sabbath, a sign between the LORD and Israel that he sanctifies them, grounded in God’s work and rest at creation. God then gives Moses the two tablets written with God’s finger. Arminian and Reformed readers share the main teaching, while Christians in both traditions apply the Sabbath command in different ways.

Exodus 31:12

12The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses again, turning to the Sabbath. Work on the tabernacle is set beside the command to rest.

Arminian readers note the placement: even holy work must stop on the Sabbath. Service to God does not override the rest God gives.

Reformed

The instruction about the sanctuary closes with the Sabbath. Building God’s house does not suspend God’s day.

Reformed readers see the Sabbath framing the whole work. Both traditions read the verse as a transition that links worship and rest.

Exodus 31:13

13“Speak also to the children of Israel, saying, ‘Most certainly you shall keep my Sabbaths; for it is a sign between me and you throughout your generations, that you may know that I am the LORD who sanctifies you.

Arminian

The Sabbath is a sign between God and Israel, that they may know “I am the LORD who sanctifies you.” Rest proclaims who sets Israel apart.

Arminian readers stress that sanctification is God’s work, received and lived out by his people. Keeping the Sabbath is a response to his grace, not a way of earning it.

Reformed

The Sabbath signifies that the LORD is the one who sanctifies his people. Holiness begins with God’s action.

Reformed readers stress God’s initiative in making Israel his own. Both traditions agree on the core point; they differ more broadly over how the Sabbath command applies to Christians, and views vary within each tradition.

Exodus 31:14

14You shall keep the Sabbath therefore, for it is holy to you. Everyone who profanes it shall surely be put to death; for whoever does any work therein, that soul shall be cut off from among his people.

Arminian

Profaning the Sabbath brings death or being cut off from the people. The penalty shows how seriously the sign is taken.

Arminian readers read the penalty within Israel’s covenant law, where the Sabbath marked the nation’s identity. Christians generally do not apply this civil penalty today.

Reformed

The severe penalty reflects the Sabbath’s role as a covenant sign. Violating it denies the relationship it signifies.

Reformed readers often distinguish the civil penalties of Israel’s law from enduring moral principles. Both traditions agree the penalty belonged to Israel’s covenant order and is not a model for Christian practice.

Exodus 31:15

15Six days shall work be done, but on the seventh day is a Sabbath of solemn rest, holy to the LORD. Whoever does any work on the Sabbath day shall surely be put to death.

Arminian

Six days of work, then a seventh day of solemn rest holy to the LORD. The rhythm is set out plainly.

Arminian readers see work and rest both given by God. Rest is not laziness but holy time.

Reformed

The pattern of six and one reflects God’s ordering of time. The seventh day belongs to the LORD.

Reformed readers see a creation pattern for human life. Both traditions value the gift of rest, while differing internally on how the Sabbath relates to the Lord’s Day.

Exodus 31:16

16Therefore the children of Israel shall keep the Sabbath, to observe the Sabbath throughout their generations, for a perpetual covenant.

Arminian

Israel is to keep the Sabbath throughout their generations as a perpetual covenant. The command is meant to last.

Arminian readers see the Sabbath as a lasting covenant sign for Israel. Christians interpret its continuing force in different ways.

Reformed

The Sabbath is a perpetual covenant for Israel’s generations. Its permanence is emphasised.

Reformed readers have often connected the Sabbath principle with the Christian Lord’s Day, though not all agree. Both traditions affirm the Sabbath’s significance for Israel without one agreed Christian application.

Exodus 31:17

17It is a sign between me and the children of Israel forever; for in six days the LORD made heaven and earth, and on the seventh day he rested, and was refreshed.’ ”

Arminian

The Sabbath is a sign forever, grounded in creation: the LORD made heaven and earth in six days and rested on the seventh. God himself sets the pattern.

Arminian readers see the Creator inviting his people to share his rest. The language of God being refreshed is figurative, not a sign of weariness.

Reformed

The reason for the Sabbath is God’s own work and rest in creation. Israel imitates the Creator.

Reformed readers see creation and covenant linked. Both traditions read “refreshed” as human language for God’s satisfaction in his finished work, not a weakness in God.

Exodus 31:18

18When he finished speaking with him on Mount Sinai, he gave Moses the two tablets of the covenant, stone tablets, written with God’s finger.

Arminian

God gives Moses the two tablets of the covenant, written with God’s finger. The law comes directly from God.

Arminian readers see the covenant terms given personally by God. The phrase echoes the magicians’ confession in Egypt, “This is God’s finger.”

Reformed

The tablets are God’s own writing, marking the law as divine in origin. The covenant is not Moses’ invention.

Reformed readers stress the authority of God’s written word. Both traditions agree the tablets are a sign of God’s direct gift, about to be broken at the foot of the mountain.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
