Passage

Exodus 24

Verses 1–11 · WEB

The covenant is ratified at Sinai. Moses tells the people all the LORD’s words, and they answer together that they will do them. Moses writes the words, builds an altar with twelve pillars, and has offerings made. He reads the book of the covenant, and after the people pledge obedience he sprinkles them with the blood of the covenant. Then Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy elders go up, see the God of Israel, and eat and drink in his presence unharmed.

Exodus 24:1

1He said to Moses, “Come up to the LORD, you, and Aaron, Nadab, and Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel; and worship from a distance.

Arminian

God summons Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy elders to come up and worship from a distance. Arminian readers note the graded access: the whole people is represented, yet distance is kept before God’s holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the ordered approach to God’s holiness: representatives, mediation, and distance. That structure points forward to the tabernacle and finally to Christ, through whom believers draw near, as Hebrews 10:19–22 says.

Exodus 24:2

2Moses alone shall come near to the LORD, but they shall not come near. The people shall not go up with him.”

Arminian

Moses alone may come near. Arminian readers see Moses’ unique role as mediator, appointed by God, standing between the holy God and the people.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress mediation as God’s appointed way of approach. Moses foreshadows Christ, the one mediator between God and humanity in 1 Timothy 2:5 and the mediator of a better covenant in Hebrews 8:6.

Exodus 24:3

3Moses came and told the people all the LORD’s words, and all the ordinances; and all the people answered with one voice, and said, “All the words which the LORD has spoken we will do.”

Arminian

The people respond with one voice: All the words which the LORD has spoken we will do. Arminian readers see a genuine, voluntary covenant commitment, a free response to God’s gracious initiative in the exodus.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a sincere pledge. They note that Israel’s later failures expose the limits of human resolve and point to the need for the new covenant in which God writes his law on the heart, as Jeremiah 31:31–34 says.

Key contrast Both see a real and freely spoken commitment that Israel soon breaks. Arminian readers typically emphasize the genuine human response in covenant-making. Reformed readers typically emphasize what the pledge reveals about human inability apart from new-covenant grace, though they too affirm the pledge was sincere.

Exodus 24:4

4Moses wrote all the LORD’s words, then rose up early in the morning and built an altar at the base of the mountain, with twelve pillars for the twelve tribes of Israel.

Arminian

Moses writes all the LORD’s words and builds an altar with twelve pillars for the twelve tribes. Arminian readers note the importance of the written covenant, which every generation can read and answer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the inscripturation of God’s word as foundational: covenant and canon go together. The twelve pillars show that the whole people stands in the covenant before God.

Exodus 24:5

5He sent young men of the children of Israel, who offered burnt offerings and sacrificed peace offerings of cattle to the LORD.

Arminian

Young men offer burnt offerings and peace offerings. Arminian readers see consecration and fellowship: burnt offerings express full devotion, and peace offerings express communion with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sacrifice as the necessary ground of covenant fellowship. Before Aaronic priests were appointed, sacrifice already shows that sinners approach God through blood.

Exodus 24:6

6Moses took half of the blood and put it in basins, and half of the blood he sprinkled on the altar.

Arminian

Half the blood is put in basins and half sprinkled on the altar. Arminian readers note that the ritual binds both parties: God’s side is signified by the altar and the people’s side by the blood still to be sprinkled.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the two-part rite as uniting God and people in covenant through blood, with the altar representing God’s presence and acceptance.

Exodus 24:7

7He took the book of the covenant and read it in the hearing of the people, and they said, “We will do all that the LORD has said, and be obedient.”

Arminian

Moses reads the book of the covenant, and the people pledge to do all and be obedient. Arminian readers stress that the people respond to a clearly heard word: informed, public consent.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the word preached before the covenant is sealed, which they relate to word and sacrament in Christian worship. The people’s vow echoes 24:3.

Exodus 24:8

8Moses took the blood, and sprinkled it on the people, and said, “Look, this is the blood of the covenant, which the LORD has made with you concerning all these words.”

Arminian

Moses sprinkles the people with the blood of the covenant. Jesus echoes these words at the Last Supper in Matthew 26:28, and Hebrews 9:18–20 cites this scene. Arminian readers see the old covenant inaugurated by blood, pointing to Christ’s blood shed for all.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Christ’s words fulfilling this scene: his blood inaugurates the new covenant. They commonly connect the covenant blood with its effective application to God’s covenant people.

Key contrast Both affirm Christ’s blood of the covenant as the fulfillment of this rite. Arminian readers typically connect it with an atonement provided for all and received by faith. Reformed readers typically connect it with Christ’s definite work for his covenant people, though the tradition’s confessions also teach its infinite sufficiency.

Exodus 24:9

9Then Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy of the elders of Israel went up.

Arminian

Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy elders go up. Arminian readers note the solemn privilege of Israel’s leaders, and the sobering fact that Nadab and Abihu later die for unauthorized fire in Leviticus 10.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that great privilege does not guarantee faithfulness, as Nadab and Abihu’s later judgment shows.

Exodus 24:10

10They saw the God of Israel. Under his feet was like a paved work of sapphire stone, like the skies for clearness.

Arminian

They saw the God of Israel. The text describes only what was under his feet, a pavement like sapphire, which WEB’s footnote says may be lapis lazuli, clear as the sky. Arminian readers note the restraint: they see a revelation of God, not his full being, in light of 33:20.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same restraint. Many identify what they saw as a theophany, a visible manifestation, while God’s essence remains invisible, as John 1:18 and 1 Timothy 6:16 say. Ezekiel 1:26 uses similar imagery.

Exodus 24:11

11He didn’t lay his hand on the nobles of the children of Israel. They saw God, and ate and drank.

Arminian

God did not lay his hand on the nobles; they saw God and ate and drank. Arminian readers see grace: sinners in God’s presence are spared and welcomed to a covenant meal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a covenant meal of communion made possible by the blood just sprinkled. Many Christian readers see an anticipation of the Lord’s Supper and the marriage supper of the Lamb.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
