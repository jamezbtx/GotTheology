Passage

Exodus 24

Verses 12–18 · WEB

The LORD calls Moses up the mountain to receive the stone tablets of law and command. Moses goes with Joshua, leaving Aaron and Hur to settle disputes among the elders. The cloud covers Sinai and the LORD’s glory settles there for six days. On the seventh day God calls Moses from the cloud. To Israel below, the glory looks like devouring fire. Moses enters the cloud and stays on the mountain forty days and forty nights.

Exodus 24:12

12The LORD said to Moses, “Come up to me on the mountain, and stay here, and I will give you the stone tablets with the law and the commands that I have written, that you may teach them.”

Arminian

God calls Moses up to receive the stone tablets with the law and commands that I have written, that you may teach them. Arminian readers note that the law is given to be taught, so that people may understand and obey.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress God’s own authorship of the tablets and the authority of his written word. They also note the teaching office, since the law shapes the covenant people through instruction.

Exodus 24:13

13Moses rose up with Joshua, his servant, and Moses went up onto God’s Mountain.

Arminian

Moses goes up with Joshua his servant. Arminian readers note Joshua being prepared for future leadership by being near Moses at the mountain.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence preparing Israel’s next leader through service and proximity to the mediator.

Exodus 24:14

14He said to the elders, “Wait here for us, until we come again to you. Behold, Aaron and Hur are with you. Whoever is involved in a dispute can go to them.”

Arminian

Moses tells the elders to wait and appoints Aaron and Hur to settle disputes. Arminian readers note sensible delegation and the irony that Aaron, left in charge, will make the golden calf in chapter 32.

Reformed

Reformed readers note orderly government in the covenant community and the frailty of human leaders that chapter 32 will expose.

Exodus 24:15

15Moses went up on the mountain, and the cloud covered the mountain.

Arminian

The cloud covers the mountain. Arminian readers see the cloud as a sign of God’s presence both revealing and veiling him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same cloud that led Israel out of Egypt, signifying God’s presence while hiding his glory from direct sight.

Exodus 24:16

16The LORD’s glory settled on Mount Sinai, and the cloud covered it six days. The seventh day he called to Moses out of the middle of the cloud.

Arminian

The LORD’s glory settles on Sinai for six days, and on the seventh he calls Moses. Arminian readers note the waiting and preparation before God speaks. Moses approaches only when called.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the pattern of six days and a seventh, echoing creation. They also stress God’s initiative: Moses enters when God calls him.

Exodus 24:17

17The appearance of the LORD’s glory was like devouring fire on the top of the mountain in the eyes of the children of Israel.

Arminian

The glory looks like devouring fire to the people below. Arminian readers see God’s holiness as both attractive and dangerous, a theme Hebrews 12:29 draws on: our God is a consuming fire.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress God’s holiness and the fear proper to sinners before him, which is why mediation and sacrifice are necessary.

Exodus 24:18

18Moses entered into the middle of the cloud, and went up on the mountain; and Moses was on the mountain forty days and forty nights.

Arminian

Moses enters the cloud and stays forty days and forty nights. Arminian readers note the extended communion with God that prepares Moses to receive the tabernacle instructions in chapters 25–31.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses as mediator dwelling with God on the people’s behalf. Many note parallels with Elijah’s forty days and Jesus’ forty days in the wilderness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
