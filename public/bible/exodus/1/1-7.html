Passage

Exodus 1

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Exodus opens by picking up the thread of Genesis: the seventy who went down to Egypt with Jacob have become a multitude. Joseph and his generation are gone, but the promise of fruitfulness given to Adam and renewed to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob is visibly coming true.

Exodus 1:1

1Now these are the names of the sons of Israel, who came into Egypt (every man and his household came with Jacob):

Arminian

Exodus begins with “Now these are the names,” tying the book to Genesis 46. The family that went down to Egypt is named man by man, households included. Redemption will come to a real people with a real history, not to an abstraction.

Arminian readers hear continuity of promise carried through generations of ordinary families. God works his purpose in history through people who trust, fail, and are kept by grace; the names remind Israel that they belong to a story larger than any single generation.

Reformed

The opening “Now” links Exodus to Genesis as one story. The names recall the sons to whom the covenant promises were passed down, and the households show that God deals with families as well as individuals.

Reformed readers stress covenant continuity. The God who bound himself to Abraham does not start over in Egypt. Across the move from Canaan and the passing of the patriarchs, his purpose stays one and his faithfulness unbroken.

Exodus 1:2

2Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and Judah,

Arminian

Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and Judah—Leah’s sons head the list. Genesis has not hidden their failures (Gen 34; 35:22; 38), yet they remain heads of Israel.

The list quietly shows grace that keeps a flawed family within the promise. Arminian readers stress that this grace calls for repentance and faithfulness in each generation; Judah’s change in Genesis 44 shows what that response looks like.

Reformed

Leah’s four eldest sons come first, among them Levi, whose line will produce Moses (2:1), and Judah, the line of kings (Gen 49:10).

Reformed readers see God’s choosing at work in these names. He takes up flawed men into his purposes, and the tribes that carry the promise forward are there because of his mercy, not their merit.

Exodus 1:3

3Issachar, Zebulun, and Benjamin,

Arminian

Issachar, Zebulun, and Benjamin complete the sons of Leah and Rachel (Joseph is already in Egypt, v. 5). Benjamin, the youngest, whose life Judah once offered to protect, is counted among the heads of Israel.

The roll is simple, but each name carries a remembered story. The God of Exodus is the God of these particular families.

Reformed

The list follows the mothers’ order rather than strict birth order, grouping Leah’s and Rachel’s sons before the handmaids’ sons. Benjamin, Rachel’s second son, closes this group.

The careful ordering reflects Genesis’ attention to the whole family of promise. No tribe is forgotten when God later remembers his covenant (2:24).

Exodus 1:4

4Dan and Naphtali, Gad and Asher.

Arminian

Dan and Naphtali (Bilhah’s sons), Gad and Asher (Zilpah’s sons): the sons of the servant women are full heirs among the twelve.

Arminian readers note how widely the promise reaches within Israel. Birth to a servant mother does not shut anyone out of the covenant people; the grace that carries the family is broad.

Reformed

The handmaids’ sons complete the twelve. The family includes the complicated rivalries of Genesis 30, yet God makes one people from it.

Reformed readers stress God’s sovereign grace in forming his people. The tribes are not ranked by the mothers’ standing but bound together by the promise God made to their father.

Exodus 1:5

5All the souls who came out of Jacob’s body were seventy souls, and Joseph was in Egypt already.

Arminian

Seventy souls came from Jacob’s body, matching Genesis 46:27 and Deuteronomy 10:22. The Septuagint and a Qumran manuscript of Exodus read seventy-five, the figure Stephen cites in Acts 7:14, probably by counting additional descendants of Joseph. The WEB follows the Hebrew’s seventy.

The small number matters: Deuteronomy 10:22 contrasts the seventy who went down with the multitude like the stars. The growth that follows is God’s doing.

Reformed

“Seventy souls” is the Hebrew count shared with Genesis 46:27; the variant seventy-five (Septuagint, Acts 7:14) reflects a different way of reckoning the family, not a different event. Joseph, already in Egypt, completes the number.

Reformed readers note the humble beginning. God fulfils his promise of a great nation (Gen 12:2) from a household small enough to count, so the glory of the increase belongs to him.

Exodus 1:6

6Joseph died, as did all his brothers, and all that generation.

Arminian

Joseph dies, then his brothers, then the whole generation. The people who remembered the famine and Joseph’s rescue pass from the scene.

Generations end, but God’s promise does not. Joseph’s own dying word was that God would surely visit them (Gen 50:24–25). Arminian readers hear a call to keep faith when living memory of God’s past works has faded.

Reformed

The death of a generation clears the stage for a new chapter. Human mediators, even one as gifted as Joseph, die; the covenant does not die with them.

Reformed readers stress that God’s faithfulness does not depend on any one leader. The same God who provided through Joseph keeps watch over Joseph’s people in Egypt.

Exodus 1:7

7The children of Israel were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied, and grew exceedingly mighty; and the land was filled with them.

Arminian

“Fruitful… multiplied… the land was filled” echoes the creation blessing (Gen 1:28) and the promises to the patriarchs (Gen 17:6; 35:11). The language piles up to show how abundant the growth is.

Arminian and Reformed readers share this ground: God is keeping his word. Arminian readers add that God’s blessing works through ordinary means—marriages, births, families—where his gifts are received and enjoyed.

Reformed

The verse gathers creation and covenant vocabulary: be fruitful, increase, multiply, fill. What God commanded and promised in Genesis is now happening in Egypt.

Reformed readers see providence keeping the promise even in a foreign land. The multiplication is God’s gift, not Israel’s achievement, and it sets up the conflict with Pharaoh, who will try to undo what God has done.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
