Passage

Exodus 1

Verses 15–22 · WEB

When forced labor fails, Pharaoh orders the Hebrew midwives Shiphrah and Puah to kill newborn sons. They fear God rather than the king, and God deals well with them, so Pharaoh widens the decree to all his people: every son is to be thrown into the river.

Exodus 1:15

15The king of Egypt spoke to the Hebrew midwives, of whom the name of the one was Shiphrah, and the name of the other Puah,

Arminian

Shiphrah and Puah are named, while the king of Egypt is not. Scripture honors the faithful women and leaves the mighty man anonymous.

Arminian readers often linger on the dignity of these midwives. Ordinary women in a humble vocation become agents of God’s protection. Whether “Hebrew midwives” means Hebrew women or midwives to the Hebrews is debated; either way, they act with courage.

Reformed

The naming is striking: the midwives are remembered and Pharaoh is not. God’s kingdom reverses the world’s scale of importance.

Reformed readers see God raising humble instruments to guard the line of promise. Whether the midwives were Hebrews or Egyptians serving Hebrews is uncertain; what matters is that they feared God.

Exodus 1:16

16and he said, “When you perform the duty of a midwife to the Hebrew women, and see them on the birth stool, if it is a son, then you shall kill him; but if it is a daughter, then she shall live.”

Arminian

Pharaoh’s order is infanticide by stealth: kill the sons at birth, let the daughters live.

Arminian readers hear a clear moral horror. The decree attacks the image of God in the most vulnerable and asks midwives to turn their calling of life into death. The command lays a real choice before them, and their response matters.

Reformed

The decree targets the sons, aiming to end Israel as a people. It is an assault on the promised seed.

Reformed readers note that the conflict foretold in Genesis 3:15 is at work again. Behind Pharaoh’s policy stands enmity against the people through whom the Deliverer will come, and God will not let it succeed.

Exodus 1:17

17But the midwives feared God, and didn’t do what the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the baby boys alive.

Arminian

“But the midwives feared God.” Reverence for God outweighs fear of the king. They do not do what he commanded and let the boys live.

Arminian readers see responsive faith in action: the fear of God shown in costly obedience. This is an early example in Scripture of obeying God rather than men (Acts 5:29). Their courage is real and honored.

Reformed

The fear of God, the beginning of wisdom (Prov 9:10), governs their conduct. They refuse a wicked command at personal risk.

Reformed readers count such fear of God as itself God’s gift, the fruit of his work in the heart (Jer 32:40). They also honor the midwives’ choice as truly theirs. Grace does not cancel their courage; it produces it.

Exodus 1:18

18The king of Egypt called for the midwives, and said to them, “Why have you done this thing and saved the boys alive?”

Arminian

Pharaoh summons them to account: “Why have you done this thing?” The powerful demand explanations from the faithful.

Arminian readers note the danger the midwives face. Faithfulness is not abstract; it means standing before a king who has power of life and death.

Reformed

The interrogation raises the stakes. The midwives must answer the most powerful man in Egypt.

Reformed readers see God holding his servants in this moment. Their lives are in Pharaoh’s hands only as far as God permits, and God will deal well with them (v. 20).

Exodus 1:19

19The midwives said to Pharaoh, “Because the Hebrew women aren’t like the Egyptian women; for they are vigorous and give birth before the midwife comes to them.”

Arminian

The midwives answer that Hebrew women are vigorous and give birth before a midwife arrives. Interpreters have long debated whether this was a lie and how to judge it.

Arminian readers, like Christians generally, are not of one mind here. Some judge the answer at least partly untrue while honoring the midwives’ fear of God and their protection of life; others treat misleading a murderous ruler as no breach of truthfulness. The text itself does not stop to comment on their words.

Reformed

The answer protects the babies and outwits Pharaoh. Christian interpreters have long been divided on whether it was a lie and whether such a lie can be excused.

Augustine argued that God rewarded the midwives’ mercy, not their deceit (Against Lying, Contra Mendacium 15.32), and many Reformed interpreters have followed that line, honoring their fear of God without approving any falsehood. Others in the tradition hold that deceiving a murderer is not the lying the ninth commandment forbids. The narrator leaves the ethics for readers to weigh.

Exodus 1:20

20God dealt well with the midwives, and the people multiplied, and grew very mighty.

Arminian

“God dealt well with the midwives.” The people keep multiplying and grow very mighty. God’s blessing rests both on the midwives and on Israel.

Arminian readers see God’s favor toward those who fear him. God takes notice of faithful action and blesses it, as a gracious response to faith rather than wages earned.

Reformed

God’s kindness to the midwives and Israel’s growth are tied together. The faithful midwives share in the blessing they protected.

Reformed readers stress that God’s dealing well is grace. He crowns his own gifts in his people, rewarding a fear of him that he himself has worked in them.

Exodus 1:21

21Because the midwives feared God, he gave them families.

Arminian

“Because the midwives feared God, he gave them families.” The reward is fitting: those who preserved households receive households.

Arminian readers see God’s gracious reward for a grace-enabled response. The midwives could have obeyed Pharaoh; they chose to fear God, and God honored that choice. This is no merit that obliges God, yet their faithful choice truly matters to the outcome.

Reformed

The midwives receive families. They guarded Israel’s homes, and God builds homes for them.

Reformed readers see a reward of grace: the fear of God is God’s gift (Jer 32:40), and his reward crowns what he has given. The “because” is real, but it rests on God’s faithfulness to work and bless, not on human merit.

Key contrast Both traditions agree that God rewarded the midwives’ fear of him and that the reward was gracious, not earned. Arminians typically emphasize that the midwives’ choice, enabled by grace, is the real ground on which God responds, so their faithfulness genuinely affects the outcome. Reformed interpreters typically stress that the fear of God is itself God’s gift, so the reward crowns what God has worked in them.

Exodus 1:22

22Pharaoh commanded all his people, saying, “You shall cast every son who is born into the river, and every daughter you shall save alive.”

Arminian

When secrecy fails, Pharaoh widens the decree to all his people: every son is to be thrown into the river. Genocide becomes public policy.

Arminian readers do not soften this. Evil can escalate. The river where Hebrew boys are to die is where Moses will be saved (2:3–5); God brings deliverance in the very place of the threat.

Reformed

The decree turns the Nile, Egypt’s source of life, into an instrument of death. Every Egyptian is now enlisted against Israel’s sons.

Reformed readers see the irony God weaves into history. The river of death becomes the place where the deliverer is drawn out (2:10), and later the waters will judge Egypt itself (Exod 14:28).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
