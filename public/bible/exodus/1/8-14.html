Passage

Exodus 1

Verses 8–14 · WEB

A new king who did not know Joseph treats Israel’s growth as a threat and answers it with forced labor. Yet the harder Egypt presses, the more Israel multiplies, and the oppression foretold to Abram (Gen 15:13) settles into bitter bondage.

Exodus 1:8

8Now there arose a new king over Egypt, who didn’t know Joseph.

Arminian

“A new king… who didn’t know Joseph.” Whether he was ignorant of Joseph or simply chose to disregard him, the debt Egypt owed to Joseph’s rescue is forgotten. Political memory is short.

Arminian readers note that the king’s turn is his own. Human rulers make real choices, and this one chooses suspicion over gratitude. God does not need Pharaoh’s favor to keep his promise, but Pharaoh will answer for how he treats God’s people.

Reformed

The new king’s not knowing Joseph marks the turning of the story from favor to oppression, as foretold to Abram (Gen 15:13–14). The one who saved Egypt is forgotten in Egypt.

Reformed readers see that this reversal sits within God’s announced plan without making God the author of Pharaoh’s cruelty. The king acts freely and wickedly; God has already promised judgment on the nation Israel serves and a going out with great possessions.

Exodus 1:9

9He said to his people, “Behold, the people of the children of Israel are more and mightier than we.

Arminian

Pharaoh frames Israel’s blessing as a threat: “more and mightier than we.” Whether exaggeration or alarm, his speech turns fear into policy.

The pattern is familiar. Fear of the stranger becomes a reason for harsh measures. Arminian readers hear a pastoral warning against letting fear of others become cruelty, and notice that the very blessing God promised is what the world finds threatening.

Reformed

Pharaoh’s words unwittingly confirm God’s promise. The fruitfulness of v. 7 is so obvious that Israel’s enemy names it.

Reformed readers see the old conflict of Genesis 3:15 surfacing: the powers of the world set themselves against the people through whom blessing will come. Pharaoh’s fear is a backhanded witness to God’s faithfulness.

Exodus 1:10

10Come, let’s deal wisely with them, lest they multiply, and it happen that when any war breaks out, they also join themselves to our enemies and fight against us, and escape out of the land.”

Arminian

“Let’s deal wisely with them”—worldly shrewdness set against God’s purpose. Pharaoh fears war, alliance with enemies, and Israel leaving the land.

Ironically, Pharaoh names the outcome he dreads: Israel will go up out of the land. Arminian readers see human scheming that cannot finally thwart God, even while it does real harm to real people.

Reformed

Pharaoh’s “wisdom” is the wisdom of the world, which God makes foolish (1 Cor 1:20). His fear that Israel will “escape out of the land” anticipates the exodus itself.

Reformed readers note how God turns the schemes of the wicked toward his own ends. Pharaoh’s counsel is genuinely his, and genuinely evil, yet it serves the purpose he is trying to block.

Exodus 1:11

11Therefore they set taskmasters over them to afflict them with their burdens. They built storage cities for Pharaoh: Pithom and Raamses.

Arminian

Taskmasters and forced labor: Israel builds the store cities of Pithom and Raamses. The people of promise become state laborers.

Arminian readers do not soften the suffering. God’s people can endure real injustice for a long time. Scripture records it honestly, and God’s later hearing (2:24) is not a denial that it was real.

Reformed

The oppression is organized and deliberate, with taskmasters, burdens, and building projects for Pharaoh. The city names are recorded as part of a historical account.

Reformed readers hold together God’s sovereign plan and human guilt. The affliction was foretold (Gen 15:13), but Egypt’s cruelty is still cruelty, and God will judge it (Gen 15:14).

Exodus 1:12

12But the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and the more they spread out. They started to dread the children of Israel.

Arminian

“The more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied.” Oppression produces the opposite of what Pharaoh intended. Egypt begins to dread Israel.

Both traditions rejoice here. Arminian readers see God’s blessing persisting under pressure, a pattern the church has often seen when persecution has led to growth.

Reformed

The verse states the paradox outright: affliction and multiplication rise together. God’s promise cannot be suppressed by the methods of men.

Reformed readers see providence at work through, not merely despite, opposition. The dread that seizes Egypt shows that Israel’s growth is the work of a power greater than Pharaoh’s.

Exodus 1:13

13The Egyptians ruthlessly made the children of Israel serve,

Arminian

“Ruthlessly” stresses the harshness: labor imposed with rigor and without mercy.

Arminian readers hear God’s concern for the oppressed. The Law will later forbid Israel to rule over a fellow Israelite with rigor (Lev 25:43, 46), echoing this experience. Israel must not do to others what Egypt did to them.

Reformed

Egypt makes Israel serve with ruthlessness. The word for serve (ʿabad) will later describe Israel’s worship of God: the people are delivered from bondage to Pharaoh in order to serve the Lord (Exod 4:23).

Reformed readers note this theme of exchanging one master for another, from oppressive slavery to the service of God, which is perfect freedom.

Exodus 1:14

14and they made their lives bitter with hard service in mortar and in brick, and in all kinds of service in the field, all their service, in which they ruthlessly made them serve.

Arminian

Lives made bitter: mortar, brick, field labor, all of it imposed with rigor. The repetition of “service” presses the weight down.

Arminian readers note that bitterness of life is remembered at Passover in the bitter herbs (Exod 12:8). God does not ask his people to forget their suffering but to remember it as the backdrop of his deliverance.

Reformed

The verse closes the section with a heavy, repetitive description: service, service, service. The story slows to make the reader feel the burden.

Reformed readers see the misery of bondage as the dark backdrop to grace. Deliverance will come not because Israel earned it but because God remembers his covenant (2:24).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
