Passage

Exodus 18

Verses 13–27 · WEB

The next day Jethro sees Moses judging the people alone from morning to evening, warns that the load will wear him out, and advises Moses to teach God’s statutes and appoint capable, God-fearing, truthful men as rulers over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens, bringing only hard cases to Moses, as God commands, and Moses does it. This passage is largely common ground: both traditions see God’s wisdom in shared leadership, practical counsel, and character-qualified office.

Exodus 18:13

13On the next day, Moses sat to judge the people, and the people stood around Moses from the morning to the evening.

Arminian

Moses sits to judge the people from morning to evening. Arminian readers see a faithful servant whose diligence has outgrown his strength.

The people stand all day. Good intentions can still produce a system that burdens everyone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the burden of a single mediator. Moses’ load anticipates the need for an ordered ministry of many servants (Num 11:14–17).

Zeal is not the problem. The arrangement is.

Exodus 18:14

14When Moses’ father-in-law saw all that he did to the people, he said, “What is this thing that you do for the people? Why do you sit alone, and all the people stand around you from morning to evening?”

Arminian

Jethro asks why Moses sits alone while the people wait all day. Arminian readers note that wisdom can come from an outsider, and that a leader should be willing to hear it.

The question is kind and direct. Good counsel begins by naming what it sees.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s common gifts of wisdom are not limited to Israel’s leaders. God provides counsel through a Midianite father-in-law.

The question exposes a hidden cost: Moses’ habit leaves the people waiting as well.

Exodus 18:15

15Moses said to his father-in-law, “Because the people come to me to inquire of God.

Arminian

Moses explains that the people come to inquire of God. Arminian readers see a real and good desire: people want God’s will for their disputes.

Moses’ role is genuine. The issue is not whether he should serve but how.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses as the mediator of God’s word to the people. Inquiring of God through Moses points to the need for God’s revealed will in ordinary life.

That need is good. The question is how God’s word can reach everyone without crushing his servant.

Exodus 18:16

16When they have a matter, they come to me, and I judge between a man and his neighbor, and I make them know the statutes of God, and his laws.”

Arminian

Moses judges disputes and teaches God’s statutes and laws. Arminian readers note the two tasks: settling cases and forming the people’s understanding.

God’s statutes are already being taught before Sinai, so instruction precedes the formal covenant code.

Reformed

Reformed readers see judgment and teaching joined. Justice applies God’s law, and teaching shapes people who can walk in it.

The mention of statutes before chapter 20 shows that God’s will was already guiding Israel’s life.

Exodus 18:17

17Moses’ father-in-law said to him, “The thing that you do is not good.

Arminian

The thing that you do is not good. Arminian readers appreciate the honesty: a faithful work can be done in an unwise way.

Jethro speaks plainly because he cares about Moses. Loving counsel can be blunt.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that a man of God can be corrected about method without being accused of unfaithfulness. Counsel is a gift God uses (Prov 15:22).

Not good here means unsustainable, not sinful.

Exodus 18:18

18You will surely wear away, both you, and this people that is with you; for the thing is too heavy for you. You are not able to perform it yourself alone.

Arminian

You will wear away, both you and the people. Arminian readers see concern for Moses’ limits and for the people’s welfare. Human strength is finite, and wisdom respects that.

The load is too heavy to carry alone. Even God’s chosen servant cannot do everything himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers see creaturely limits acknowledged. God does not ask his servants to be everywhere at once.

A leader’s exhaustion harms the people too. Sharing the work is an act of love for both.

Exodus 18:19

19Listen now to my voice. I will give you counsel, and God be with you. You represent the people before God, and bring the causes to God.

Arminian

Jethro offers counsel and prays, God be with you. Moses is still to represent the people before God and bring their cases to him. Arminian readers note that the advice keeps Moses’ central role while freeing him for it.

The counsel is offered under God, not as a replacement for seeking God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses’ unique mediatorial role preserved. Others will help, but Moses still stands before God for the people.

The prayer, God be with you, places the plan under God’s blessing from the start.

Exodus 18:20

20You shall teach them the statutes and the laws, and shall show them the way in which they must walk, and the work that they must do.

Arminian

Moses is to teach the statutes and laws and show the way to walk and the work to do. Arminian readers stress instruction that equips people to live faithfully and handle smaller matters with wisdom.

Teaching multiplies the leader’s work by forming others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priority of the word: teaching God’s law shapes the whole community. Good teaching reduces disputes before they arise.

The way to walk and the work to do joins belief and practice, as later Scripture does (Mic 6:8).

Exodus 18:21

21Moreover you shall provide out of all the people able men which fear God: men of truth, hating unjust gain; and place such over them, to be rulers of thousands, rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens.

Arminian

Leaders must be able, God-fearing, truthful, and hate unjust gain. Arminian readers note that character comes before competence in the list of qualities, though both are needed.

The tiered structure of thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens is practical. Good order serves people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see qualifications for office that anticipate the New Testament’s lists for elders and deacons (Acts 6:3; 1 Tim 3:1–13). Fear of God and integrity are basic.

Shared rule under God’s law is a gift. Many hands with sound character protect the people from both overload and corruption.

Exodus 18:22

22Let them judge the people at all times. It shall be that every great matter they shall bring to you, but every small matter they shall judge themselves. So shall it be easier for you, and they shall share the load with you.

Arminian

Small matters go to the appointed judges, great matters to Moses. Arminian readers see sensible delegation that respects both the people’s needs and the leader’s limits.

Shared load is easier load. God’s work is meant to be carried together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a principle of ordered ministry: different gifts and offices for different needs (Rom 12:4–8).

The structure does not reduce Moses’ authority. It extends faithful judgment to every level of the camp.

Exodus 18:23

23If you will do this thing, and God commands you so, then you will be able to endure, and all these people also will go to their place in peace.”

Arminian

If God commands you so, then you will endure. Jethro submits his advice to God’s approval. Arminian readers note the humility: counsel is offered, but God’s word decides.

The goal is endurance for Moses and peace for the people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom placed under divine authority. Even good advice needs God’s sanction.

Peace for the people is the fruit of right order. God cares about the ordinary well-being of his community.

Exodus 18:24

24So Moses listened to the voice of his father-in-law, and did all that he had said.

Arminian

Moses listens and does what Jethro said. Arminian readers see humility in a great leader who accepts correction from his father-in-law.

Teachability is part of godly leadership.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Moses’ willingness to receive counsel is itself a grace. God’s servant is not above learning.

The change is put into practice, not only agreed with.

Exodus 18:25

25Moses chose able men out of all Israel, and made them heads over the people, rulers of thousands, rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens.

Arminian

Moses chooses able men and sets them over the people in tiers. Arminian readers see the plan carried out faithfully, with leadership shared among many.

Ordinary Israelites are trusted with real responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s people ordered for justice. The structure prepares Israel for life under the covenant soon to be given.

Deut 1:13–15 recalls that the people helped identify wise, respected men, so the appointments involved the community as well as Moses.

Exodus 18:26

26They judged the people at all times. They brought the hard cases to Moses, but every small matter they judged themselves.

Arminian

The judges serve at all times, bringing hard cases to Moses and settling small ones themselves. Arminian readers note that the system works: justice becomes accessible and Moses is relieved.

Shared ministry blesses both leaders and people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the plan bearing fruit. God’s wisdom through good order brings justice near to everyone.

Hard cases still reach Moses, so unity and consistency are preserved.

Exodus 18:27

27Moses let his father-in-law depart, and he went his way into his own land.

Arminian

Moses sends Jethro off, and he returns to his own land. Arminian readers note the gracious parting. Jethro’s contribution is remembered even though he does not stay with Israel (compare Num 10:29–32).

God’s people can receive blessing from those who walk with them for a season.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the episode closing with peace. An outsider has confessed the LORD and helped order Israel, and he leaves with honor.

The story moves on to Sinai. Jethro’s visit stands as a quiet picture of the nations blessing God and his people.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
