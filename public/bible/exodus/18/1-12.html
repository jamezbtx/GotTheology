Passage

Exodus 18

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Jethro, priest of Midian and Moses’ father-in-law, hears what God has done for Israel and brings Zipporah and her two sons to Moses at the Mountain of God. After hearing Moses’ account, Jethro blesses the LORD, confesses that he is greater than all gods, and shares a sacrificial meal with Aaron and the elders. Both traditions celebrate an outsider’s confession of Israel’s God, and they differ mainly in how they explain where such a response comes from.

Exodus 18:1

1Now Jethro, the priest of Midian, Moses’ father-in-law, heard of all that God had done for Moses and for Israel his people, how the LORD had brought Israel out of Egypt.

Arminian

Jethro hears of all that God has done for Moses and for Israel. Arminian readers note that God’s acts are public. The news of the exodus reaches the nations and invites them to respond (cf. Josh 2:9–11).

Jethro, the priest of Midian, is the same father-in-law who sheltered Moses earlier (2:16–21; 3:1). Old kindness now becomes the setting for a new confession.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s purpose that his name be known beyond Israel (9:16). The report about God’s acts is how God draws an outsider near.

His title, priest of Midian, raises the question of what Jethro worshiped before this point. The text does not settle it, and both traditions should be careful not to say more than it does.

Exodus 18:2

2Jethro, Moses’ father-in-law, received Zipporah, Moses’ wife, after he had sent her away,

Arminian

Jethro has been keeping Zipporah since Moses sent her away. The text does not say when or why she was sent back. Exodus 4:20–26 last shows her traveling with Moses toward Egypt. Arminian readers leave the gap unfilled.

What matters here is reunion. Moses’ family is restored to him in the wilderness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the gap and resist speculating. The narrative is concerned with bringing Moses’ household back to him at the place of God’s appearing.

God’s care extends to the families of those he calls. Moses’ public work does not erase his private life.

Exodus 18:3

3and her two sons. The name of one son was Gershom, for Moses said, “I have lived as a foreigner in a foreign land”.

Arminian

Gershom’s name recalls Moses’ years as a foreigner in Midian (2:22). Arminian readers hear honest memory: Moses named his son after a season of displacement.

Israel too has been foreigners in Egypt, and Moses’ family story mirrors the nation’s.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses’ exile carried in his son’s name. God’s people often live as strangers before they reach rest (Heb 11:13).

The name is a confession of dependence. Moses had no secure home except in God’s keeping.

Exodus 18:4

4The name of the other was Eliezer, for he said, “My father’s God was my help and delivered me from Pharaoh’s sword.”

Arminian

Eliezer’s name confesses that the God of Moses’ father was his help and delivered him from Pharaoh’s sword. Arminian readers note that Moses had given a testimony long before the plagues, in the name of a child.

Personal rescue and national rescue match each other. The God who saved Moses from Pharaoh has now saved Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear covenant continuity: My father’s God. Moses’ help comes from the God of his fathers, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (3:6).

The deliverance from Pharaoh’s sword (2:15) looks ahead to the larger deliverance. God’s faithfulness to one servant previews his faithfulness to his people.

Exodus 18:5

5Jethro, Moses’ father-in-law, came with Moses’ sons and his wife to Moses into the wilderness where he was encamped, at the Mountain of God.

Arminian

Jethro brings the family to Moses at the Mountain of God, where Moses first met God in the bush (3:1, 12). Arminian readers note the setting: the reunion takes place at the mountain where God promised to bring Israel to worship.

The order of events raises a question. Israel only arrives at Sinai in 19:1–2, and Deut 1:9–18 recalls the appointing of judges near the departure from Horeb, so some readers think this chapter is arranged by theme. The text’s meaning does not depend on resolving this.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the promise of 3:12 coming into view: Israel will serve God on this mountain. An outsider arrives at the same place, a hint of the nations coming to God.

The chronological question is real and can be held with humility. Exodus places the scene here so that Jethro’s welcome comes before the giving of the law.

Exodus 18:6

6He said to Moses, “I, your father-in-law Jethro, have come to you with your wife, and her two sons with her.”

Arminian

Jethro sends word ahead to announce himself and the family. Arminian readers note the courtesy and order: the reunion is arranged with care.

This is ordinary family life in the middle of a story about divine acts. God’s work does not cancel human relationships.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the plain dignity of family relationships honored in Scripture. The announcement prepares for a warm welcome.

Small details like this show that providence covers both small and large matters.

Exodus 18:7

7Moses went out to meet his father-in-law, and bowed and kissed him. They asked each other of their welfare, and they came into the tent.

Arminian

Moses goes out, bows, kisses Jethro, and they ask about each other’s welfare. Arminian readers see humility in Israel’s leader: he honors his father-in-law despite his own new prominence.

They enter the tent together. Fellowship sets the stage for testimony.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Moses’ greatness does not make him proud. He honors the man who once received him as a fugitive.

Godly leadership keeps ordinary respect. The fifth commandment’s spirit is visible before the commandment is given (20:12).

Exodus 18:8

8Moses told his father-in-law all that the LORD had done to Pharaoh and to the Egyptians for Israel’s sake, all the hardships that had come on them on the way, and how the LORD delivered them.

Arminian

Moses tells everything: what the LORD did to Pharaoh, the hardships on the way, and the LORD’s deliverance. Arminian readers see testimony as a means God uses to awaken faith in those who hear (Rom 10:17).

Moses includes the hardships as well as the victories. Honest testimony makes room for both.

Reformed

Reformed readers notice that Moses credits everything to the LORD. The story is about God’s acts for Israel’s sake, not about Moses’ leadership.

Testimony is the ordinary way God extends his fame. God uses Moses’ words to bring Jethro to a confession.

Exodus 18:9

9Jethro rejoiced for all the goodness which the LORD had done to Israel, in that he had delivered them out of the hand of the Egyptians.

Arminian

Jethro rejoices in the goodness the LORD has shown Israel. Arminian readers note the genuine response of joy: Jethro welcomes what he hears instead of resisting it.

Rejoicing in another people’s deliverance is a sign of a heart turning toward God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see joy as the fruit of God’s goodness truly perceived. Jethro’s delight is the beginning of worship.

The focus stays on the LORD’s goodness. True joy has its source in what God has done, not in human achievement.

Exodus 18:10

10Jethro said, “Blessed be the LORD, who has delivered you out of the hand of the Egyptians, and out of the hand of Pharaoh; who has delivered the people from under the hand of the Egyptians.

Arminian

Jethro blesses the LORD for delivering Israel from Egypt and Pharaoh. Arminian readers see an outsider using Israel’s language of praise and naming the LORD as the one who saved.

The repeated phrase, out of the hand of, stresses that rescue came from God alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear a doxology from a Midianite priest. God receives praise from the nations, as he intended in redeeming Israel (Ps 67:3–4).

Blessing God is the fitting response to grace. Jethro’s words turn the story into worship.

Exodus 18:11

11Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods because of the way that they treated people arrogantly.”

Arminian

Now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods. Arminian readers see God’s acts and Moses’ testimony as grace enabling Jethro to respond, and Jethro truly responds with knowledge and confession. Like Rahab and Naaman, he moves from hearing to confessing (Josh 2:11; 2 Kgs 5:15).

The reason given is how the Egyptians acted arrogantly against Israel. God humbles the proud (Prov 16:18).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God fulfilling his purpose that the nations would know he is the LORD (7:5; 14:4). Jethro’s Now I know is the fruit of God revealing himself, and God effectively brings him to confess.

Greater than all gods echoes the Song of the Sea (15:11). Whether Jethro was converting or deepening an earlier faith is debated, and the text leaves that open.

Key contrast Both celebrate Jethro’s confession as real and see God’s acts and testimony behind it. Arminian readers describe it as a free response to grace that could have been resisted. Reformed readers describe it as the result of God’s effective work, bringing Jethro to know him. Interpreters in both camps differ on whether this is Jethro’s conversion or a deepened faith.

Exodus 18:12

12Jethro, Moses’ father-in-law, took a burnt offering and sacrifices for God. Aaron came with all the elders of Israel, to eat bread with Moses’ father-in-law before God.

Arminian

Jethro brings a burnt offering and sacrifices to God, and Aaron and the elders eat with him before God. Arminian readers see a meal of fellowship around worship, with an outsider welcomed at the table.

The text does not make clear whether Jethro himself officiates. The emphasis is on shared worship before God, which anticipates the covenant meal in 24:11.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a Gentile included in worship before the law is given, a foretaste of the nations gathered to God (Isa 56:6–7).

The details of Jethro’s priestly role are not explained. Eating before God signals fellowship with God and peace among the worshipers.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
