Passage

Exodus 4

Verses 1–17 · WEB

Moses continues to resist the call, and God answers each objection in turn. Against the fear of unbelief, God gives three signs: the rod that becomes a snake, the hand made leprous and restored, and water from the river turned to blood. Against Moses’ slowness of speech, God claims the making of every mouth; when Moses still asks God to send someone else, God’s anger burns, yet he graciously appoints Aaron as spokesman.

Exodus 4:1

1Moses answered, “But, behold, they will not believe me, nor listen to my voice; for they will say, ‘The LORD has not appeared to you.’ ”

Arminian

Moses fears that the people will not believe him. Arminian readers take the objection seriously: belief is a real response that people can give or withhold, and God provides evidence to help it. Both traditions agree that Moses’ fear is understandable, though it sits awkwardly beside God’s promise in 3:18 that they would listen.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God has already said the elders will listen (3:18), so Moses’ objection reveals weak faith in God’s word. God nevertheless answers with patience. Both traditions agree that the signs which follow serve to confirm the message and to support faith, not to replace it.

Exodus 4:2

2The LORD said to him, “What is that in your hand?” He said, “A rod.”

Arminian

God begins with what Moses already holds: an ordinary shepherd’s rod. Arminian readers see God using ordinary means and ordinary people when they make themselves available. Both traditions note that the rod becomes “God’s rod” (4:20), an instrument of his power throughout the exodus story.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God taking up a common tool for his purposes, showing that power lies in him, not in the instrument. The question invites Moses to notice what God will do with what is in his hand. Shared ground: the rod becomes a visible sign of God’s authority.

Exodus 4:3

3He said, “Throw it on the ground.” He threw it on the ground, and it became a snake; and Moses ran away from it.

Arminian

The rod becomes a snake, and Moses flees. Arminian readers note the very human reaction of fear before a sign of power. Many interpreters connect the snake with Egyptian royal and religious imagery, though the text does not spell this out. Both traditions agree that God demonstrates authority over created things.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign power over creation displayed in a way that also humbles Moses. Some connect the snake to the serpent symbolism of Egyptian royalty, though the text itself does not say so. Both traditions agree the sign is meant to show that the LORD is master over what Egypt revered.

Exodus 4:4

4The LORD said to Moses, “Stretch out your hand, and take it by the tail.” He stretched out his hand, and took hold of it, and it became a rod in his hand.

Arminian

Taking a snake by the tail is risky, and Moses must obey before the transformation happens. Arminian readers see trust expressed in obedient action. Both traditions note that the sign is reversed at God’s command, demonstrating control both to produce and to undo the wonder.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God commands an act that requires faith and then honors it by restoring the rod. The miracle depends entirely on God’s word. Both traditions agree that obedience under God’s command is the pattern Moses must learn for the confrontations ahead.

Exodus 4:5

5“This is so that they may believe that the LORD, the God of their fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has appeared to you.”

Arminian

The purpose of the sign is stated clearly: so that the people may believe. Arminian readers stress that God supplies grounds for faith and genuinely seeks a believing response. Both traditions agree that the signs authenticate Moses as a messenger of the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that signs are given to confirm God’s word, though they also stress that faith ultimately depends on God’s work in the heart. The repetition of the patriarchs’ names roots the sign in covenant promise. Shared ground: the signs serve faith and point to the God of the fathers.

Exodus 4:6

6The LORD said furthermore to him, “Now put your hand inside your cloak.” He put his hand inside his cloak, and when he took it out, behold, his hand was leprous, as white as snow.

Arminian

The second sign is personal and alarming: Moses’ own hand becomes leprous. Arminian readers note that God shows power over disease and over the body. The Hebrew term rendered “leprous” covers a range of skin diseases. Both traditions see the sign as demonstrating God’s power to afflict and to heal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the sign God’s authority over life, health, and uncleanness. Moses experiences the power personally before he displays it. Both traditions note that the term translated “leprous” likely covers various skin conditions, and that the point is the sudden onset and reversal by God’s word.

Exodus 4:7

7He said, “Put your hand inside your cloak again.” He put his hand inside his cloak again, and when he took it out of his cloak, behold, it had turned again as his other flesh.

Arminian

The hand is restored, showing that God can heal as well as strike. Arminian readers see grace following judgment, a pattern repeated throughout Exodus. Both traditions agree that this sign, like the first, demonstrates God’s power to reverse what he has done.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God both wounds and heals, a theme later stated in Deuteronomy 32:39. The restoration is complete. Shared ground: the sign reveals God’s sovereign power over the body and confirms that Moses speaks for a God who controls life and health.

Exodus 4:8

8“It will happen, if they will not believe you or listen to the voice of the first sign, that they will believe the voice of the latter sign.

Arminian

God anticipates that some may not believe the first sign but will believe the second. Arminian readers see this as God accommodating human hesitation and offering further evidence to people who can genuinely respond. Both traditions agree that God is patient with weak faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers note God’s knowledge of how people will respond and his provision of multiple confirmations. The phrase “the voice of the sign” shows that signs speak a message. Both traditions agree that God graciously multiplies evidence for those who struggle to believe.

Exodus 4:9

9It will happen, if they will not believe even these two signs or listen to your voice, that you shall take of the water of the river, and pour it on the dry land. The water which you take out of the river will become blood on the dry land.”

Arminian

A third sign is held in reserve: water from the river becomes blood. The Nile was Egypt’s source of life, and the sign anticipates the first plague. Arminian readers see God’s patience in providing yet more evidence. Both traditions agree this sign foreshadows God’s judgment on Egypt.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the third sign as a preview of the plagues, showing God’s power over the river Egypt depended on. God’s provision of escalating signs shows his commitment to the mission. Shared ground: the signs authenticate Moses and anticipate God’s coming judgment.

Exodus 4:10

10Moses said to the LORD, “O Lord, I am not eloquent, neither before now, nor since you have spoken to your servant; for I am slow of speech, and of a slow tongue.”

Arminian

Moses objects that he is not eloquent. The WEB footnotes that “Lord” here translates Adonai, a title rather than the divine name. Arminian readers see an honest admission of limitation, though it becomes resistance. Both traditions agree that Moses’ focus on his own abilities misses the point of God’s presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Moses addresses God as Lord (Adonai, per the WEB footnote) while resisting his commission. His claim of slow speech may be humility, but it also reflects unbelief. Both traditions agree God’s call does not depend on natural gifts, though God does not dismiss human weakness.

Exodus 4:11

11The LORD said to him, “Who made man’s mouth? Or who makes one mute, or deaf, or seeing, or blind? Isn’t it I, the LORD?

Arminian

God answers by asserting that he made the human mouth and governs all human abilities. Arminian readers affirm God as Creator and sovereign over human capacities, while often reading the verse as stressing God’s ability to equip rather than a detailed decree behind every disability. Both traditions agree the point is that God can empower Moses.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a strong statement of God’s sovereignty over all human conditions, including disability, which comes under his providence. Yet the main point here is pastoral: the God who made the mouth can use it. Both traditions agree this verse is meant to answer Moses’ fear with God’s power and authority.

Exodus 4:12

12Now therefore go, and I will be with your mouth, and teach you what you shall speak.”

Arminian

God commands Moses to go with the promise, “I will be with your mouth.” Arminian readers see God offering enabling grace that Moses must accept. Both traditions agree the promise of presence is the answer to human weakness, echoing 3:12.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s promise of effectual help: God will teach Moses what to say. The command and the promise belong together. Both traditions agree that God’s presence makes Moses adequate for the task; the promise anticipates later assurances of divine help in speaking (compare Luke 12:11–12).

Exodus 4:13

13Moses said, “Oh, Lord, please send someone else.”

Arminian

Moses finally asks God to send someone else. Arminian readers see the real possibility of resisting God’s call; Moses is not a mere puppet. Both traditions agree that this request crosses a line from honest weakness into unwillingness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Moses’ reluctance is sinful unbelief, yet God does not abandon his chosen instrument. The narrative shows God’s persevering grace toward a reluctant servant. Both traditions agree that the request is a failure of trust after God has answered every objection.

Exodus 4:14

14The LORD’s anger burned against Moses, and he said, “What about Aaron, your brother, the Levite? I know that he can speak well. Also, behold, he is coming out to meet you. When he sees you, he will be glad in his heart.

Arminian

God’s anger burns, yet he provides Aaron. Arminian readers stress that God responds to human resistance and adjusts the means of his plan, while still achieving his purpose. Both traditions agree that God’s anger is real, but it is mixed with mercy, since Aaron is already on his way.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s anger at unbelief alongside his foreordained provision: Aaron is already coming to meet Moses. God’s plan is not frustrated by Moses’ weakness. Both traditions agree the verse shows both divine displeasure and divine mercy.

Exodus 4:15

15You shall speak to him, and put the words in his mouth. I will be with your mouth, and with his mouth, and will teach you what you shall do.

Arminian

God will be with both Moses’ and Aaron’s mouths and teach them what to do. Arminian readers see God working through cooperative human partnership. Both traditions agree that the message remains God’s even when it is spoken through Aaron.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God ensuring the faithful transmission of his word through human agents, a pattern relevant to how Reformed theology understands prophecy and inspiration. Shared ground: God’s word comes through human mouths without ceasing to be God’s word.

Exodus 4:16

16He will be your spokesman to the people. It will happen that he will be to you a mouth, and you will be to him as God.

Arminian

Aaron will be Moses’ spokesman, and Moses will be “as God” to him. Arminian readers see a structure of delegated authority, not divinization. Both traditions agree that Moses stands to Aaron as God stands to a prophet, giving the message that Aaron relays.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the language of being “as God” describes a role of authority and revelation, later echoed in 7:1. The prophetic pattern is clear: God to Moses, Moses to Aaron, Aaron to the people. Both traditions agree that this is functional language about speaking for God.

Exodus 4:17

17You shall take this rod in your hand, with which you shall do the signs.”

Arminian

The rod is to go with Moses as the instrument for the signs. Arminian readers see God equipping his servant with a visible tool that strengthens faith. Both traditions agree that the rod’s power lies entirely in God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the rod as a means through which God displays his power, without any power residing in the rod itself. Shared ground: the rod will become a recurring symbol of God’s authority throughout the exodus and wilderness narratives.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
