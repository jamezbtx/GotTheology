Passage

Exodus 4

Verses 18–26 · WEB

Moses takes leave of Jethro and sets out for Egypt with his family and the rod of God. On the way God previews the conflict ahead: Moses will perform the wonders, God will harden Pharaoh’s heart, and Pharaoh’s refusal to release Israel, God’s firstborn son, will end in the death of Pharaoh’s firstborn. The section closes with the brief and difficult lodging-place episode, in which Zipporah’s circumcision of her son averts the LORD’s threat against Moses.

Exodus 4:18

18Moses went and returned to Jethro his father-in-law, and said to him, “Please let me go and return to my brothers who are in Egypt, and see whether they are still alive.” Jethro said to Moses, “Go in peace.”

Arminian

Moses respectfully seeks Jethro’s permission before leaving. Arminian readers note that obedience to God’s call does not cancel ordinary family obligations. Jethro’s blessing, “Go in peace,” affirms Moses’ departure. Both traditions value the courtesy shown here.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providence smoothing Moses’ path through Jethro’s ready consent. Moses does not reveal the full commission, speaking only of seeing his brothers. Both traditions agree that the scene shows respect for family and that God’s call works through ordinary relationships.

Exodus 4:19

19The LORD said to Moses in Midian, “Go, return into Egypt; for all the men who sought your life are dead.”

Arminian

God reassures Moses that those who sought his life are dead. Arminian readers see God’s attentiveness to Moses’ fears. Both traditions agree that this removes one obstacle to Moses’ return and shows God’s care for his servant’s safety.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence in the timing: the deaths of Moses’ enemies clear the way. God orders events for his purposes. Shared ground: God speaks to Moses’ fear with concrete reassurance, and Matthew 2:20 uses similar wording for the return of the child Jesus from Egypt.

Exodus 4:20

20Moses took his wife and his sons, and set them on a donkey, and he returned to the land of Egypt. Moses took God’s rod in his hand.

Arminian

Moses sets out with his family and “God’s rod.” Arminian readers note that the ordinary shepherd’s staff is now consecrated to God’s purpose. Both traditions agree that Moses’ departure marks obedience after his earlier reluctance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the transfer of the rod from Moses’ possession to God’s service as a picture of vocation. Moses begins the journey in obedience. Both traditions agree the phrase “God’s rod” highlights that the coming signs are God’s work.

Exodus 4:21

21The LORD said to Moses, “When you go back into Egypt, see that you do before Pharaoh all the wonders which I have put in your hand, but I will harden his heart and he will not let the people go.

Arminian

God tells Moses that he will harden Pharaoh’s heart. Classical Arminian readers usually understand this as judicial hardening: God confirms and gives Pharaoh over to a resistance that is genuinely Pharaoh’s own, and the narrative also says Pharaoh hardened his heart. God’s action is real, but it is a response to Pharaoh’s settled opposition.

Reformed

Reformed readers take “I will harden his heart” as God’s sovereign ordination of Pharaoh’s hardening for the display of his glory, while insisting that God works through Pharaoh’s own will and is not the author of sin. Pharaoh acts freely, willingly, and culpably. Both affirm that God is just and Pharaoh responsible.

Key contrast Both agree that God is just, that Pharaoh is fully responsible, and that the narrative attributes hardening both to God and to Pharaoh himself. Arminian readers usually see God’s hardening as judicial, confirming a resistance that originates in Pharaoh; Reformed readers usually see it as God’s sovereign ordination working through Pharaoh’s own will, without making God the author of sin.

Exodus 4:22

22You shall tell Pharaoh, ‘The LORD says, Israel is my son, my firstborn,

Arminian

Israel is called God’s firstborn son, a striking statement of covenant relationship. Arminian readers stress God’s love for his people and his commitment to them. Both traditions agree that this sonship language grounds God’s claim on Israel and shapes the conflict with Pharaoh.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Israel’s sonship as an act of sovereign grace, a status given rather than earned. The language anticipates later themes of adoption in the New Testament. Both traditions agree that Israel’s identity as God’s son is the basis for God’s demand that Pharaoh release them.

Exodus 4:23

23and I have said to you, “Let my son go, that he may serve me;” and you have refused to let him go. Behold, I will kill your firstborn son.’ ”

Arminian

Pharaoh’s refusal to release God’s son will lead to the death of Pharaoh’s son. Arminian readers see a warning that leaves room for Pharaoh to respond, yet points to judgment if he refuses. Both traditions agree the threat anticipates the final plague and Passover.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the warning as announcing God’s righteous judgment, which will come because God has foreknown and ordained it. The principle of measure for measure is clear. Both traditions agree that the verse anticipates the final plague, and both read it as justice for Pharaoh’s oppression of Israel.

Exodus 4:24

24On the way at a lodging place, the LORD met Moses and wanted to kill him.

Arminian

This is one of the most difficult passages in Exodus. The LORD meets Moses and seeks to kill him, apparently because his son is uncircumcised. Arminian readers see the seriousness of covenant obedience even for God’s chosen servant. Both traditions acknowledge the obscurity and avoid overconfident explanations.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the seriousness of the covenant sign: the leader of the covenant people cannot neglect circumcision. The passage is obscure, and interpreters differ on details, including whom the LORD meets. Both traditions agree the main point is the danger of disregarding God’s covenant command.

Exodus 4:25

25Then Zipporah took a flint, and cut off the foreskin of her son, and cast it at his feet; and she said, “Surely you are a bridegroom of blood to me.”

Arminian

Zipporah acts decisively, circumcising her son with a flint and casting the foreskin at his feet. The phrase “bridegroom of blood” is difficult and much debated. Arminian readers note that Zipporah’s quick obedience averts judgment. Both traditions acknowledge uncertainty about the precise meaning of her words and actions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Zipporah fulfilling the covenant obligation and thus averting God’s wrath, perhaps foreshadowing the Passover blood. The meaning of “bridegroom of blood” remains uncertain, and “his feet” may refer to Moses or the child. Both traditions agree the episode emphasizes covenant obedience.

Exodus 4:26

26So he let him alone. Then she said, “You are a bridegroom of blood,” because of the circumcision.

Arminian

God relents, and Zipporah repeats the phrase, now linked explicitly to circumcision. Arminian readers see God’s mercy responding to obedience. Both traditions agree that the episode underlines the importance of the covenant sign before Moses confronts Pharaoh.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the averting of judgment through the blood of circumcision as a covenant pattern later developed in Passover. The narrator’s explanation shows the phrase is tied to circumcision. Both traditions agree that the passage remains mysterious in detail but clear in emphasis on covenant faithfulness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
