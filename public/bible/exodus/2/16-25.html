Passage

Exodus 2

Verses 16–25 · WEB

In Midian Moses defends the daughters of Reuel the priest at a well, is welcomed into the household, marries Zipporah, and names his son Gershom as a sojourner. Meanwhile Israel’s cry under bondage rises to God, who hears, remembers his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and sees.

Exodus 2:16

16Now the priest of Midian had seven daughters. They came and drew water, and filled the troughs to water their father’s flock.

Arminian

The priest of Midian has seven daughters who come to water their father’s flock. The setting echoes the well scenes of Genesis 24 and 29.

Arminian readers notice that God is at work outside Israel, in Midian. The Midianites were descendants of Abraham through Keturah (Gen 25:2).

Reformed

A well, daughters, and a stranger: readers of Genesis know what follows. The pattern signals God’s provision for a new household.

Reformed readers see providence guiding Moses to the very family that will shelter him for forty years.

Exodus 2:17

17The shepherds came and drove them away; but Moses stood up and helped them, and watered their flock.

Arminian

Shepherds drive the daughters away, but Moses stands up, helps them, and waters their flock. He again defends the weak.

Arminian readers see Moses’ character persisting in exile. His instinct to rescue the oppressed, now without violence, shows a man being shaped for leadership.

Reformed

Moses delivers the daughters from the shepherds. The verb “helped” (literally “saved”) anticipates his greater work.

Reformed readers see Moses as a type of the deliverer even in small things. God is forming his servant in humble acts of protection.

Exodus 2:18

18When they came to Reuel, their father, he said, “How is it that you have returned so early today?”

Arminian

Their father is named Reuel, a name often explained as “friend of God.” Elsewhere he is called Jethro (Exod 3:1; 18:1), and Numbers 10:29 mentions Hobab, son of Reuel the Midianite. The relationship among these names is debated; the WEB simply gives “Reuel” here.

Arminian readers note the name and the welcome. A Midianite priest who will later bless the Lord (Exod 18:10–11) appears with a name honoring God.

Reformed

Reuel’s question shows surprise: the daughters are home early, which hints that the shepherds’ bullying was routine.

The priest bears two names in Exodus, Reuel here and Jethro later; explanations include a personal name and a title, or a clan name. Reformed readers simply note that God prepares a place of refuge and later counsel (Exod 18) for Moses through this man.

Exodus 2:19

19They said, “An Egyptian delivered us out of the hand of the shepherds, and moreover he drew water for us, and watered the flock.”

Arminian

“An Egyptian delivered us.” To the daughters, Moses looks and sounds Egyptian. He did more than rescue them; he drew water for them.

Arminian readers see generous service. Moses went beyond defense to practical help, a pattern of leadership that serves.

Reformed

The daughters call Moses an Egyptian. His identity is ambiguous to outsiders, though he has chosen his people.

Reformed readers note the word “delivered,” which sounds the theme of the book. God’s deliverer is quietly at work even as a stranger in a foreign land.

Exodus 2:20

20He said to his daughters, “Where is he? Why is it that you have left the man? Call him, that he may eat bread.”

Arminian

Reuel rebukes his daughters for leaving the stranger behind and invites him to eat bread. Hospitality is honored.

Arminian readers see the welcome of strangers as part of God’s care. Moses, a fugitive, finds a table.

Reformed

The invitation to eat bread is generous hospitality. A family outside Israel shelters God’s servant.

Reformed readers see providence giving Moses a home and a table in exile, provided through the kindness of others.

Exodus 2:21

21Moses was content to dwell with the man. He gave Moses Zipporah, his daughter.

Arminian

Moses agrees to stay, and Reuel gives him his daughter Zipporah as wife. A fugitive becomes a husband and a member of a household.

Arminian readers see God’s kindness in ordinary gifts. Settled life in Midian is not a detour outside God’s care.

Reformed

Moses is content to dwell with the man. The marriage to Zipporah ties him to Midian for many years.

Reformed readers see the long wilderness years as part of God’s preparation. Moses will shepherd Jethro’s flock before he shepherds Israel (Exod 3:1).

Exodus 2:22

22She bore a son, and he named him Gershom, for he said, “I have lived as a foreigner in a foreign land.”

Arminian

Moses names his son Gershom, saying, “I have lived as a foreigner in a foreign land.” The WEB notes that “Gershom” sounds like the Hebrew for “an alien there.”

Arminian readers hear honest grief and displacement. Moses names his experience. Faith can include the lament of being far from home, and Israel will be told to love the stranger because they were strangers (Exod 22:21).

Reformed

The son’s name records Moses’ sense of exile. The WEB footnote explains the wordplay (ger, “sojourner”; sham, “there”).

Reformed readers see a link with the patriarchs, who lived as sojourners in a land of promise (Heb 11:9, 13). Moses shares their pilgrim condition. God’s people are strangers on earth, and their home is the promise of God.

Exodus 2:23

23In the course of those many days, the king of Egypt died, and the children of Israel sighed because of the bondage, and they cried, and their cry came up to God because of the bondage.

Arminian

Many days pass; the king of Egypt dies, but the bondage does not end. Israel sighs and cries out, and their cry rises to God.

Arminian readers see the importance of crying out to God. The text does not say the cry was addressed in faith, only that it came up to God, yet it matters. God hears the cry of the oppressed.

Reformed

The narrator speaks of the cry going up to God. The death of the king brings no relief, so the cry continues.

Reformed readers note that the cry is not said to be faith-filled. What moves the story is not the quality of Israel’s prayer but God’s faithfulness to his covenant (v. 24).

Exodus 2:24

24God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob.

Arminian

God heard their groaning and remembered his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. To “remember” in Scripture is not to recall something forgotten but to act on a commitment.

Arminian readers see a covenant-keeping God responding personally to his people’s groaning. The cry and God’s remembrance belong together: God makes promises, hears the cry of his people, and acts in history in faithful response.

Reformed

God remembers his covenant. The basis for the coming deliverance is God’s oath to the patriarchs, not Israel’s worthiness.

Reformed readers see the covenant of grace as the root of redemption. God has bound himself by promise, and when he “remembers,” his eternal purpose moves into action at the time he has appointed.

Key contrast Both confess that God is faithful to his covenant and acts to deliver his people. Arminians typically see God’s remembering as a responsive act toward the cry of his people within his covenant purpose, with human prayer playing a genuine part in the relationship. Reformed interpreters typically see it as the unfolding of God’s eternal decree at the appointed time, with the cry itself part of the means God ordained.

Exodus 2:25

25God saw the children of Israel, and God understood.

Arminian

“God saw the children of Israel, and God understood.” The Hebrew is literally “God knew.” He sees their suffering and knows them personally.

Arminian readers stress God’s compassion and relational knowledge. He is not distant. This prepares for the burning bush, where God says he has seen, heard, and knows their sorrows (Exod 3:7).

Reformed

The chapter ends with God seeing and knowing, the Hebrew verb unqualified. God’s knowledge is personal, covenantal, and active.

Reformed readers see God’s knowledge as both compassionate and sovereign. He knows his people (Amos 3:2; 2 Tim 2:19), and his knowing guarantees that their deliverance will come.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
