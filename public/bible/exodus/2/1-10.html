Passage

Exodus 2

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Under Pharaoh’s death decree a Levite couple has a son. His mother hides him and then sets him in a basket among the reeds; Pharaoh’s daughter finds him, his sister arranges for his own mother to nurse him, and the child raised in Pharaoh’s house is named Moses.

Exodus 2:1

1A man of the house of Levi went and took a daughter of Levi as his wife.

Arminian

A Levite man marries a Levite woman. The story moves from Pharaoh’s decree to one ordinary household, unnamed for now (later named Amram and Jochebed, Exod 6:20).

Arminian readers see how God works through faithful families. In a time of terror this couple marries and hopes, and their trust becomes the setting for deliverance.

Reformed

The verse is plain and quiet. The line of Levi, scattered in Jacob’s blessing (Gen 49:5–7), will produce the deliverer and later the priesthood.

Reformed readers note that God’s great works often begin in hidden places. Providence is at work in a marriage no one in Egypt would notice.

Exodus 2:2

2The woman conceived and bore a son. When she saw that he was a fine child, she hid him three months.

Arminian

She sees that the child is “fine” (literally “good”), and hides him for three months. Hebrews 11:23 credits Moses’ parents with faith: they were not afraid of the king’s command.

Arminian readers stress that faith acts. The mother’s courage and resourcefulness are not passive waiting; they are faith working through love, at great risk.

Reformed

“Fine” echoes God seeing that creation was “good” (Gen 1). Hebrews 11:23 commends the parents’ faith in hiding him.

Reformed readers see the faith of Moses’ parents as a gift of grace that moves them to act. God’s secret providence and their real courage work together to preserve the child.

Exodus 2:3

3When she could no longer hide him, she took a papyrus basket for him, and coated it with tar and with pitch. She put the child in it, and laid it in the reeds by the river’s bank.

Arminian

When hiding is no longer possible, she makes a papyrus basket, seals it with tar and pitch, and sets it among the reeds. The word for basket (tevah) is the same used for Noah’s ark.

Arminian readers see faith that does everything it can and then entrusts the outcome to God. In a sense she puts the boy in the river as Pharaoh ordered, but in a way that seeks his life.

Reformed

The tevah links Moses to Noah: a small ark carries one through deadly waters to preserve God’s purpose. The river of the decree (1:22) becomes the place of protection.

Reformed readers see providence threaded through each careful act. The mother’s planning is real, and God’s overruling care is greater.

Exodus 2:4

4His sister stood far off, to see what would be done to him.

Arminian

His sister stands at a distance to see what will happen. Her watchfulness will matter (v. 7).

Arminian readers note the faithful part played by a young girl. God’s purposes involve the attentive love of ordinary family members.

Reformed

The sister, usually identified with Miriam (Num 26:59), keeps watch. The narrator builds suspense: what will be done to him?

Reformed readers see a quiet picture of providence. The family watches and waits, while the reader knows God is watching over the child.

Exodus 2:5

5Pharaoh’s daughter came down to bathe at the river. Her maidens walked along by the riverside. She saw the basket among the reeds, and sent her servant to get it.

Arminian

Pharaoh’s own daughter comes to bathe and sees the basket among the reeds. She sends her servant to fetch it.

Arminian readers see how God works through people outside the covenant. Pharaoh’s daughter acts freely, and her choices become part of the rescue.

Reformed

Providence brings the daughter of the persecutor to the very spot. Of all the places along the Nile, she comes to this one.

Reformed readers do not see chance here. God orders times and places so that the house of Pharaoh will raise the one who will bring Pharaoh down.

Exodus 2:6

6She opened it, and saw the child, and behold, the baby cried. She had compassion on him, and said, “This is one of the Hebrews’ children.”

Arminian

She opens it, the baby cries, and she has compassion, knowing he is a Hebrew child. Compassion overrides her father’s decree.

Arminian readers treasure this moment. A human heart, moved by a baby’s cry, chooses mercy. Common grace and genuine human compassion are at work in an Egyptian princess.

Reformed

She recognizes the child as Hebrew and still pities him. Her compassion contradicts her father’s policy.

Reformed readers see God turning hearts as he wills (Prov 21:1) without violating them. Her mercy is truly hers, and it is also God’s means to preserve his servant.

Exodus 2:7

7Then his sister said to Pharaoh’s daughter, “Should I go and call a nurse for you from the Hebrew women, that she may nurse the child for you?”

Arminian

The sister boldly offers to find a Hebrew nurse. Her quick thinking turns a rescue into a homecoming.

Arminian readers note the initiative of a child. Faithful courage in small moments can open the way for God’s larger deliverance.

Reformed

The sister’s offer is timely and wise. The narrator shows God’s provision through human action at exactly the right moment.

Reformed readers see the sister as an instrument of providence. Her words are hers, but the timing belongs to God.

Exodus 2:8

8Pharaoh’s daughter said to her, “Go.” The young woman went and called the child’s mother.

Arminian

“Go,” says Pharaoh’s daughter. The girl brings the child’s own mother.

Arminian readers smile at the irony: the mother who had to give up her child receives him back. God delights to restore what faith entrusts to him.

Reformed

The child’s mother is brought in, unknown to Pharaoh’s daughter. The plan unfolds through one simple word.

Reformed readers see how God works through ordinary decisions to bring his purposes about with quiet precision.

Exodus 2:9

9Pharaoh’s daughter said to her, “Take this child away, and nurse him for me, and I will give you your wages.” The woman took the child, and nursed him.

Arminian

Pharaoh’s daughter hires the child’s own mother to nurse him and promises wages. The mother is paid to raise her own son.

Arminian readers see the generosity of God’s providence. Not only is the child spared; the family is restored and even provided for.

Reformed

Pharaoh’s household pays for the deliverer’s upbringing. The decree meant to destroy Israel’s sons ends up supporting one of them.

Reformed readers see God making the wrath of man serve him (Ps 76:10). The mother nurses her child under the protection of the very court that ordered his death.

Exodus 2:10

10The child grew, and she brought him to Pharaoh’s daughter, and he became her son. She named him Moses, and said, “Because I drew him out of the water.”

Arminian

The child grows, is brought to Pharaoh’s daughter, and becomes her son. She names him Moses, explaining, “Because I drew him out of the water.” The WEB notes that “Moses” sounds like the Hebrew for “draw out”; many scholars also note a similar Egyptian word meaning “son” or “born.”

Arminian readers see a life marked from the start by rescue. Raised in Egypt’s court, Moses gained Egyptian learning (Acts 7:22), all of which God would later use.

Reformed

Pharaoh’s daughter names him for being drawn out of the water. The WEB footnote observes the wordplay with the Hebrew “draw out” (mashah); the Hebrew form of the name can also suggest “one who draws out,” fitting the one who will draw Israel through the sea. An Egyptian background for the name is also often suggested.

Reformed readers see the name as a sign of God’s purpose. The child drawn out of the water will lead God’s people out through the waters.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
