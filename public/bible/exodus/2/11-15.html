Passage

Exodus 2

Verses 11–15 · WEB

Grown up in Pharaoh’s household, Moses goes out to his Hebrew brothers, kills an Egyptian he sees beating one of them, and is rebuffed the next day by a Hebrew who asks who made him a judge. With Pharaoh seeking his life, he flees to Midian.

Exodus 2:11

11In those days, when Moses had grown up, he went out to his brothers and saw their burdens. He saw an Egyptian striking a Hebrew, one of his brothers.

Arminian

Grown up, Moses goes out to “his brothers” and sees their burdens. Though raised as an Egyptian, he identifies with Israel. Hebrews 11:24–26 describes his refusal to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter.

Arminian readers see a real choice of solidarity. Moses could have stayed comfortable in the palace; he chooses to see the suffering of his people and to share it.

Reformed

The text twice calls the Hebrews “his brothers.” Moses’ identity is with the people of promise, not the palace.

Reformed readers, with Hebrews 11, see faith at work in this choice: he counted reproach for Christ greater riches than the treasures of Egypt. The faith is real and freely exercised, and it is also God’s gift.

Exodus 2:12

12He looked this way and that way, and when he saw that there was no one, he killed the Egyptian, and hid him in the sand.

Arminian

Moses looks around, sees no one, kills the Egyptian, and hides him in the sand. The act is secret and violent.

Interpreters differ on how to judge it. Some see a zeal for justice that was premature and in his own strength; Stephen says Moses supposed his brothers understood that God was delivering them by his hand (Acts 7:25). Arminian readers note that God later uses Moses despite this, not because of it.

Reformed

The furtive glance “this way and that way” suggests Moses knew the act would not stand in the open. Interpreters have long debated whether this was justified defense or rash action.

Reformed readers often see a man with a true calling acting before God’s time and by his own means. God will train him in the wilderness for forty years before sending him. Grace does not endorse every act of God’s servants, but it does not abandon them.

Exodus 2:13

13He went out the second day, and behold, two men of the Hebrews were fighting with each other. He said to him who did the wrong, “Why do you strike your fellow?”

Arminian

The next day Moses tries to stop two Hebrews from fighting: “Why do you strike your fellow?” He seeks to bring peace among his own people.

Arminian readers see a heart for justice among God’s people, not only against Egypt. Pastoral wisdom recognizes that the hardest conflicts are often within the community.

Reformed

Moses intervenes between brothers. The deliverer must deal not only with Egypt’s oppression but with Israel’s internal discord.

Reformed readers see a preview of the long story of Israel’s quarrelling. The people God saves are still sinners who need a mediator.

Exodus 2:14

14He said, “Who made you a prince and a judge over us? Do you plan to kill me, as you killed the Egyptian?” Moses was afraid, and said, “Surely this thing is known.”

Arminian

“Who made you a prince and a judge over us?” The Hebrew rejects Moses, and his secret is out. Moses is afraid.

Arminian readers see that God’s chosen deliverer is first rejected by his own people. Stephen draws the line from this rejection to Israel’s later rejection of Christ (Acts 7:35). Moses’ fear shows a real man learning that zeal alone is not enough.

Reformed

The question is ironic: God will indeed make Moses ruler and deliverer (Acts 7:35). The man who rejects him speaks better than he knows.

Reformed readers note the pattern of the rejected deliverer that Stephen applies to Christ. Moses’ fear also reveals that his own attempt at deliverance has failed. God will send him in his own way and time.

Exodus 2:15

15Now when Pharaoh heard this thing, he sought to kill Moses. But Moses fled from the face of Pharaoh, and lived in the land of Midian, and he sat down by a well.

Arminian

Pharaoh hears and seeks to kill Moses. Moses flees to Midian and sits down by a well.

Arminian readers see failure that is not final. Moses’ flight looks like the end of his calling, yet it is not. God meets people in exile and prepares them for what is next.

Reformed

The deliverer becomes a fugitive. Pharaoh’s hostility now falls on Moses personally.

Reformed readers see the wilderness as God’s school. The well recalls Genesis scenes where God provided wives for Isaac and Jacob (Gen 24; 29). Even in flight, God’s providence is preparing the next chapter.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
