Passage

Exodus 23

Verses 20–33 · WEB

The Book of the Covenant ends with promise and warning. God sends an angel in whom his name dwells to guard Israel on the way and bring them into the prepared place. If Israel listens and obeys, God will oppose their enemies, bless their bread and water, remove sickness, and drive out the nations little by little. Israel must make no covenant with the peoples or their gods, because their gods would become a snare.

Exodus 23:20

20“Behold, I send an angel before you, to keep you by the way, and to bring you into the place which I have prepared.

Arminian

God sends an angel before Israel to keep them on the way and bring them to the place he has prepared. Arminian readers see God’s faithful guidance accompanying a people who must still walk the road and choose to follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress God’s gracious provision: the destination is prepared and the guide is given. God’s purpose to bring his people in is secure, and the means include his guardian presence.

Exodus 23:21

21Pay attention to him, and listen to his voice. Don’t provoke him, for he will not pardon your disobedience, for my name is in him.

Arminian

Listen to him and do not provoke him, for he will not pardon your disobedience, for my name is in him. Arminian readers stress that the guide’s authority is divine and that rebellion has real consequences. Interpreters differ on whether this angel is a created messenger bearing God’s authority or a manifestation of God himself. Many Arminian readers, like many Reformed readers, have seen the Son here.

Reformed

Reformed readers often identify the angel bearing God’s name with the Angel of the LORD and, following many church fathers and Reformers, see a preincarnate appearance of the Son. Others in the tradition are more cautious. All agree the angel speaks with God’s full authority.

Exodus 23:22

22But if you indeed listen to his voice, and do all that I speak, then I will be an enemy to your enemies, and an adversary to your adversaries.

Arminian

If you listen and do all that I speak, God will be an enemy to your enemies. Arminian readers note the conditional form: covenant blessings are tied to Israel’s responsive obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree the promise is conditional within the covenant administration. They frame obedience as the fruit of grace and note that God’s commitment to his people underlies even the conditions.

Key contrast Both read the verse as a real covenant condition. Arminian readers typically treat such conditions as showing that human response genuinely affects whether blessing comes. Reformed readers typically treat them as the ordained way God brings his people to the blessings he has purposed, and they ground the obedience in his grace.

Exodus 23:23

23For my angel shall go before you, and bring you in to the Amorite, the Hittite, the Perizzite, the Canaanite, the Hivite, and the Jebusite; and I will cut them off.

Arminian

God’s angel will bring Israel to the peoples of Canaan, and I will cut them off. Arminian readers often read the judgment in light of Genesis 15:16, where the iniquity of the Amorites was not yet complete, so they see it as long-delayed justice rather than arbitrary favoritism.

Reformed

Reformed readers also appeal to Genesis 15:16 and Deuteronomy 9:4–5: Israel receives the land not for its righteousness but by God’s promise, while the nations are judged for their wickedness. Both traditions treat the conquest as a unique, God-commanded act in redemptive history, not a pattern for today.

Exodus 23:24

24You shall not bow down to their gods, nor serve them, nor follow their practices, but you shall utterly overthrow them and demolish their pillars.

Arminian

Israel must not bow to Canaan’s gods or follow their practices, but demolish their pillars. Arminian readers see the danger of gradual assimilation and the need for decisive breaks with idolatry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the first and second commandments applied. Pillars were sacred stones tied to Canaanite worship, and allowing them to remain invites false worship.

Exodus 23:25

25You shall serve the LORD your God, and he will bless your bread and your water, and I will take sickness away from among you.

Arminian

Serve the LORD your God and he will bless your bread and water and remove sickness. Arminian readers see God’s generous response to covenant faithfulness in the ordinary goods of life.

Reformed

Reformed readers read these blessings within the Sinai covenant’s temporal, land-centered promises. Christians have generally not taken them as a promise of guaranteed health today, while affirming that God cares for the bodily needs of his people.

Exodus 23:26

26No one will miscarry or be barren in your land. I will fulfill the number of your days.

Arminian

No miscarriage or barrenness, and a full number of days. Arminian readers see the fullness of covenant blessing extending to family and long life.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God as Lord over life, conception, and length of days, as Psalm 139:16 confesses. The promise belongs to Israel’s national covenant blessings.

Exodus 23:27

27I will send my terror before you, and will confuse all the people to whom you come, and I will make all your enemies turn their backs to you.

Arminian

God will send his terror before Israel and confuse their enemies. Arminian readers note that victory is God’s doing, as Rahab later testifies in Joshua 2:9–11.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God working directly on the hearts of the nations, as he did with Pharaoh, to accomplish his purpose for his people.

Exodus 23:28

28I will send the hornet before you, which will drive out the Hivite, the Canaanite, and the Hittite, from before you.

Arminian

The hornet will drive out the Hivite, the Canaanite, and the Hittite. Interpreters debate whether this means literal insects, a figure for panic, or a hostile power such as Egypt. Joshua 24:12 says the hornet drove out kings without Israel’s sword or bow.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise acknowledge the uncertainty while stressing the point: God uses whatever means he chooses to fight for his people.

Exodus 23:29

29I will not drive them out from before you in one year, lest the land become desolate, and the animals of the field multiply against you.

Arminian

God will not drive them out in one year, lest the land become desolate and wild animals multiply. Arminian readers see God’s wise, practical care: the timing of the gift fits Israel’s capacity to receive it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence ordering even the pace of fulfillment for his people’s good. Delay is not failure but wisdom.

Exodus 23:30

30Little by little I will drive them out from before you, until you have increased and inherit the land.

Arminian

Little by little, until Israel increases and inherits the land. Arminian readers often draw a pastoral parallel: growth in holiness and in God’s promises is gradual and calls for continued faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers also commonly draw the parallel to progressive sanctification, a lifelong work God carries forward step by step in his people.

Exodus 23:31

31I will set your border from the Red Sea even to the sea of the Philistines, and from the wilderness to the River; for I will deliver the inhabitants of the land into your hand, and you shall drive them out before you.

Arminian

God sets Israel’s borders from the Red Sea to the sea of the Philistines and from the wilderness to the River, usually understood as the Euphrates. The ideal extent was approached under David and Solomon, as 1 Kings 4:21 notes.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s faithfulness to the promise of Genesis 15:18. Many also see the land promise expanding in the New Testament toward the inheritance of the whole renewed earth, as in Romans 4:13.

Exodus 23:32

32You shall make no covenant with them, nor with their gods.

Arminian

No covenant with them or their gods. Arminian readers see that exclusive loyalty to the LORD rules out alliances that would bind Israel to rival worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the antithesis between the covenant people and idolatry. Covenants in the ancient world often invoked the parties’ gods, so a treaty would compromise Israel’s worship.

Exodus 23:33

33They shall not dwell in your land, lest they make you sin against me, for if you serve their gods, it will surely be a snare to you.”

Arminian

They shall not dwell in the land, lest they make you sin. Their gods would be a snare. Arminian readers note the real possibility of apostasy that Israel’s later history in Judges confirms: covenant people can be led away.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the danger as real and the warning as one of God’s means to keep his people. Judges 2 shows the consequences when the command was neglected.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
