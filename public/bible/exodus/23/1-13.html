Passage

Exodus 23

Verses 1–13 · WEB

The Book of the Covenant (Exodus 20:22–23:33) continues with laws for honest courts, kindness toward an enemy’s animals, justice for the poor and the alien, the sabbatical year, and the weekly Sabbath. It closes with a warning to keep all God has said and not to invoke other gods. Israel, once oppressed in Egypt, is to be a just and merciful people under the LORD.

Exodus 23:1

1“You shall not spread a false report. Don’t join your hand with the wicked to be a malicious witness.

Arminian

A false report and a malicious witness corrupt justice at its root, applying the ninth commandment to the courtroom. Arminian readers note that the law addresses Israelites as responsible moral agents who can refuse to join the wicked. Covenant life calls for truthful speech in public and in private.

Reformed

Reformed readers read these judicial laws as applications of the moral law summarized in the Ten Commandments. God’s people are to reflect his truthfulness. Many Reformed writers distinguish Israel’s civil statutes from the abiding moral principles they express, here the duty of truthful witness.

Exodus 23:2

2“You shall not follow a crowd to do evil. You shall not testify in court to side with a multitude to pervert justice.

Arminian

Israel must not follow a crowd to do evil or side with the majority to pervert justice. Arminian readers see personal responsibility that peer pressure cannot excuse: each witness answers to God for his own testimony.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that justice is measured by God’s law, not by numbers. Conscience is bound to God’s word even against the majority. Both traditions see a lasting principle for believers in every community.

Exodus 23:3

3You shall not favor a poor man in his cause.

Arminian

You shall not favor a poor man in his cause balances 23:6. Pity must not tilt a verdict any more than wealth may. Arminian readers note that God’s concern for the poor works through fair courts, not partiality, as Leviticus 19:15 also says.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s impartial justice as the pattern for judges. Partiality in either direction denies that the case should be decided on its merits before God, who shows no favoritism.

Exodus 23:4

4“If you meet your enemy’s ox or his donkey going astray, you shall surely bring it back to him again.

Arminian

If you meet your enemy’s ox or donkey going astray, bring it back. Arminian readers see the law already shaping love for enemies, in active goodwill that does not wait for friendship. Jesus’ teaching in Matthew 5:44 draws out what is present here in seed form.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see that the law demands more than outward restraint. It requires positive kindness toward those who wrong us. The moral law reaches the heart and anticipates the fuller command to love enemies.

Exodus 23:5

5If you see the donkey of him who hates you fallen down under his burden, don’t leave him. You shall surely help him with it.

Arminian

Help even the donkey of him who hates you when it has fallen under its load. Arminian readers note compassion for the animal and the practical reconciliation that shared work can bring between estranged neighbors.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law restraining personal hatred and requiring mercy, as Proverbs 25:21 echoes. Godliness is shown in concrete help, even to an opponent.

Exodus 23:6

6“You shall not deny justice to your poor people in their lawsuits.

Arminian

Israel must not deny justice to the poor in their lawsuits. Arminian readers stress God’s protective concern for the vulnerable, who are most easily ignored or wronged in court.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that God defends the poor and that judges answer to him for how they treat the weak. Together with 23:3 the principle is straight justice for rich and poor alike.

Exodus 23:7

7“Keep far from a false charge, and don’t kill the innocent and righteous; for I will not justify the wicked.

Arminian

Keep far from a false charge and do not kill the innocent, for I will not justify the wicked. Arminian readers see God’s own justice as the guarantee behind the law: human courts may fail, but he will not acquit the guilty.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the language of justification: God will not declare the wicked righteous. Many relate this to Romans 4:5, where God justifies the ungodly through faith in Christ, and to Romans 3:26, which says God remains just in doing so because of Christ’s atoning work.

Exodus 23:8

8“You shall take no bribe, for a bribe blinds those who have sight and perverts the words of the righteous.

Arminian

No bribe, for a bribe blinds those who have sight. Arminian readers note how sin distorts moral perception even in people who know the truth. Judges must guard their own hearts and hands.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the corrupting power of self-interest on judgment, which shows how easily the righteous can be twisted. Integrity in office is a matter of fearing God, as Deuteronomy 16:19 repeats.

Exodus 23:9

9“You shall not oppress an alien, for you know the heart of an alien, since you were aliens in the land of Egypt.

Arminian

You know the heart of an alien, since you were aliens in Egypt. Arminian readers see memory of redemption shaping ethics: those shown mercy must show it to outsiders.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that grace received is the motive for obedience. Redemption from Egypt grounds Israel’s treatment of strangers, just as the gospel grounds Christian love for others.

Exodus 23:10

10“For six years you shall sow your land, and shall gather in its increase,

Arminian

For six years Israel sows and gathers. Arminian readers note that ordinary work is affirmed as part of covenant life before the sabbatical rest is commanded.

Reformed

Reformed readers see labor as a calling under God. The pattern of six and one echoes creation and the Sabbath command and orders time under God’s rule.

Exodus 23:11

11but the seventh year you shall let it rest and lie fallow, that the poor of your people may eat; and what they leave the animal of the field shall eat. In the same way, you shall deal with your vineyard and with your olive grove.

Arminian

In the seventh year the land rests so that the poor and even the wild animals may eat. Arminian readers see the sabbatical year as trust and generosity, since Israel releases its claim on the land’s produce for the sake of others.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the land is the LORD’s, as Leviticus 25:23 says, and Israel holds it as a steward. The sabbatical year teaches dependence on God’s provision and care for the poor.

Exodus 23:12

12“Six days you shall do your work, and on the seventh day you shall rest, that your ox and your donkey may have rest, and the son of your servant, and the alien may be refreshed.

Arminian

The weekly Sabbath gives rest to ox, donkey, servant, and alien. Arminian readers note its humane purpose: rest is a gift for the weakest in the household, not only a ritual duty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Sabbath as a creation ordinance renewed in the covenant, with mercy for servants and animals built in. Reformed traditions have often carried its principle into the Lord’s Day, though views on how it applies vary within the tradition.

Exodus 23:13

13“Be careful to do all things that I have said to you; and don’t invoke the name of other gods or even let them be heard out of your mouth.

Arminian

Be careful to do all I have said, and do not even mention other gods. Arminian readers see covenant faithfulness requiring exclusive loyalty in speech as well as worship, guarding the heart from drift.

Reformed

Reformed readers read this as an application of the first commandment. God alone is to be honored, and invoking other gods dishonors him. True worship begins with refusing every rival.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
