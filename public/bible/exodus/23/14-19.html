Passage

Exodus 23

Verses 14–19 · WEB

Israel is to keep three annual feasts before the LORD: Unleavened Bread in the month Abib, recalling the exodus; Harvest, the first fruits of the field; and Ingathering at the year’s end. All males are to appear before the Lord GOD three times a year, and no one is to come empty-handed. Brief rules follow on sacrificial blood and fat, the best first fruits, and not boiling a young goat in its mother’s milk.

Exodus 23:14

14“You shall observe a feast to me three times a year.

Arminian

Three times a year Israel is to keep a feast to the LORD. Arminian readers see worship structured into the calendar as regular, joyful acts of remembering and responding to God’s goodness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God sets the terms of his own worship. The feasts are his appointment, not human invention, and they order Israel’s life around his works.

Exodus 23:15

15You shall observe the feast of unleavened bread. Seven days you shall eat unleavened bread, as I commanded you, at the time appointed in the month Abib (for in it you came out of Egypt), and no one shall appear before me empty.

Arminian

Unleavened Bread recalls the exodus in the month Abib. No one shall appear before me empty means worship includes giving back from what God has given. Arminian readers see grateful response at the heart of the feast.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the feast as a memorial of redemption and, with Passover, a type fulfilled in Christ, as 1 Corinthians 5:7–8 says. Coming with gifts acknowledges God as Redeemer and Provider.

Exodus 23:16

16And the feast of harvest, the first fruits of your labors, which you sow in the field; and the feast of ingathering, at the end of the year, when you gather in your labors out of the field.

Arminian

The feasts of Harvest and Ingathering, later called Weeks and Booths, celebrate the land’s first fruits and final yield. Arminian readers see thanksgiving for ordinary provision as part of faithful life with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence over seasons and harvest honored in worship. Many Christian readers note that Pentecost, the later name for the feast of Weeks, became the day the Spirit was poured out in Acts 2.

Exodus 23:17

17Three times in the year all your males shall appear before the Lord GOD.

Arminian

All males appear before the Lord GOD three times a year. Arminian readers note the gathering of the whole covenant people before their sovereign Lord, with representatives standing for each household.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the title Lord GOD, sovereign Master and covenant LORD. Israel appears before its King in the assembly he summons.

Exodus 23:18

18“You shall not offer the blood of my sacrifice with leavened bread. The fat of my feast shall not remain all night until the morning.

Arminian

Sacrificial blood is not to be offered with leaven, and the fat of the feast is not to remain until morning. Arminian readers see reverence for holy things and careful obedience in worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God regulating the details of sacrifice. Many note that leaven often symbolizes corruption, though the precise reason for each rule is not explained in the text.

Exodus 23:19

19You shall bring the first of the first fruits of your ground into the house of the LORD your God. “You shall not boil a young goat in its mother’s milk.

Arminian

The first of the first fruits belongs in the LORD’s house: God receives the best, not leftovers. Do not boil a young goat in its mother’s milk is repeated in 34:26 and Deuteronomy 14:21. Its reason is uncertain. Proposals include avoiding a pagan rite, showing humane respect for the bond of life, or keeping categories of life and death distinct. The text does not say.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress giving God the first and best. On the young goat they acknowledge uncertainty about its original purpose. Later Jewish tradition built its separation of meat and milk on it, while Christian interpreters have usually read it as one of Israel’s ceremonial laws.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
