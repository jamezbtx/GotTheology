Passage

Exodus 10

Verses 1–20 · WEB

The eighth plague opens with God telling Moses why he has hardened Pharaoh: so his signs may be shown and told to future generations. Pharaoh’s own officials beg him to give in, and he offers to let only the men go. Locusts strip Egypt bare. Pharaoh confesses sin and asks for prayer, the locusts are blown into the sea, and the LORD hardens Pharaoh’s heart again.

Exodus 10:1

1The LORD said to Moses, “Go in to Pharaoh, for I have hardened his heart and the heart of his servants, that I may show these my signs among them;

Arminian

God says, “I have hardened his heart and the heart of his servants” (Hebrew kbd, made heavy, the same verb used of Pharaoh’s own act in 9:34). Classical Arminian readers see God confirming in judgment what Pharaoh and his servants chose in 9:34, giving them over to their own stubbornness (cf. Romans 1:24–28) so that his signs may be displayed.

God’s hardening here serves revelation, not a reversal of Pharaoh’s will.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God claims the hardening as his own act, using the same verb that described Pharaoh’s self-hardening a verse earlier (9:34). Both are true together: Pharaoh freely and sinfully hardened his heart, and God was sovereignly at work in that hardening to accomplish his purpose of displaying his signs.

The purpose clause, “that I may show these my signs,” shows God directing even resistance toward his glory.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm that the same act is described as Pharaoh’s (9:34) and God’s (10:1), and that its purpose is to display God’s signs. Classical Arminians typically read God’s act as judicial confirmation of Pharaoh’s prior choice. Reformed theology typically sees God’s sovereign purpose and Pharaoh’s free sin as concurrent, with God’s hardening not merely following Pharaoh’s but governing it.

Exodus 10:2

2and that you may tell in the hearing of your son, and of your son’s son, what things I have done to Egypt, and my signs which I have done among them; that you may know that I am the LORD.”

Arminian

The signs are for Israel’s children and grandchildren: “that you may know that I am the LORD.” The exodus becomes the story every generation tells (cf. 12:26–27; 13:14).

Arminians stress that remembering what God has done calls each new generation to trust him for themselves.

Reformed

Reformed readers see covenant nurture here: God’s mighty acts are handed down so that children know the LORD. Faith is shaped by remembering redemption.

Shared ground: the exodus is a teaching story for God’s people, and parents are charged to pass it on.

Exodus 10:3

3Moses and Aaron went in to Pharaoh, and said to him, “This is what the LORD, the God of the Hebrews, says: ‘How long will you refuse to humble yourself before me? Let my people go, that they may serve me.

Arminian

“How long will you refuse to humble yourself before me?” The question exposes Pharaoh’s pride and still calls him to humility.

Arminian readers hear genuine appeal in “how long”: God is still addressing a person who could humble himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the question as exposing culpability. Even under God’s hardening, the refusal to humble himself is Pharaoh’s own.

Both agree that pride is the core issue, and the call to humility is real.

Exodus 10:4

4Or else, if you refuse to let my people go, behold, tomorrow I will bring locusts into your country,

Arminian

The warning is specific: locusts tomorrow. Again, time is given.

God’s pattern of warning before judgment shows his patience.

Reformed

The specificity reveals God’s sovereign control of nature and time.

Shared ground: the warning makes Pharaoh’s choice clear.

Exodus 10:5

5and they shall cover the surface of the earth, so that one won’t be able to see the earth. They shall eat the residue of that which has escaped, which remains to you from the hail, and shall eat every tree which grows for you out of the field.

Arminian

The locusts will eat what the hail left (9:31–32). Egypt’s remaining food supply is at stake.

Arminians note the escalating cost of refusal, still offered as reason to relent.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the plague completing what the hail began, a deliberate, measured sequence of judgment.

Both note how the plagues build on one another.

Exodus 10:6

6Your houses shall be filled, and the houses of all your servants, and the houses of all the Egyptians, as neither your fathers nor your fathers’ fathers have seen, since the day that they were on the earth to this day.’ ” He turned, and went out from Pharaoh.

Arminian

The locusts will fill every house, unlike anything seen in living memory. Moses turns and leaves without waiting for an answer.

Moses’ departure leaves Pharaoh to weigh the warning.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the unprecedented scale as marking God’s hand. Moses’ abrupt exit shows the warning stands on God’s word alone.

Shared ground: the message is delivered and the choice left with Pharaoh.

Exodus 10:7

7Pharaoh’s servants said to him, “How long will this man be a snare to us? Let the men go, that they may serve the LORD, their God. Don’t you yet know that Egypt is destroyed?”

Arminian

Pharaoh’s own servants plead with him: “Don’t you yet know that Egypt is destroyed?” Even those whose hearts were hardened with his (10:1) now see the folly of resistance.

Arminians note that pressure and counsel come from every side, heightening Pharaoh’s responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the servants, though hardened in 9:34 and 10:1, now speak with clarity. God’s hardening does not make people incapable of seeing facts; it concerns the heart’s refusal to bow.

Shared ground: Pharaoh resists even his own counselors.

Exodus 10:8

8Moses and Aaron were brought again to Pharaoh, and he said to them, “Go, serve the LORD your God; but who are those who will go?”

Arminian

Pharaoh calls Moses back and appears to yield, but immediately asks who will go. He wants to negotiate terms.

Arminians see partial obedience offered in place of real submission.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Pharaoh seeking to keep control even while appearing to give way.

Both agree that bargaining with God’s command is a form of resistance.

Exodus 10:9

9Moses said, “We will go with our young and with our old. We will go with our sons and with our daughters, with our flocks and with our herds; for we must hold a feast to the LORD.”

Arminian

Moses insists everyone must go: young and old, sons and daughters, flocks and herds. Worship of the LORD involves the whole community, including children.

Arminian readers see the inclusive scope of God’s call to his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers often point to the inclusion of children in the worshiping community, a theme that matters in Reformed covenant theology.

Both agree that Moses refuses to divide the people or their worship.

Exodus 10:10

10He said to them, “The LORD be with you if I let you go with your little ones! See, evil is clearly before your faces.

Arminian

Pharaoh’s reply is sarcastic: “The LORD be with you if I let you go with your little ones!” He suspects evil intent. He mocks the very blessing he refuses to allow.

Arminians see the hardened heart expressed in contempt.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Pharaoh’s scorn as the fruit of a heart set against God.

Shared ground: Pharaoh’s words show he understands what is at stake and still refuses.

Exodus 10:11

11Not so! Go now you who are men, and serve the LORD; for that is what you desire!” Then they were driven out from Pharaoh’s presence.

Arminian

Pharaoh offers that only the men may go, likely to keep the families as a guarantee of return, and drives Moses and Aaron out.

Arminians see a half-measure that is really refusal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Pharaoh keeping his grip on God’s people while posing as reasonable.

Both agree partial obedience is disobedience when God has commanded all.

Exodus 10:12

12The LORD said to Moses, “Stretch out your hand over the land of Egypt for the locusts, that they may come up on the land of Egypt, and eat every herb of the land, even all that the hail has left.”

Arminian

God commands Moses to stretch out his hand for the locusts. The plague is clearly God’s response to Pharaoh’s refusal.

The command follows promptly, showing God’s word does not wait on Pharaoh’s terms.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God acting through his servant to do what he announced.

Shared ground: God keeps his word.

Exodus 10:13

13Moses stretched out his rod over the land of Egypt, and the LORD brought an east wind on the land all that day, and all night; and when it was morning, the east wind brought the locusts.

Arminian

God uses an east wind blowing all day and night to bring the locusts. Ordinary means serve God’s purpose.

Arminians and Reformed alike see God working through natural forces.

Reformed

Reformed theology emphasizes providence: God governs wind and creature to accomplish his will.

Both note the means does not make the event less God’s work.

Exodus 10:14

14The locusts went up over all the land of Egypt, and rested in all the borders of Egypt. They were very grievous. Before them there were no such locusts as they, nor will there ever be again.

Arminian

The locusts are unparalleled, past or future. The language underlines how extraordinary this judgment is.

The event is a sign God wants remembered (v. 2).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s power displayed on an unforgettable scale.

Shared ground: the uniqueness of the plague testifies to God’s hand.

Exodus 10:15

15For they covered the surface of the whole earth, so that the land was darkened, and they ate every herb of the land, and all the fruit of the trees which the hail had left. There remained nothing green, either tree or herb of the field, through all the land of Egypt.

Arminian

Nothing green remains in all Egypt. The land itself goes dark under the swarm. Egypt’s food and future are stripped.

Arminians see the cost of persistent resistance to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see comprehensive judgment on a nation that defied God.

Both note the totality of the devastation.

Exodus 10:16

16Then Pharaoh called for Moses and Aaron in haste, and he said, “I have sinned against the LORD your God, and against you.

Arminian

Pharaoh summons Moses “in haste” and confesses sin against God and against Moses and Aaron. The confession is more personal than in 9:27.

Arminians see that Pharaoh is still able to acknowledge sin, though his later actions show the acknowledgment did not lead to a changed will.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a confession driven by distress, which does not prove genuine repentance (cf. 9:27, 34).

Shared ground: words of confession are not the same as turning to God.

Exodus 10:17

17Now therefore please forgive my sin again, and pray to the LORD your God, that he may also take away from me this death.”

Arminian

“Please forgive my sin again” and “take away… this death.” Pharaoh wants relief from the consequence. The word “again” shows a pattern.

Arminians see God’s willingness to hear even this request as part of his patience.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Pharaoh seeks removal of the plague, not reconciliation with God.

Both agree his words reveal desperation more than surrender.

Exodus 10:18

18Moses went out from Pharaoh, and prayed to the LORD.

Arminian

Moses prays. His intercession continues, even for a repeatedly faithless ruler.

Arminians see God’s servant modeling persistent prayer and patience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see prayer as God’s appointed means, through which he displays his power once again.

Shared ground: intercession for enemies is right.

Exodus 10:19

19The LORD sent an exceedingly strong west wind, which took up the locusts, and drove them into the Red Sea. There remained not one locust in all the borders of Egypt.

Arminian

A strong west wind drives every locust into the Red Sea (Hebrew yam suph). Not one remains. The rescue is as complete as the judgment.

God’s control of the wind foreshadows his greater act at the sea (14:21).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same sovereign power that brought the locusts removing them entirely.

Both note the sea where the locusts disappear will be the site of Egypt’s final defeat.

Exodus 10:20

20But the LORD hardened Pharaoh’s heart, and he didn’t let the children of Israel go.

Arminian

“But the LORD hardened Pharaoh’s heart” (chazaq, strengthened). Arminian readers read this as further judicial hardening: God confirms the stubbornness Pharaoh has shown again and again, especially after the relief of 9:34.

The word “but” contrasts God’s mercy in removing the locusts with Pharaoh’s continued refusal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God actively continuing what he announced in 4:21, accomplishing his purpose through Pharaoh’s refusal, while Pharaoh remains fully responsible.

The verse echoes 9:12; the theological questions there apply here too.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
