Passage

Exodus 10

Verses 21–29 · WEB

The ninth plague brings three days of darkness so thick it can be felt, while Israel has light. Pharaoh offers to let everyone go but the livestock; Moses refuses to leave a hoof behind. The LORD hardens Pharaoh’s heart, and Pharaoh threatens Moses with death if he ever sees his face again.

Exodus 10:21

21The LORD said to Moses, “Stretch out your hand toward the sky, that there may be darkness over the land of Egypt, even darkness which may be felt.”

Arminian

Darkness “which may be felt” comes without warning. Egypt honored the sun as divine, so a darkness that blots out the sun strikes at the heart of Egyptian confidence (cf. 12:12).

Arminian readers see God revealing himself as the true Lord over the powers Egypt trusted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s judgment on Egypt’s gods (12:12; Numbers 33:4), showing that the LORD alone governs light and darkness (cf. Genesis 1:3–5).

Shared ground: the plague confronts false worship.

Exodus 10:22

22Moses stretched out his hand toward the sky, and there was a thick darkness in all the land of Egypt for three days.

Arminian

Three days of thick darkness cover Egypt. The duration stresses the severity.

The darkness anticipates the deeper darkness of the final plague.

Reformed

Reformed readers see creation reversed: the light God called into being is withdrawn from Egypt.

Both note the ominous tone as judgment nears its climax.

Exodus 10:23

23They didn’t see one another, and nobody rose from his place for three days; but all the children of Israel had light in their dwellings.

Arminian

No one can see or move, “but all the children of Israel had light in their dwellings.” The distinction is striking.

Arminians see God’s faithfulness to his covenant people.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see a picture of God’s people as children of light amid darkness (cf. Isaiah 9:2; John 8:12).

Shared ground: the distinction keeps testifying who belongs to the LORD.

Exodus 10:24

24Pharaoh called to Moses, and said, “Go, serve the LORD. Only let your flocks and your herds stay behind. Let your little ones also go with you.”

Arminian

Pharaoh concedes more: the children may go, but the flocks and herds must stay. Again he bargains.

Arminians see partial concessions that still fall short of obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Pharaoh keeping a hold on Israel, since without livestock they would likely have to return.

Both agree conditional obedience is refusal.

Exodus 10:25

25Moses said, “You must also give into our hand sacrifices and burnt offerings, that we may sacrifice to the LORD our God.

Arminian

Moses insists on sacrifices and burnt offerings. Worship of the LORD requires what God commands.

Arminians see worship defined by God, not by Pharaoh.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the principle that God determines how he is to be worshiped.

Shared ground: worship cannot be dictated by an enemy of God.

Exodus 10:26

26Our livestock also shall go with us. Not a hoof shall be left behind, for of it we must take to serve the LORD our God; and we don’t know with what we must serve the LORD, until we come there.”

Arminian

“Not a hoof shall be left behind… we don’t know with what we must serve the LORD, until we come there.” Israel must go fully dependent on God’s direction.

Arminians see trust: Israel will learn what worship requires as they follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see full surrender to God’s claim: nothing belongs to Pharaoh, and Israel must wait on God’s word for how to worship.

Both agree God’s claim on his people is total.

Exodus 10:27

27But the LORD hardened Pharaoh’s heart, and he wouldn’t let them go.

Arminian

“But the LORD hardened Pharaoh’s heart, and he wouldn’t let them go.” The same pattern as 9:12 and 10:20.

Arminian readers continue to see judicial hardening, God giving Pharaoh over to the refusal he has chosen.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s purpose proceeding toward the final plague and the exodus, through Pharaoh’s free and culpable refusal.

The repeated phrasing sets up the climax in chapters 11–12.

Exodus 10:28

28Pharaoh said to him, “Get away from me! Be careful to see my face no more; for in the day you see my face you shall die!”

Arminian

Pharaoh drives Moses away and threatens death if he sees his face again. His heart has hardened into open hostility.

Arminians see the end of the negotiation and the cost of repeated refusal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Pharaoh’s rage as the fruit of a heart set against God and his messenger.

Shared ground: Pharaoh rejects God’s word and the one who brings it.

Exodus 10:29

29Moses said, “You have spoken well. I will see your face again no more.”

Arminian

Moses agrees: “I will see your face again no more.” His words in 11:4–8 seem to belong to this same audience, since 11:8 ends with him leaving Pharaoh in hot anger. Exodus 12:31 says Pharaoh called for Moses and Aaron by night, so interpreters differ on whether they met face to face again or Pharaoh sent word.

Arminians see the solemn closing of an opportunity long extended.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses’ word as God’s verdict on Pharaoh’s defiance, with judgment now imminent.

Both agree the scene marks a turning point before the final plague.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
