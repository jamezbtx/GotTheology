Passage

Exodus 35

Verses 1–3 · WEB

After the golden calf crisis and the renewal of the covenant in chapters 32–34, Moses gathers Israel to begin building the tabernacle. He opens with the Sabbath command, repeating 31:12–17, so that even holy work stops for holy rest. Christians in both traditions hold differing views on how the Sabbath carries into the new covenant; those views cut across the Arminian/Reformed line.

Exodus 35:1

1Moses assembled all the congregation of the children of Israel, and said to them, “These are the words which the LORD has commanded, that you should do them.

Arminian

Moses gathers the whole community to hear what the LORD commanded. Arminian readers note the setting after the golden calf and covenant renewal: a restored people is again called to obedience. Grace and command come together as the people resume the work God intended.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God renewing his relationship with a people who had sinned grievously and then giving them his word again. Obedience follows forgiveness rather than earning it. Both traditions read the gathering as a fresh start grounded in God’s mercy.

Exodus 35:2

2‘Six days shall work be done, but on the seventh day there shall be a holy day for you, a Sabbath of solemn rest to the LORD: whoever does any work in it shall be put to death.

Arminian

Six days of work are followed by a holy Sabbath of rest, with death as the penalty for working. Arminian readers see the seriousness of honoring God’s holy time. Placing the command before tabernacle construction teaches that even sacred labor must stop for rest.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Sabbath as a sign of the covenant (31:13–17), set before the building work. The severe penalty belongs to Israel’s covenant law. Views on how Sabbath observance continues for Christians vary within both traditions, so this should not be assigned to one camp.

Exodus 35:3

3You shall kindle no fire throughout your habitations on the Sabbath day.’ ”

Arminian

No fire is to be kindled in their dwellings on the Sabbath. Arminian readers see the command applied to ordinary household life, not only to large projects. Interpreters discuss whether the rule targets cooking, craft work, or both, and the text does not explain further.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a concrete application of Sabbath rest in daily life. Numbers 15:32–36 shows how seriously Israel treated Sabbath breaking. Christians in both traditions differ on how such specific regulations apply today, and those differences do not follow the Arminian/Reformed divide.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
