Passage

Exodus 35

Verses 30–35 · WEB

Moses announces that the LORD has called Bezalel of Judah and filled him with the Spirit of God for craftsmanship, joined by Oholiab of Dan as a fellow worker and teacher. The passage repeats 31:1–11. Both traditions agree that artistic and practical skill can be a gift of the Spirit for the service of God’s dwelling.

Exodus 35:30

30Moses said to the children of Israel, “Behold, the LORD has called by name Bezalel the son of Uri, the son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah.

Arminian

Moses announces that the LORD has called Bezalel of Judah by name. Arminian readers see God personally choosing and equipping someone for a specific task. Calling to service is God’s initiative.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s sovereign calling of a particular person for a particular work. The language repeats 31:2. Both traditions agree that this is a vocational calling to service, not a statement about salvation.

Exodus 35:31

31He has filled him with the Spirit of God, in wisdom, in understanding, in knowledge, and in all kinds of workmanship;

Arminian

Bezalel is filled with the Spirit of God for wisdom, understanding, knowledge, and craftsmanship. Arminian readers see the Spirit empowering practical skill, not only prophecy or leadership. Artistic work can be Spirit-enabled service.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Spirit as the source of every good gift, including craftsmanship. This verse is often cited in Christian reflection on the arts. Both traditions share this reading.

Exodus 35:32

32and to make skillful works, to work in gold, in silver, in bronze,

Arminian

He is equipped to work in gold, silver, and bronze. Arminian readers see specific technical abilities given for God’s purposes. Skill is a gift to be developed and used.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God gifting concrete abilities for his work. Both traditions affirm the goodness of material craft in God’s service. Metalwork for the ark, lampstand, and altars will depend on these abilities.

Exodus 35:33

33in cutting of stones for setting, and in carving of wood, to work in all kinds of skillful workmanship.

Arminian

He can cut stones and carve wood in all kinds of skillful work. Arminian readers see breadth of ability matching the variety of the project. God provides what the work requires.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God supplying comprehensive skill for the task he commanded. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. The Spirit’s gifting reaches wood and stone as well as precious metals.

Exodus 35:34

34He has put in his heart that he may teach, both he and Oholiab, the son of Ahisamach, of the tribe of Dan.

Arminian

God gives Bezalel and Oholiab of Dan the ability to teach. Arminian readers see gifts shared and multiplied through instruction. Skill is passed on so that many can join the work.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see teaching as a gift God gives for building up others. Pairing a worker from Judah with one from Dan suggests the whole people sharing in the work. Both traditions see this as shared ground.

Exodus 35:35

35He has filled them with wisdom of heart to work all kinds of workmanship, of the engraver, of the skillful workman, and of the embroiderer, in blue, in purple, in scarlet, and in fine linen, and of the weaver, even of those who do any workmanship, and of those who make skillful works.

Arminian

They are filled with wisdom of heart for engraving, design, embroidery, and weaving. Arminian readers see a wide range of crafts honored as gifts from God. The work of artisans is sacred service.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s generosity in equipping workers for every kind of craft. Both traditions affirm that ordinary vocations can be callings from God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
