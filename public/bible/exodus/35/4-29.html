Passage

Exodus 35

Verses 4–29 · WEB

Moses invites a freewill offering for the tabernacle and calls skilled workers to make everything the LORD commanded in chapters 25–31. The people respond generously, men and women alike, rulers and ordinary households. Both traditions see here a shared picture: God stirs hearts, and his people give freely and gladly for the place where he will dwell among them.

Exodus 35:4

4Moses spoke to all the congregation of the children of Israel, saying, “This is the thing which the LORD commanded, saying,

Arminian

Moses introduces the instructions as what the LORD commanded. Arminian readers see the people invited to participate in God’s purposes through obedience. The work that follows is not human invention but a response to God’s word.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s command as the foundation for the building project. The tabernacle originates in divine instruction, not human religious creativity. Both traditions read the verse as establishing the authority behind the work.

Exodus 35:5

5‘Take from among you an offering to the LORD. Whoever is of a willing heart, let him bring it as the LORD’s offering: gold, silver, bronze,

Arminian

The offering is to come from those with a willing heart, beginning with gold, silver, and bronze. Arminian readers stress that the gift is voluntary: God desires glad generosity rather than compelled payment. This echoes the earlier instruction in 25:2.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see willing giving as the fitting response of a people God has redeemed and forgiven. The willingness itself is welcomed by God and, as later verses say, stirred by him. Both traditions agree that this offering is free, not a tax.

Exodus 35:6

6blue, purple, scarlet, fine linen, goats’ hair,

Arminian

Colored yarns, fine linen, and goats’ hair are listed. Arminian readers see that ordinary household materials, not only precious metals, have a place in God’s work. Many people can contribute something.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a wide range of gifts serving the one project. The colors and fabrics anticipate the curtains and garments described later. Both traditions see diverse resources gathered for God’s dwelling.

Exodus 35:7

7rams’ skins dyed red, sea cow hides, acacia wood,

Arminian

Dyed rams’ skins, hides, and acacia wood are listed. Arminian readers see practical materials for coverings and frames. The WEB renders one item as sea cow hides; the Hebrew term is uncertain, and translations differ on what animal is meant.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see durable materials for the structure. Uncertainty about the exact animal behind the hides should be acknowledged rather than resolved by speculation. Both traditions see the list simply as provision for the work.

Exodus 35:8

8oil for the light, spices for the anointing oil and for the sweet incense,

Arminian

Oil for light and spices for anointing oil and incense are listed. Arminian readers see materials for ongoing worship, not only construction. The tabernacle is meant to be a place of continual service before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see light, anointing, and incense as part of the worship God ordained in chapters 27, 30, and elsewhere. These items serve the priestly ministry. Both traditions read them as provisions for regular worship.

Exodus 35:9

9onyx stones, and stones to be set for the ephod and for the breastplate.

Arminian

Onyx and other stones for the ephod and breastplate are listed. Arminian readers see the most precious materials devoted to the priestly garments that represent the tribes before God. Costly gifts serve the people’s representation in worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect these stones with the high priest bearing Israel’s names before the LORD (28:9–21). Christians in both traditions see the priest’s representative role fulfilled in Christ, a shared reading drawn from Hebrews.

Exodus 35:10

10“ ‘Let every wise-hearted man among you come, and make all that the LORD has commanded:

Arminian

Every wise-hearted person is invited to come and make what the LORD commanded. Arminian readers see skill as something to be offered willingly in service. God invites human ability into his work rather than bypassing it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see skill as a gift to be used for God’s purposes. The wise-hearted are those equipped for the task, and later verses attribute that wisdom to the LORD. Both traditions affirm the dignity of skilled labor in worship.

Exodus 35:11

11the tabernacle, its outer covering, its roof, its clasps, its boards, its bars, its pillars, and its sockets;

Arminian

The list begins with the tabernacle itself and its structural parts. Arminian readers see the scale of the project and the many hands it will need. Each piece contributes to a dwelling place for God among his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a structure ordered according to God’s design. The detail reflects careful attention to his instructions. Both traditions read the list as a summary of chapters 26–27 now being put into practice.

Exodus 35:12

12the ark, and its poles, the mercy seat, the veil of the screen;

Arminian

The ark, its poles, the mercy seat, and the veil are named. Arminian readers see the most holy furnishings at the heart of the project. The mercy seat is where God promised to meet with Israel (25:22).

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the ark and mercy seat as central to God’s presence and atonement. Christians in both traditions read Romans 3:25 and Hebrews 9 as connecting this imagery with Christ. This is shared ground.

Exodus 35:13

13the table with its poles and all its vessels, and the show bread;

Arminian

The table, its vessels, and the bread of the presence are listed. Arminian readers see a sign of fellowship and provision before God. The bread set before the LORD reminded Israel of his continual care.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the table as a symbol of communion between God and his people. Both traditions read it within the tabernacle’s worship without needing to settle every detail of its symbolism.

Exodus 35:14

14the lamp stand also for the light, with its vessels, its lamps, and the oil for the light;

Arminian

The lampstand with its lamps and oil is listed. Arminian readers see light kept burning in God’s dwelling. The lampstand provided light for priestly service in the holy place.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the lampstand as part of the ordered worship God commanded. Some Christian readers connect light imagery with Christ and the Spirit; such typology is shared rather than divided along Arminian/Reformed lines.

Exodus 35:15

15and the altar of incense with its poles, the anointing oil, the sweet incense, the screen for the door, at the door of the tabernacle;

Arminian

The incense altar, anointing oil, incense, and door screen are listed. Arminian readers see prayer and consecration symbolized in the tabernacle’s furnishings. Revelation 8:3–4 later links incense with the prayers of the saints.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see incense associated with prayer and the anointing oil with consecration for service. Both traditions treat these connections as shared biblical imagery rather than a point of dispute.

Exodus 35:16

16the altar of burnt offering, with its grating of bronze, its poles, and all its vessels, the basin and its base;

Arminian

The bronze altar for burnt offerings, its grating, and the basin are listed. Arminian readers see sacrifice and cleansing at the entrance to worship. Approach to God requires atonement and purification.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see sacrifice and washing as necessary for approaching a holy God. Christians in both traditions read the sacrifices as pointing to Christ’s once-for-all offering in Hebrews 9–10. This is shared ground.

Exodus 35:17

17the hangings of the court, its pillars, their sockets, and the screen for the gate of the court;

Arminian

The court hangings, pillars, sockets, and gate screen are listed. Arminian readers see a boundary marking holy space within the camp. The court defined where worship took place.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see ordered space reflecting God’s holiness. The gate provided a designated entry. Both traditions read the court as part of the structure’s careful design.

Exodus 35:18

18the pins of the tabernacle, the pins of the court, and their cords;

Arminian

Tent pins and cords for the tabernacle and court are listed. Arminian readers see that even small, humble items are part of God’s commanded work. No contribution is too ordinary to matter.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see completeness in obedience, extending to the smallest details. The pins and cords held the structure together. Both traditions value faithfulness in small things.

Exodus 35:19

19the finely worked garments for ministering in the holy place—the holy garments for Aaron the priest, and the garments of his sons—to minister in the priest’s office.’ ”

Arminian

The holy garments for Aaron and his sons are listed. Arminian readers see the priests set apart for ministry in clothing made with care. The garments were for glory and beauty (28:2).

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the priests’ garments as marks of their consecrated office. Christians in both traditions see Christ as the great high priest, drawing on Hebrews. The reading is shared.

Exodus 35:20

20All the congregation of the children of Israel departed from the presence of Moses.

Arminian

The congregation leaves Moses’ presence. Arminian readers see a pause in which each person decides how to respond. The departure prepares for the voluntary offerings that follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the people dispersing to gather their gifts. The narrative moves from instruction to response. Both traditions read the verse as a simple transition.

Exodus 35:21

21They came, everyone whose heart stirred him up, and everyone whom his spirit made willing, and brought the LORD’s offering for the work of the Tent of Meeting, and for all of its service, and for the holy garments.

Arminian

Those whose hearts stirred them and whose spirits made them willing bring offerings. Arminian readers emphasize the genuine, uncoerced response of the givers. God moves hearts, and people freely respond to that moving.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm free and willing giving, and they emphasize God as the one who stirs hearts. Both traditions hold divine initiative and real human willingness together here. The verse is shared ground, not a point of contrast.

Exodus 35:22

22They came, both men and women, as many as were willing-hearted, and brought brooches, earrings, signet rings, and armlets, all jewels of gold; even every man who offered an offering of gold to the LORD.

Arminian

Men and women bring gold jewelry as offerings. Arminian readers note the contrast with the golden calf, when gold was given for an idol (32:2–4). Now gold is offered willingly for the true worship of God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the contrast between idolatry and true worship. Repentance shows itself in redirected generosity. Both traditions read the verse as a fruit of covenant renewal.

Exodus 35:23

23Everyone with whom was found blue, purple, scarlet, fine linen, goats’ hair, rams’ skins dyed red, and sea cow hides, brought them.

Arminian

Those with yarns, linen, goats’ hair, and skins bring them. Arminian readers see each person giving from what they actually have. The offering is proportioned to resources, not imposed equally.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God accepting gifts according to what each possesses. The principle resembles 2 Corinthians 8:12, which Christians in both traditions share. No contrast is involved.

Exodus 35:24

24Everyone who offered an offering of silver and bronze brought the LORD’s offering; and everyone with whom was found acacia wood for any work of the service, brought it.

Arminian

Silver, bronze, and acacia wood are brought. Arminian readers see broad participation across the community. Every material needed for the work is supplied by the people’s willing hands.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God providing for his work through his people’s offerings. Both traditions read the verse as part of a shared picture of generous response.

Exodus 35:25

25All the women who were wise-hearted spun with their hands, and brought that which they had spun: the blue, the purple, the scarlet, and the fine linen.

Arminian

Skilled women spin colored yarn and fine linen. Arminian readers see women’s skill and labor honored as direct contributions to God’s dwelling. Their work is named, not hidden.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the dignity of women’s craftsmanship in worship. The text explicitly credits their wisdom. Both traditions affirm the value of this contribution.

Exodus 35:26

26All the women whose heart stirred them up in wisdom spun the goats’ hair.

Arminian

Women whose hearts stirred them spin goats’ hair. Arminian readers see willingness and wisdom joined in their work. They give their time and skill as well as materials.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God-given wisdom expressed through stirred hearts. The phrase echoes verse 21. Both traditions read the women’s labor as shared ground. Their contribution is part of the same willing response.

Exodus 35:27

27The rulers brought the onyx stones and the stones to be set for the ephod and for the breastplate;

Arminian

The rulers bring onyx and setting stones. Arminian readers see leaders giving the costliest items rather than only directing others. Leadership is shown in generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see leaders participating in the offering. Their gifts serve the priestly garments. Both traditions value leaders who give as well as lead.

Exodus 35:28

28with the spice and the oil for the light, for the anointing oil, and for the sweet incense.

Arminian

The rulers also bring spices and oil. Arminian readers see leaders supplying materials for worship and anointing. Their contribution supports the ongoing life of the sanctuary.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see provision for the worship God ordained. The verse continues the rulers’ generous giving. Both traditions see no point of contrast. The leaders’ gifts serve the worship of the whole people.

Exodus 35:29

29The children of Israel brought a free will offering to the LORD; every man and woman whose heart made them willing to bring for all the work, which the LORD had commanded to be made by Moses.

Arminian

Israel brings a freewill offering, every man and woman whose heart made them willing. Arminian readers stress that the offering is the people’s free response. God’s purposes advance through willing cooperation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see free giving, and they note that the willingness is itself a work of God in his people. Both traditions agree that the giving is free and that God is honored by it. Any difference in explaining willingness is one of emphasis here, not substance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
