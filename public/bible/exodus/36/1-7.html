Passage

Exodus 36

Verses 1–7 · WEB

The skilled workers receive the people’s offerings and begin the work. The giving is so generous that the craftsmen report a surplus, and Moses must tell the people to stop bringing. Both traditions see a rare and moving picture of a forgiven people giving willingly beyond what was needed.

Exodus 36:1

1“Bezalel and Oholiab shall work with every wise-hearted man, in whom the LORD has put wisdom and understanding to know how to do all the work for the service of the sanctuary, according to all that the LORD has commanded.”

Arminian

Bezalel, Oholiab, and every skilled person are to do the work as the LORD commanded. Arminian readers see gifted people joining together in obedience. Skill and commandment work together.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God-given wisdom directed by God’s command. The work must follow his instruction. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. The sanctuary is built by gifted hands under God’s direction.

Exodus 36:2

2Moses called Bezalel and Oholiab, and every wise-hearted man, in whose heart the LORD had put wisdom, even everyone whose heart stirred him up to come to the work to do it.

Arminian

Moses calls the skilled workers whose hearts stirred them to come. Arminian readers see willing volunteers responding to a call. God gives the wisdom; the workers come of their own desire.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God placing wisdom in hearts and stirring them to action. Divine gift and willing response stand together here. Both traditions affirm both sides of the verse.

Exodus 36:3

3They received from Moses all the offering which the children of Israel had brought for the work of the service of the sanctuary, with which to make it. They kept bringing free will offerings to him every morning.

Arminian

The workers receive the offerings, and the people keep bringing more every morning. Arminian readers see sustained, daily generosity. The giving continues beyond a single moment of enthusiasm.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see continuing generosity as fruit of God’s work among his people. Both traditions read the daily offerings as a picture of glad worship.

Exodus 36:4

4All the wise men, who performed all the work of the sanctuary, each came from his work which he did.

Arminian

The skilled workers leave their tasks to speak with Moses. Arminian readers see craftsmen attentive to stewardship, not just production. They notice when resources exceed the need.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see responsible stewardship among the workers. Their concern serves the integrity of the project. Both traditions see no contrast here. Craftsmen accountable to Moses guard against waste in sacred work.

Exodus 36:5

5They spoke to Moses, saying, “The people have brought much more than enough for the service of the work which the LORD commanded to make.”

Arminian

They report that the people have brought far more than enough. Arminian readers see overflowing generosity from a forgiven people. The response surpasses expectation. Their report reflects honesty about what the work actually required.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s people giving abundantly. The surplus is a mark of grace at work among them. Both traditions share this reading. Gratitude for mercy received overflows in giving.

Exodus 36:6

6Moses gave a commandment, and they caused it to be proclaimed throughout the camp, saying, “Let neither man nor woman make anything else for the offering for the sanctuary.” So the people were restrained from bringing.

Arminian

Moses proclaims that no one should bring anything more, and the people are restrained. Arminian readers see a rare instance in which giving must be stopped. Leaders refuse to take more than needed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see integrity in leadership that does not exploit generosity. Both traditions find the scene a model of faithful stewardship. The people’s willingness to stop is as instructive as their willingness to give.

Exodus 36:7

7For the stuff they had was sufficient to do all the work, and too much.

Arminian

The materials are sufficient and more. Arminian readers see God providing fully through his people. Nothing needed for the work is lacking. The abundance contrasts sharply with Israel’s earlier complaints about scarcity in the wilderness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s provision completed. The abundance points to his faithfulness. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. The work can now proceed with everything the pattern required.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
