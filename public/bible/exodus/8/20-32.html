Passage

Exodus 8

Verses 20–32 · WEB

The fourth plague brings swarms of flies, but the LORD sets apart Goshen so that Israel is spared. Pharaoh bargains, first offering sacrifice within Egypt, then a short journey, and asks for prayer. Moses intercedes and warns against deceit, yet once the flies are removed Pharaoh hardens his heart again. The passage shows the LORD’s rule over all the earth and his distinguishing care for his people.

Exodus 8:20

20The LORD said to Moses, “Rise up early in the morning, and stand before Pharaoh; behold, he comes out to the water; and tell him, ‘This is what the LORD says, “Let my people go, that they may serve me.

Arminian

Moses is to rise early and meet Pharaoh at the water with the familiar demand. The pattern of warning resumes.

Arminian readers see God renewing his appeal after the warning-free third plague. Opportunity to respond is given again.

Reformed

God sends Moses back to the river to confront Pharaoh. The demand remains unchanged: let my people go to serve me.

Reformed readers see the consistency of God’s word throughout the cycle. Both traditions note that the confrontation is ongoing and personal.

Exodus 8:21

21Else, if you will not let my people go, behold, I will send swarms of flies on you, and on your servants, and on your people, and into your houses. The houses of the Egyptians shall be full of swarms of flies, and also the ground they are on.

Arminian

If Pharaoh refuses, swarms of flies will fill Egypt’s houses and land. The warning is again conditional.

Arminian readers stress that the outcome depends on Pharaoh’s choice. The conditional threat assumes he can listen.

Reformed

God announces the next plague in detail. The flies will invade the houses of Pharaoh, his servants, and his people.

Reformed readers see a sincere warning set within God’s purpose. Both traditions agree that the warning makes Pharaoh fully accountable for what follows.

Exodus 8:22

22I will set apart in that day the land of Goshen, in which my people dwell, that no swarms of flies shall be there, to the end you may know that I am the LORD on the earth.

Arminian

God will set apart Goshen, where his people live, so that no flies will be there. The purpose is that Pharaoh may know “I am the LORD on the earth.”

Arminian readers see God’s protective care for Israel and a new kind of evidence for Pharaoh. The distinction shows that the plagues are deliberate, not random disasters.

Reformed

The LORD begins to separate Israel from Egypt in the plagues themselves. His sovereignty extends “on the earth,” not only over one nation’s gods.

Reformed readers see God’s distinguishing care for his covenant people. Both traditions share the point that the LORD rules all lands and protects those who belong to him.

Exodus 8:23

23I will put a division between my people and your people. This sign shall happen by tomorrow.” ’ ”

Arminian

God will put a division between his people and Pharaoh’s people, with the sign coming tomorrow. The separation is announced in advance.

Arminian readers see the distinction as a gracious protection given to Israel and a clear sign to Egypt. Its timing removes doubt about its source.

Reformed

The division underscores that God treats his people differently. The sign is set for a specific day.

Reformed readers connect the division with God’s covenant purposes for Israel. Both traditions read it first in its narrative setting, as protection during judgment, before any wider theological application.

Exodus 8:24

24The LORD did so; and there came grievous swarms of flies into the house of Pharaoh, and into his servants’ houses. In all the land of Egypt the land was corrupted by reason of the swarms of flies.

Arminian

The LORD sends heavy swarms of flies into Pharaoh’s house, his servants’ houses, and all Egypt, ruining the land. The warning is fulfilled.

Arminian readers note that this time the text says simply that the LORD did it, without mention of the rod. God’s direct action intensifies the pressure on Pharaoh.

Reformed

God himself brings the plague. The narrator stresses the corruption of the land under the swarms.

Reformed readers see the LORD acting directly to accomplish his word. Both traditions agree the plague is unmistakably his work.

Exodus 8:25

25Pharaoh called for Moses and for Aaron, and said, “Go, sacrifice to your God in the land!”

Arminian

Pharaoh offers a compromise: sacrifice to your God “in the land.” He tries to keep Israel within Egypt.

Arminian readers see Pharaoh bargaining rather than obeying. A partial concession lets him look reasonable while refusing what God actually asked.

Reformed

Pharaoh’s counter-offer would keep Israel under his control. It is a concession on his terms, not submission to the LORD’s.

Reformed readers see the persistence of a heart that will not yield. Both traditions recognise that partial obedience on one’s own terms is not obedience to God.

Exodus 8:26

26Moses said, “It isn’t appropriate to do so; for we shall sacrifice the abomination of the Egyptians to the LORD our God. Behold, if we sacrifice the abomination of the Egyptians before their eyes, won’t they stone us?

Arminian

Moses refuses, explaining that Israel’s sacrifices would be an abomination to the Egyptians and might provoke violence. Worship cannot be conducted as Pharaoh proposes.

Exactly why the sacrifices would offend is uncertain; interpreters often suggest animal sacrifice clashing with Egyptian practice, possibly involving animals Egyptians held sacred. Arminian readers see Moses’ practical wisdom in rejecting a dangerous compromise.

Reformed

Moses’ answer protects the integrity of Israel’s worship. The reasons for Egyptian offence are not explained and remain debated.

Reformed readers see Moses refusing to blend the LORD’s worship with Egyptian sensibilities. Both traditions agree that Israel must worship as God commands, not as Pharaoh allows.

Exodus 8:27

27We will go three days’ journey into the wilderness, and sacrifice to the LORD our God, as he shall command us.”

Arminian

Moses insists on a three days’ journey into the wilderness to sacrifice as the LORD commands. The original request stands.

Arminian readers see faithfulness to God’s instruction rather than accommodation. Moses keeps to what was asked from the start.

Reformed

Worship must follow God’s command, including its place. Moses will not negotiate the terms the LORD has set.

Reformed readers stress the principle that God determines how he is to be worshipped. Both traditions read the verse as fidelity to God’s word in the face of pressure.

Exodus 8:28

28Pharaoh said, “I will let you go, that you may sacrifice to the LORD your God in the wilderness, only you shall not go very far away. Pray for me.”

Arminian

Pharaoh agrees to let Israel go into the wilderness, but not very far, and asks Moses to pray for him. He concedes while keeping control.

Arminian readers see Pharaoh again responding to pressure with a limited, conditional promise. The request for prayer shows awareness of the LORD’s power without surrender.

Reformed

Pharaoh grants permission hedged with limits and asks for intercession. His bargaining continues.

Reformed readers see a king who acknowledges God’s power while refusing his authority. Both traditions note the pattern: concession under pressure, then retreat when relief comes.

Exodus 8:29

29Moses said, “Behold, I am going out from you. I will pray to the LORD that the swarms of flies may depart from Pharaoh, from his servants, and from his people, tomorrow; only don’t let Pharaoh deal deceitfully any more in not letting the people go to sacrifice to the LORD.”

Arminian

Moses agrees to pray, but warns Pharaoh not to deal deceitfully again. He names the previous broken promise directly.

Arminian readers see Moses holding Pharaoh accountable while still extending mercy. Prayer for an enemy does not mean pretending he has kept his word.

Reformed

Moses will intercede, and he also rebukes Pharaoh’s deceit. Mediation includes truth-telling.

Reformed readers see the prophet speaking honestly to power. Both traditions value Moses’ combination of mercy and candour, without assuming Pharaoh’s response.

Exodus 8:30

30Moses went out from Pharaoh, and prayed to the LORD.

Arminian

Moses leaves Pharaoh and prays to the LORD. Intercession follows promise.

Arminian readers see Moses’ faithfulness in praying even for a deceitful king. Prayer is a real part of how the story moves.

Reformed

The mediator goes from Pharaoh to God. The verse records simple obedience in prayer.

Reformed readers see prayer as one of God’s appointed means. Both traditions agree that Moses’ intercession matters within the narrative.

Exodus 8:31

31The LORD did according to the word of Moses, and he removed the swarms of flies from Pharaoh, from his servants, and from his people. There remained not one.

Arminian

The LORD does as Moses asks and removes every fly; not one remains. The answer is complete.

Arminian readers see God’s responsiveness to prayer and his mercy toward Egypt. The relief gives Pharaoh one more occasion to keep his word.

Reformed

God answers Moses’ prayer exactly and thoroughly. Not one fly is left.

Reformed readers see the LORD’s total control over the plague. Both traditions agree that the relief, like the plague, comes from God alone.

Exodus 8:32

32Pharaoh hardened his heart this time also, and he didn’t let the people go.

Arminian

Pharaoh hardened his heart this time also and did not let the people go. The WEB again names Pharaoh as the one who hardens.

Arminian readers see a repeated, deliberate pattern: relief, then refusal. Pharaoh’s self-hardening is confirmed, which Arminians see as the ground for God’s later judicial hardening.

Reformed

Pharaoh again breaks his word once relief comes. The verse describes his own action without mentioning God’s.

Reformed readers accept Pharaoh’s self-hardening as real and culpable, while reading it within the purpose God announced in 7:3. Both traditions agree that Pharaoh is responsible for refusing to let Israel go.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
