Passage

Exodus 8

Verses 1–15 · WEB

The second plague brings frogs over Egypt, reaching houses, beds, and ovens. The magicians imitate it, but Pharaoh must ask Moses to pray, and he promises to let Israel go. When the frogs die and relief comes, Pharaoh hardens his heart. The passage highlights the LORD’s incomparability, Moses’ intercession, and Pharaoh’s bargaining under pressure.

Exodus 8:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, “Go in to Pharaoh, and tell him, ‘This is what the LORD says, “Let my people go, that they may serve me.

Arminian

Moses is sent again with the same demand: let my people go, that they may serve me. The message does not soften after the first refusal.

Arminian readers see God’s patience in repeating the summons. Pharaoh receives another real opportunity before the next plague falls.

Reformed

The LORD renews his command through Moses. Worship remains the purpose of the release.

Reformed readers see God’s word pressing steadily on Pharaoh. Both traditions agree the repeated demand keeps the king’s accountability clear at every stage.

Exodus 8:2

2If you refuse to let them go, behold, I will plague all your borders with frogs.

Arminian

If Pharaoh refuses, frogs will plague all his borders. The warning is conditional and specific.

Arminian readers stress the conditional form. The plague depends on Pharaoh’s response, which shows that his choice genuinely matters in the narrative.

Reformed

God sets out the consequence of refusal in advance. Pharaoh knows exactly what is coming.

Reformed readers note that conditional warnings are fully compatible with God’s foreknown purpose. Both traditions agree the warning is sincere and that refusal will bring judgment.

Exodus 8:3

3The river will swarm with frogs, which will go up and come into your house, and into your bedroom, and on your bed, and into the house of your servants, and on your people, and into your ovens, and into your kneading troughs.

Arminian

The frogs will invade houses, bedrooms, beds, ovens, and kneading troughs. The plague reaches the most private and domestic spaces.

Arminian readers see how relentless defiance brings consequences into every corner of life. No place is beyond the reach of the LORD’s warning.

Reformed

The detailed list stresses that nothing in Egypt is sealed off from God. Even the king’s bedroom is not his own.

Reformed readers see the LORD’s sovereignty extending into ordinary life. A frog-headed goddess associated with fertility is commonly proposed as a background, though such links remain debated.

Exodus 8:4

4The frogs shall come up both on you, and on your people, and on all your servants.” ’ ”

Arminian

The frogs will come on Pharaoh, his people, and his servants. The plague moves from the river onto persons.

Arminian readers note that the king himself is named first. The one with power to release Israel will feel the plague most directly.

Reformed

God targets Pharaoh personally along with his court and people. The warning is addressed to the one responsible.

Reformed readers likewise see the king singled out. Both traditions read the verse as part of God’s direct confrontation with Pharaoh’s authority.

Exodus 8:5

5The LORD said to Moses, “Tell Aaron, ‘Stretch out your hand with your rod over the rivers, over the streams, and over the pools, and cause frogs to come up on the land of Egypt.’ ”

Arminian

The LORD instructs Moses to tell Aaron to stretch out his rod over the waters and bring up frogs. The pattern of mediation continues.

Arminian readers see God working through his chosen servants and their obedience. The rod is a sign of delegated authority, not a source of power in itself.

Reformed

God gives the command, Moses relays it, and Aaron acts. Every link in the chain answers to the LORD.

Reformed readers stress that the instrument has no power apart from God’s word. Both traditions agree that the plagues come from the LORD through his messengers.

Exodus 8:6

6Aaron stretched out his hand over the waters of Egypt; and the frogs came up, and covered the land of Egypt.

Arminian

Aaron stretches out his hand and frogs cover the land. The warning is fulfilled at once.

Arminian readers see God’s word proving reliable. What Moses announced comes to pass, giving Pharaoh further reason to listen.

Reformed

The frogs come up exactly as commanded. Obedience and fulfilment follow one another.

Reformed readers see God’s power working through an ordinary gesture. Both traditions read the scene as one more demonstration that the LORD does what he says.

Exodus 8:7

7The magicians did the same thing with their enchantments, and brought up frogs on the land of Egypt.

Arminian

The magicians bring up frogs too with their enchantments. They add to the problem but cannot remove it.

Arminian readers note the irony that Egypt’s experts only make more frogs. Their imitation offers Pharaoh a reason to doubt, but no relief.

Reformed

Egypt’s experts again match the outward sign. As before, the narrative does not explain their method.

Reformed readers note that imitation shows the limits of Egypt’s powers rather than their equality with God. Both traditions see the counterfeit as hollow beside the LORD’s work.

Exodus 8:8

8Then Pharaoh called for Moses and Aaron, and said, “Entreat the LORD, that he take away the frogs from me and from my people; and I will let the people go, that they may sacrifice to the LORD.”

Arminian

Pharaoh calls for Moses and Aaron and asks them to entreat the LORD, promising to let the people go to sacrifice. For the first time he names the LORD and asks for prayer.

Arminian readers see a real moment of pressure and possibility. Pharaoh could keep this promise; whether he does will reveal his heart.

Reformed

Pharaoh’s request acknowledges the LORD’s power, at least under duress. His promise is a bargaining move made to end the plague.

Reformed readers see that relief-seeking is not repentance. Both traditions note the gap between Pharaoh’s words and his later actions.

Exodus 8:9

9Moses said to Pharaoh, “I give you the honor of setting the time that I should pray for you, and for your servants, and for your people, that the frogs be destroyed from you and your houses, and remain in the river only.”

Arminian

Moses lets Pharaoh choose the time for the prayer. The offer highlights that the removal will clearly be God’s doing.

Arminian readers see Moses honouring Pharaoh with a choice that invites trust. The arrangement removes any doubt about whose power is at work.

Reformed

Moses’ offer sets up an unmistakable sign. When the frogs leave at the appointed time, the cause will be plain.

Reformed readers see the LORD’s sovereignty demonstrated through a precise answer to prayer. Both traditions agree the detail serves the narrative’s aim of making God known.

Exodus 8:10

10Pharaoh said, “Tomorrow.” Moses said, “Let it be according to your word, that you may know that there is no one like the LORD our God.

Arminian

Pharaoh says “Tomorrow,” and Moses answers so that he may know there is no one like the LORD our God. The incomparability of the LORD is the point.

Arminian readers see knowledge of God offered to Pharaoh again. The timing of relief is a sign meant to lead him to acknowledge the LORD.

Reformed

Moses connects the answer to prayer with the confession that no god is like the LORD. Egypt’s religious world is being challenged directly.

Reformed readers stress the uniqueness of Israel’s God. Both traditions share the central claim that the LORD has no rival.

Exodus 8:11

11The frogs shall depart from you, and from your houses, and from your servants, and from your people. They shall remain in the river only.”

Arminian

Moses promises that the frogs will leave houses, servants, and people, remaining only in the river. The relief will be as thorough as the plague.

Arminian readers see God’s readiness to lift judgment when asked. Mercy is extended even to the oppressor.

Reformed

The plague will be reversed in every place it struck. God controls both the coming and the going of the frogs.

Reformed readers see the LORD’s mastery over creation in both judgment and relief. Both traditions honour the mercy shown here, whatever Pharaoh will do with it.

Exodus 8:12

12Moses and Aaron went out from Pharaoh, and Moses cried to the LORD concerning the frogs which he had brought on Pharaoh.

Arminian

Moses leaves and cries to the LORD about the frogs “which he had brought on Pharaoh.” Moses intercedes for the king who oppresses his people.

Arminian readers see a striking picture of intercession for an enemy. Prayer is a real means by which God acts in the story.

Reformed

The verse names God as the one who brought the frogs and Moses as the one who prays for their removal. Mediation runs in both directions.

Reformed readers see prayer as an appointed means within God’s purpose. Both traditions value Moses’ role as mediator and his willingness to pray for Pharaoh.

Exodus 8:13

13The LORD did according to the word of Moses, and the frogs died out of the houses, out of the courts, and out of the fields.

Arminian

The LORD does according to Moses’ word, and the frogs die in houses, courts, and fields. God answers the prayer precisely.

Arminian readers see a genuine responsiveness in God to intercession. The relationship between God and his servant is personal and real.

Reformed

God fulfils what Moses requested. The answer confirms Moses as the LORD’s spokesman.

Reformed readers see God honouring the word he gave his servant to speak. Both traditions agree that the frogs die because the LORD acts, not because of Moses’ own power.

Exodus 8:14

14They gathered them together in heaps, and the land stank.

Arminian

The frogs are piled in heaps, and the land stinks. Relief comes, but the consequences remain.

Arminian readers see that ending a judgment does not erase all its effects. The stench lingers as a reminder of what defiance has cost.

Reformed

The heaps of dead frogs make the plague’s reality impossible to forget. The land itself bears the marks.

Reformed readers see the lingering smell as part of God’s witness to Egypt. Both traditions read the detail as vivid narrative rather than a doctrinal statement.

Exodus 8:15

15But when Pharaoh saw that there was a respite, he hardened his heart, and didn’t listen to them, as the LORD had spoken.

Arminian

When Pharaoh sees respite, “he hardened his heart” and did not listen, as the LORD had spoken. The WEB here names Pharaoh as the one who hardens.

Arminian readers treat this as a key verse: Pharaoh’s self-hardening is explicit, coming after relief and a broken promise. God’s judicial hardening elsewhere is understood as confirming this deliberate choice, not overriding a willing heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers fully accept that Pharaoh hardens himself here; he breaks his word freely when pressure eases. The phrase “as the LORD had spoken” ties his action to God’s announced purpose in 7:3.

In Reformed reading, God’s hardening and Pharaoh’s self-hardening are not rivals; God works his purpose through the king’s real and culpable choices. Explanations of how the two relate vary within the tradition.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm that here Pharaoh hardens his own heart and is fully responsible. Arminian readers commonly treat this explicit self-hardening as prior to and the basis for God’s judicial hardening in later plagues; Reformed readers commonly see Pharaoh’s self-hardening and God’s announced hardening (7:3) as concurrent, with God working his purpose through the king’s real choices. Accounts vary within each tradition.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
