Passage

Exodus 5

Verses 15–23 · WEB

The Israelite officers appeal directly to Pharaoh and are dismissed with the same charge: you are idle. Leaving the palace, they meet Moses and Aaron and call down the LORD’s judgment on them for making Israel a stench to Pharaoh. Moses then takes the complaint to the LORD himself: why have you brought trouble on this people, and why did you send me? Both traditions see honest lament here, spoken inside the relationship God began, and they note that the narrative leaves Moses’ question open until the LORD answers in chapter 6.

Exodus 5:15

15Then the officers of the children of Israel came and cried to Pharaoh, saying, “Why do you deal this way with your servants?

Arminian

The Israelite officers go straight to Pharaoh and cry out: why do you treat your servants this way? They still appeal to Pharaoh as the source of relief.

Arminian readers note that the officers take a reasonable step, seeking justice through the channel they know. Their appeal shows they do not yet look to the LORD for rescue.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the officers call themselves Pharaoh’s servants. The question of whose servants Israel are is at the heart of the exodus.

Both traditions read the appeal with sympathy; the officers are trapped in an unjust system.

Exodus 5:16

16No straw is given to your servants, and they tell us, ‘Make brick!’ and behold, your servants are beaten; but the fault is in your own people.”

Arminian

They lay out the injustice: no straw, the same demand, beatings, and the fault lies with Pharaoh’s own people. Their complaint is accurate.

Arminian readers note that the officers speak truthfully about the wrong done to them. Scripture does not silence honest protest against injustice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the officers place the blame correctly, yet they cannot change Pharaoh’s heart by argument. The problem is deeper than a policy error.

Both traditions affirm that naming injustice plainly is right, even when it brings no immediate result.

Exodus 5:17

17But Pharaoh said, “You are idle! You are idle! Therefore you say, ‘Let’s go and sacrifice to the LORD.’

Arminian

Pharaoh repeats his charge: you are idle, you are idle. He links their desire to sacrifice to the LORD with laziness.

Arminian readers see Pharaoh refusing to reconsider even when confronted with the facts. Repeated refusal hardens into a settled stance.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Pharaoh treats the LORD’s name as an excuse. His contempt for the people reflects his contempt for their God.

Both traditions hear Pharaoh’s repetition as a sign of a closed mind, not as a fair assessment.

Exodus 5:18

18Go therefore now, and work; for no straw shall be given to you; yet you shall deliver the same number of bricks!”

Arminian

Go and work: no straw, the same number of bricks. Pharaoh’s answer is final.

Arminian readers note that Pharaoh has been given a clear opportunity to act justly and refuses it. His responsibility grows with each refusal.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that human appeals to Pharaoh have now failed entirely. The way is cleared for the LORD to act in a manner no one can mistake.

Both traditions see the officers leaving with nothing, which explains their anger in the following verses.

Exodus 5:19

19The officers of the children of Israel saw that they were in trouble when it was said, “You shall not diminish anything from your daily quota of bricks!”

Arminian

The officers realize they are in trouble. The order not to reduce the daily quota leaves them no way out.

Arminian readers note the officers’ despair. Their position is impossible, and the narrative lets the reader feel its weight.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that human resources are exhausted. The story is moving toward a point where only God can deliver.

Both traditions recognize that trouble can deepen precisely when people first begin to obey God’s call.

Exodus 5:20

20They met Moses and Aaron, who stood along the way, as they came out from Pharaoh.

Arminian

Leaving Pharaoh, they meet Moses and Aaron waiting along the way. The encounter is tense.

Arminian readers note that Moses and Aaron seem to have been waiting for the result. They are now face to face with the human cost of their message.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the messengers must bear the people’s reaction. Faithfulness to God’s word does not shield them from blame.

Both traditions see this meeting as the turning point that drives Moses to prayer.

Exodus 5:21

21They said to them, “May the LORD look at you and judge, because you have made us a stench to be abhorred in the eyes of Pharaoh, and in the eyes of his servants, to put a sword in their hand to kill us!”

Arminian

The officers call on the LORD to judge Moses and Aaron for making Israel a stench to Pharaoh and putting a sword in his hand. They blame the messengers for the suffering.

Arminian readers note that the officers invoke the LORD but misread the situation. Disappointment can lead people to turn God’s name against his servants.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the people judging God’s work by immediate results. Their accusation is understandable, but it reverses the truth: Pharaoh, not Moses, is the oppressor.

Both traditions read this pastorally: leaders who speak God’s word may face blame when obedience brings hardship.

Exodus 5:22

22Moses returned to the LORD, and said, “Lord, why have you brought trouble on this people? Why is it that you have sent me?

Arminian

Moses returns to the LORD: Lord, why have you brought trouble on this people? Why did you send me? He brings his confusion directly to God.

Arminian readers see honest lament as an act of faith. Moses does not abandon his calling; he wrestles with God about it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Moses takes the people’s complaint to the right place. He questions God, but within a relationship God established.

Both traditions recognize lament as a biblical form of prayer and see God receiving it here without rebuke.

Exodus 5:23

23For since I came to Pharaoh to speak in your name, he has brought trouble on this people. You have not rescued your people at all!”

Arminian

Since Moses spoke to Pharaoh in God’s name, things have only worsened, and God has not rescued his people at all. The chapter ends on this unresolved cry.

Arminian readers note the painful gap between promise and experience. Faith must sometimes wait in that gap without seeing a way through.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the LORD’s answer in 6:1 begins with what he will do, not with an explanation. God’s timing is not measured by the speed of the first results.

Both traditions draw pastoral comfort: God hears accusing prayers from servants who feel he has failed them.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
