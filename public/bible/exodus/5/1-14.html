Passage

Exodus 5

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Moses and Aaron deliver the LORD’s demand that Pharaoh let Israel go to hold a feast in the wilderness. Pharaoh answers that he does not know the LORD and will not release Israel; he calls the request idleness and orders that straw no longer be supplied while the brick quota stays the same. The people scatter to gather stubble, and Israelite officers are beaten for the shortfall. Both traditions read the scene as the opening clash between the LORD and a king who claims ultimate rights over Israel’s labor and worship. Obedience to God’s word brings, at first, heavier suffering rather than quick relief.

Exodus 5:1

1Afterward Moses and Aaron came, and said to Pharaoh, “This is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says, ‘Let my people go, that they may hold a feast to me in the wilderness.’ ”

Arminian

Moses and Aaron finally stand before Pharaoh and speak in the LORD’s name: Let my people go. The word “my” claims Israel as the LORD’s own people, not Pharaoh’s property.

Arminian readers note that the request is addressed to Pharaoh as a real moral agent. God’s demand is a genuine summons, and Pharaoh is responsible for how he answers it.

Reformed

The messengers bring the LORD’s word, not their own proposal. The command assumes a prior claim: Israel belongs to the God who called them, and their worship is owed to him.

Reformed readers note that the LORD has already told Moses that Pharaoh will resist (3:19; 4:21). The summons is still sincere, and it is the first step in a contest whose outcome God has announced.

Exodus 5:2

2Pharaoh said, “Who is the LORD, that I should listen to his voice to let Israel go? I don’t know the LORD, and moreover I will not let Israel go.”

Arminian

Pharaoh’s answer is blunt: Who is the LORD? He refuses to listen to a God he does not acknowledge. His question frames the rest of the plague narrative, in which the LORD makes himself known.

Arminian readers hear a proud refusal that is Pharaoh’s own. Ignorance of the LORD is not innocent here; he is unwilling to learn, and he says plainly that he will not let Israel go.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the confrontation as the LORD’s chosen occasion to make his name known in Egypt (compare 7:5). Pharaoh’s question will be answered by judgment and deliverance.

Both traditions agree that Pharaoh’s defiance is real and culpable. The God he claims not to know is the God before whom he will be judged.

Exodus 5:3

3They said, “The God of the Hebrews has met with us. Please let us go three days’ journey into the wilderness, and sacrifice to the LORD, our God, lest he fall on us with pestilence, or with the sword.”

Arminian

Moses and Aaron soften the request: the God of the Hebrews has met with us; please let us go three days’ journey to sacrifice. This follows the wording the LORD gave in 3:18.

Arminian readers note the humility of the appeal and the reasonable scope of the request. Pharaoh is given every opportunity to grant a modest petition.

Reformed

The messengers speak as the LORD instructed, including the warning that neglecting his worship is dangerous. The phrase “lest he fall on us” shows that the true threat is the LORD, not Pharaoh.

Reformed readers note that the modest request exposes Pharaoh’s heart. Refusing even three days shows his opposition to the LORD’s claim on Israel.

Exodus 5:4

4The king of Egypt said to them, “Why do you, Moses and Aaron, take the people from their work? Get back to your burdens!”

Arminian

Pharaoh treats the request as interference with labor: Get back to your burdens! He sees worship as a distraction from what Israel owes him.

Arminian readers note how quickly the conflict turns from theology to economics. Pharaoh will not tolerate any allegiance that competes with his own demands.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Pharaoh claims the authority to define Israel’s purpose. The exodus will show that Israel exists to serve the LORD, not Egypt’s building projects.

Both traditions see a lasting pattern here: powers that treat people as tools resent any claim of God on their lives.

Exodus 5:5

5Pharaoh said, “Behold, the people of the land are now many, and you make them rest from their burdens.”

Arminian

Pharaoh points to the people’s growing numbers. Their multiplication, which began as a sign of God’s blessing (1:7), is to him only a labor force that must not rest.

Arminian readers note the irony: the very fruitfulness God gave becomes the reason for heavier oppression. Human sin can twist God’s blessing into an occasion of cruelty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s promise to Abraham at work in Israel’s growth, even while Pharaoh tries to exploit it. His policy cannot cancel what God has begun.

Both traditions read the verse as revealing Pharaoh’s outlook: rest for the people is lost production, not a human good.

Exodus 5:6

6The same day Pharaoh commanded the taskmasters of the people and their officers, saying,

Arminian

That same day, Pharaoh acts. He gives orders to the taskmasters and to the Israelite officers who serve under them.

Arminian readers note the speed of the response. Pharaoh’s choice is immediate and deliberate, which underlines his responsibility for the harm that follows.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that oppression often works through layers of officials, some drawn from the oppressed people. The structure of Egypt’s system is part of the bondage.

Both traditions observe that the narrative does not hurry past the human cost of Pharaoh’s decision.

Exodus 5:7

7“You shall no longer give the people straw to make brick, as before. Let them go and gather straw for themselves.

Arminian

The new policy: no more straw supplied. The people must gather it themselves. Straw was used in making brick, so the burden grows without any change to the expected output.

Arminian readers see deliberate cruelty meant to crush the desire to worship. Pharaoh uses exhaustion to silence the call of God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the LORD allows his people’s condition to worsen before he delivers. The deepening bondage will make the coming rescue unmistakably his work.

Both traditions avoid treating suffering as proof of God’s absence; the narrative has already shown that he hears (3:7).

Exodus 5:8

8You shall require from them the number of the bricks which they made before. You shall not diminish anything of it, for they are idle. Therefore they cry, saying, ‘Let’s go and sacrifice to our God.’

Arminian

The quota stays the same. Pharaoh calls the people idle and claims that is why they want to sacrifice to their God.

Arminian readers note how Pharaoh dismisses a request for worship as laziness. Contempt for God often shows itself in contempt for those who seek him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Pharaoh interpreting spiritual desire through his own priorities. In his view, the only cure for wanting to worship is more work.

Both traditions hear a warning against treating rest and worship as wasted time.

Exodus 5:9

9Let heavier work be laid on the men, that they may labor in it. Don’t let them pay any attention to lying words.”

Arminian

Heavier work is meant to make the people ignore “lying words.” Pharaoh calls the LORD’s message a lie.

Arminian readers note that Pharaoh tries to stop the people from hearing God’s word by exhausting them. He knows the message has the power to awaken hope.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Pharaoh labels the LORD’s word false. The plague narrative will show whose word is true.

Both traditions see that the people’s later inability to listen (6:9) is connected to this deliberate crushing.

Exodus 5:10

10The taskmasters of the people went out with their officers, and they spoke to the people, saying, “This is what Pharaoh says: ‘I will not give you straw.

Arminian

The taskmasters and officers deliver the decree: This is what Pharaoh says. The phrase echoes “This is what the LORD says” in 5:1.

Arminian readers note the contrast between two competing words. Israel is caught between the LORD’s promise and Pharaoh’s command, and must learn whose word to trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the parallel as deliberate: two kings speak, but only one is sovereign. Pharaoh’s word claims an authority it does not finally have.

Both traditions read the narrative as a contest over who rightly commands Israel.

Exodus 5:11

11Go yourselves, get straw where you can find it, for nothing of your work shall be diminished.’ ”

Arminian

The people must find straw wherever they can, and none of the work will be reduced. Pharaoh leaves them no relief.

Arminian readers note that the order makes success nearly impossible. Unjust demands like this reveal the oppressor’s character.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the people are now in a situation they cannot fix by their own effort. Their deliverance will have to come from outside.

Both traditions draw pastoral comfort here: God sees burdens that cannot be met by human strength.

Exodus 5:12

12So the people were scattered abroad throughout all the land of Egypt to gather stubble for straw.

Arminian

The people scatter through the land gathering stubble in place of straw. The picture is of a whole people exhausted and dispersed.

Arminian readers note that the people obey Pharaoh because they have no choice. Their scattering contrasts with the gathered worship God intends for them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the people driven to the edge of endurance. This sets the stage for a deliverance that depends on God alone.

Both traditions note the cost borne by ordinary people when rulers resist God.

Exodus 5:13

13The taskmasters were urgent saying, “Fulfill your work quota daily, as when there was straw!”

Arminian

The taskmasters press them: complete your daily quota, as when there was straw. The impossible demand is enforced.

Arminian readers note the relentless pressure. Oppression is not only one decision but a daily system of coercion.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the narrative shows the full weight of Egypt’s bondage before the LORD acts. The suffering is real, not staged.

Both traditions see God’s concern for the oppressed as part of his revealed character.

Exodus 5:14

14The officers of the children of Israel, whom Pharaoh’s taskmasters had set over them, were beaten, and were asked, “Why haven’t you fulfilled your quota both yesterday and today, in making brick as before?”

Arminian

The Israelite officers are beaten for the shortfall. Those set over their own people take the blame for an impossible standard.

Arminian readers note the injustice of punishing people for failing a goal designed to be unreachable. Pharaoh’s cruelty falls on those caught in the middle.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the officers suffer because of a decision they did not make. Their pain will turn into bitterness toward Moses in the next scene.

Both traditions see that faithfulness to God’s call can bring short-term suffering for others as well as oneself.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
