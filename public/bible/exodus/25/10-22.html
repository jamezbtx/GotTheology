Passage

Exodus 25

Verses 10–22 · WEB

The first furnishing described is the ark, the gold-covered chest that will hold the covenant tablets, topped by the mercy seat and two cherubim. God names this place as the point where he will meet with Moses and speak his commands for Israel.

Exodus 25:10

10“They shall make an ark of acacia wood. Its length shall be two and a half cubits, its width a cubit and a half, and a cubit and a half its height.

Arminian

The ark comes first, because it is the center of the sanctuary. It is a chest of acacia wood, about 2½ by 1½ by 1½ cubits. The WEB footnote gives a cubit as about 18 inches or 46 centimeters, making the ark roughly 3¾ by 2¼ by 2¼ feet.

Arminian readers note how modest the ark is in size, though it signals God’s throne among his people.

Reformed

The instructions begin with the ark and move outward, from the place of God’s presence toward the entrance. The order teaches that everything centers on God.

Reformed readers see a God who is near his people yet holy, and who sets the terms of that nearness.

Exodus 25:11

11You shall overlay it with pure gold. You shall overlay it inside and outside, and you shall make a gold molding around it.

Arminian

Gold inside and out, with a gold molding around it. The ark is to be fitting for the most holy place.

Arminian readers see reverence expressed in costly care. The best is given for the place nearest God.

Reformed

Pure gold overlay marks the ark as belonging to the realm of greatest holiness.

Reformed readers note that the ark is made by human hands but at divine command. Its glory comes from what God appoints it to be.

Exodus 25:12

12You shall cast four rings of gold for it, and put them in its four feet. Two rings shall be on the one side of it, and two rings on the other side of it.

Arminian

Four gold rings are cast and set on the ark’s feet, two on each side. The ark is designed to travel.

Arminian readers see a God who journeys with his people. He is not tied to one place but goes with Israel through the wilderness.

Reformed

The rings prepare the ark for carrying. God’s throne among Israel is portable, accompanying them on the way to the land.

Reformed readers see God’s faithful presence with his pilgrim people, a theme Hebrews picks up for the church on its way (Heb 13:13–14).

Exodus 25:13

13You shall make poles of acacia wood, and overlay them with gold.

Arminian

Poles of acacia wood are overlaid with gold, matching the ark itself.

Arminian readers note the care for every detail. Even the carrying poles share in the sanctuary’s dignity.

Reformed

The poles are part of the holy furniture and are treated with the same materials.

Reformed readers note that later the ark is to be carried by Levites with these poles, not handled directly (Num 4:15). The poles serve reverence.

Exodus 25:14

14You shall put the poles into the rings on the sides of the ark to carry the ark.

Arminian

The poles go through the rings so the ark can be carried.

Arminian readers recall that when the ark was later moved on a cart and touched, judgment followed (2 Sam 6:3–7). God’s appointed way of carrying it mattered.

Reformed

The ark is to be carried, not dragged or touched. The instruction protects the holiness of the ark.

Reformed readers likewise point to 2 Samuel 6 and the later correction in 1 Chronicles 15:13–15, where the ark is carried “as Moses commanded.”

Exodus 25:15

15The poles shall be in the rings of the ark. They shall not be taken from it.

Arminian

The poles are to remain in the rings. The ark is always ready to move.

Arminian readers see readiness to follow God’s leading. Israel is a people on the move with God among them.

Reformed

The poles are never removed, which also avoids repeated handling of the holy object. Even in Solomon’s temple the poles remained (1 Kgs 8:8).

Reformed readers see the permanence of God’s arrangements for his presence among his people.

Exodus 25:16

16You shall put the covenant which I shall give you into the ark.

Arminian

The covenant God will give, the tablets of the law (cf. 31:18; 40:20), goes into the ark. WEB says “covenant” here, where some translations have “testimony.”

Arminian readers note that God’s word lies at the heart of the sanctuary. The relationship rests on his revealed will.

Reformed

The tablets of the covenant are placed in the ark, beneath the mercy seat. The law stands at the center of Israel’s worship.

Reformed readers stress that God’s presence is bound to his word. Hebrews 9:4 also mentions a jar of manna and Aaron’s rod in the ark.

Exodus 25:17

17You shall make a mercy seat of pure gold. Two and a half cubits shall be its length, and a cubit and a half its width.

Arminian

The “mercy seat” is a gold cover on top of the ark, the same size as the ark. The Hebrew term is related to the language of atonement, and many modern versions render it “atonement cover.”

Arminian readers see here the place where God deals with sin. On the Day of Atonement, blood will be sprinkled here (Lev 16:14–15).

Reformed

The mercy seat is the place of atonement. The Greek Old Testament renders it with the word used in Romans 3:25 of Christ as a propitiation and in Hebrews 9:5 for the mercy seat itself.

Reformed readers see Christ as the true mercy seat. He is where God’s justice and mercy meet in atonement for his people.

Exodus 25:18

18You shall make two cherubim of hammered gold. You shall make them at the two ends of the mercy seat.

Arminian

Two cherubim of hammered gold stand at the two ends of the mercy seat. Cherubim guarded Eden’s way (Gen 3:24); here they guard the place of meeting.

Arminian readers see both holiness and invitation. The way to God is guarded yet open through his appointed means.

Reformed

Cherubim are heavenly beings associated with God’s throne and holiness (cf. 1 Sam 4:4; Ezek 10).

Reformed readers see an echo of Eden. The guarded presence lost in Genesis 3 is graciously restored, under strict conditions, at the mercy seat.

Exodus 25:19

19Make one cherub at the one end, and one cherub at the other end. You shall make the cherubim on its two ends of one piece with the mercy seat.

Arminian

The cherubim are made of one piece with the mercy seat, not attached later.

Arminian readers do not press symbolism onto every craft detail. The unity of the work fits the unity of God’s throne.

Reformed

The cherubim and mercy seat form a single hammered work.

Reformed readers likewise see a unified throne-room image without needing to assign meaning to each technical detail.

Exodus 25:20

20The cherubim shall spread out their wings upward, covering the mercy seat with their wings, with their faces toward one another. The faces of the cherubim shall be toward the mercy seat.

Arminian

The cherubim spread their wings upward, covering the mercy seat, facing each other and looking toward it.

Arminian readers see an image of reverence. Even heavenly beings attend to the place where God meets with sinners.

Reformed

The cherubim’s gaze is fixed on the mercy seat. Some readers connect this with 1 Peter 1:12, where angels long to look into the things of salvation.

Reformed readers see the mercy seat as the focus of heaven’s attention, though they hold such connections with appropriate restraint.

Exodus 25:21

21You shall put the mercy seat on top of the ark, and in the ark you shall put the covenant that I will give you.

Arminian

The mercy seat goes on top of the ark, with the covenant inside. The repetition from verse 16 underlines the arrangement.

Arminian readers note that mercy covers the law. God’s covenant demands are met at the place of atonement.

Reformed

The law lies beneath the mercy seat. Blood sprinkled on the cover will stand between the holy God and the broken law.

Reformed readers see a vivid picture of atonement satisfying God’s justice, fulfilled in Christ (Rom 3:25–26).

Exodus 25:22

22There I will meet with you, and I will tell you from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim which are on the ark of the covenant, all that I command you for the children of Israel.

Arminian

“There I will meet with you.” From above the mercy seat, between the cherubim, God speaks his commands for Israel.

Arminian readers see God’s desire for communication and relationship. The sanctuary is a meeting place where God speaks and his people hear and respond.

Reformed

The ark becomes the place of revelation. God speaks from his throne, between the cherubim (cf. Num 7:89).

Reformed readers stress that God meets his people where he chooses, through atonement and his word. In Christ, believers now draw near with confidence (Heb 4:16).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
