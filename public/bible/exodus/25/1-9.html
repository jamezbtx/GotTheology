Passage

Exodus 25

Verses 1–9 · WEB

After the covenant is ratified at Sinai (Exodus 24), the LORD gives Moses instructions for a sanctuary. The section opens with a freewill offering of materials and states its purpose plainly: God will dwell among his people, and everything is to follow the pattern he shows Moses.

Exodus 25:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

This begins one of the longest instruction blocks in the Pentateuch (Exodus 25–31). The LORD who redeemed Israel from Egypt and made covenant with them now speaks about how he will live among them.

Arminian readers note that the initiative is God’s. The sanctuary is his gracious idea, not Israel’s attempt to reach him, and it flows out of the relationship he has already established.

Reformed

The formula “The LORD spoke to Moses” marks what follows as divine revelation, not human religious invention. Moses has gone up into the cloud on the mountain (24:15–18), and this is what he receives there.

Reformed readers stress that worship begins with God’s word. The God who redeemed and covenanted with Israel also defines how he will be approached.

Exodus 25:2

2“Speak to the children of Israel, that they take an offering for me. From everyone whose heart makes him willing you shall take my offering.

Arminian

The offering is to come from “everyone whose heart makes him willing.” It is not a tax; it is a gift freely given. Later the people bring so much that they must be told to stop (36:5–7).

Arminian readers see here the kind of free, uncoerced response God values in his people (cf. 2 Cor 9:7). God enables and invites, and the people genuinely give from their own hearts.

Reformed

The gifts are given willingly, and God still calls them “my offering.” Much of what Israel brings likely came from the Egyptians at the exodus (12:35–36), so they are giving back what God provided.

Reformed readers see God at work in the willing heart (cf. Phil 2:13; 1 Chr 29:14, where David confesses that all things come from God and Israel only gives back what is his). They do not deny the willingness; they trace it to grace.

Key contrast Both traditions read the willing heart as real willingness, not compulsion, and both see God’s grace behind it. Arminians typically describe grace as enabling and inviting a response the giver freely makes and could withhold. Reformed interpreters typically describe grace as producing the willingness itself, so that the heart made willing is God’s work and also truly the giver’s own.

Exodus 25:3

3This is the offering which you shall take from them: gold, silver, bronze,

Arminian

The list begins with metals in descending value: gold, silver, bronze. Each will be used in its proper place, with the most precious nearest the presence of God.

Arminian readers note that everyone can contribute something. The list is broad enough that people of differing means can share in building God’s dwelling.

Reformed

The metals reflect a gradation of holiness that the tabernacle will make visible. Gold dominates inside, while bronze is used farther out (27:1–6).

Reformed readers see order and purpose in God’s instructions. Even the choice of materials teaches Israel about approaching a holy God.

Exodus 25:4

4blue, purple, scarlet, fine linen, goats’ hair,

Arminian

Blue, purple and scarlet refer to dyed yarns, costly in the ancient world, alongside fine linen and goats’ hair for weaving.

Arminian readers see beauty and craft offered to God. The people’s skills and resources are welcomed into his service.

Reformed

These colored yarns reappear in the curtains, the veil and the priestly garments (26:1, 31; 28:5–6), tying the whole sanctuary together visually.

Reformed readers note that God values beauty in what he commands. Worship according to his word is not drab; it is ordered and glorious.

Exodus 25:5

5rams’ skins dyed red, sea cow hides, acacia wood,

Arminian

Rams’ skins dyed red and sea cow hides will form the outer coverings (26:14). The WEB footnotes “sea cow hides” with “or, fine leather”; the Hebrew term is uncertain, and translations vary.

Arminian readers do not build doctrine on the uncertain term. The point is plain: durable materials will protect the dwelling.

Reformed

Acacia wood, a hard desert wood, will form the ark, table, frames and poles. The exact animal behind “sea cow hides” is uncertain, and the text does not depend on settling it.

Reformed readers likewise see ordinary materials of the wilderness taken up into God’s service.

Exodus 25:6

6oil for the light, spices for the anointing oil and for the sweet incense,

Arminian

Oil for the lamp, spices for anointing oil and incense: these supply the ongoing life of worship, not just the building (cf. 27:20–21; 30:22–38).

Arminian readers note that the people’s giving sustains daily worship. Their devotion continues after the construction is finished.

Reformed

Light, anointing and incense will mark the priestly service of the sanctuary. The anointing oil consecrates; the incense rises before the LORD.

Reformed readers see the means of worship as appointed by God. Many readers in both traditions connect incense with prayer (Ps 141:2; Rev 5:8).

Exodus 25:7

7onyx stones, and stones to be set for the ephod and for the breastplate.

Arminian

Onyx and other stones are for the ephod and breastplate of the high priest, described in detail in Exodus 28. There the names of Israel’s tribes are engraved on stones the priest bears before the LORD (28:9–12, 21, 29).

Arminian readers note that the people’s gifts will literally carry their names into God’s presence.

Reformed

The ephod and breastplate belong to the high priest’s garments (Exodus 28). The stones bearing the tribes’ names show the priest representing the people before God.

Reformed readers often see in this a picture of Christ, the great High Priest who bears his people before the Father (Heb 4:14–16).

Exodus 25:8

8Let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them.

Arminian

Here is the purpose of the whole project: “Let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them.” The holy God chooses to live in the midst of his redeemed people.

Arminian readers hear God’s desire for fellowship with his people. That desire moves through Scripture to the Word who “dwelt” (literally “tabernacled”) among us (John 1:14) and to God dwelling with all his people (Rev 21:3).

Reformed

This verse is the theological heart of the passage. The exodus aimed at this: a people with God dwelling among them (cf. 29:45–46).

Reformed readers trace this covenant promise through Scripture: “I will be their God, and they will be my people.” It is fulfilled in Christ (John 1:14) and the Spirit-indwelt church (1 Cor 3:16; Eph 2:21–22), and consummated in Rev 21:3.

Exodus 25:9

9According to all that I show you, the pattern of the tabernacle, and the pattern of all of its furniture, even so you shall make it.

Arminian

Moses is to build “according to all that I show you, the pattern.” The design comes from God, not human imagination.

Arminian readers note that faithful obedience shapes how God’s people approach him. Hebrews later reads the earthly tabernacle as a copy and shadow of heavenly realities (Heb 8:5; 9:23–24).

Reformed

The word “pattern” stresses that God himself designs the place where he will dwell. Israel is to follow it exactly.

Reformed readers have often cited this verse in discussing worship according to God’s revelation. With Hebrews (8:5), they read the tabernacle as a copy pointing beyond itself to Christ’s heavenly ministry.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
