Passage

Exodus 25

Verses 23–40 · WEB

Two more furnishings for the holy place follow: the gold-covered table that holds the bread of the presence, and the hammered gold lampstand with its seven lamps. The section closes with a renewed charge to build according to the pattern shown on the mountain.

Exodus 25:23

23“You shall make a table of acacia wood. Its length shall be two cubits, and its width a cubit, and its height one and a half cubits.

Arminian

The table, also of acacia wood, measures about 2 by 1 by 1½ cubits. It will stand in the holy place outside the veil (26:35).

Arminian readers see a table as a sign of fellowship. God provides a place where his provision is set before him continually.

Reformed

The table belongs to the holy place, the room the priests enter daily.

Reformed readers note that the sanctuary’s furnishings each have a role in the worship God appoints, and the table’s role is defined in verse 30.

Exodus 25:24

24You shall overlay it with pure gold, and make a gold molding around it.

Arminian

Like the ark, the table is overlaid with pure gold and given a gold molding.

Arminian readers see consistent honor given to everything that belongs to God’s house.

Reformed

The gold overlay marks the table as holy furniture.

Reformed readers note the visual unity of the holy place: gold furnishings in a gold-overlaid room (26:29).

Exodus 25:25

25You shall make a rim of a hand width around it. You shall make a golden molding on its rim around it.

Arminian

A rim a handbreadth wide runs around the table, with a gold molding on it, perhaps keeping items from sliding off.

Arminian readers accept such practical details without forcing hidden meanings onto them.

Reformed

The rim is a construction detail. The text gives no symbolic explanation.

Reformed readers, too, generally resist building doctrine on details the text leaves unexplained.

Exodus 25:26

26You shall make four rings of gold for it, and put the rings in the four corners that are on its four feet.

Arminian

Four gold rings are attached at the corners by the feet, as with the ark.

Arminian readers again see a sanctuary built for a journey with God.

Reformed

The table is portable like the ark.

Reformed readers note that the whole sanctuary is designed to move with Israel through the wilderness, under God’s leading by the cloud (40:36–38).

Exodus 25:27

27The rings shall be close to the rim, for places for the poles to carry the table.

Arminian

The rings sit close to the rim to hold the carrying poles.

Arminian readers see careful practical planning in God’s instructions.

Reformed

The rings serve the poles that carry the table.

Reformed readers note that God’s design attends to function as well as meaning.

Exodus 25:28

28You shall make the poles of acacia wood, and overlay them with gold, that the table may be carried with them.

Arminian

Poles of acacia wood overlaid with gold carry the table.

Arminian readers note the parallel with the ark’s poles (25:13–14). Each furnishing is treated with reverence.

Reformed

Like the ark, the table is carried by gold-covered poles.

Reformed readers see the same reverent handling applied across the holy furniture (cf. Num 4:7–8, 15).

Exodus 25:29

29You shall make its dishes, its spoons, its ladles, and its bowls with which to pour out offerings. You shall make them of pure gold.

Arminian

Dishes, spoons (or pans), ladles and bowls, all of pure gold, are made for the table, including vessels for pouring out offerings.

Arminian readers see the whole service of worship, down to its utensils, dedicated to God.

Reformed

The vessels serve the drink offerings and the table’s service.

Reformed readers note that even the smallest tools of worship are made according to God’s direction and set apart for him.

Exodus 25:30

30You shall set bread of the presence on the table before me always.

Arminian

The “bread of the presence” is set on the table “before me always.” Leviticus 24:5–9 adds that there are twelve loaves, renewed each Sabbath and eaten by the priests.

Arminian readers see the tribes of Israel continually before God and God’s constant provision for them. Many readers also recall Jesus as the bread of life (John 6:35).

Reformed

The bread is set “before me,” literally before God’s face. Twelve loaves, one for each tribe (Lev 24:5–9), represent the covenant people in God’s presence.

Reformed readers see covenant fellowship sustained by God’s provision. Jesus refers to David eating this bread (Matt 12:3–4), and many readers in both traditions connect it with Christ the bread of life (John 6:35).

Exodus 25:31

31“You shall make a lamp stand of pure gold. The lamp stand shall be made of hammered work. Its base, its shaft, its cups, its buds, and its flowers shall be of one piece with it.

Arminian

The lampstand is hammered from pure gold, with its base, shaft, cups, buds and flowers of one piece.

Arminian readers see beauty and light in God’s house. The floral design suggests life and growth.

Reformed

The lampstand is one hammered piece, a work of great skill.

Reformed readers note that the holy place, with no windows mentioned, is lit only by the light God appoints. Many connect this with Christ, the light of the world (John 8:12), while handling the link with care.

Exodus 25:32

32There shall be six branches going out of its sides: three branches of the lamp stand out of its one side, and three branches of the lamp stand out of its other side;

Arminian

Six branches extend from the central shaft, three on each side.

Arminian readers see symmetry and beauty in the design. God is honored by careful, ordered work.

Reformed

Seven lights in all, the central shaft and six branches, give the lampstand its familiar shape.

Reformed readers note that seven often signals completeness in Scripture, though the text does not explain the number here.

Exodus 25:33

33three cups made like almond blossoms in one branch, a bud and a flower; and three cups made like almond blossoms in the other branch, a bud and a flower, so for the six branches going out of the lamp stand;

Arminian

Each branch carries three cups shaped like almond blossoms, with bud and flower.

Arminian readers often note that the almond is among the first trees to blossom. Aaron’s rod later buds with almonds as a sign of God’s choice (Num 17:8).

Reformed

The almond motif may echo the wordplay in Jeremiah 1:11–12, where the almond (“shaqed”) recalls God watching (“shoqed”) over his word.

Reformed readers note the link carefully. The text itself only specifies the design.

Exodus 25:34

34and in the lamp stand four cups made like almond blossoms, its buds and its flowers;

Arminian

The central shaft has four almond-blossom cups with buds and flowers.

Arminian readers see a tree-like image of life in God’s presence. Some connect the tabernacle’s imagery with Eden.

Reformed

The lampstand looks like a stylized flowering tree.

Reformed readers who see Eden imagery in the sanctuary (cherubim, gold, tree-like lampstand) read it as God restoring access to his presence, while acknowledging that such readings are interpretive.

Exodus 25:35

35and a bud under two branches of one piece with it, and a bud under two branches of one piece with it, and a bud under two branches of one piece with it, for the six branches going out of the lamp stand.

Arminian

A bud sits under each pair of branches where they join the shaft, all of one piece.

Arminian readers see the craftsman’s care as an offering in itself: skill given wholly to God’s house.

Reformed

The repeated phrasing stresses the lampstand’s unified design.

Reformed readers do not press symbolic meaning onto each bud.

Exodus 25:36

36Their buds and their branches shall be of one piece with it, all of it one beaten work of pure gold.

Arminian

Everything is one beaten work of pure gold.

Arminian readers see integrity in the design: nothing is pieced together carelessly.

Reformed

The lampstand is beaten from a single mass of gold.

Reformed readers see costly craftsmanship devoted to God’s service.

Exodus 25:37

37You shall make its lamps seven, and they shall light its lamps to give light to the space in front of it.

Arminian

Seven lamps give light to the space in front of the lampstand. The lamps are to burn continually (27:20–21; Lev 24:2–4).

Arminian readers see God’s people kept in the light of his presence. In Revelation, lampstands represent the churches called to shine (Rev 1:12, 20).

Reformed

The lamps light the holy place where the priests serve before the table.

Reformed readers see God giving light for worship. Zechariah 4 and Revelation 1 develop lampstand imagery for God’s people empowered by his Spirit.

Exodus 25:38

38Its snuffers and its snuff dishes shall be of pure gold.

Arminian

Even the snuffers and trays for the wicks are pure gold.

Arminian readers note that maintaining the light is part of holy service.

Reformed

The tools for tending the lamps share the lampstand’s holiness.

Reformed readers see faithfulness in small things as part of worship.

Exodus 25:39

39It shall be made of a talent of pure gold, with all these accessories.

Arminian

The lampstand and its accessories use a talent of pure gold. The WEB footnote gives a talent as about 30 kilograms, or 66 pounds.

Arminian readers see generous, costly devotion.

Reformed

A talent of gold is a large amount, about 30 kilograms according to the WEB footnote.

Reformed readers note that the cost reflects the worth of the God for whom it is made.

Exodus 25:40

40See that you make them after their pattern, which has been shown to you on the mountain.

Arminian

“See that you make them after their pattern, which has been shown to you on the mountain.” Hebrews 8:5 quotes this verse to show that the earthly sanctuary was a copy and shadow of heavenly things.

Arminian readers agree that the tabernacle points to Christ’s priestly ministry, though they differ among themselves on how far to press typology into its details. Methodist and Wesleyan churches have generally allowed worship practices that Scripture does not forbid.

Reformed

The repeated charge to follow the pattern (cf. 25:9) shows that God, not Moses, designs his sanctuary. Hebrews 8:5 reads it as a copy of heavenly realities.

Some Reformed writers point to texts like this when discussing the regulative principle of worship: worship ordered by what God commands. Others stress that the tabernacle itself belongs to the ceremonial law fulfilled in Christ, and Reformed interpreters also differ on how much detail to read typologically.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
