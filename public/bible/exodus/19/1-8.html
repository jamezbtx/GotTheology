Passage

Exodus 19

Verses 1–8 · WEB

Three months after leaving Egypt, Israel camps before Sinai. God reminds the people that he carried them on eagles’ wings to himself and offers them a covenant: if they obey, they will be his treasured possession, a kingdom of priests and a holy nation. The people pledge to do all the LORD has spoken. Both traditions stress that redemption precedes the law, while explaining the covenant condition differently.

Exodus 19:1

1In the third month after the children of Israel had gone out of the land of Egypt, on that same day they came into the wilderness of Sinai.

Arminian

Israel reaches the wilderness of Sinai in the third month after leaving Egypt. Arminian readers see the journey arriving at the place where the rescued people will hear God’s covenant word. Redemption from Egypt comes first; the law that follows is given to a people already delivered, not to slaves trying to earn their freedom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the careful dating tie Sinai to the exodus that has just occurred. God brings his redeemed people to the mountain before giving them his covenant instruction. Both traditions agree that the order matters: deliverance precedes the law, which frames everything that follows in these chapters.

Exodus 19:2

2When they had departed from Rephidim, and had come to the wilderness of Sinai, they encamped in the wilderness; and there Israel encamped before the mountain.

Arminian

Israel camps before the mountain after leaving Rephidim. Arminian readers see a pause in the journey that prepares the people to meet God. The camp facing the mountain gives the scene its setting, as a whole community gathers to receive the words that will shape its life with the LORD.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the people positioned before Sinai, the place of covenant meeting. The repeated mention of encamping slows the narrative and fixes attention on the mountain. Both traditions read the verse as staging for the encounter, not as a moment carrying independent doctrinal weight.

Exodus 19:3

3Moses went up to God, and the LORD called to him out of the mountain, saying, “This is what you shall tell the house of Jacob, and tell the children of Israel:

Arminian

Moses goes up, and the LORD calls to him with a message for the house of Jacob. Arminian readers see God taking the initiative to speak and to address the whole people through a mediator. The double naming, Jacob and Israel, recalls the patriarchal promises now coming to bear on the nation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the LORD calling from the mountain and appointing Moses as mediator of his word. The covenant begins with divine speech, not human proposal. Both traditions see here the pattern of God revealing himself and his will through a chosen messenger to his people.

Exodus 19:4

4‘You have seen what I did to the Egyptians, and how I bore you on eagles’ wings, and brought you to myself.

Arminian

God reminds Israel of what they have seen: judgment on Egypt and his carrying them like eagles’ wings to himself. Arminian readers see grace demonstrated in history before any command is given. The goal of the rescue is relationship: God brought them to himself, inviting a grateful and trusting response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant grounded in God’s prior saving action. The image of eagles’ wings portrays strong, protective care, and the phrase to myself shows that communion with God is the purpose of redemption. Both traditions treat this verse as the gracious foundation for the obligations that follow.

Exodus 19:5

5Now therefore, if you will indeed obey my voice and keep my covenant, then you shall be my own possession from among all peoples; for all the earth is mine;

Arminian

If Israel obeys God’s voice and keeps his covenant, they will be his treasured possession among all peoples, for the whole earth is his. Arminian readers take the condition as a real call to faithful response, with covenant blessing tied to Israel continuing in obedience. God’s universal ownership makes his choice of Israel an act of free grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Israel’s special status flowing from God’s sovereign choice, since all the earth already belongs to him. The condition calls for covenant faithfulness, and Reformed interpreters differ among themselves on how the Mosaic covenant relates to the covenant of grace. All agree that obedience here responds to redemption rather than purchasing it.

Key contrast Both affirm that Israel was chosen by grace and is called to real obedience. Arminian readers typically treat the covenant condition as genuinely contingent on Israel’s response. Reformed readers typically read the conditional administration within God’s unconditional electing purpose, though they differ internally on the Mosaic covenant’s exact relation to the covenant of grace.

Exodus 19:6

6and you shall be to me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation.’ These are the words which you shall speak to the children of Israel.”

Arminian

Israel is to be a kingdom of priests and a holy nation. Arminian readers see a vocation for the whole people, set apart to represent God and draw near to him. Holiness is a calling to be lived out, giving Israel a role among the nations rather than merely a privilege to enjoy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Israel’s identity defined by God’s purpose: priestly service and holiness belong to the whole nation. The status is given before it is lived, and the people’s conduct should reflect it. Both traditions recognize the corporate calling here and the high honor of being set apart for God.

Exodus 19:7

7Moses came and called for the elders of the people, and set before them all these words which the LORD commanded him.

Arminian

Moses calls the elders and sets the LORD’s words before them. Arminian readers see God’s message presented openly so the people can understand and respond. The elders serve as representatives, and the covenant offer is placed before the community rather than imposed without hearing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faithful mediation: Moses delivers exactly what the LORD commanded. The use of elders shows the ordered structure of the community receiving the word. Both traditions emphasize that the covenant is grounded in God’s speech, communicated faithfully through appointed leaders.

Exodus 19:8

8All the people answered together, and said, “All that the LORD has spoken we will do.” Moses reported the words of the people to the LORD.

Arminian

The people answer together that they will do all the LORD has spoken. Arminian readers see a sincere, willing commitment, even though later events will reveal their weakness. The response is real and corporate, and Moses carries it back to God, completing the exchange.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an earnest pledge that will soon be tested and broken, which they often read as showing human inability apart from grace. Yet the commitment is not mocked by the text. Both traditions see genuine assent here, and both see the later narrative exposing how much the people need God’s continued mercy.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
