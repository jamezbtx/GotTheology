Passage

Exodus 19

Verses 9–15 · WEB

God promises to come in a thick cloud so the people will hear him speak with Moses and trust Moses forever. The people consecrate themselves, wash their clothes, and prepare for the third day, while boundaries around the mountain guard against presumptuous approach. Both traditions see God’s holiness and his desire to meet his people held together in these preparations.

Exodus 19:9

9The LORD said to Moses, “Behold, I come to you in a thick cloud, that the people may hear when I speak with you, and may also believe you forever.” Moses told the words of the people to the LORD.

Arminian

The LORD promises to come in a thick cloud so the people will hear him speak with Moses and trust Moses forever. Arminian readers see God accommodating the people’s need for assurance, confirming his messenger publicly. Faith in Moses’ mediation is meant to rest on what they themselves witness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God authenticating his appointed mediator before the people. The thick cloud both reveals and conceals, showing God’s presence while guarding his holiness. Both traditions see this as divine provision for trust, grounding the people’s confidence in God’s own testimony.

Exodus 19:10

10The LORD said to Moses, “Go to the people, and sanctify them today and tomorrow, and let them wash their garments,

Arminian

God tells Moses to consecrate the people for two days and have them wash their garments. Arminian readers see preparation as a real responsibility the people must carry out. Approaching the holy God requires readiness, and the outward washing expresses an inward seriousness about the meeting.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God setting the terms for approaching him; consecration is commanded, not invented. The washing signals that sinful people cannot casually come into his presence. Both traditions see the ritual preparation as fitting within the Sinai setting and pointing to God’s holiness.

Exodus 19:11

11and be ready for the third day; for on the third day the LORD will come down in the sight of all the people on Mount Sinai.

Arminian

The people must be ready for the third day, when the LORD will descend on Sinai in their sight. Arminian readers see anticipation building toward a public encounter with God. The whole people will witness his coming, so the event is shared rather than reserved for a few.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God fixing the day and manner of his appearing. The descent in the sight of all the people makes the revelation public and corporate. Both traditions see God’s initiative and the people’s preparation joined together, with the third day marking a decisive moment.

Exodus 19:12

12You shall set bounds to the people all around, saying, ‘Be careful that you don’t go up onto the mountain, or touch its border. Whoever touches the mountain shall be surely put to death.

Arminian

Moses must set boundaries around the mountain, and anyone who touches it will be put to death. Arminian readers see the seriousness of God’s holiness and the real danger of presumption. The boundaries protect the people as much as they restrict them, teaching reverence through a clear limit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the boundary as a vivid lesson in the distance between the holy God and sinful humanity. The severe penalty underlines that God is not approached on human terms. Both traditions read the command within the Sinai setting rather than as a timeless rule about sacred places.

Exodus 19:13

13No hand shall touch him, but he shall surely be stoned or shot through; whether it is animal or man, he shall not live.’ When the trumpet sounds long, they shall come up to the mountain.”

Arminian

Anyone who crosses must be stoned or shot, without being touched, whether animal or person; when the trumpet sounds long, they may come up. Arminian readers see the gravity of violating holy space and the care taken to avoid spreading contact. The trumpet signal shows that access is governed by God’s timing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see holiness so serious that even contact with the offender is avoided. The sounding trumpet marks the appointed moment for approach. Both traditions note that the details belong to this particular event and teach reverence for God’s presence rather than establishing a general method of judgment.

Exodus 19:14

14Moses went down from the mountain to the people, and sanctified the people; and they washed their clothes.

Arminian

Moses goes down, consecrates the people, and they wash their clothes. Arminian readers see obedience carried out promptly as the people prepare. The narrative shows Moses fulfilling his role and the people responding, a moment of cooperation before the dramatic encounter.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the commanded preparation carried out exactly as instructed. Moses’ mediation and the people’s compliance prepare them to meet God. Both traditions see faithful response to God’s word here, set in the context of the coming revelation.

Exodus 19:15

15He said to the people, “Be ready by the third day. Don’t have sexual relations with a woman.”

Arminian

Moses tells the people to be ready by the third day and to abstain from sexual relations. Arminian readers see temporary abstinence as part of ritual readiness in this setting, not a judgment that marriage is impure. The instruction focuses attention wholly on the meeting with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a temporary ceremonial requirement tied to the Sinai encounter. It does not teach that marital relations are sinful but sets this moment apart as uniquely solemn. Both traditions read the verse within its ritual context rather than as a general rule for believers.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
