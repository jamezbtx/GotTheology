Passage

Exodus 15

Verses 1–21 · WEB

After the crossing of the sea, Moses and Israel sing of the LORD’s victory over Pharaoh’s army, confess him as their strength and salvation, and look ahead to being planted in his sanctuary. Miriam leads the women in the refrain. Both traditions read the song as worship that gives God all the glory for a rescue Israel could not achieve. They differ mainly in how the redemption of a people relates to later themes of election and perseverance, not in praising the God who delivers.

Exodus 15:1

1Then Moses and the children of Israel sang this song to the LORD, and said, “I will sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously. He has thrown the horse and his rider into the sea.

Arminian

Israel answers deliverance with worship, and the song credits the victory wholly to the LORD. Arminian readers see grateful praise as the fitting response of a rescued people who received salvation they could not achieve.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a song that gives God all the glory for a rescue Israel did not accomplish. Both traditions agree that worship, not self-congratulation, is the first response to redemption.

Exodus 15:2

2The LORD is my strength and song. He has become my salvation. This is my God, and I will praise him; my father’s God, and I will exalt him.

Arminian

The singer confesses the LORD as strength, song, and salvation, and links personal praise with the God of the fathers. Arminian readers see covenant faithfulness received and owned in personal trust and praise.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that salvation is something the LORD has become for his people, not something they produced. Both hear continuity between the God of the patriarchs and the God who has just delivered Israel.

Exodus 15:3

3The LORD is a man of war. The LORD is his name.

Arminian

Calling the LORD a man of war praises him as the one who fought for Israel when they could not fight. Arminian readers stress God’s just intervention against oppression, not a license for human violence in his name.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the divine warrior defending his people and judging their oppressor. Both traditions read this as praise for God’s action, not a mandate for believers to wage holy war.

Exodus 15:4

4He has cast Pharaoh’s chariots and his army into the sea. His chosen captains are sunk in the Red Sea.

Arminian

Pharaoh’s army and chosen captains are overthrown in the sea. Arminian readers see judgment falling on a power that persistently resisted God’s word through the plagues and pursued Israel to destroy them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign power over the mightiest military force of the day. Both agree the verse celebrates judgment on an oppressor rather than inviting delight in suffering for its own sake.

Exodus 15:5

5The deeps cover them. They went down into the depths like a stone.

Arminian

The sinking image underscores how completely the threat is ended. Arminian readers see a once-terrifying enemy rendered powerless, giving the freed people reason to trust God for the journey ahead.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the finality of God’s judgment on those who set themselves against him. The shared pastoral point is that what threatened God’s people no longer has power over them.

Exodus 15:6

6Your right hand, LORD, is glorious in power. Your right hand, LORD, dashes the enemy in pieces.

Arminian

The LORD’s right hand symbolizes his effective power. Arminian readers see God acting decisively in history to rescue his people, while still calling them to respond in faith and obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the glory and sufficiency of divine power displayed in the rescue. Both traditions agree that Israel’s deliverance rested on God’s might rather than on their own strength or worthiness.

Exodus 15:7

7In the greatness of your excellency, you overthrow those who rise up against you. You send out your wrath. It consumes them as stubble.

Arminian

God overthrows those who rise against him, and his wrath consumes them like stubble. Arminian readers see righteous judgment on persistent rebellion, holding God’s justice together with his patience shown during the plagues.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the majesty of God against which no opposition can stand. Both affirm that divine wrath here is just response to hostility against God and his people, not arbitrary rage.

Exodus 15:8

8With the blast of your nostrils, the waters were piled up. The floods stood upright as a heap. The deeps were congealed in the heart of the sea.

Arminian

Poetic imagery describes God’s breath piling up the waters. Arminian readers see creation responding to the Creator’s command in order to make a way for his people to go free.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s lordship over the created order serving his redemptive purpose. Both traditions read the poetry as celebrating God’s real action without reducing it to mere metaphor or overpressing every image.

Exodus 15:9

9The enemy said, ‘I will pursue. I will overtake. I will divide the plunder. My desire will be satisfied on them. I will draw my sword. My hand will destroy them.’

Arminian

The song quotes the enemy’s boasting to show its arrogance before the fall. Arminian readers see human pride that trusted its own strength and despised God’s warnings until judgment came.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the enemy’s confident plans, which God overturned in a moment. Both traditions see a warning that human boasting against God and his people cannot stand.

Exodus 15:10

10You blew with your wind. The sea covered them. They sank like lead in the mighty waters.

Arminian

A single breath from God reverses the enemy’s boast. Arminian readers see how quickly self-confident opposition collapses when God acts on behalf of those he has called out.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the ease with which God accomplishes his purpose; the mighty army sinks like lead. Both agree the contrast between the enemy’s many words and God’s one act is deliberate.

Exodus 15:11

11Who is like you, LORD, among the gods? Who is like you, glorious in holiness, fearful in praises, doing wonders?

Arminian

The song asks who is like the LORD among the gods, praising his incomparable holiness and wonders. Arminian readers see the exodus as God revealing his character so that people may know and trust him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the LORD’s holiness and uniqueness magnified against the claims of Egypt’s gods. Both traditions confess God as utterly distinct, worthy of awe, and active in history.

Exodus 15:12

12You stretched out your right hand. The earth swallowed them.

Arminian

The earth swallowing the enemy expresses total defeat under God’s outstretched hand. Arminian readers read the image as poetic summary of judgment completed in the sea.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read it as a poetic picture of God’s complete victory. Both caution against building precise physical claims from the image while affirming the reality of the deliverance it celebrates.

Exodus 15:13

13“You, in your loving kindness, have led the people that you have redeemed. You have guided them in your strength to your holy habitation.

Arminian

God’s loving kindness leads the people he has redeemed toward his holy dwelling. Arminian readers see a redeemed covenant people guided by steadfast love; Scripture later shows many of the delivered generation falling through unbelief.

Reformed

Reformed readers see redemption flowing from God’s covenant love and leading to its intended goal of dwelling with him. They often read the exodus as a type of the definite redemption Christ accomplishes for his people.

Key contrast Both affirm that God redeemed Israel out of covenant love and guides them toward his dwelling. Arminian readers stress that many in this redeemed generation later perished through unbelief, warning that the redeemed must continue in faith; Reformed readers distinguish national redemption from individual salvation and often see here a type of Christ’s definite redemption of his people.

Exodus 15:14

14The peoples have heard. They tremble. Pangs have taken hold of the inhabitants of Philistia.

Arminian

The nations hear and tremble. Arminian readers see God’s deeds becoming public testimony, which elsewhere in Scripture can lead outsiders such as Rahab to faith as well as to fear.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s glory spreading among the nations through Israel’s rescue. Both affirm that God’s acts for his people also confront surrounding peoples with his power.

Exodus 15:15

15Then the chiefs of Edom were dismayed. Trembling takes hold of the mighty men of Moab. All the inhabitants of Canaan have melted away.

Arminian

Edom, Moab, and Canaan are pictured as dismayed. Arminian readers see the song anticipating the journey ahead and the obstacles that God’s power will overcome for his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read this as confidence that the God who acted at the sea will carry his promises forward. Both traditions see faith looking ahead on the basis of what God has already done.

Exodus 15:16

16Terror and dread falls on them. By the greatness of your arm they are as still as a stone, until your people pass over, LORD, until the people you have purchased pass over.

Arminian

Dread holds the nations still until God’s purchased people pass over. Arminian readers see a people acquired by God through gracious deliverance, owned by him and called to live as his possession.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the language of purchase as God securing a people for himself by his own act. Both affirm that Israel belongs to God by redemption, not by their own merit or strength.

Exodus 15:17

17You will bring them in, and plant them in the mountain of your inheritance, the place, LORD, which you have made for yourself to dwell in: the sanctuary, Lord, which your hands have established.

Arminian

God will plant his people on the mountain of his inheritance, the sanctuary his hands established. Arminian readers see the journey’s goal as communion with God in the place he provides.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s purpose to dwell among his people, accomplished by his own hands. Both traditions read the verse as hope directed to God’s presence rather than to land or possession alone.

Exodus 15:18

18The LORD will reign forever and ever.”

Arminian

The song ends with the confession that the LORD will reign forever. Arminian readers see the exodus as a sign of God’s rightful rule, inviting ongoing trust and loyalty from his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the climax of the song in God’s eternal kingship. Both traditions agree that the deliverance points beyond itself to the everlasting reign of God over all powers.

Exodus 15:19

19For the horses of Pharaoh went in with his chariots and with his horsemen into the sea, and the LORD brought back the waters of the sea on them; but the children of Israel walked on dry land in the middle of the sea.

Arminian

A prose summary restates the event the song celebrates. Arminian readers note the contrast: judgment falls on the pursuing army while Israel walks through on dry ground by God’s provision.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the same waters serving both judgment and salvation according to God’s will. Both agree the narrative grounds the song’s praise in an act God actually performed.

Exodus 15:20

20Miriam the prophetess, the sister of Aaron, took a tambourine in her hand; and all the women went out after her with tambourines and with dances.

Arminian

Miriam the prophetess leads the women in music and dance. Arminian readers note the public, communal leadership in worship given to Miriam and the full participation of women in Israel’s praise.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that Scripture names Miriam a prophetess and shows women leading this act of thanksgiving. Traditions differ internally on how this applies to church offices, a question this verse does not settle.

Exodus 15:21

21Miriam answered them, “Sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously. He has thrown the horse and his rider into the sea.”

Arminian

Miriam’s refrain echoes the opening of the song. Arminian readers see joyful response spreading through the community as the people take up the confession of God’s victory.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the repeated words fixing the community’s memory on what God has done. Both traditions see worship that is shared, repeated, and centered on God’s action rather than human achievement.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
