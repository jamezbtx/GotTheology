Passage

Exodus 15

Verses 22–27 · WEB

Three days after the song, the people find bitter water at Marah and murmur against Moses. God sweetens the water in answer to Moses’ cry, tests the people, promises protection to an obedient people as the LORD who heals, and leads them to the springs of Elim. Both traditions see redemption followed by real hardship, gracious provision, and covenant obedience that responds to deliverance rather than earning it.

Exodus 15:22

22Moses led Israel onward from the Red Sea, and they went out into the wilderness of Shur; and they went three days in the wilderness, and found no water.

Arminian

Three days into the wilderness without water, the newly delivered people face an immediate trial. Arminian readers see that redemption does not remove hardship but leads into a journey where trust must be exercised.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the wilderness as the place where God trains his redeemed people. Both agree that deliverance from Egypt was followed, not cancelled, by real need and testing.

Exodus 15:23

23When they came to Marah, they couldn’t drink from the waters of Marah, for they were bitter. Therefore its name was called Marah.

Arminian

At Marah the water is bitter, and the place is named for it. Arminian readers see a disappointment that tests whether the people will remember God’s recent faithfulness and continue trusting.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a providential hardship placed in the path of God’s people. Both note the sharp contrast between the song of victory and the bitterness that follows days later.

Exodus 15:24

24The people murmured against Moses, saying, “What shall we drink?”

Arminian

The people murmur against Moses. Arminian readers see how quickly gratitude can turn to complaint, a real danger for those who have experienced God’s saving power.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see remaining unbelief and ingratitude in a redeemed people. Both traditions read the murmuring as sinful distrust, while recognizing that thirst was a genuine need.

Exodus 15:25

25Then he cried to the LORD. The LORD showed him a tree, and he threw it into the waters, and the waters were made sweet. There he made a statute and an ordinance for them, and there he tested them.

Arminian

Moses cries to the LORD, God shows him a tree that sweetens the water, and there he tests them. Arminian readers see prayer answered and a test that calls the people to trust and obey.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God both providing for the need and using it to prove his people. Both agree that God answers Moses’ cry with mercy and that the remedy comes from God, not from the tree’s natural properties alone.

Exodus 15:26

26He said, “If you will diligently listen to the LORD your God’s voice, and will do that which is right in his eyes, and will pay attention to his commandments, and keep all his statutes, I will put none of the diseases on you which I have put on the Egyptians; for I am the LORD who heals you.”

Arminian

God promises protection from Egypt’s diseases if the people listen and obey, revealing himself as the LORD who heals. Arminian readers see a genuine covenant condition inviting responsive obedience within God’s gracious relationship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm real covenant obligations, reading them as the shape of life for a people God has already redeemed. Both agree obedience here responds to deliverance rather than earning it.

Exodus 15:27

27They came to Elim, where there were twelve springs of water and seventy palm trees. They encamped there by the waters.

Arminian

At Elim the people find twelve springs and seventy palm trees. Arminian readers see God’s generous provision after testing, encouraging trust for the hardships still ahead on the journey.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s kindness refreshing his people after Marah. Both traditions caution against allegorizing the numbers while receiving Elim as a gracious resting place in the wilderness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
