Passage

Exodus 39

Verses 1–21 · WEB

The artisans make Aaron’s holy garments: the ephod with engraved shoulder stones and the breastplate set with twelve stones bearing the names of Israel’s tribes. The repeated refrain, as the LORD commanded Moses, marks careful obedience to the instructions of Exodus 28. Both traditions see skilled, willing service, representative priesthood bearing the people before God, and worship shaped by his word; these verses are largely shared ground.

Exodus 39:1

1Of the blue, purple, and scarlet, they made finely worked garments for ministering in the holy place, and made the holy garments for Aaron, as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

The craftsmen make the holy garments exactly as the LORD commanded Moses, the refrain that runs through the chapter. Arminian readers see willing, skilled obedience offered by the people in response to God’s gracious instructions.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the work governed by God’s command rather than human invention, especially in worship. Both traditions agree the garments serve ministry in God’s presence and honor the holiness of the place where he meets Israel.

Exodus 39:2

2He made the ephod of gold, blue, purple, scarlet, and fine twined linen.

Arminian

The ephod is made of gold, colored yarns, and fine twined linen, matching the instructions given earlier in Exodus 28. Arminian readers see costly materials freely given by the people for God’s service.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see beauty and costliness appropriate to the priest who represents Israel before God. Both note that the narrative repeats the instructions to show careful correspondence between God’s word and the finished work.

Exodus 39:3

3They beat the gold into thin plates, and cut it into wires, to work it in with the blue, the purple, the scarlet, and the fine linen, the work of the skillful workman.

Arminian

Gold is beaten into thin plates and cut into wires to be woven into the fabric. Arminian readers see human skill and patient labor dignified when they are offered to God in obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s gifting of artisans, introduced earlier with Bezalel and Oholiab, applied to sacred work. Both traditions honor craftsmanship as a real service to God rather than a merely secular activity.

Exodus 39:4

4They made shoulder straps for it, joined together. It was joined together at the two ends.

Arminian

Shoulder straps join the ephod together at both ends. Arminian readers see attention to practical detail that makes the garment usable in ministry, not mere ornament without function.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the details carried out exactly as specified. Both traditions resist allegorizing every structural feature while recognizing that the care taken reflects reverence for the God being served.

Exodus 39:5

5The skillfully woven band that was on it, with which to fasten it on, was of the same piece, like its work: of gold, of blue, purple, scarlet, and fine twined linen, as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

The woven band is made of one piece with the ephod, using the same materials, as the LORD commanded. Arminian readers see consistent, wholehearted obedience extending even to fastenings and seams.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the repeated refrain stressing that worship is shaped by God’s instruction. Both note the unity of the garment as part of its careful construction, without needing to assign it a hidden meaning.

Exodus 39:6

6They worked the onyx stones, enclosed in settings of gold, engraved with the engravings of a signet, according to the names of the children of Israel.

Arminian

Onyx stones are set in gold and engraved with the names of Israel’s sons. Arminian readers see the people represented by name before God, highlighting his personal concern for the whole covenant community.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the priest bearing the tribes’ names as their representative. Both traditions find in this a picture of mediation in which God’s people are remembered before him.

Exodus 39:7

7He put them on the shoulder straps of the ephod, to be stones of memorial for the children of Israel, as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

The stones sit on the shoulders of the ephod as stones of memorial for Israel. Arminian readers see the priest carrying the people before God, a sign of God’s gracious attention to all the tribes.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see representative mediation, and some read it as anticipating Christ’s priestly work as described in Hebrews. Both agree the memorial expresses God’s covenant remembrance of his people.

Exodus 39:8

8He made the breastplate, the work of a skillful workman, like the work of the ephod: of gold, of blue, purple, scarlet, and fine twined linen.

Arminian

The breastplate is made with the same skill and materials as the ephod. Arminian readers see the priestly garments forming a coherent whole, designed for service in which the priest stands for the people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see continuity between the breastplate and ephod in their purpose of representation. Both traditions note that Exodus 28 links the breastplate with judgment and decision-making before the LORD.

Exodus 39:9

9It was square. They made the breastplate double. Its length was a span, and its width a span, being double.

Arminian

The breastplate is square, doubled, and a span in each direction. Arminian readers see precise measurement following God’s instructions, showing that the work was done carefully rather than approximately.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see exact fidelity to the pattern. Both note the WEB footnote explaining a span as roughly half a cubit, an approximate modern equivalent rather than a precise historical claim.

Exodus 39:10

10They set in it four rows of stones. A row of ruby, topaz, and beryl was the first row;

Arminian

Four rows of stones are set in the breastplate, beginning with ruby, topaz, and beryl. Arminian readers see costly gems given for God’s service, displaying beauty fitting for his dwelling.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see richness appropriate to the holy garments. Both traditions acknowledge that identifying ancient gem names with modern stones is uncertain and avoid building doctrine on particular identifications.

Exodus 39:11

11and the second row, a turquoise, a sapphire, and an emerald;

Arminian

The second row holds turquoise, sapphire, and emerald. Arminian readers see the continued listing as a record of faithful completion, each stone placed according to the instructions given.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see careful obedience in every detail. Both note the WEB footnote offering lapis lazuli as an alternative for sapphire, reflecting uncertainty in matching ancient terms to modern names.

Exodus 39:12

12and the third row, a jacinth, an agate, and an amethyst;

Arminian

The third row holds jacinth, agate, and amethyst. Arminian readers see variety and beauty gathered into one garment, as the many tribes are gathered into one people before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the stones prepared for bearing the tribes’ names. Both traditions caution against speculative symbolism for individual stones while appreciating the overall picture of a people precious to God.

Exodus 39:13

13and the fourth row, a chrysolite, an onyx, and a jasper. They were enclosed in gold settings.

Arminian

The fourth row holds chrysolite, onyx, and jasper, all set in gold. Arminian readers see the completed arrangement as evidence that the people finished what God asked of them.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the finished breastplate as the product of obedience to the revealed pattern. Both note that the gold settings secure each stone, completing the twelve.

Exodus 39:14

14The stones were according to the names of the children of Israel, twelve, according to their names; like the engravings of a signet, everyone according to his name, for the twelve tribes.

Arminian

The twelve stones bear the names of the tribes, engraved like a signet. Arminian readers see every tribe included and named, emphasizing God’s care for the whole people rather than a favored few.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the priest bearing the covenant people before God by name. Both traditions find comfort in the picture of God’s people remembered individually and corporately in his presence.

Exodus 39:15

15They made on the breastplate chains like cords, of braided work of pure gold.

Arminian

Braided chains of pure gold are made for the breastplate. Arminian readers see the craftsmen attending to how the breastplate will be secured, so that the representation of the people is not lost.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see detailed fidelity to the instructions. Both traditions read the chains primarily as practical fastenings, not as carriers of a separate doctrinal message.

Exodus 39:16

16They made two settings of gold, and two gold rings, and put the two rings on the two ends of the breastplate.

Arminian

Gold settings and rings are attached to the ends of the breastplate. Arminian readers see orderly, step-by-step obedience in completing a complex task assigned by God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the work proceeding according to the pattern given at Sinai. Both note the narrative’s patience with detail as a way of showing that nothing commanded was neglected.

Exodus 39:17

17They put the two braided chains of gold in the two rings at the ends of the breastplate.

Arminian

The two braided chains are fastened into the rings. Arminian readers see the parts of the garment joined so that the breastplate stays in place during the priest’s ministry before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see completion of each instruction in turn. Both traditions read this as part of the narrative’s demonstration that the people did all that was commanded.

Exodus 39:18

18The other two ends of the two braided chains they put on the two settings, and put them on the shoulder straps of the ephod, in its front.

Arminian

The other ends of the chains are attached to the settings on the shoulder straps. Arminian readers see the breastplate and shoulder stones connected, so the names on both remain together before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the joining of the parts into one integrated garment. Both caution against overreading the connection while recognizing its role in keeping the priest’s representative garments secure.

Exodus 39:19

19They made two rings of gold, and put them on the two ends of the breastplate, on its edge, which was toward the side of the ephod inward.

Arminian

Two more gold rings are placed on the inner edge of the breastplate. Arminian readers see thoroughness that leaves no part loose or unfinished in the work offered to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the exact correspondence between instruction and execution. Both traditions value the narrative’s witness that careful obedience extends to unseen details. Nothing is left half-finished.

Exodus 39:20

20They made two more rings of gold, and put them on the two shoulder straps of the ephod underneath, in its front, close by its coupling, above the skillfully woven band of the ephod.

Arminian

Additional rings are placed on the shoulder straps above the woven band. Arminian readers see craftsmanship serving function so that the priest can minister without the garment shifting.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see precision in following the given pattern. Both agree the verse describes practical construction and does not need to be pressed for symbolic meaning.

Exodus 39:21

21They bound the breastplate by its rings to the rings of the ephod with a lace of blue, that it might be on the skillfully woven band of the ephod, and that the breastplate might not come loose from the ephod, as the LORD commanded Moses.

Arminian

A blue lace binds the breastplate to the ephod so it will not come loose, as the LORD commanded Moses. Arminian readers see the refrain marking another stage of faithful completion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the repeated formula affirming obedience to God’s word. Both traditions note that the secure fastening keeps the tribes’ names continually before the LORD as Exodus 28 intends.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
