Passage

Exodus 13

Verses 1–16 · WEB

Fresh from the Passover night of Exodus 12, Israel receives instructions that turn deliverance into lasting memory. The LORD claims every firstborn as his own, and Moses links the seven days of Unleavened Bread to the day the LORD brought the people out of the house of bondage by strength of hand. Parents are to explain the rites to their children, and firstborn sons are redeemed rather than sacrificed, so each generation learns that its life was bought back by God. Both traditions read here a God who redeems first and then shapes his people through remembered grace, worship, and teaching; they differ mainly in how they relate the covenant community’s outward signs to the personal faith each member must come to own.

Exodus 13:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the instructions which follow begin with God speaking, not with Israel’s own initiative. The redeemed community is shaped by the word of the God who has just delivered it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the LORD taking the lead in ordering the life of the people he has redeemed. Both traditions agree that worship and obedience answer God’s prior word and work.

Exodus 13:2

2“Sanctify to me all the firstborn, whatever opens the womb among the children of Israel, both of man and of animal. It is mine.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see God’s claim on the firstborn as a reminder that Israel’s life now belongs to the One who spared it on Passover night. The claim calls for grateful, willing consecration rather than grudging duty.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear in “It is mine” a declaration of God’s rightful ownership over the people he has redeemed. Both traditions read the firstborn as representing the whole nation, set apart because the LORD has acted for them.

Exodus 13:3

3Moses said to the people, “Remember this day, in which you came out of Egypt, out of the house of bondage; for by strength of hand the LORD brought you out from this place. No leavened bread shall be eaten.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that Moses calls the people to remember, linking their daily practice to a real past deliverance. Memory nourishes ongoing trust and helps guard against drifting back toward the mindset of bondage.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the exodus is described as the LORD’s work by strength of hand, not Israel’s achievement. Both traditions agree that redemption comes first and that the rule about leaven expresses gratitude for it rather than earning it.

Exodus 13:4

4Today you go out in the month Abib.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the naming of the month Abib as fixing a real moment in time when God acted. Faith rests on what God has actually done in history, not on a timeless idea.

Reformed

Reformed readers also value the concrete date as a marker of God’s faithful timing in fulfilling his word. Both traditions find comfort in a salvation anchored in an actual day of deliverance.

Exodus 13:5

5It shall be, when the LORD brings you into the land of the Canaanite, and the Hittite, and the Amorite, and the Hivite, and the Jebusite, which he swore to your fathers to give you, a land flowing with milk and honey, that you shall keep this service in this month.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the observance looks ahead to life in the promised land, which the people must still enter and inhabit. God’s oath to the fathers is sure, while the people are called to keep faith along the way.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the land is given on the basis of what the LORD swore to the fathers. Both traditions agree that the feast is to be kept in grateful response to a faithful, promise-keeping God.

Exodus 13:6

6Seven days you shall eat unleavened bread, and in the seventh day shall be a feast to the LORD.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the seven days of unleavened bread as a sustained, embodied act of remembering. The feast trains the people to keep returning to God’s deliverance with joy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the feast to the LORD as worship ordered by God himself, not a ritual Israel devised. Both traditions agree that the rhythm of the feast directs the people’s attention back to his saving act.

Exodus 13:7

7Unleavened bread shall be eaten throughout the seven days; and no leavened bread shall be seen with you. No yeast shall be seen with you, within all your borders.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice the thoroughness of removing leaven from all their borders, a picture of wholehearted participation. The command invites the whole household to take part rather than observing at a distance.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe the careful completeness of the command and the holiness it signals for the redeemed community. Both traditions recognize that the leaven rules recall the haste of the exodus and mark Israel as set apart.

Exodus 13:8

8You shall tell your son in that day, saying, ‘It is because of that which the LORD did for me when I came out of Egypt.’

Arminian

Arminian readers value the father’s personal confession, “what the LORD did for me,” as a model of passing on living faith. Each generation is invited to make the story its own.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the household as a primary place where God’s covenant mercies are taught and remembered. Both traditions agree that parents are called to tell their children plainly what the LORD has done.

Exodus 13:9

9It shall be for a sign to you on your hand, and for a memorial between your eyes, that the LORD’s law may be in your mouth; for with a strong hand the LORD has brought you out of Egypt.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the sign on hand and forehead as calling for the LORD’s law to shape speech and conduct in everyday life. Remembered grace is meant to lead to willing obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect the sign with the law in the mouth, showing that redemption leads into a life ordered by God’s word. Interpreters differ on whether the sign is literal or figurative, but both traditions affirm its aim: lives marked by the memory of God’s strong hand.

Exodus 13:10

10You shall therefore keep this ordinance in its season from year to year.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the yearly ordinance as a gracious means of keeping faith fresh across time. Regular remembering helps God’s people continue in trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the appointed season as God’s provision for sustaining his people in covenant faithfulness. Both traditions value the regular, ordered rhythms of worship that keep his works in view.

Exodus 13:11

11“It shall be, when the LORD brings you into the land of the Canaanite, as he swore to you and to your fathers, and will give it to you,

Arminian

Arminian readers again notice the forward look to the land God has promised to give. The promise is certain on God’s side and invites continued faithfulness on Israel’s side.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the land comes as the fulfillment of God’s sworn word to the fathers and to the present generation. Both traditions see God’s faithfulness to his oath as the ground of Israel’s hope.

Exodus 13:12

12that you shall set apart to the LORD all that opens the womb, and every firstborn that comes from an animal which you have. The males shall be the LORD’s.

Arminian

Arminian readers see setting apart the firstborn as an ongoing act of grateful dedication. The people offer back to the LORD what he has spared, as a sign that all they have belongs to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the phrase “the males shall be the LORD’s” God’s claim on what he has redeemed. Both traditions read the practice as a lasting memorial of the Passover night.

Exodus 13:13

13Every firstborn of a donkey you shall redeem with a lamb; and if you will not redeem it, then you shall break its neck; and you shall redeem all the firstborn of man among your sons.

Arminian

Arminian readers see in the redemption of the firstborn son a picture of life bought back by a substitute. It points to the costly mercy of God, which every person is invited to receive.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see the lamb that redeems as a pattern of substitution that later Scripture develops more fully. Both traditions agree that redemption by a substitute is at the heart of the passage, even as they differ elsewhere on how its benefits are applied.

Exodus 13:14

14It shall be, when your son asks you in time to come, saying, ‘What is this?’ that you shall tell him, ‘By strength of hand the LORD brought us out from Egypt, from the house of bondage.

Arminian

Arminian readers value the expectation that children will ask questions, showing that faith is passed on through real conversation. The answer centers on what the LORD has done, not on human merit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the scripted answer as covenant instruction that keeps God’s mighty deliverance at the center of the household’s life. Both traditions agree that the next generation needs both the practice and its meaning.

Exodus 13:15

15When Pharaoh stubbornly refused to let us go, the LORD killed all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, both the firstborn of man, and the firstborn of livestock. Therefore I sacrifice to the LORD all that opens the womb, being males; but all the firstborn of my sons I redeem.’

Arminian

Arminian readers note that Pharaoh is described as stubbornly refusing, keeping his responsibility in view. The striking of Egypt’s firstborn is presented as judgment on persistent resistance to God’s word.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the death of Egypt’s firstborn as the LORD’s righteous judgment and the sparing of Israel’s firstborn as his mercy. Both traditions agree that Pharaoh was truly culpable and that Israel’s redemption came at a great cost.

Exodus 13:16

16It shall be for a sign on your hand, and for symbols between your eyes; for by strength of hand the LORD brought us out of Egypt.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see the closing refrain as a call to keep God’s saving power constantly before the mind. Visible reminders support a faith that must keep choosing to remember.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the repeated “by strength of hand” as underlining that deliverance is entirely the LORD’s work. Both traditions find in this refrain the heart of the section: God redeemed his people, so they belong to him.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
