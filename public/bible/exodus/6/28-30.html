Passage

Exodus 6

Verses 28–30 · WEB

After the genealogy, the narrative resumes where verse 12 left off. The LORD repeats his self-identification and his command that Moses tell Pharaoh everything he is told, and Moses repeats his objection that he has uncircumcised lips. The repetition brings the reader back to the commission just before chapter 7, where the LORD answers by appointing Aaron as Moses’ spokesman. Both traditions see the LORD’s steady word set against the messenger’s felt inadequacy.

Exodus 6:28

28On the day when the LORD spoke to Moses in the land of Egypt,

Arminian

The narrative resumes: on the day the LORD spoke to Moses in Egypt. This line picks up the thread left at verse 12.

Arminian readers see God renewing his word to his servant at the point of discouragement. He returns to the conversation Moses had broken off.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the literary technique of resumption, which brings the reader back to the commission after the genealogy.

Both traditions read the verse as a bridge, not a new event.

Exodus 6:29

29The LORD said to Moses, “I am the LORD. Tell Pharaoh king of Egypt all that I tell you.”

Arminian

The LORD says again: I am the LORD. Tell Pharaoh all that I tell you. The name and the command are repeated.

Arminian readers note that God’s command is clear and complete. Moses is responsible to speak everything he is given.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the authority of the message lies in the speaker’s name, not in the messenger. Moses is to convey God’s word fully.

Both traditions see faithful speech as the servant’s part, and results as God’s.

Exodus 6:30

30Moses said before the LORD, “Behold, I am of uncircumcised lips, and how shall Pharaoh listen to me?”

Arminian

Moses repeats his objection: I am of uncircumcised lips; how will Pharaoh listen? The chapter ends with the messenger’s weakness in view.

Arminian readers treat Moses’ hesitation sympathetically. God does not reject him but answers by appointing Aaron (7:1–2).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s purpose does not depend on Moses’ eloquence. The answer in chapter 7 shows that God supplies what his servant lacks.

Both traditions take comfort that God works through weak servants.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
