Passage

Exodus 6

Verses 14–27 · WEB

The narrative pauses for a selective genealogy. It lists the heads of Reuben and Simeon briefly, then traces Levi in detail through Kohath and Amram to Aaron and Moses, and on to Aaron’s sons and grandson Phinehas, along with the Korahites. The closing verses state the purpose: these are the Aaron and Moses whom the LORD sent to Pharaoh. Both traditions read the list as locating the two messengers within Israel’s family and anticipating the priestly line, not as a full census. Several names reappear later in Israel’s story, for good and for ill.

Exodus 6:14

14These are the heads of their fathers’ houses. The sons of Reuben the firstborn of Israel: Hanoch, and Pallu, Hezron, and Carmi; these are the families of Reuben.

Arminian

The genealogy begins with the heads of their fathers’ houses. Reuben, Israel’s firstborn, comes first, with four sons, matching Genesis 46:9.

Arminian readers note that the list places Moses and Aaron within Israel’s families. The calling of individuals happens within a people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the genealogy begins with Jacob’s firstborn but moves quickly to Levi. Birth order does not determine whom God uses.

Both traditions read the list as purposeful, not as filler between narratives.

Exodus 6:15

15The sons of Simeon: Jemuel, and Jamin, and Ohad, and Jachin, and Zohar, and Shaul the son of a Canaanite woman; these are the families of Simeon.

Arminian

Simeon’s sons are listed, including Shaul, son of a Canaanite woman, as in Genesis 46:10. The list is brief and moves on.

Arminian readers note the inclusion of a son of a Canaanite woman without comment. Israel’s family is not as tidy as it might appear.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Reuben and Simeon are given only briefly, which highlights the focus on Levi. The text is selective by design.

Both traditions avoid speculating beyond what the text says about these families.

Exodus 6:16

16These are the names of the sons of Levi according to their generations: Gershon, and Kohath, and Merari; and the years of the life of Levi were one hundred thirty-seven years.

Arminian

Levi’s sons are Gershon, Kohath, and Merari. Levi lived one hundred thirty-seven years. The fuller detail signals that Levi is the focus.

Arminian readers see the tribe that will serve at the tabernacle introduced here. God prepares servants through ordinary family lines.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the priestly and Levitical service of later chapters is anticipated in this lineage. God’s purposes span generations.

Both traditions read the ages as part of the record and avoid building theories on them.

Exodus 6:17

17The sons of Gershon: Libni and Shimei, according to their families.

Arminian

Gershon’s sons are Libni and Shimei. The list continues family by family.

Arminian readers note that these families will later receive specific tabernacle duties. Every family has a place in Israel’s service.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the genealogy records ordinary names that later receive roles in worship. God’s order includes the unremarkable.

Both traditions resist inventing details about individuals the text only names.

Exodus 6:18

18The sons of Kohath: Amram, and Izhar, and Hebron, and Uzziel; and the years of the life of Kohath were one hundred thirty-three years.

Arminian

Kohath’s sons are Amram, Izhar, Hebron, and Uzziel. Kohath lived one hundred thirty-three years.

Arminian readers note that the line narrows toward Amram and Moses. The genealogy leads the reader to the messengers.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the Kohathite line will include both Aaron’s priestly family and Korah. Shared ancestry does not guarantee shared faithfulness.

Both traditions note that some interpreters treat genealogies like this as selective, which bears on how they relate to the 430 years in 12:40; the question remains debated.

Exodus 6:19

19The sons of Merari: Mahli and Mushi. These are the families of the Levites according to their generations.

Arminian

Merari’s sons are Mahli and Mushi. These complete the families of the Levites.

Arminian readers see the Levites listed as a whole tribe before the focus narrows. God calls a tribe and individuals within it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the orderly completion of Levi’s families. The structure prepares for the specific line of Aaron and Moses.

Both traditions treat the list as part of the inspired record without overloading its details.

Exodus 6:20

20Amram took Jochebed his father’s sister to himself as wife; and she bore him Aaron and Moses. The years of the life of Amram were one hundred thirty-seven years.

Arminian

Amram married Jochebed, his father’s sister, and she bore Aaron and Moses. Amram lived one hundred thirty-seven years.

Arminian readers note that this marriage would later be prohibited under Leviticus 18:12. Scripture records Israel’s history honestly, before the Law was given.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God raised up Israel’s deliverers through an ordinary, imperfect family. Grace does not wait for ideal circumstances.

Both traditions avoid moralizing beyond what the text says, while noting the later prohibition.

Exodus 6:21

21The sons of Izhar: Korah, and Nepheg, and Zichri.

Arminian

Izhar’s sons are Korah, Nepheg, and Zichri. Korah’s name anticipates the rebellion recorded in Numbers 16.

Arminian readers note that Korah, a close relative of Moses and Aaron, will later rebel. Privileged position does not take away personal responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the same family tree holds both faithful leaders and a future rebel. Outward closeness to God’s work is not the same as a true heart.

Both traditions read the name soberly, without assuming the outcome is visible here.

Exodus 6:22

22The sons of Uzziel: Mishael, Elzaphan, and Sithri.

Arminian

Uzziel’s sons are Mishael, Elzaphan, and Sithri. Mishael and Elzaphan later carry out the bodies of Nadab and Abihu (Leviticus 10:4).

Arminian readers note that ordinary relatives appear later in solemn moments. God’s service includes hard tasks.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the genealogy prepares for later narrative without explaining it here. The list is not random.

Both traditions treat these names briefly, as the text does.

Exodus 6:23

23Aaron took Elisheba, the daughter of Amminadab, the sister of Nahshon, as his wife; and she bore him Nadab and Abihu, Eleazar and Ithamar.

Arminian

Aaron married Elisheba, daughter of Amminadab and sister of Nahshon. She bore Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar. Nahshon is a leader of Judah (Numbers 1:7) and appears in David’s ancestry (Ruth 4:20).

Arminian readers note the joining of Levi and Judah in this marriage. Aaron’s sons will have very different stories.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Nadab and Abihu will later die for offering unauthorized fire (Leviticus 10:1–2), while Eleazar continues the priesthood. Privilege is no substitute for obedience.

Both traditions read the four names as a preview of the priestly line, with warning and promise.

Exodus 6:24

24The sons of Korah: Assir, Elkanah, and Abiasaph; these are the families of the Korahites.

Arminian

Korah’s sons are Assir, Elkanah, and Abiasaph, the families of the Korahites. The Korahite line continues.

Arminian readers note that, although Korah later rebels, his line is listed here as a family within Levi. The text records a continuing family.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the genealogy keeps Korah’s descendants in view. Numbers 26:11 later states that the sons of Korah did not die.

Both traditions avoid reading too much into the list beyond its role in tracing families.

Exodus 6:25

25Eleazar Aaron’s son took one of the daughters of Putiel as his wife; and she bore him Phinehas. These are the heads of the fathers’ houses of the Levites according to their families.

Arminian

Eleazar married one of the daughters of Putiel, and she bore Phinehas. These are the heads of the Levite fathers’ houses.

Arminian readers note that Phinehas will later act zealously for God (Numbers 25). The genealogy anticipates future faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priestly line continuing through Eleazar to Phinehas, to whom a covenant of peace is later given (Numbers 25:12–13). God sustains the line he appoints.

Both traditions read this as preparation for the priesthood’s later role.

Exodus 6:26

26These are that Aaron and Moses to whom the LORD said, “Bring out the children of Israel from the land of Egypt according to their armies.”

Arminian

These are the Aaron and Moses to whom the LORD said, Bring out the children of Israel according to their armies. The genealogy states its purpose.

Arminian readers note that God works through specific, named people. Their calling is personal and real.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God identifies his chosen instruments within a known lineage. Their authority comes from his word.

Both traditions see the genealogy confirming that the messengers are real members of Israel, not outsiders.

Exodus 6:27

27These are those who spoke to Pharaoh king of Egypt, to bring out the children of Israel from Egypt. These are that Moses and Aaron.

Arminian

These are the ones who spoke to Pharaoh to bring Israel out of Egypt: Moses and Aaron. The order of the names shifts from verse 26, which may simply vary the emphasis.

Arminian readers see the human agents of the mission affirmed. God calls them to speak, and they speak.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the repetition frames the genealogy and returns to the mission. The names matter because of the task God gave.

Both traditions read the closing as tying genealogy back to narrative.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
