Passage

Exodus 6

Verses 1–13 · WEB

The LORD answers Moses’ lament: now you will see what I will do to Pharaoh. He grounds the coming deliverance in his name, his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and his hearing of Israel’s groaning. A series of “I will” promises—bringing out, rescuing, redeeming, taking Israel as his people, being their God, and giving the land—frames the exodus as covenant faithfulness. Israel, crushed by anguish and cruel bondage, does not listen, and Moses protests that he has uncircumcised lips. Both traditions locate the hope of the passage in God’s self-revelation and promise rather than in Israel’s readiness or Moses’ eloquence. Verse 3 is a well-known interpretive crux, and the notes set out its main readings without settling it.

Exodus 6:1

1The LORD said to Moses, “Now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh, for by a strong hand he shall let them go, and by a strong hand he shall drive them out of his land.”

Arminian

The LORD answers Moses’ complaint: now you will see what I will do to Pharaoh. A strong hand will make Pharaoh let them go and even drive them out.

Classical Arminians read God’s strong hand as decisive power exercised against a ruler who has already set himself against God. Pharaoh will act under compulsion, and his responsibility for his earlier refusals remains his own.

Reformed

The LORD does not explain Moses’ suffering; he points to what he will do. The deliverance will be his work, so that Pharaoh’s release of Israel is forced by God’s hand, not a change of heart.

Reformed readers hear in “Now you shall see” a sovereign purpose that includes Pharaoh’s resistance, as 3:19–20 and 4:21 have announced. God governs the conflict from beginning to end.

Key contrast Both traditions agree that the LORD’s strong hand, not Pharaoh’s goodwill, will bring Israel out, and that Pharaoh remains responsible for his refusals. Classical Arminians generally read the hardening that follows (anticipated in 4:21) as judicial: God confirms and uses a resistance Pharaoh already chose, for the sake of his redemptive purpose for Israel, without a decree to reprobation for Pharaoh personally. Reformed readers generally see God’s sovereign purpose standing behind Pharaoh’s refusals from the start, as Paul’s use of the Pharaoh story in Romans 9:17–18 suggests to them, while insisting that Pharaoh acts willingly and God is not the author of sin.

Exodus 6:2

2God spoke to Moses, and said to him, “I am the LORD.

Arminian

God speaks again: I am the LORD. The divine name stands at the head of the answer, and it will frame this whole speech (6:2, 6, 8).

Arminian readers see God answering disappointment by revealing himself. The solution to Moses’ crisis is not a strategy but a renewed knowledge of who God is.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God grounds the promises in his own name and character. What he will do flows from who he is.

Both traditions agree that the repeated “I am the LORD” is the anchor of the passage and the basis of Israel’s hope.

Exodus 6:3

3I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, as God Almighty; but by my name the LORD I was not known to them.

Arminian

God appeared to the patriarchs as God Almighty, but “by my name the LORD I was not known to them.” Readers have long noticed that Genesis uses the name in patriarchal stories (for example, Genesis 15:7 and 28:13).

One common reading, held by many Arminian and Reformed interpreters alike, is that the patriarchs knew the name but had not yet experienced what it signifies in redemption and covenant fulfillment. Others read the verse as a statement that the name itself was first revealed to Moses, which raises questions about how Genesis was composed. A smaller number take the clause as a question. The text does not decide between these.

Reformed

“God Almighty” recalls God’s self-revelation to Abraham and Jacob (Genesis 17:1; 35:11). The contrast is between the patriarchs’ experience of promise and Israel’s coming experience of fulfillment.

Reformed interpreters often adopt the “known in character, not merely in label” reading, though some acknowledge the other options and their difficulties. Both traditions agree that the verse presents the exodus as a fresh and fuller revelation of the same God who spoke to the fathers.

Exodus 6:4

4I have also established my covenant with them, to give them the land of Canaan, the land of their travels, in which they lived as aliens.

Arminian

God has established his covenant to give the patriarchs the land where they lived as aliens. The promise reaches back to Genesis and has not been forgotten.

Arminian readers note that the covenant is a gracious promise. The patriarchs lived by faith in it, and their descendants are now invited to trust the same promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the covenant rests on God’s initiative and oath. He established it, and he will bring it to fulfillment.

Both traditions see continuity between Genesis and Exodus: the exodus is the outworking of God’s faithfulness to Abraham.

Exodus 6:5

5Moreover I have heard the groaning of the children of Israel, whom the Egyptians keep in bondage, and I have remembered my covenant.

Arminian

God has heard Israel’s groaning and remembered his covenant, echoing 2:24. “Remembered” does not mean God had forgotten; it means he is about to act on his promise.

Arminian readers see God’s compassion responding to his people’s cry. Their suffering moves him, and he acts in faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s action is grounded in the covenant, not in any merit of the groaning people. His memory is his faithfulness.

Both traditions take comfort that God hears suffering even when his people are not yet able to trust him.

Exodus 6:6

6Therefore tell the children of Israel, ‘I am the LORD, and I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians, and I will rid you out of their bondage, and I will redeem you with an outstretched arm, and with great judgments.

Arminian

Moses is to tell Israel: I am the LORD, and I will bring you out, rid you of bondage, and redeem you with an outstretched arm and great judgments. “Redeem” suggests the act of a near kinsman who rescues family.

Arminian readers see God freely offering deliverance to his people. The promise is addressed to Israel’s hearing and calls for trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers see redemption as God’s decisive work, accomplished by his outstretched arm and judgments on Egypt. The deliverance does not depend on Israel’s strength.

Both traditions see the exodus as a pattern of redemption that later Scripture develops, including in the work of Christ.

Exodus 6:7

7I will take you to myself for a people. I will be your God; and you shall know that I am the LORD your God, who brings you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians.

Arminian

God promises: I will take you to myself for a people, and I will be your God, and you will know that I am the LORD. This is covenant language that recurs through Scripture (compare Leviticus 26:12; Jeremiah 31:33).

Classical Arminians read God’s choice of Israel as gracious and corporate, calling the nation into relationship and inviting each member to respond in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign initiative in taking a people for himself. The relationship begins with his choice, not with Israel’s qualifications (compare Deuteronomy 7:6–8).

Both traditions agree that knowing the LORD is the goal of the exodus, and that this knowledge comes through what he does for his people.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm that God freely and graciously takes Israel as his people, apart from any merit of theirs. Classical Arminians typically read this election as corporate and covenantal: God chooses a people for a purpose, and individuals share in its blessings through faith, so that some within the elect nation can fail to enter its promises. Reformed readers affirm the corporate and covenantal sense as well, but they often see it as a pattern that the New Testament develops into God’s particular choice of individuals within the visible people. The text itself speaks of Israel as a whole, so both sides draw their wider doctrine from other passages.

Exodus 6:8

8I will bring you into the land which I swore to give to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob; and I will give it to you for a heritage: I am the LORD.’ ”

Arminian

God will bring Israel into the land promised to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and give it to them as a heritage. The speech closes as it began: I am the LORD.

Arminian readers note that the promise invites Israel to trust and follow. Later chapters show that a generation can fail to enter through unbelief.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the land is given as a heritage by God’s oath. The promise rests on his faithfulness, framed at both ends by his name.

Both traditions see the land as gift, received rather than earned.

Exodus 6:9

9Moses spoke so to the children of Israel, but they didn’t listen to Moses for anguish of spirit, and for cruel bondage.

Arminian

Moses delivers the promise, but the people do not listen because of anguish of spirit and cruel bondage. Their failure to hear is linked to what they have suffered.

Arminian readers note that the text gives a reason for their not listening: crushing oppression, not settled rebellion. God does not abandon them for this but continues to act on their behalf.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that even a glorious promise can fall on ears closed by despair. Deliverance will come not because Israel has first believed but because God acts.

Both traditions avoid reading this verse as a full doctrine of human inability; it describes a people broken by suffering. Pastoral readers in both traditions find here God’s patience with those too wounded to hope.

Exodus 6:10

10The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses again. Despite the people’s failure to listen, God does not withdraw his commission.

Arminian readers see God’s persistence. He continues to work with his people and his messenger even when the response is discouraging.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s word moves forward regardless of the response. His plan does not wait on human readiness.

Both traditions take encouragement that God keeps speaking when his servants feel they have failed.

Exodus 6:11

11“Go in, speak to Pharaoh king of Egypt, that he let the children of Israel go out of his land.”

Arminian

Moses must go back to Pharaoh and tell him to let Israel go. The command is unchanged.

Arminian readers note that God continues to address Pharaoh with a real demand, giving him further opportunity to obey.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the repeated command keeps exposing Pharaoh’s resistance. God’s purpose advances through each demand and refusal.

Both traditions see God’s demand as sincere and Pharaoh as responsible for his answer.

Exodus 6:12

12Moses spoke before the LORD, saying, “Behold, the children of Israel haven’t listened to me. How then shall Pharaoh listen to me, when I have uncircumcised lips?”

Arminian

Moses protests: if Israel will not listen, how will Pharaoh? He also pleads that he has uncircumcised lips, an image of speech that feels unfit or ineffective (compare 4:10).

Arminian readers treat Moses’ honesty sympathetically. His fear is understandable, and God works with his weakness rather than dismissing him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Moses measures the mission by results and by his own ability. God’s answer will show that the power lies in God, not in the messenger.

Both traditions recognize that the image of “uncircumcised” elsewhere describes ears or hearts closed to God (Jeremiah 6:10; Deuteronomy 10:16), though here it concerns Moses’ sense of unfitness.

Exodus 6:13

13The LORD spoke to Moses and to Aaron, and gave them a command to the children of Israel, and to Pharaoh king of Egypt, to bring the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt.

Arminian

The LORD gives Moses and Aaron a charge for both Israel and Pharaoh: bring Israel out of Egypt. Aaron is now explicitly included.

Arminian readers note that God responds to Moses’ objection by restating the mission and including Aaron, in keeping with 4:14–16.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s answer is a command, not an argument. His charge stands over both the people and the king.

Both traditions see that God’s call does not depend on the messenger’s confidence.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
