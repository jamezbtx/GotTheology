Passage

Exodus 9

Verses 8–12 · WEB

The sixth plague comes without warning: Moses throws furnace ashes skyward and boils break out on people and animals. The magicians cannot even stand before Moses. For the first time the narrator says plainly that the LORD hardened Pharaoh’s heart, as he had told Moses he would.

Exodus 9:8

8The LORD said to Moses and to Aaron, “Take handfuls of ashes of the furnace, and let Moses sprinkle it toward the sky in the sight of Pharaoh.

Arminian

Ashes from a furnace become the means of judgment. The action is public, done “in the sight of Pharaoh,” so that he cannot mistake who is acting.

This plague comes with no recorded warning, which many readers take as a sign that the time of repeated warnings is narrowing after Pharaoh’s refusals.

Reformed

God uses an ordinary, humble means, ashes, to accomplish an extraordinary judgment. Reformed readers see here the freedom of God to work through whatever means he chooses.

Shared ground: the sign is visible, deliberate and from the LORD.

Exodus 9:9

9It shall become small dust over all the land of Egypt, and shall be boils and blisters breaking out on man and on animal, throughout all the land of Egypt.”

Arminian

The judgment now touches human bodies, not only animals and water. The escalation is moral as well as physical: Pharaoh’s refusals cost his people more each time.

Arminians read the progression as both judgment and warning, still holding out reasons to relent.

Reformed

The plague reaches “man and animal, throughout all the land.” Reformed readers see the comprehensive reach of God’s judgment on a nation that enslaved his people.

Both traditions note the reversal: Egypt afflicted Israel’s bodies with hard labor; now Egypt’s bodies are afflicted.

Exodus 9:10

10They took ashes of the furnace, and stood before Pharaoh; and Moses sprinkled it up toward the sky; and it became boils and blisters breaking out on man and on animal.

Arminian

Moses and Aaron obey exactly, and the word comes true exactly. Their faithfulness models simple obedience in front of a hostile king.

The text keeps the focus on God’s word and its fulfillment, not on technique.

Reformed

Reformed readers highlight that the human agents act, but the result is God’s. Moses sprinkles; the LORD sends the boils.

Shared ground: God commands, his servants obey, and the result follows his word.

Exodus 9:11

11The magicians couldn’t stand before Moses because of the boils; for the boils were on the magicians and on all the Egyptians.

Arminian

The magicians, who imitated the first two plagues and confessed “the finger of God” at the third (8:19), now cannot even stand before Moses. Their own bodies are afflicted. Egypt’s religious experts are shown to be powerless.

Arminian readers note that Pharaoh had increasing testimony even from his own advisers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God humbling the powers that stood against him. Egypt’s wisdom and religion are exposed, a theme that runs through the plagues (12:12).

Both agree the magicians’ collapse removes any excuse that the plagues were tricks anyone could perform.

Exodus 9:12

12The LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh, and he didn’t listen to them, as the LORD had spoken to Moses.

Arminian

This is the first time the narrator says outright that the LORD hardened Pharaoh’s heart. It comes after five plagues in which Pharaoh hardened his own heart or his heart “was hardened.” Classical Arminians read it as judicial hardening: God confirms and gives Pharaoh over to the course he has repeatedly chosen, so that the hardening is a just response, not the starting cause of his rebellion.

The phrase “as the LORD had spoken” shows God’s foreknowledge and announced purpose (4:21; 7:3).

Reformed

Reformed readers take the statement at face value: God is the one acting here, fulfilling what he announced before the first plague (4:21; 7:3). The same narrative also says Pharaoh hardened his own heart (8:15, 32), so God’s sovereign work and Pharaoh’s willing sin are both real. God does not put evil into an innocent heart; Pharaoh sins freely, and God governs that sin for his purpose.

The verb here (chazaq, to make strong or firm) means God strengthened Pharaoh’s resolve in the direction it already ran.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm God hardened Pharaoh and that Pharaoh was fully guilty. Classical Arminians typically stress the order of the narrative: God’s hardening follows Pharaoh’s own repeated hardening as a judicial response. Reformed theology typically holds that God’s announced purpose (4:21) governs the whole sequence, with Pharaoh’s free sin and God’s sovereign decree working together rather than in sequence.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
