Passage

Exodus 9

Verses 13–35 · WEB

The seventh plague is the longest announcement yet. God explains why Pharaoh is still standing: to display his power and spread his name through all the earth. Then comes hail unlike anything in Egypt’s history, with a warning that lets God-fearing Egyptians save their servants and animals. Pharaoh confesses sin, Moses prays, the storm stops, and Pharaoh hardens his heart again.

Exodus 9:13

13The LORD said to Moses, “Rise up early in the morning, and stand before Pharaoh, and tell him, ‘This is what the LORD, the God of the Hebrews, says: “Let my people go, that they may serve me.

Arminian

Again the same summons, delivered at dawn. God does not tire of calling. The repetition presses home that Pharaoh’s problem is not ignorance of what God wants.

Arminian readers see continued, genuine opportunity: the command is still real, and obedience still possible as far as the call is concerned.

Reformed

The persistence of the command, even after v. 12, shows that God’s purposes in hardening never cancel human duty. Pharaoh remains obligated to obey.

Shared ground: the demand is clear, public and repeated.

Exodus 9:14

14For this time I will send all my plagues against your heart, against your officials, and against your people; that you may know that there is no one like me in all the earth.

Arminian

“All my plagues against your heart” suggests the coming judgments are aimed at Pharaoh’s inner resistance. The goal is knowledge: “that you may know that there is no one like me.”

Arminians emphasize that the plagues are revelatory. God is making himself known, even to an enemy.

Reformed

The purpose clause frames everything: God acts so that his uniqueness is known. Reformed theology sees God’s glory as the final end of his works, in judgment as well as mercy.

Both agree the plagues are theological, not merely punitive. They answer Pharaoh’s question in 5:2, “Who is the LORD?”

Exodus 9:15

15For now I would have stretched out my hand, and struck you and your people with pestilence, and you would have been cut off from the earth;

Arminian

God says he could already have wiped Pharaoh out with pestilence. That he has not is restraint, not weakness.

Arminian readers see mercy mixed into judgment: Pharaoh has been spared, and every day spared is time to turn.

Reformed

God’s power is not in doubt; the delay is deliberate. Reformed readers see sovereign restraint serving a larger purpose explained in the next verse.

Shared ground: Pharaoh lives only because God allows it.

Exodus 9:16

16but indeed for this cause I have made you stand: to show you my power, and that my name may be declared throughout all the earth,

Arminian

God has “made you stand,” that is, kept Pharaoh alive and in place, to show his power and spread his name in all the earth. Paul quotes this verse in Romans 9:17. Many Arminian interpreters read both Exodus and Romans 9 as speaking of God’s use of Pharaoh in redemptive history, his role in the drama of the exodus, rather than a decree about his eternal destiny. God raised up a man already hostile and used his hostility to make his name known.

The purpose is missional in scope: “throughout all the earth” (cf. Joshua 2:9–11, where Rahab has heard).

Reformed

Reformed readers take this verse, especially as Paul uses it (Romans 9:17–18), as a central text for God’s sovereign freedom: he has mercy on whom he wills and hardens whom he wills. God did not merely react to Pharaoh; he raised him up for this very purpose. Many Reformed interpreters see Paul applying the principle beyond Pharaoh’s historical role to God’s freedom in salvation, while affirming that Pharaoh was a willing sinner.

The outcome is the same in both readings: God’s name is declared through all the earth.

Key contrast Both traditions agree that God kept Pharaoh in place to display his power and spread his name, and that Paul draws on this verse in Romans 9. Many Arminian interpreters read Romans 9:17 as concerning God’s use of Pharaoh in history and Israel’s redemption, not individual eternal destiny. Reformed interpreters typically read Paul as drawing a principle about God’s sovereign freedom in mercy and hardening that bears on salvation itself.

Exodus 9:17

17because you still exalt yourself against my people, that you won’t let them go.

Arminian

“You still exalt yourself” names the root problem: pride. Pharaoh sets himself against God by holding God’s people.

Arminians stress that this is Pharaoh’s own ongoing stance, which is why judgment falls on him justly.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Pharaoh’s self-exaltation is real and culpable. God’s purpose in v. 16 runs through Pharaoh’s pride, not around it.

Shared ground: Pharaoh is judged for what he does and wants.

Exodus 9:18

18Behold, tomorrow about this time I will cause it to rain a very grievous hail, such as has not been in Egypt since the day it was founded even until now.

Arminian

A hail “such as has not been in Egypt since the day it was founded.” The timing is announced, again giving a day to respond.

The announcement itself is an act of grace, giving warning before the blow.

Reformed

God controls weather as he controls disease and insects. Reformed readers see creation itself enlisted in God’s cause.

Both stress the unprecedented scale, marking this as God’s work.

Exodus 9:19

19Now therefore command that all of your livestock and all that you have in the field be brought into shelter. The hail will come down on every man and animal that is found in the field, and isn’t brought home, and they will die.” ’ ”

Arminian

Remarkably, God tells Egypt how to save its servants and animals. Even in judgment he provides a way of escape for anyone who heeds his word.

Arminian readers see here a clear picture of God’s desire that people respond and live, extended even to Egyptians.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see mercy here: God’s warning is a real kindness, and it divides Egypt by its response to his word.

Shared ground: the warning is sincere, and heeding it saves lives.

Exodus 9:20

20Those who feared the LORD’s word among the servants of Pharaoh made their servants and their livestock flee into the houses.

Arminian

Some of Pharaoh’s own officials “feared the LORD’s word” and acted on it. Faith and fear of God appear among Egyptians, not only Israelites (cf. the “mixed multitude” in 12:38).

Arminians often point to this as evidence that God’s word calls for and receives real responses across national lines.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God at work drawing some Egyptians to fear him, a foretaste of his purpose to bless the nations (Genesis 12:3).

Both agree that the dividing line in this verse is response to God’s word, not ethnicity.

Exodus 9:21

21Whoever didn’t respect the LORD’s word left his servants and his livestock in the field.

Arminian

Others ignored the word and lost their servants and livestock. The same warning, heard by all, brought opposite outcomes.

Arminian readers stress personal responsibility: each official chose how to respond.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree the disregard is culpable and add that the difference between those who feared and those who did not ultimately lies within God’s providence, without excusing anyone.

Shared ground: disregarding God’s word has real consequences.

Exodus 9:22

22The LORD said to Moses, “Stretch out your hand toward the sky, that there may be hail in all the land of Egypt, on man, and on animal, and on every herb of the field, throughout the land of Egypt.”

Arminian

God commands Moses to stretch out his hand; the scope is all Egypt: people, animals and crops.

The pattern of command and obedience continues to mark Moses as God’s faithful servant.

Reformed

God works through a mediator. Reformed readers often see Moses as a type of the greater mediator to come (Deuteronomy 18:15).

Both agree the power is God’s, the hand is Moses’.

Exodus 9:23

23Moses stretched out his rod toward the heavens, and the LORD sent thunder and hail; and lightning flashed down to the earth. The LORD rained hail on the land of Egypt.

Arminian

Thunder, hail and lightning: the storm is described in the language of God’s presence and power (cf. 19:16).

The narrative attributes the hail directly to the LORD, not to natural forces alone.

Reformed

“The LORD rained hail.” Reformed theology sees here God’s direct governance of nature, with ordinary forces always under his hand.

Shared ground: God, not chance or Egypt’s gods, controls the sky.

Exodus 9:24

24So there was very severe hail, and lightning mixed with the hail, such as had not been in all the land of Egypt since it became a nation.

Arminian

Fire mingled with hail heightens the terror. The repeated claim of no precedent “since it became a nation” stresses the uniqueness of the event.

The scale leaves no room for Pharaoh to dismiss it.

Reformed

The severity matches the gravity of Pharaoh’s defiance. Reformed readers see judgment as proportionate and purposeful.

Both note the storm’s unprecedented character as a sign of God’s hand.

Exodus 9:25

25The hail struck throughout all the land of Egypt all that was in the field, both man and animal; and the hail struck every herb of the field, and broke every tree of the field.

Arminian

The destruction falls on all that remained in the field, as the warning in v. 19 said it would. Those who heeded were spared; those who did not lost what was in the open.

Arminians stress the fairness of a judgment announced in advance with a way of escape.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the precision of God’s word: what he threatened, he did.

Shared ground: the outcome tracks the warning exactly.

Exodus 9:26

26Only in the land of Goshen, where the children of Israel were, there was no hail.

Arminian

Goshen is spared again. God’s distinction continues, protecting the people he has pledged to deliver.

Arminian readers see covenant faithfulness to Israel as a people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s preserving care for his people in the midst of judgment, a recurring biblical pattern.

Both note how the distinction keeps testifying who the LORD is.

Exodus 9:27

27Pharaoh sent and called for Moses and Aaron, and said to them, “I have sinned this time. The LORD is righteous, and I and my people are wicked.

Arminian

Pharaoh’s confession sounds right: “I have sinned… The LORD is righteous.” Yet v. 30 and v. 34 show it did not go deep. Arminian readers see a man who acknowledges the truth under pressure but does not submit his will.

The words “this time” may hint that Pharaoh treats sin as an episode rather than a settled rebellion.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish confession extracted by distress from genuine repentance (cf. 2 Corinthians 7:10). Pharaoh admits facts but does not turn.

Both agree his words are a lesson in how confession without real change falls short.

Exodus 9:28

28Pray to the LORD; for there has been enough of mighty thunderings and hail. I will let you go, and you shall stay no longer.”

Arminian

Pharaoh asks for prayer and promises release. The request shows he knows where the power lies.

Arminians note that Moses still treats the promise seriously enough to pray, keeping the door open.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Pharaoh bargaining for relief rather than seeking God. His promise depends on the pressure being on.

Shared ground: Pharaoh knows the LORD can stop the storm.

Exodus 9:29

29Moses said to him, “As soon as I have gone out of the city, I will spread out my hands to the LORD. The thunders shall cease, and there will not be any more hail; that you may know that the earth is the LORD’s.

Arminian

Moses will pray, and the storm will stop “that you may know that the earth is the LORD’s.” Even relief is a lesson about God’s ownership of creation.

Arminians see God’s willingness to grant relief as further patience toward Pharaoh.

Reformed

The phrase “the earth is the LORD’s” (cf. Psalm 24:1) states the theological point of the plagues. Reformed readers see God’s sovereign ownership displayed in both judgment and relief.

Both agree the purpose is knowledge of God.

Exodus 9:30

30But as for you and your servants, I know that you don’t yet fear the LORD God.”

Arminian

Moses knows Pharaoh and his servants do not yet fear the LORD. Prayer is offered without illusion. Arminians read this as Moses’ honest assessment of where Pharaoh stands, not a closing of the door.

The word “yet” may suggest the matter is still open.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses speaking with insight into Pharaoh’s heart, consistent with what God has told him (4:21).

Shared ground: Moses prays for relief even for an unrepentant ruler, an example of persistent intercession.

Exodus 9:31

31The flax and the barley were struck, for the barley had ripened and the flax was blooming.

Arminian

The flax and barley are destroyed because they were already mature. The detail anchors the account in Egypt’s agricultural seasons.

The loss strikes Egypt’s economy hard.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the narrator records concrete agricultural detail, presenting the account as real history in a real land.

Both agree the detail sets up v. 32.

Exodus 9:32

32But the wheat and the spelt were not struck, for they had not grown up.

Arminian

Wheat and spelt survive because they had not yet come up. Something remains, which the locusts will later consume (10:5, 15).

Arminians see the spared crops as leaving Pharaoh something to lose, another reason to relent.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God measuring his judgments. Something is left, and the story is not over.

Shared ground: the partial preservation sets up the next plague.

Exodus 9:33

33Moses went out of the city from Pharaoh, and spread out his hands to the LORD; and the thunders and hail ceased, and the rain was not poured on the earth.

Arminian

Moses keeps his word and prays outside the city, and the storm stops at once. God answers prayer, even prayer on behalf of an enemy.

Arminian readers see the power of intercession and God’s readiness to relent from judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God ordaining prayer as a means through which he acts, here demonstrating his authority over the storm.

Both agree that the storm’s instant end confirms God’s control.

Exodus 9:34

34When Pharaoh saw that the rain and the hail and the thunders had ceased, he sinned yet more, and hardened his heart, he and his servants.

Arminian

As soon as relief comes, Pharaoh “sinned yet more, and hardened his heart, he and his servants.” The narrator calls it sin and names Pharaoh as the one doing the hardening (Hebrew kbd, made heavy). His officials join him.

Arminians see the classic pattern of a person who turns back to God under pressure and away from him as soon as it lifts.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm without hesitation that this is Pharaoh’s own sin. The same narrative that says God hardens Pharaoh says Pharaoh hardens himself, and God’s purpose never makes the sinner less guilty.

Shared ground: relief without repentance can harden rather than soften.

Exodus 9:35

35The heart of Pharaoh was hardened, and he didn’t let the children of Israel go, just as the LORD had spoken through Moses.

Arminian

“The heart of Pharaoh was hardened… just as the LORD had spoken.” The passive voice leaves the agent open, and the stress falls on the outcome God foretold.

Arminian readers see God’s foreknowledge confirmed: he knew how Pharaoh would respond (cf. 3:19).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s announced purpose (4:21; 7:3) coming to pass through Pharaoh’s own choices, as v. 34 shows.

Both affirm that God’s word stands and Pharaoh is responsible.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
