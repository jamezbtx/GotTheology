Passage

Exodus 9

Verses 1–7 · WEB

The fifth plague strikes Egypt’s livestock in the field with pestilence. The LORD sets a time, draws a line between Israel’s animals and Egypt’s, and keeps his word to the day, yet Pharaoh’s heart stays heavy even after he checks and finds Israel spared.

Exodus 9:1

1Then the LORD said to Moses, “Go in to Pharaoh, and tell him, ‘This is what the LORD, the God of the Hebrews, says: “Let my people go, that they may serve me.

Arminian

The demand has not changed since 5:1: release Israel so they may serve the LORD. The repetition shows patient persistence. God keeps putting the same simple call before Pharaoh, and each time it is a real summons he could obey.

Arminian readers see here the long-suffering of God toward a resisting king, of a piece with God’s patience with sinners more broadly (cf. Romans 2:4).

Reformed

The repeated formula “the LORD, the God of the Hebrews” marks God’s covenant claim on a people Pharaoh treats as his property. Israel was redeemed for worship, not merely set free.

Reformed readers note that the command reveals Pharaoh’s duty whether or not God has purposed to grant compliance. The call is real even where the heart will refuse it.

Exodus 9:2

2For if you refuse to let them go, and hold them still,

Arminian

The conditional “if you refuse” addresses Pharaoh as a responsible agent. The warning only makes sense if his choice matters to what follows.

Both traditions agree that God’s judgments in Exodus are announced beforehand and answer Pharaoh’s refusal. The plagues are not random cruelty.

Reformed

Reformed theology affirms with equal force that Pharaoh’s refusal is his own. God’s decree does not make the warning insincere; it is the means by which Pharaoh’s guilt is exposed and his accountability made plain.

Shared ground is wide here: a clear command, a clear warning, and a real refusal.

Exodus 9:3

3behold, the LORD’s hand is on your livestock which are in the field, on the horses, on the donkeys, on the camels, on the herds, and on the flocks with a very grievous pestilence.

Arminian

“The LORD’s hand” is the language of personal action. Egypt’s wealth and transport, everything from horses to flocks, is shown to be at God’s disposal, not Pharaoh’s.

Arminians often stress that judgment is escalating step by step, leaving room at each stage for Pharaoh to turn before worse comes.

Reformed

The scope of the list underlines God’s lordship over creation and over the economy Pharaoh thought he controlled. The plagues dismantle Egypt’s confidence piece by piece.

Reformed readers see providence at work: disease in animals is not chance but falls within God’s governing hand.

Exodus 9:4

4The LORD will make a distinction between the livestock of Israel and the livestock of Egypt; and nothing shall die of all that belongs to the children of Israel.” ’ ”

Arminian

The distinction between Israel’s livestock and Egypt’s (as with the flies in 8:22–23) makes the source of the plague unmistakable. It is aimed, not accidental.

Arminian readers note that the distinction is covenantal and historical, the LORD keeping faith with the people he promised to bring out (3:7–8).

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the distinction a picture of God’s discriminating grace: he knows his people and preserves them in the midst of judgment.

Both traditions agree that the distinction is a sign meant to be seen. It teaches Pharaoh, Egypt and Israel who is the LORD.

Exodus 9:5

5The LORD appointed a set time, saying, “Tomorrow the LORD shall do this thing in the land.”

Arminian

Setting a time removes any doubt about coincidence. Pharaoh is given a day of warning, which also gives him and his people a day to respond.

God’s precision here supports the Arminian emphasis that God’s dealings are open, announced and fair.

Reformed

A fixed appointment displays God’s sovereign control of timing as well as outcome. What he says “tomorrow,” he does tomorrow.

Shared ground: God’s word is reliable, and the plague is a public demonstration of it.

Exodus 9:6

6The LORD did that thing on the next day; and all the livestock of Egypt died, but of the livestock of the children of Israel, not one died.

Arminian

“All the livestock of Egypt died” should be read with v. 3, which limits the plague to livestock “in the field.” Later plagues still mention Egyptian animals (9:19–25), so the narrator’s “all” is best read in that sense, or as sweeping summary language.

The fulfillment to the letter shows God doing exactly what he said.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read “all” in light of the stated scope in v. 3, a common feature of Hebrew narrative. The point is total fulfillment of the word, with Israel untouched.

Both traditions stress the contrast: Egypt’s loss, Israel’s preservation, and not one exception.

Exodus 9:7

7Pharaoh sent, and, behold, there was not so much as one of the livestock of the Israelites dead. But the heart of Pharaoh was stubborn, and he didn’t let the people go.

Arminian

Pharaoh investigates and finds the distinction exactly as announced, yet his heart is “stubborn” (Hebrew: heavy, from the root kbd). The text does not here say the LORD did it. The evidence is in front of him and he still refuses.

Arminian readers find in this sequence a heart setting itself, with Pharaoh accountable for resisting what he plainly saw.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the narrator reports the heart’s condition without naming its cause in this verse, while 4:21 and 7:3 have already announced that the LORD will harden Pharaoh. Divine purpose and Pharaoh’s own stubbornness run together.

Both agree that unbelief here is not for lack of evidence. Pharaoh saw, and still would not yield.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
