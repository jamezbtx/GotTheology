Passage

Exodus 30

Verses 11–16 · WEB

When Israel is counted, each man twenty years old and upward is to give half a shekel as a ransom for his life, so that no plague comes on them. Rich and poor give the same amount, and the money supports the service of the Tent of Meeting as a memorial before the LORD. Both traditions read the passage as teaching that every Israelite stands before God in equal need of atonement, and that belonging to God’s people is not a matter of wealth. Why a census carried this danger is not fully explained here, and the notes treat later links, such as David’s census in 2 Samuel 24, cautiously.

Exodus 30:11

11The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses again. A new section begins with instructions for the census payment.

Arminian readers note that God addresses practical matters. His concern reaches into the organization of his people’s life.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the instructions come by God’s word. Even administrative matters are governed by divine command.

Both traditions see this as part of the larger tabernacle instructions.

Exodus 30:12

12“When you take a census of the children of Israel, according to those who are counted among them, then each man shall give a ransom for his soul to the LORD when you count them, that there be no plague among them when you count them.

Arminian

When Israel is counted, each man must give a ransom for his life so that no plague comes on them. The text links census and danger without fully explaining why.

Arminian readers note the seriousness of being counted among God’s people. Each person stands before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the ransom is “for his soul.” Every Israelite needs atonement before God.

Both traditions note that some interpreters connect this verse with the plague after David’s census (2 Samuel 24), though the link is not stated and the reasons for the danger are debated.

Exodus 30:13

13They shall give this, everyone who passes over to those who are counted, half a shekel according to the shekel of the sanctuary (the shekel is twenty gerahs); half a shekel for an offering to the LORD.

Arminian

Each one counted gives half a shekel by the sanctuary shekel, which is twenty gerahs. WEB footnotes give approximate modern weights, which vary among estimates.

Arminian readers note that the payment is fixed and fair. Everyone gives the same amount.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the sanctuary standard is God’s measure, not the market’s. The offering is set by divine command.

Both traditions recognize that the weight of the ancient shekel is not precisely known.

Exodus 30:14

14Everyone who passes over to those who are counted, from twenty years old and upward, shall give the offering to the LORD.

Arminian

Everyone counted from twenty years old and upward gives the offering. Age twenty marks those counted for service.

Arminian readers note that adult men are each responsible to give. The obligation is personal.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the age matches the later census in Numbers 1:3. The offering is tied to being counted among Israel’s men.

Both traditions read the age limit as part of the census structure.

Exodus 30:15

15The rich shall not give more, and the poor shall not give less, than the half shekel, when they give the offering of the LORD, to make atonement for your souls.

Arminian

The rich may not give more and the poor may not give less than the half shekel. The offering makes atonement for their souls.

Arminian readers see equal value before God. No one can buy a better standing, and no one is too poor to belong.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that atonement is not proportional to wealth. All stand in equal need before God.

Both traditions draw pastoral application: God shows no partiality, and 1 Peter 1:18–19 speaks of redemption not with silver or gold but with Christ’s precious blood.

Exodus 30:16

16You shall take the atonement money from the children of Israel, and shall appoint it for the service of the Tent of Meeting; that it may be a memorial for the children of Israel before the LORD, to make atonement for your souls.”

Arminian

The atonement money is used for the service of the Tent of Meeting, as a memorial before the LORD. Exodus 38:25–28 reports silver from the census used in the tabernacle’s construction.

Arminian readers note that the offering supports worship. What is given to God serves the whole people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the money is a memorial before the LORD. It reminds both God and Israel of the ransom.

Both traditions see the support of worship as a fitting use of the offering; the later temple tax (compare Matthew 17:24–27) is often associated with this practice.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
