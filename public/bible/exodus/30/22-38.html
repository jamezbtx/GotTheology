Passage

Exodus 30

Verses 22–38 · WEB

The LORD gives the recipe for a holy anointing oil, made from spices and olive oil, to anoint the tent, its furnishings, and the priests. It must not be copied or used on ordinary people, and anyone who misuses it will be cut off. A unique holy incense is likewise prescribed and restricted to the LORD’s use. Both traditions see here the distinction between holy and common, and the seriousness of treating what God has set apart as if it were ordinary. Several of the ingredients cannot be identified with certainty, and the notes hedge them accordingly.

Exodus 30:22

22Moreover the LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

The LORD speaks to Moses again. The instructions turn to the anointing oil.

Arminian readers note that God prescribes even the materials of worship. His instructions are thorough.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the repeated introduction of God’s speech. The anointing oil is commanded, not invented.

Both traditions read this as part of the ordered preparation for worship.

Exodus 30:23

23“Also take fine spices: of liquid myrrh, five hundred shekels; and of fragrant cinnamon half as much, even two hundred and fifty; and of fragrant cane, two hundred and fifty;

Arminian

Fine spices are taken: liquid myrrh, fragrant cinnamon, and fragrant cane in specified weights. The exact botanical identity of some ingredients is uncertain.

Arminian readers note the richness of the ingredients. What is used in God’s service is costly.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the careful proportions. The oil is made according to God’s recipe.

Both traditions acknowledge that ancient names such as “fragrant cane” cannot always be matched with confidence to modern plants.

Exodus 30:24

24and of cassia five hundred, according to the shekel of the sanctuary; and a hin of olive oil.

Arminian

Cassia and a hin of olive oil complete the recipe, by the sanctuary shekel. WEB footnotes give approximate modern equivalents for the hin and the shekel.

Arminian readers note the generous quantities. The oil is prepared for lasting use.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the sanctuary shekel is again the standard. God’s measure governs the work.

Both traditions recognize that the identity of cassia and the exact measures are not certain.

Exodus 30:25

25You shall make it into a holy anointing oil, a perfume compounded after the art of the perfumer: it shall be a holy anointing oil.

Arminian

The ingredients are made into a holy anointing oil, compounded by the art of the perfumer. Skilled work serves holy purposes.

Arminian readers note that craftsmanship is honored in God’s service. Skill can be offered to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the oil is holy because God sets it apart. Its holiness lies in its purpose, not only in its ingredients.

Both traditions affirm the place of skilled human work in worship.

Exodus 30:26

26You shall use it to anoint the Tent of Meeting, the ark of the covenant,

Arminian

The oil is used to anoint the Tent of Meeting and the ark of the covenant. The dwelling and its central object are set apart.

Arminian readers note that the whole tabernacle is consecrated. God’s dwelling is set apart for him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that anointing marks objects as belonging to God. The tent and ark are not common.

Both traditions read the anointing as consecration for holy use.

Exodus 30:27

27the table and all its articles, the lamp stand and its accessories, the altar of incense,

Arminian

The table and its articles, the lampstand and its accessories, and the incense altar are anointed. Every furnishing is included.

Arminian readers note that nothing in God’s service is left unconsecrated. Each item matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the thoroughness of the anointing. The whole sanctuary, from table to lampstand to incense altar, belongs to God.

Both traditions avoid treating any furnishing as less important.

Exodus 30:28

28the altar of burnt offering with all its utensils, and the basin with its base.

Arminian

The altar of burnt offering with its utensils and the basin with its base are anointed. The outer furnishings are consecrated too.

Arminian readers note that the court furnishings are included. Holiness extends to every part of worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the anointing reaches from the inner tent to the outer court. God’s claim covers the whole.

Both traditions read the list as complete.

Exodus 30:29

29You shall sanctify them, that they may be most holy. Whatever touches them shall be holy.

Arminian

The objects are sanctified so that they are most holy. Whatever touches them becomes holy.

Arminian readers note the intensity of holiness. Contact with these objects is serious.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the phrase repeats 29:37 and is debated: it may mean that what touches them becomes holy, or that only what is holy may touch them.

Both traditions hold this clause with caution.

Exodus 30:30

30You shall anoint Aaron and his sons, and sanctify them, that they may minister to me in the priest’s office.

Arminian

Aaron and his sons are anointed and sanctified to serve as priests. The priests share the anointing of the sanctuary.

Arminian readers note that the priests are consecrated with the same oil as the furnishings. They belong wholly to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the anointing of priests points forward to the Anointed One, Christ. The priesthood finds its fulfillment in him.

Both traditions see the anointing as God’s appointment to service.

Exodus 30:31

31You shall speak to the children of Israel, saying, ‘This shall be a holy anointing oil to me throughout your generations.

Arminian

Israel is told that this will be a holy anointing oil to the LORD throughout their generations. The oil is permanent in its holiness.

Arminian readers note that the people are taught to respect the oil. Holiness is a shared responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the oil’s holiness is declared by God and lasts across generations.

Both traditions read the instruction as protecting what God has set apart.

Exodus 30:32

32It shall not be poured on man’s flesh, and do not make any like it, according to its composition. It is holy. It shall be holy to you.

Arminian

The oil must not be poured on ordinary people or copied. It is holy.

Arminian readers note that holy things are not for private use. Their purpose must be respected.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the sharp line between holy and common. What God sets apart must not be treated as ordinary.

Both traditions draw pastoral application about reverence for what belongs to God.

Exodus 30:33

33Whoever compounds any like it, or whoever puts any of it on a stranger, he shall be cut off from his people.’ ”

Arminian

Anyone who makes it or puts it on a stranger will be cut off from his people. The penalty is severe.

Arminian readers note the seriousness of misusing holy things. God’s warnings are real.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that “cut off” is a grave sanction, though its exact meaning is debated among interpreters.

Both traditions agree that the penalty shows how seriously God treats his holiness.

Exodus 30:34

34The LORD said to Moses, “Take to yourself sweet spices, gum resin, onycha, and galbanum: sweet spices with pure frankincense. There shall be an equal weight of each.

Arminian

The incense is made from sweet spices, gum resin, onycha, galbanum, and pure frankincense in equal weight. Several ingredients are hard to identify with certainty.

Arminian readers note the balance of the ingredients. God’s design is precise.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that onycha and gum resin are especially uncertain. Interpreters offer different identifications, both animal and plant.

Both traditions avoid claiming certainty about the ingredients.

Exodus 30:35

35You shall make incense of it, a perfume after the art of the perfumer, seasoned with salt, pure and holy.

Arminian

The incense is a perfume made by the perfumer’s art, seasoned with salt, pure and holy.

Arminian readers note that salt, often associated with covenant (compare Leviticus 2:13), is included. Purity is emphasized.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the incense is holy because God commands it. Its purity matters.

Both traditions hold the meaning of salt here cautiously.

Exodus 30:36

36You shall beat some of it very small, and put some of it before the covenant in the Tent of Meeting, where I will meet with you. It shall be to you most holy.

Arminian

Some of it is beaten small and placed before the covenant in the Tent, where God meets with Moses. It is most holy.

Arminian readers note that the incense is placed near God’s presence. It is part of meeting with him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the phrase “where I will meet with you.” The incense belongs to God’s chosen place of encounter.

Both traditions see the incense as part of approach to God.

Exodus 30:37

37You shall not make this incense, according to its composition, for yourselves: it shall be to you holy for the LORD.

Arminian

The incense must not be made for personal use. It is holy to the LORD.

Arminian readers note that holy things must not be used for private enjoyment. They belong to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the sharp distinction between holy and common. God claims this incense for himself alone.

Both traditions draw pastoral application about reverence for what God sets apart.

Exodus 30:38

38Whoever shall make any like that, to smell of it, he shall be cut off from his people.”

Arminian

Anyone who makes it to enjoy its smell will be cut off. The warning is severe.

Arminian readers note the seriousness of turning holy things to personal pleasure. God’s holiness is not to be trivialized.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the parallel with the anointing oil. Both are protected by the same severe sanction.

Both traditions see the warning as protecting the holiness of worship.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
