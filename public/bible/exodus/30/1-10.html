Passage

Exodus 30

Verses 1–10 · WEB

The LORD gives instructions for a small gold-covered altar for burning incense, placed before the veil near the ark. Aaron is to burn incense on it morning and evening, no unauthorized incense or other offerings may be placed on it, and once a year its horns receive the blood of the sin offering of atonement. Both traditions see here the holiness of approach to God and the need for atonement even for the instruments of worship. Many Christian readers in both traditions connect incense with prayer (Psalm 141:2; Revelation 5:8; 8:3–4), though the passage itself does not draw that link.

Exodus 30:1

1“You shall make an altar to burn incense on. You shall make it of acacia wood.

Arminian

An altar for burning incense is to be made of acacia wood. It is different from the bronze altar of burnt offering.

Arminian readers note that the incense altar has its own place in worship. Approaching God involves more than sacrifice alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this altar stands inside the tent, nearer to the presence of God. Its location gives it particular significance.

Both traditions read the incense altar as part of the ordered access to God that the tabernacle provides.

Exodus 30:2

2Its length shall be a cubit, and its width a cubit. It shall be square, and its height shall be two cubits. Its horns shall be of one piece with it.

Arminian

The altar is a cubit square and two cubits high, with horns of one piece with it. A WEB footnote gives a cubit as about 18 inches, an approximate value.

Arminian readers note the precise dimensions. God’s design is detailed and deliberate.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the horns, which will receive atoning blood once a year (30:10). Their form is part of the altar’s purpose.

Both traditions avoid assigning symbolic meaning to the dimensions themselves.

Exodus 30:3

3You shall overlay it with pure gold, its top, its sides around it, and its horns; and you shall make a gold molding around it.

Arminian

The altar is overlaid with pure gold, including its top, sides, and horns, with a gold molding around it. Gold marks it for use near God’s presence.

Arminian readers note the beauty of the altar. Worship near God’s presence is marked with honor.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the use of gold places this altar with the furnishings inside the holy place. Its materials match its location.

Both traditions see the craftsmanship as expressing reverence rather than as an end in itself.

Exodus 30:4

4You shall make two golden rings for it under its molding; on its two ribs, on its two sides you shall make them; and they shall be for places for poles with which to bear it.

Arminian

Two golden rings are placed under the molding for the carrying poles. The altar is portable.

Arminian readers note that the altar travels with Israel. God’s presence goes with his people on their journey.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the portability fits Israel’s wilderness life. God’s dwelling moves with his people.

Both traditions draw comfort from the picture of God accompanying his people.

Exodus 30:5

5You shall make the poles of acacia wood, and overlay them with gold.

Arminian

The poles are made of acacia wood and overlaid with gold. They match the altar itself.

Arminian readers note the consistency of materials. Even the poles are made with care.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that holy objects are carried by poles so that they are not handled directly. Proper handling of holy things matters (compare 2 Samuel 6:6–7).

Both traditions read this as part of the tabernacle’s careful design.

Exodus 30:6

6You shall put it before the veil that is by the ark of the covenant, before the mercy seat that is over the covenant, where I will meet with you.

Arminian

The altar is placed before the veil near the ark and the mercy seat, where God meets with Moses. It stands close to God’s throne.

Arminian readers note that the incense altar is near God’s presence. Worship draws near to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the altar stands outside the veil. Access to God’s presence is real but still restricted, until it is opened through Christ (Hebrews 10:19–20).

Both traditions see the veil as marking both nearness and distance.

Exodus 30:7

7Aaron shall burn incense of sweet spices on it every morning. When he tends the lamps, he shall burn it.

Arminian

Aaron burns sweet-smelling incense on it every morning when he tends the lamps. Incense and light go together.

Arminian readers note the daily rhythm of worship. Aaron’s duty is regular and faithful.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the incense accompanies the care of the lamps. Worship is continual, not occasional.

Both traditions see in the daily incense an expression of ongoing devotion before God.

Exodus 30:8

8When Aaron lights the lamps at evening, he shall burn it, a perpetual incense before the LORD throughout your generations.

Arminian

When Aaron lights the lamps at evening, he burns incense again, a perpetual incense before the LORD.

Arminian readers note that incense rises morning and evening. Many Christians connect this with prayer (Psalm 141:2), though the text itself does not.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that “perpetual” emphasizes constant worship before God. Revelation 5:8 and 8:3–4 connect incense with the prayers of the saints.

Both traditions hold the connection with prayer as a fitting application rather than the passage’s stated meaning.

Exodus 30:9

9You shall offer no strange incense on it, nor burnt offering, nor meal offering; and you shall pour no drink offering on it.

Arminian

No strange incense, burnt offering, meal offering, or drink offering may be offered on it. The altar has one purpose.

Arminian readers note that worship must follow God’s instructions. Unauthorized worship is dangerous (compare Leviticus 10:1–2).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this verse is often cited in discussions of worship regulated by God’s word. They hold that principle as an inference drawn from many texts, including this one.

Both traditions agree that God determines how he is to be worshiped.

Exodus 30:10

10Aaron shall make atonement on its horns once in the year; with the blood of the sin offering of atonement once in the year he shall make atonement for it throughout your generations. It is most holy to the LORD.”

Arminian

Once a year Aaron makes atonement on its horns with the blood of the sin offering. It is most holy to the LORD.

Arminian readers note that even the incense altar requires atonement. The annual rite recalls the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Hebrews 9:7 describes the high priest entering once a year, not without blood. The annual repetition points to Christ’s once-for-all offering.

Both traditions agree that the yearly atonement anticipates the sufficient sacrifice of Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
