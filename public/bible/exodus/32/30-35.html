Passage

Exodus 32

Verses 30–35 · WEB

Moses returns to the LORD to seek atonement, confessing the people’s great sin and offering to be blotted out of God’s book in their place. God refuses a substitute, saying the guilty will be blotted out, but sends Moses on with the promise of his angel while reserving punishment. Moses’ self-giving intercession is widely read as foreshadowing Christ, while interpretations of the book differ.

Exodus 32:30

30On the next day, Moses said to the people, “You have sinned a great sin. Now I will go up to the LORD. Perhaps I shall make atonement for your sin.”

Arminian

The next day Moses tells the people they have sinned a great sin and says he will go up to the LORD; perhaps he can make atonement. His “perhaps” shows humility.

Arminian readers see a mediator who does not presume on God’s answer. Intercession is a real request, not a formality.

Reformed

Moses names the sin truthfully and seeks atonement. He goes up uncertain of the outcome.

Reformed readers see Moses acting as mediator between a holy God and a guilty people. Both traditions see this as pointing toward Christ’s perfect mediation.

Exodus 32:31

31Moses returned to the LORD, and said, “Oh, this people have sinned a great sin, and have made themselves gods of gold.

Arminian

Moses confesses to the LORD that the people have sinned greatly and made gods of gold. He does not minimise the sin.

Arminian readers see honest confession as the starting point of intercession. Mercy is sought without excuses.

Reformed

Moses’ confession is full and direct. True intercession begins with agreeing with God about sin.

Reformed readers see a model of confession. Both traditions agree that Moses stands with the people while naming their guilt.

Exodus 32:32

32Yet now, if you will, forgive their sin—and if not, please blot me out of your book which you have written.”

Arminian

Moses asks God to forgive the people—or else to blot him out of God’s book. He offers himself in their place.

Arminian readers see self-giving love that foreshadows Christ. Many take the book as a register of the living or of God’s people, and do not see Moses offering to lose salvation in a calculated sense.

Reformed

Moses’ offer is an extraordinary act of identification with the people. It anticipates the true Mediator who gave himself.

Reformed readers likewise see Christ foreshadowed. Interpreters across both traditions differ over what “your book” means: a register of life, of the covenant people, or of those destined for eternal life.

Exodus 32:33

33The LORD said to Moses, “Whoever has sinned against me, I will blot him out of my book.

Arminian

God answers that whoever has sinned against him, he will blot out of his book. Judgment falls on the guilty, not on a substitute Moses.

Classical Arminian readers often see here support for the possibility that names can be removed from God’s book through persistent sin, linking it with warnings elsewhere in Scripture. Some Arminians take the book as physical life rather than final salvation, so views vary.

Reformed

God refuses Moses’ offer and affirms that each sinner is accountable. Both traditions share that point.

Reformed readers often understand the book here as the register of the living or of the covenant people, so being blotted out means death or removal from the community rather than loss of eternal election. Others distinguish this book from the Lamb’s book of life in Revelation; views differ within the tradition.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm that God holds the guilty personally accountable and that Moses cannot simply take their place. Arminian readers often see the verse supporting the possibility of being blotted out of God’s book through persistent sin, though some take the book as earthly life; Reformed readers often read the book as a register of life or of the covenant people, distinct from God’s eternal election. There is diversity within both traditions on what the book represents.

Exodus 32:34

34Now go, lead the people to the place of which I have spoken to you. Behold, my angel shall go before you. Nevertheless, in the day when I punish, I will punish them for their sin.”

Arminian

God sends Moses to lead the people, promising his angel will go before them, but he will still punish their sin in his time. Mercy and judgment both remain.

Arminian readers see God continuing the journey with his people while holding them accountable.

Reformed

God keeps his promise to bring Israel to the land, while reserving judgment. His faithfulness and justice are held together.

Reformed readers see God’s covenant purpose continuing despite sin. Both traditions note that the full restoration of his presence is still to be resolved in the next chapters.

Exodus 32:35

35The LORD struck the people, because of what they did with the calf, which Aaron made.

Arminian

The LORD struck the people because of what they did with the calf Aaron made. The chapter ends with judgment.

Arminian readers see the consequence of sin following, even after intercession. Forgiveness of the nation does not erase every consequence.

Reformed

The narrator assigns responsibility both to the people and to Aaron. God’s judgment is just.

Reformed readers see God’s holiness upheld. Both traditions read the verse as a sober close to the episode.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
