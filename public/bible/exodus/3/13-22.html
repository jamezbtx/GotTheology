Passage

Exodus 3

Verses 13–22 · WEB

Moses’ second objection asks for God’s name, and God answers with “I AM WHO I AM” and the covenant name rendered “the LORD” in the WEB, tying it to the patriarchs as his name forever. God then outlines the mission in advance: gather the elders, go to Pharaoh, request a three days’ journey to sacrifice. He forewarns that the king will refuse until God strikes Egypt with wonders, and promises that Israel will leave with the plunder of Egypt.

Exodus 3:13

13Moses said to God, “Behold, when I come to the children of Israel, and tell them, ‘The God of your fathers has sent me to you,’ and they ask me, ‘What is his name?’ what should I tell them?”

Arminian

Moses anticipates Israel’s skepticism and asks for God’s name. Arminian readers note that he asks how to make the message credible to people who must decide whether to believe it. Both traditions read the question as seeking the character and authority of the God who sends him, not merely a label.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the question as a request for God’s self-revelation: Israel can know God only as he names himself. Moses is concerned with credibility before the elders. Both traditions agree that in the ancient context a name expresses identity and character, so the request is weighty.

Exodus 3:14

14God said to Moses, “I AM WHO I AM,” and he said, “You shall tell the children of Israel this: ‘I AM has sent me to you.’ ”

Arminian

“I AM WHO I AM” is notoriously difficult; the Hebrew can also be understood as “I will be what I will be.” Arminian readers often stress God’s faithful, active presence with his people in history. Both traditions agree the name reveals a God who is self-existent and cannot be controlled or defined by humans.

Reformed

Reformed readers have traditionally heard in the name God’s self-existence and independence: he depends on nothing outside himself. Many also hear the promise of covenant presence from 3:12. Both traditions hold both emphases together, and neither claims the phrase can be fully exhausted; the difference is one of emphasis, not substance.

Exodus 3:15

15God said moreover to Moses, “You shall tell the children of Israel this, ‘The LORD, the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you.’ This is my name forever, and this is my memorial to all generations.

Arminian

God links the name rendered “the LORD” to the patriarchs and calls it his memorial for all generations. The WEB footnote notes that LORD in capitals represents the Hebrew name usually pronounced Yahweh. Arminian readers stress the relational, covenant quality of the name. Both traditions treat it as God’s personal, enduring name.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the covenant name is bound to God’s promises: the God who is is also the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The name is a memorial to be invoked in worship forever. Shared ground: God’s name expresses unchanging faithfulness, as the WEB footnote on the divine name indicates.

Exodus 3:16

16Go and gather the elders of Israel together, and tell them, ‘The LORD, the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, of Isaac, and of Jacob, has appeared to me, saying, “I have surely visited you, and seen that which is done to you in Egypt.

Arminian

Moses must begin with Israel’s elders, respecting the community’s leadership. The message is that God has “surely visited” his people, recalling Joseph’s dying words in Genesis 50. Arminian readers see God’s faithfulness inviting a believing response from the people. Both traditions note how promise and fulfillment connect across the generations.

Reformed

Reformed readers highlight that the visitation fulfills promises God made long before, showing his faithfulness over centuries. The God who appeared to Moses is the God of the fathers. Both traditions agree the elders are the proper first audience and that God’s visitation means active intervention, not mere observation.

Exodus 3:17

17I have said, I will bring you up out of the affliction of Egypt to the land of the Canaanite, the Hittite, the Amorite, the Perizzite, the Hivite, and the Jebusite, to a land flowing with milk and honey.” ’

Arminian

God restates the promise in his own words: out of affliction and into a good land. Arminian readers note the promise is announced to the whole people, who will be called to trust it. Both traditions see the movement from affliction to inheritance as the basic shape of the exodus story.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the promise is stated as God’s settled intention: “I will bring you up.” The certainty rests on God, not on Israel’s strength. Shared ground: the land is a gift, the nations are listed as in earlier promises, and the deliverance is God’s own work.

Exodus 3:18

18They will listen to your voice. You shall come, you and the elders of Israel, to the king of Egypt, and you shall tell him, ‘The LORD, the God of the Hebrews, has met with us. Now please let us go three days’ journey into the wilderness, that we may sacrifice to the LORD, our God.’

Arminian

God predicts that the elders will listen, and the request to Pharaoh is modest: a three days’ journey to sacrifice. Arminian readers see a reasonable request that exposes Pharaoh’s unwillingness. Both traditions note that the request centers on worship, and both discuss how this limited request relates to the full departure later.

Reformed

Reformed readers note God’s foreknowledge that the elders will listen, fulfilled in 4:31. The modest request reveals Pharaoh’s heart when even this is refused. Both traditions agree that worship is central to the mission, and both acknowledge that interpreters differ on how to understand the limited scope of the request.

Exodus 3:19

19I know that the king of Egypt won’t give you permission to go, no, not by a mighty hand.

Arminian

God foreknows that Pharaoh will refuse. Arminian readers typically take this as God’s foreknowledge of what Pharaoh will freely choose; knowing it in advance does not cause it. The phrase “not by a mighty hand” is difficult and may mean Pharaoh will not yield except under compulsion. Both traditions agree Pharaoh’s refusal is real and culpable.

Reformed

Reformed readers typically ground God’s foreknowledge of Pharaoh’s refusal in God’s sovereign purpose for the plagues, though Pharaoh acts willingly and is responsible. The phrase “not by a mighty hand” is difficult, perhaps meaning he will not yield except under compulsion. Both traditions agree God is not caught off guard.

Key contrast Both affirm that God knows Pharaoh’s refusal in advance and that Pharaoh is responsible for it. Arminian readers usually understand this foreknowledge as God knowing what Pharaoh will freely choose, without determining it; Reformed readers usually understand God’s foreknowledge as resting on his sovereign plan, which ordains the refusal while working through Pharaoh’s own will.

Exodus 3:20

20I will reach out my hand and strike Egypt with all my wonders which I will do among them, and after that he will let you go.

Arminian

God will strike Egypt with wonders, and then Pharaoh will let Israel go. Arminian readers see God’s power responding to Pharaoh’s obstinacy; the plagues are judgments on real resistance. Both traditions agree that the outcome is certain because God himself will act, and that the wonders reveal his power over Egypt.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the plagues as part of God’s stated plan to display his power and glory before Egypt and Israel. The “after that” shows the outcome is fixed by God’s purpose. Shared ground: the release comes through God’s mighty hand, not through Moses’ persuasion or Pharaoh’s goodwill.

Exodus 3:21

21I will give this people favor in the sight of the Egyptians, and it will happen that when you go, you shall not go empty-handed.

Arminian

God promises favor in the eyes of the Egyptians so that Israel will not leave empty-handed. Arminian readers note that God works through ordinary human attitudes as well as miracles. Both traditions see an echo of the promise to Abraham that his descendants would come out with great possessions (Genesis 15:14).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence over human hearts: he turns the Egyptians’ attitudes toward Israel. This does not erase their agency but directs it. Both traditions recognize the fulfillment of Genesis 15:14 and read the favor as a reversal of Israel’s long humiliation.

Exodus 3:22

22But every woman shall ask of her neighbor, and of her who visits her house, jewels of silver, jewels of gold, and clothing. You shall put them on your sons, and on your daughters. You shall plunder the Egyptians.”

Arminian

The Israelite women will ask for silver, gold, and clothing, and so plunder Egypt. Arminian readers see a kind of justice: back wages for years of unpaid labor. Both traditions note the reversal, as the oppressed leave enriched, and observe that this wealth later furnishes the tabernacle.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s justice and providence: the oppressors’ wealth is transferred to the oppressed, and later supplies materials for the tabernacle. Both traditions read “plunder” as describing a victory God wins without Israel fighting, and the text presents the giving as the fruit of God-given favor (3:21), not of trickery.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
