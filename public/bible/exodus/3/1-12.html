Passage

Exodus 3

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Forty years after fleeing Egypt, Moses is a shepherd in Midian when God meets him at Horeb in a bush that burns without being consumed. God identifies himself as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, announces that he has seen Israel’s affliction and come down to deliver them, and commissions Moses to go to Pharaoh. Moses’ first objection, “Who am I?”, is answered not with a résumé but with a promise of presence and a sign that Israel will worship on this mountain.

Exodus 3:1

1Now Moses was keeping the flock of Jethro, his father-in-law, the priest of Midian, and he led the flock to the back of the wilderness, and came to God’s mountain, to Horeb.

Arminian

Moses is in the ordinary work of a shepherd, far from the centers of power, when God takes the initiative. Arminian readers note that the call comes to a man with a history of failure and flight, and that God’s grace meets him where he is. Both traditions read “God’s mountain” as anticipating Sinai.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providence preparing Moses through forty quiet years in Midian; nothing in his exile was wasted. The narrative stresses that God seeks Moses, not the reverse. Both traditions agree that Horeb, already called God’s mountain, points ahead to the covenant at Sinai.

Exodus 3:2

2The LORD’s angel appeared to him in a flame of fire out of the middle of a bush. He looked, and behold, the bush burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed.

Arminian

The angel of the LORD appears in fire, a sign of holy presence, yet the bush is not consumed. Arminian readers often see here God’s presence among his afflicted people without destroying them. The WEB footnotes that LORD in capitals renders the divine name, usually pronounced Yahweh; both traditions read this as a genuine divine appearance.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly identify the angel of the LORD with God himself, many seeing a pre-incarnate appearance of the Son, a view held in both traditions. The unconsumed bush pictures God’s preserving grace toward Israel in the furnace of Egypt. The capitalized LORD, per the WEB footnote, renders the covenant name.

Exodus 3:3

3Moses said, “I will go now, and see this great sight, why the bush is not burned.”

Arminian

Moses turns aside out of curiosity, and God responds to that turning. Arminian readers value the real human response in the scene: God draws attention with a sign, and Moses chooses to come and see. Both traditions agree the sign is God’s initiative and the encounter is entirely of grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the sight is designed to draw Moses, so that even his curiosity serves God’s purpose. Yet Moses genuinely decides to go and look; divine initiative does not erase human agency. Shared ground: the meeting begins with God’s self-disclosure, not with Moses’ spiritual searching.

Exodus 3:4

4When the LORD saw that he came over to see, God called to him out of the middle of the bush, and said, “Moses! Moses!” He said, “Here I am.”

Arminian

God calls Moses by name only when he sees him turn aside, which Arminian readers take as a real interaction between divine call and human response. The doubled name signals urgency and intimacy, and Moses answers with the posture of availability that Abraham showed. Both traditions read this as a personal call to service.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an effectual personal call: God names Moses twice, as he did Abraham and later Samuel, and the summons sets him apart for a task God has determined. Moses’ “Here I am” is a genuine response. Both traditions agree the call is personal, gracious, and directed to vocation.

Exodus 3:5

5He said, “Don’t come close. Take off your sandals, for the place you are standing on is holy ground.”

Arminian

Holiness sets the terms of the encounter. Moses may come near to hear, but not too near, and he must remove his sandals. Arminian readers stress that grace invites approach while preserving reverence. Shared ground: the ground is holy because of God’s presence, not because of anything in the place itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the Creator-creature distinction: even the chosen servant must approach on God’s terms. The place is holy only because God is present there. Both traditions agree that reverence and nearness belong together, and that this anticipates the boundaries set around Sinai later in Exodus.

Exodus 3:6

6Moreover he said, “I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.” Moses hid his face because he was afraid to look at God.

Arminian

God identifies himself through his promises to the patriarchs, so the coming rescue flows from covenant faithfulness. Arminian readers note God’s steady love to a family across generations. Moses’ fear is fitting before holiness. Both traditions agree that the God of Exodus is the same God who spoke to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant of grace in continuity: the God who bound himself to Abraham now acts on that promise. Moses’ hidden face shows the right response of a sinner before a holy God. Shared ground: deliverance is grounded in God’s prior word, not in Israel’s worthiness.

Exodus 3:7

7The LORD said, “I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt, and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters, for I know their sorrows.

Arminian

God sees, hears, and knows his people’s suffering. Arminian readers highlight God’s genuine compassion and responsiveness to real affliction; the cry of the oppressed matters to him. Reformed readers affirm the same compassion, so this verse is firm shared ground: God is not indifferent to injustice, and he calls Israel “my people” before the exodus.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that “my people” marks Israel as God’s covenant possession before deliverance; he redeems those already his by promise. His seeing and knowing express covenant care, not new information. Both traditions affirm God’s genuine compassion here and hear the language of seeing and hearing as personal, not abstract.

Exodus 3:8

8I have come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians, and to bring them up out of that land to a good and large land, to a land flowing with milk and honey; to the place of the Canaanite, the Hittite, the Amorite, the Perizzite, the Hivite, and the Jebusite.

Arminian

“I have come down” expresses God’s personal involvement in rescue. The goal is twofold: deliverance from Egypt and entrance into a good land. Arminian readers note that the promise is offered to a whole people who must still follow in faith. Both traditions read the land as fulfillment of the patriarchal promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a pattern of redemption: God delivers out of bondage and brings into inheritance, foreshadowing salvation in Christ. The list of nations recalls the promise of Genesis 15. Both traditions agree the land is a gift of grace and that God himself is the agent of rescue.

Exodus 3:9

9Now, behold, the cry of the children of Israel has come to me. Moreover I have seen the oppression with which the Egyptians oppress them.

Arminian

God repeats that the cry has reached him and that he has seen the oppression. The repetition underlines that suffering is not hidden from God. Arminian readers find here a God moved to act by his people’s plight. Both traditions agree that oppression is an injustice God opposes.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s response to the cry is rooted in his covenant remembrance (2:24). The repetition stresses certainty: God will act. Both traditions affirm that God hears prayer and that the oppression of Israel provokes his righteous intervention against Egypt.

Exodus 3:10

10Come now therefore, and I will send you to Pharaoh, that you may bring my people, the children of Israel, out of Egypt.”

Arminian

God sends Moses as his instrument. Arminian readers stress that God works through human cooperation: the deliverance is God’s, yet Moses must actually go. Both traditions note the shift from “I have come down” to “I will send you,” showing that God’s action and human agency are not rivals in this story.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign purpose carried out through appointed means. Moses is commissioned, not consulted, though he is free to respond and does object. Shared ground: the rescue is God’s work from start to finish, and Moses is a servant through whom God acts.

Exodus 3:11

11Moses said to God, “Who am I, that I should go to Pharaoh, and that I should bring the children of Israel out of Egypt?”

Arminian

Moses’ question shows humility mixed with reluctance. Arminian readers note that God treats the objection as a real one and answers it patiently rather than overriding it. Both traditions agree Moses’ sense of inadequacy is not wrong in itself; the issue is whether he will trust the God who sends him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in Moses the frailty of every human instrument; the question “Who am I?” is answered by who God is. God’s patience with his servant is itself grace. Both traditions agree that Moses’ qualification for the task lies in God’s presence rather than in his own abilities.

Exodus 3:12

12He said, “Certainly I will be with you. This will be the token to you, that I have sent you: when you have brought the people out of Egypt, you shall serve God on this mountain.”

Arminian

God’s answer is presence: “I will be with you.” The sign is unusual because it comes after obedience: worship on this mountain will confirm the call. Arminian readers see faith required to act before the confirmation. Both traditions agree that the promise of presence is the heart of the commission.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the sign confirms God’s purpose by its future fulfillment, so Moses must trust the promise in the meantime. “I will be with you” anticipates the divine name in 3:14. Both traditions agree that the goal of the exodus is worship, not merely political freedom.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
