Passage

Exodus 14

Verses 23–31 · WEB

The Egyptians pursue Israel into the middle of the sea, but in the morning watch the LORD looks out through the pillar and throws their army into confusion. Even the Egyptians admit that the LORD fights for Israel, and when Moses stretches out his hand again, the waters return and cover Pharaoh’s whole army. Israel, which walked through on dry land, sees the Egyptians dead on the shore, and the people fear the LORD and believe in him and in his servant Moses. Both traditions read this as a shared account of deliverance and judgment that calls for reverent faith; the faith and fear of verse 31 are common ground, and the traditions differ mainly in how they describe the relationship between God’s saving act and the people’s believing response.

Exodus 14:23

23The Egyptians pursued, and went in after them into the middle of the sea: all of Pharaoh’s horses, his chariots, and his horsemen.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the Egyptians pressing into the sea as the culmination of their persistent defiance. They rush into danger because they refuse to recognize God’s hand.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Egyptians willingly following into the place where God will judge them. Both traditions agree that their pursuit is their own and that it fulfills what God said would happen.

Exodus 14:24

24In the morning watch, the LORD looked out on the Egyptian army through the pillar of fire and of cloud, and confused the Egyptian army.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the LORD looks out through the pillar and throws the army into confusion. The battle is truly his, as Moses promised.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the LORD acting directly against the enemy at the decisive moment. Both traditions agree that this verse shows God as the warrior who fights for his people.

Exodus 14:25

25He took off their chariot wheels, and they drove them heavily; so that the Egyptians said, “Let’s flee from the face of Israel, for the LORD fights for them against the Egyptians!”

Arminian

Arminian readers find it striking that even the Egyptians confess that the LORD fights for Israel. Their recognition comes too late to save them but proves God’s purpose to be known.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Egyptians’ words as fulfilling God’s declaration that they would know that he is the LORD. Both traditions agree that this admission vindicates Moses’ promise in verse 14.

Exodus 14:26

26The LORD said to Moses, “Stretch out your hand over the sea, that the waters may come again on the Egyptians, on their chariots, and on their horsemen.”

Arminian

Arminian readers see God again working through Moses’ obedience to bring the deliverance to completion. Faithful service is the channel, not the source, of God’s power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s command to Moses as the means by which his judgment on Egypt is carried out. Both traditions agree that the God who opened the sea now closes it.

Exodus 14:27

27Moses stretched out his hand over the sea, and the sea returned to its strength when the morning appeared; and the Egyptians fled against it. The LORD overthrew the Egyptians in the middle of the sea.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the return of the waters as a sobering picture of judgment on those who persist in opposing God. The same sea that became a path for Israel becomes the end of Egypt’s army.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the LORD overthrew the Egyptians, presenting judgment as his righteous act. Both traditions read salvation and judgment as two sides of the same event.

Exodus 14:28

28The waters returned, and covered the chariots and the horsemen, even all Pharaoh’s army that went in after them into the sea. There remained not so much as one of them.

Arminian

Arminian readers note the completeness of the judgment, with not one of the pursuing army remaining. It warns against treating God’s patience as permission to keep resisting.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the total overthrow of the army the certainty of God’s victory over his enemies. Both traditions agree that this verse confirms Moses’ promise that Israel would never again see these Egyptians.

Exodus 14:29

29But the children of Israel walked on dry land in the middle of the sea, and the waters were a wall to them on their right hand and on their left.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the contrast between the drowned army and the people who walked on dry land as a call to trust God’s way of salvation. The same waters that judged Egypt sheltered Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the repeated description of the walls of water as stressing that Israel’s safety was entirely God’s doing. Both traditions find in this verse a lasting picture of deliverance through judgment.

Exodus 14:30

30Thus the LORD saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians; and Israel saw the Egyptians dead on the seashore.

Arminian

Arminian readers rejoice in the clear statement that the LORD saved Israel that day. Salvation is God’s work, and it is meant to be seen and remembered.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise treasure this summary of the LORD as the one who saves. Both traditions read this verse as shared gospel ground: God rescued his people from an enemy they could not defeat.

Exodus 14:31

31Israel saw the great work which the LORD did to the Egyptians, and the people feared the LORD; and they believed in the LORD and in his servant Moses.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the people’s fear of the LORD and belief as a fitting response to what God has done. Faith answers God’s mighty acts and is meant to grow into faithful trust as the journey continues.

Reformed

Reformed readers see reverent fear and faith as the fruit of God’s saving work in his people. Both traditions agree that seeing God’s deliverance leads to worship and trust, and they hold this verse as shared ground rather than a point of division.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
