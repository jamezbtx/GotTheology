Passage

Exodus 14

Verses 1–14 · WEB

The LORD directs Israel to turn back and camp by the sea, a position that makes them look trapped in Pharaoh’s eyes. The text says the LORD will harden Pharaoh’s heart so that he pursues, and that God will gain honor over Pharaoh so the Egyptians will know that he is the LORD; Pharaoh and his servants also change their own minds and set out with chariots. Facing the army, Israel cries out in fear and turns bitterly on Moses, who answers, “Stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD… The LORD will fight for you.” Both traditions read this as a shared, gospel-shaped picture of salvation as God’s work for helpless people; they differ mainly in how they explain God’s hardening of Pharaoh’s heart, while both affirm God’s justice and Pharaoh’s real guilt.

Exodus 14:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, saying,

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the next movement of the story again begins with God’s word to Moses. The people’s path is directed by the LORD, not by their own planning.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the LORD initiating each step of the deliverance. Both traditions agree that the sea crossing unfolds according to God’s stated purpose.

Exodus 14:2

2“Speak to the children of Israel, that they turn back and encamp before Pihahiroth, between Migdol and the sea, before Baal Zephon. You shall encamp opposite it by the sea.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the command to turn back as a test of trust, since the new camp seems to make little sense. Obedience here means following God’s direction when the reasons are not yet clear.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the LORD deliberately placing his people where his power will be unmistakably displayed. Both traditions recognize that God’s ways can look strange before they prove wise.

Exodus 14:3

3Pharaoh will say of the children of Israel, ‘They are entangled in the land. The wilderness has shut them in.’

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that Pharaoh reads the situation by appearances and concludes that Israel is trapped. His proud misjudgment sets the stage for his own downfall.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God anticipating Pharaoh’s reasoning and using it within his plan. Both traditions agree that human calculations that leave God out of the picture are badly mistaken.

Exodus 14:4

4I will harden Pharaoh’s heart, and he will follow after them; and I will get honor over Pharaoh, and over all his armies; and the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD.” They did so.

Arminian

Arminian readers commonly understand God’s hardening here as a judicial act that confirms and gives over a heart Pharaoh had already set against God through repeated refusals. God’s aim to gain honor and make himself known even to the Egyptians shows that his judgment serves the revelation of his name.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly see God’s hardening as a sovereign act serving his purpose to gain honor over Pharaoh and make himself known. They hold that God does this justly, without being the author of Pharaoh’s sin, since Pharaoh acts willingly and remains fully responsible.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm God’s justice and Pharaoh’s real culpability, and each has internal diversity on how the hardening statements relate. Arminian readers commonly read this as judicial hardening of an already resistant heart, while Reformed readers commonly emphasize God’s sovereign purpose working through Pharaoh’s willing rebellion.

Exodus 14:5

5The king of Egypt was told that the people had fled; and the heart of Pharaoh and of his servants was changed toward the people, and they said, “What is this we have done, that we have let Israel go from serving us?”

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that Pharaoh and his servants change their minds, highlighting their own responsibility and regret over losing their slaves. Their question exposes a heart still bent on control.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the human decision to pursue as real and culpable, even as it serves what God said would happen. Both traditions agree that the Egyptians act willingly and that their choice reveals what they value.

Exodus 14:6

6He prepared his chariot, and took his army with him;

Arminian

Arminian readers see Pharaoh personally preparing for pursuit, an active and deliberate choice. Resistance to God is not passive here but energetic.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note Pharaoh’s deliberate action, carried out freely within God’s overarching plan. Both traditions see him moving toward the judgment he does not foresee.

Exodus 14:7

7and he took six hundred chosen chariots, and all the chariots of Egypt, with captains over all of them.

Arminian

Arminian readers note the overwhelming military force that seems to make Israel’s situation hopeless. The scale of the threat will make the coming deliverance all the more clearly God’s work.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the display of Egypt’s strength as the backdrop against which the LORD will show that no power can resist him. Both traditions agree that the point is not Egypt’s might but God’s greater might.

Exodus 14:8

8The LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh king of Egypt, and he pursued the children of Israel; for the children of Israel went out with a high hand.

Arminian

Arminian readers read the LORD’s hardening alongside verse 5, where Pharaoh changes his own mind, and see God confirming a course Pharaoh freely chose. The note that Israel went out “with a high hand” (or “defiantly,” as a WEB footnote allows) contrasts the freed people’s boldness with the king’s stubbornness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s hardening and Pharaoh’s own will working together, with God’s purpose ruling over Pharaoh’s real decisions. Both traditions hold that Pharaoh is not an innocent victim but a responsible ruler pursuing a people he had oppressed.

Exodus 14:9

9The Egyptians pursued them. All the horses and chariots of Pharaoh, his horsemen, and his army overtook them encamping by the sea, beside Pihahiroth, before Baal Zephon.

Arminian

Arminian readers sense the mounting danger as Egypt’s whole army closes in on the camp by the sea. The moment exposes how little Israel can do to save itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the people placed exactly where God said they would be, now overtaken by the enemy. Both traditions agree that the situation is humanly impossible, which is the point of the story.

Exodus 14:10

10When Pharaoh came near, the children of Israel lifted up their eyes, and behold, the Egyptians were marching after them; and they were very afraid. The children of Israel cried out to the LORD.

Arminian

Arminian readers note that the people’s fear is real and that their cry to the LORD is a natural response of the helpless. God hears the cries of his people even when their faith is shaky.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Israel’s terror as a picture of human helplessness before an enemy too strong for them. Both traditions agree that crying out to the LORD is the right instinct, even when mixed with fear.

Exodus 14:11

11They said to Moses, “Because there were no graves in Egypt, have you taken us away to die in the wilderness? Why have you treated us this way, to bring us out of Egypt?

Arminian

Arminian readers see how quickly fear can turn into complaint and accusation. Believers can recognize their own tendency to doubt God’s good intentions when danger appears.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the people’s complaint does not cancel God’s purpose to save them. Both traditions see grace that persists despite the weakness and ingratitude of those being delivered.

Exodus 14:12

12Isn’t this the word that we spoke to you in Egypt, saying, ‘Leave us alone, that we may serve the Egyptians’? For it would have been better for us to serve the Egyptians than to die in the wilderness.”

Arminian

Arminian readers note the pull of familiar bondage over the uncertainty of freedom with God. Faith involves continuing to trust God rather than retreating to old securities.

Reformed

Reformed readers see how deeply the habits of slavery shaped the people’s outlook. Both traditions agree that God’s deliverance does not depend on the people’s strength of faith in this moment.

Exodus 14:13

13Moses said to the people, “Don’t be afraid. Stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD, which he will work for you today; for you will never again see the Egyptians whom you have seen today.

Arminian

Arminian readers hear Moses’ call not to fear and to stand still as an invitation to trust what God is about to do. Salvation here is entirely God’s gift, received by faith rather than achieved by effort.

Reformed

Reformed readers see “the salvation of the LORD” as a clear picture of salvation as God’s work alone. Both traditions treasure this verse as shared gospel ground: God saves those who cannot save themselves.

Exodus 14:14

14The LORD will fight for you, and you shall be still.”

Arminian

Arminian readers find in this promise great comfort for those who are overwhelmed: the LORD fights for his people. Being still is not passivity but trust that rests in God’s action.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the LORD as the warrior who wins the battle for his people. Both traditions agree that this verse captures the heart of redemption: God fights, and his people are called to trust him.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
