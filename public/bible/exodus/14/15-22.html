Passage

Exodus 14

Verses 15–22 · WEB

The LORD tells Moses to stop crying out and to have the people go forward, then to stretch his hand over the sea so that Israel can cross on dry ground. God again says he will harden the hearts of the Egyptians so that they follow, and that he will gain honor over Pharaoh’s army. The angel of God and the pillar of cloud move behind Israel to stand between the two camps, and through the night a strong east wind drives the sea back until the waters stand as a wall on either side. Both traditions see here the decisive act of redemption that Israel later remembers as the pattern of God’s saving power; they differ mainly in how they read the renewed hardening language of verse 17, while agreeing that the Egyptians’ pursuit was truly their own.

Exodus 14:15

15The LORD said to Moses, “Why do you cry to me? Speak to the children of Israel, that they go forward.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that God now calls for movement: prayer is to be joined by obedient steps forward. Trust and action belong together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s command as the means by which he carries out his saving purpose. Both traditions agree that standing still in trust and going forward in obedience are not opposites but two sides of faith.

Exodus 14:16

16Lift up your rod, and stretch out your hand over the sea and divide it. Then the children of Israel shall go into the middle of the sea on dry ground.

Arminian

Arminian readers see Moses’ outstretched hand as an act of obedience through which God chooses to work. God uses human instruments without sharing his glory with them.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that dividing the sea is wholly God’s work, with Moses’ rod as the sign of his authority. Both traditions see the promise of dry ground as God making a way where there was none.

Exodus 14:17

17Behold, I myself will harden the hearts of the Egyptians, and they will go in after them. I will get myself honor over Pharaoh, and over all his armies, over his chariots, and over his horsemen.

Arminian

Arminian readers read this hardening of the Egyptians as God confirming them in the course they have already set by pursuing. God’s honor is shown in judging persistent, willing opposition.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign purpose extending to the Egyptians’ pursuit, so that his glory is displayed over Pharaoh’s army. Both traditions agree that the Egyptians act willingly and are justly judged, as discussed more fully at verse 4.

Exodus 14:18

18The Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD when I have gotten myself honor over Pharaoh, over his chariots, and over his horsemen.”

Arminian

Arminian readers note that even the Egyptians are meant to come to know that God is the LORD. His acts reveal his identity to the nations, not only to Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s glory over Pharaoh as the goal of the whole episode. Both traditions agree that the sea crossing is about making God’s name known.

Exodus 14:19

19The angel of God, who went before the camp of Israel, moved and went behind them; and the pillar of cloud moved from before them, and stood behind them.

Arminian

Arminian readers take comfort that God’s presence moves to stand between his people and the danger behind them. He protects them as well as leading them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the angel of God and the pillar shifting to guard the camp as a sign of the LORD’s protective covenant care. Both traditions treasure the picture of God standing between his people and their enemy.

Exodus 14:20

20It came between the camp of Egypt and the camp of Israel. There was the cloud and the darkness, yet it gave light by night. One didn’t come near the other all night.

Arminian

Arminian readers notice that the same cloud brings darkness to one camp and light to the other. How people stand toward God’s presence matters greatly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God making a distinction between Egypt and Israel, protecting one and confounding the other. Both traditions agree that God’s presence is both comforting and fearful, depending on where people stand.

Exodus 14:21

21Moses stretched out his hand over the sea, and the LORD caused the sea to go back by a strong east wind all night, and made the sea dry land, and the waters were divided.

Arminian

Arminian readers see God using a strong east wind, a created means, to accomplish a wonder. His power works through the natural order as well as beyond it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the LORD as sovereign over wind and sea, directing creation to accomplish his purpose. Both traditions affirm that the dividing of the waters is a real act of God, whatever means he used.

Exodus 14:22

22The children of Israel went into the middle of the sea on the dry ground; and the waters were a wall to them on their right hand and on their left.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the people walking into the sea as a step of faith, entering the path God opened. Their walking does not create the way but follows it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the waters standing as walls as a vivid display of God’s saving power. Both traditions, with later Scripture in 1 Corinthians 10:1–2, read this passage through the sea as a picture of redemption.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
