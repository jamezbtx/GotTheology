Passage

Exodus 26

Verses 31–37 · WEB

A veil of blue, purple, scarlet and fine linen, worked with cherubim, is to divide the holy place from the most holy place, where the ark and mercy seat stand. The table and lampstand stand outside the veil, and an embroidered screen closes the entrance of the tent.

Exodus 26:31

31“You shall make a veil of blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine twined linen, with cherubim. It shall be the work of a skillful workman.

Arminian

A veil of blue, purple, scarlet and fine linen, worked with cherubim, is made by a skilled craftsman.

Arminian readers see both beauty and barrier. The cherubim recall the guardians of Eden (Gen 3:24).

Reformed

The veil echoes the cherubim that guarded the way to the tree of life (Gen 3:24).

Reformed readers see the veil as a sign that sinners cannot casually enter God’s presence, preparing for the way Christ opens.

Exodus 26:32

32You shall hang it on four pillars of acacia overlaid with gold; their hooks shall be of gold, on four sockets of silver.

Arminian

The veil hangs on four gold-covered acacia pillars set in silver sockets.

Arminian readers see the veil given a dignified, stable setting, matching its importance.

Reformed

The pillars and hooks match the materials of the holy place.

Reformed readers note the dignity given to the veil’s supports.

Exodus 26:33

33You shall hang up the veil under the clasps, and shall bring the ark of the covenant in there within the veil. The veil shall separate the holy place from the most holy for you.

Arminian

The veil separates the holy place from the most holy place, where the ark stands. Only the high priest enters, once a year (Lev 16; Heb 9:7). At Christ’s death the temple veil is torn (Matt 27:51).

Arminian readers see the way to God opened for all who will come through Christ (Heb 10:19–22).

Reformed

The veil marks the boundary of the most holy place. Hebrews says the way into the holy places was not yet opened while the first tent stood (Heb 9:8), and the veil was torn at Christ’s death (Matt 27:51).

Reformed readers stress that Christ has entered once for all with his own blood (Heb 9:12) and opened a new and living way “through the veil, that is to say, his flesh” (Heb 10:20).

Exodus 26:34

34You shall put the mercy seat on the ark of the covenant in the most holy place.

Arminian

The mercy seat rests on the ark in the most holy place.

Arminian readers see the place of atonement at the very center of the sanctuary.

Reformed

The mercy seat, the place of atonement (25:17–22), sits in the innermost room.

Reformed readers see atonement at the heart of God’s dwelling with his people.

Exodus 26:35

35You shall set the table outside the veil, and the lamp stand opposite the table on the side of the tabernacle toward the south. You shall put the table on the north side.

Arminian

The table goes on the north side and the lampstand on the south, both outside the veil in the holy place.

Arminian readers note that the daily service of light and bread happens before the veil.

Reformed

The arrangement sets bread and light in the holy place, facing each other.

Reformed readers see the ordered layout of the sanctuary moving from the entrance toward the presence of God.

Exodus 26:36

36“You shall make a screen for the door of the Tent, of blue, and purple, and scarlet, and fine twined linen, the work of the embroiderer.

Arminian

An embroidered screen of blue, purple, scarlet and fine linen closes the entrance of the tent.

Arminian readers see an entrance that is both a boundary and an invitation.

Reformed

The screen marks the entrance of the holy place. It is embroidered work, distinct from the cherubim-worked veil.

Reformed readers see graded access to God’s presence, entrance by entrance.

Exodus 26:37

37You shall make for the screen five pillars of acacia, and overlay them with gold. Their hooks shall be of gold. You shall cast five sockets of bronze for them.

Arminian

Five gold-covered pillars with gold hooks hold the screen, set in bronze sockets.

Arminian readers note the bronze sockets at the entrance, with silver further in (v. 19).

Reformed

Bronze at the entrance and silver within again show the gradation of holiness.

Reformed readers see the tabernacle’s careful design teaching that God is holy and must be approached on his terms, terms fulfilled in Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
