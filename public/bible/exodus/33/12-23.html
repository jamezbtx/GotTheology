Passage

Exodus 33

Verses 12–23 · WEB

Moses intercedes for the people: he asks to know God’s way and pleads that God’s presence go with them, since that presence is what sets Israel apart. God agrees. Then Moses asks to see God’s glory. God promises to make his goodness pass by and proclaim his name, declaring that he will be gracious to whom he will be gracious, but he says no one can see his face and live, so Moses will see only his back from a cleft in the rock.

Exodus 33:12

12Moses said to the LORD, “Behold, you tell me, ‘Bring up this people;’ and you haven’t let me know whom you will send with me. Yet you have said, ‘I know you by name, and you have also found favor in my sight.’

Arminian

Moses reminds God of his own words: “I know you by name, and you have also found favor in my sight.” His intercession leans on God’s prior grace.

Arminian readers see a covenant relationship in which God’s favor invites bold prayer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses resting his appeal on God’s initiative: God first knew and favored him.

Shared ground: prayer appeals to what God has already said and done.

Exodus 33:13

13Now therefore, if I have found favor in your sight, please show me your way, now, that I may know you, so that I may find favor in your sight; and consider that this nation is your people.”

Arminian

“Show me your way… that I may know you.” Moses asks for deeper knowledge of God, and he reminds God that “this nation is your people.”

Arminians see Moses pleading for the people’s restored relationship with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Moses appealing to God’s covenant ownership of Israel.

Both agree that knowing God is the heart of the request.

Exodus 33:14

14He said, “My presence will go with you, and I will give you rest.”

Arminian

“My presence will go with you, and I will give you rest.” God answers Moses’ intercession and reverses the withdrawal of v. 3.

Arminian readers see God responding to prayer in real interaction, and they stress that intercession matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm that God answers prayer and see Moses’ intercession as the means God appointed to bring about the restoration he intended.

Shared ground: God hears his mediator and promises his presence and rest.

Exodus 33:15

15Moses said to him, “If your presence doesn’t go with me, don’t carry us up from here.

Arminian

Moses would rather stay than go without God. The presence of God matters more than the land.

Arminians and Reformed readers alike see a model of godly priorities.

Reformed

Reformed readers see communion with God as the true goal of redemption, not merely land or safety.

Both agree that God himself is the greatest gift.

Exodus 33:16

16For how would people know that I have found favor in your sight, I and your people? Isn’t it that you go with us, so that we are separated, I and your people, from all the people who are on the surface of the earth?”

Arminian

God’s presence is what marks Israel as distinct from every other people. Without it, nothing sets them apart.

Arminians see Israel’s calling as a people set apart for God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the distinctiveness of God’s people grounded in his presence among them, a theme that runs to the church (cf. 2 Corinthians 6:16).

Shared ground: identity flows from God’s presence.

Exodus 33:17

17The LORD said to Moses, “I will do this thing also that you have spoken; for you have found favor in my sight, and I know you by name.”

Arminian

God grants the request “for you have found favor in my sight, and I know you by name.” His answer is personal.

Arminians see God’s readiness to respond to the prayers of his friend.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God honoring the favor he freely bestowed on Moses.

Both agree God’s answer is gracious and personal.

Exodus 33:18

18Moses said, “Please show me your glory.”

Arminian

“Please show me your glory.” Moses asks for more, longing to see God himself.

Arminians see the holy longing of a friend of God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the desire of the redeemed to behold God, fulfilled ultimately in the vision of God’s glory (cf. 2 Corinthians 4:6).

Shared ground: Moses’ request is good and holy.

Exodus 33:19

19He said, “I will make all my goodness pass before you, and will proclaim the LORD’s name before you. I will be gracious to whom I will be gracious, and will show mercy on whom I will show mercy.”

Arminian

God answers by revealing his goodness and his name. “I will be gracious to whom I will be gracious” asserts God’s freedom: no one, not even Moses, can claim mercy as a right. In context, it explains why God can forgive Israel after the calf, freely and of his own will. Paul quotes it in Romans 9:15. Many Arminian interpreters read Paul as defending God’s freedom to show mercy on his own terms, including to those who believe, Gentiles as well as Jews, rather than as teaching that God chooses individuals for salvation unconditionally.

The verse is about God’s character, his goodness and name, not about arbitrariness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here a foundational statement of God’s sovereign freedom in mercy. God does not owe grace to anyone, and he gives it according to his own will. Paul quotes it in Romans 9:15, which Reformed interpreters read as bearing on God’s free choice in salvation: “So then it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy” (Romans 9:16).

In context, the declaration is good news. A stiff-necked people is spared only because God is free to be gracious.

Key contrast Both traditions agree that God’s mercy is free and undeserved, that the verse reveals his goodness and name, and that it explains God’s forgiveness of Israel after the calf. Many Arminian interpreters read God’s freedom here, and in Romans 9:15, as his right to set the terms of mercy, such as faith, and to extend it to whom he wills, including Gentiles. Reformed interpreters typically read it as God’s sovereign, unconditional choice of whom he will save.

Exodus 33:20

20He said, “You cannot see my face, for man may not see me and live.”

Arminian

“You cannot see my face… and live.” Even Moses, God’s friend, cannot bear the full sight of God. Intimacy does not erase holiness.

Arminians see God’s protective mercy in limiting what Moses sees.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Creator–creature distinction: God is infinitely glorious, and no sinful human can behold him directly.

Shared ground: God’s holiness requires mediation (cf. John 1:18).

Exodus 33:21

21The LORD also said, “Behold, there is a place by me, and you shall stand on the rock.

Arminian

God provides a place for Moses beside him on the rock. He makes room for Moses near him.

Arminians see God’s gracious accommodation.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see a picture of refuge in God, and some see Christ as the rock (cf. 1 Corinthians 10:4), though Paul is not interpreting this verse there.

Both agree God provides the place to stand.

Exodus 33:22

22It will happen, while my glory passes by, that I will put you in a cleft of the rock, and will cover you with my hand until I have passed by;

Arminian

God places Moses in a cleft of the rock and covers him with his hand. God both reveals and protects.

Arminian readers see tender care in God’s protecting hand.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign grace shielding Moses from what would destroy him.

Shared ground: revelation comes with protection.

Exodus 33:23

23then I will take away my hand, and you will see my back; but my face shall not be seen.”

Arminian

Moses sees God’s “back” but not his face. The language is figurative, describing a real but partial revelation suited to human limits.

Arminians see God giving as much as Moses can bear.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an accommodated revelation, true but not exhaustive, and look forward to the fuller revelation of God’s glory in Christ (2 Corinthians 4:6).

Both agree what Moses sees is real, but partial.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
