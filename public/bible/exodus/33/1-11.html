Passage

Exodus 33

Verses 1–11 · WEB

After the golden calf, the LORD tells Israel to go on to the promised land but says he will not go among them himself, lest he consume a stiff-necked people. The people mourn and strip off their ornaments. Moses pitches a tent of meeting outside the camp, where the pillar of cloud comes down and the LORD speaks with him face to face, as with a friend.

Exodus 33:1

1The LORD spoke to Moses, “Depart, go up from here, you and the people that you have brought up out of the land of Egypt, to the land of which I swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, saying, ‘I will give it to your offspring.’

Arminian

God still keeps his oath to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob even after the calf. The promise of the land stands, though Israel has broken faith.

Arminian readers note the tension the chapter sets up: God remains faithful to his promise while Israel’s sin threatens their enjoyment of his presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see covenant faithfulness grounded in God’s oath, not Israel’s performance. The promise to the patriarchs holds even when their descendants fail.

Shared ground: God’s word to Abraham is not cancelled by Israel’s sin.

Exodus 33:2

2I will send an angel before you; and I will drive out the Canaanite, the Amorite, and the Hittite, and the Perizzite, the Hivite, and the Jebusite.

Arminian

An angel will go before them and God will drive out the nations. Israel will get the land, but the wording sets up the loss announced in v. 3.

The gift is still generous; the question is whether the Giver himself will go along.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God still fights for his people. Victory is promised, though fellowship is under threat.

Both note the gift is offered without the full presence that made it precious.

Exodus 33:3

3Go to a land flowing with milk and honey; but I will not go up among you, for you are a stiff-necked people, lest I consume you on the way.”

Arminian

“I will not go up among you… lest I consume you.” God’s holiness and Israel’s stubbornness cannot dwell together safely. His withdrawal is, strangely, a mercy that protects them from judgment.

Arminian readers see a real, relational consequence of sin: the covenant relationship is damaged, and Moses’ intercession matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the problem the rest of Exodus resolves: how a holy God can dwell among sinners. The answer will come through mediation, atonement and the tabernacle.

Shared ground: the land without the LORD is not enough.

Exodus 33:4

4When the people heard this evil news, they mourned; and no one put on his jewelry.

Arminian

The people rightly hear this as “evil news” and mourn. They do not want the land without God.

Arminians see a fitting response of grief and repentance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the beginning of godly sorrow, a sign that the people grasp what is at stake.

Both agree the mourning is a right response to the threat of losing God’s presence.

Exodus 33:5

5The LORD had said to Moses, “Tell the children of Israel, ‘You are a stiff-necked people. If I were to go up among you for one moment, I would consume you. Therefore now take off your jewelry from you, that I may know what to do to you.’ ”

Arminian

Removing jewelry signals humility and repentance. Some of that jewelry likely recalled the gold of the calf (32:2–4). “That I may know what to do to you” leaves the outcome open, pending response.

Arminian readers see God inviting repentance and responding to it.

Reformed

Reformed readers read “that I may know what to do” as language accommodated to human understanding, since God is not uncertain. It shows God engaging his people in real interaction as their response unfolds.

Shared ground: God calls for repentance, and repentance is the right response to sin.

Exodus 33:6

6The children of Israel stripped themselves of their jewelry from Mount Horeb onward.

Arminian

The people obey and remove their ornaments from Horeb onward. Their outward act matches inward mourning.

Arminians see real, enacted repentance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an outward sign of humbling that prepares for Moses’ intercession.

Both agree the gesture is humble and fitting.

Exodus 33:7

7Now Moses used to take the tent and pitch it outside the camp, far away from the camp, and he called it “The Tent of Meeting.” Everyone who sought the LORD went out to the Tent of Meeting, which was outside the camp.

Arminian

Moses pitches a tent outside the camp, “The Tent of Meeting.” Its location outside the camp mirrors God’s distance from the sinful people. Yet anyone who sought the LORD could go out there.

Arminian readers see an open invitation to seek God even in a time of judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a provisional meeting place pointing ahead to the tabernacle and, later, to the presence of God in Christ (cf. Hebrews 13:12–13, where Jesus suffers outside the gate and believers go out to him).

Shared ground: God makes a way to be sought even when his people have sinned.

Exodus 33:8

8When Moses went out to the Tent, all the people rose up, and stood, everyone at their tent door, and watched Moses, until he had gone into the Tent.

Arminian

The people stand at their tent doors and watch Moses go. They look on as their mediator approaches God.

Arminians see a people waiting and watching to see whether God will meet with them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the people depending on a mediator they cannot replace.

Both note the reverence and expectation of the scene.

Exodus 33:9

9When Moses entered into the Tent, the pillar of cloud descended, stood at the door of the Tent, and the LORD spoke with Moses.

Arminian

The pillar of cloud comes down to the tent door and the LORD speaks with Moses. God meets with his servant.

The visible sign assures the people God has not abandoned them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God condescending to speak through his chosen mediator.

Shared ground: the cloud signals God’s real presence.

Exodus 33:10

10All the people saw the pillar of cloud stand at the door of the Tent, and all the people rose up and worshiped, everyone at their tent door.

Arminian

Seeing the cloud, the people worship at their tent doors. Their response is right: reverent worship from a distance.

Arminians see worship flowing from recognition of God’s presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see worship as the fitting response to God’s self-revelation.

Both agree the people respond rightly.

Exodus 33:11

11The LORD spoke to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend. He turned again into the camp, but his servant Joshua, the son of Nun, a young man, didn’t depart from the Tent.

Arminian

“Face to face, as a man speaks to his friend” describes intimacy and directness, not a literal sight of God’s face, as v. 20 makes clear (cf. Numbers 12:8). Joshua remains at the tent, a hint of his future leadership.

Arminian readers see the relational heart of God, who speaks with his servant as a friend.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the unique privilege of Moses as mediator, which Deuteronomy 34:10 later emphasizes, while v. 20 guards God’s transcendence.

Shared ground: the phrase describes closeness of communion, held together with God’s holiness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
