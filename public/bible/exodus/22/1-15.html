Passage

Exodus 22

Verses 1–15 · WEB

The Book of the Covenant (Exodus 20:22–23:33) moves from injury law in chapter 21 to property law. These case laws set out restitution for theft, including the household that kills a burglar at night, then damage from straying animals and spreading fire. They go on to deposits left in a neighbor’s keeping and disputes settled “before God,” and finally to animals held in trust or borrowed. The usual penalty is repayment, often multiplied, rather than physical punishment. The goal is restored relationships between neighbors in a covenant community under the LORD.

Exodus 22:1

1“If a man steals an ox or a sheep, and kills it or sells it, he shall pay five oxen for an ox, and four sheep for a sheep.

Arminian

The chapter opens with restitution, not prison: a thief who has slaughtered or sold the animal repays fivefold for an ox and fourfold for a sheep, probably because an ox was worth more as a working animal. Arminian readers in the Wesleyan and Remonstrant line share the classic threefold distinction of moral, ceremonial, and civil (judicial) law. On that view these statutes governed Israel as a nation, and their general equity still teaches justice, here the duty to make the wronged party whole (compare Zacchaeus in Luke 19:8).

Reformed

Reformed readers hold the same classic threefold distinction of moral, ceremonial, and judicial law. The Reformed confessions teach that Israel’s judicial laws expired with its national polity and still bind only as far as their general equity requires. That equity is clear here: theft is answered by restoring more than was taken, so the wrong is repaired and greed is checked. Zacchaeus’ fourfold repayment (Luke 19:8) echoes this law.

Exodus 22:2

2If the thief is found breaking in, and is struck so that he dies, there shall be no guilt of bloodshed for him.

Arminian

A thief caught breaking in, usually by digging through a mud-brick wall, may be struck down without bloodguilt for the householder. The next verse’s reference to sunrise shows the case is a night break-in, when the householder cannot see whether the intruder means to kill. Arminian readers see the law weighing real danger and limited knowledge in judging responsibility. The statute recognizes defense of one’s household while still protecting a thief’s life.

Reformed

The law excuses a householder who kills a burglar breaking in by night, when he cannot judge the intruder’s intent and cannot easily get help. Reformed readers apply this to the sixth commandment: the law that forbids murder also allows lawful defense of life and home. They read it as Israel’s civil law and not a license for private vengeance, a limit the next verse makes explicit.

Exodus 22:3

3If the sun has risen on him, he is guilty of bloodshed. He shall make restitution. If he has nothing, then he shall be sold for his theft.

Arminian

By daylight the situation changes: the householder can see, call for help, and identify the thief, so killing him now brings bloodguilt. Theft is not a capital crime; the thief must make restitution and, if he cannot pay, be sold to work off the debt. Arminian readers note that even a thief keeps the protection of the law, and the remedy aims at repayment rather than vengeance.

Reformed

Once the sun has risen, killing the thief becomes bloodshed, because the danger to life has passed and the courts can deal with him. Property never outweighs a human life in this law. The thief who cannot repay is sold into time-limited servitude (21:2), a civil remedy of Israel’s economy that Reformed readers interpret by its equity, not as a model for modern penal systems.

Exodus 22:4

4If the stolen property is found in his hand alive, whether it is ox, donkey, or sheep, he shall pay double.

Arminian

If the stolen animal is found alive in the thief’s possession, he repays double rather than four- or fivefold. The animal can be returned and the thief has not yet disposed of it, so the penalty is lower. Arminian readers see proportion in the law: penalties are scaled to the act and the harm done.

Reformed

Double restitution for a recovered animal shows graded justice: the penalty rises when the thief has gone further by slaughtering or selling. Reformed readers see the moral principle of proportion that lies behind the eighth commandment and is to be kept by every just government.

Exodus 22:5

5“If a man causes a field or vineyard to be eaten by letting his animal loose, and it grazes in another man’s field, he shall make restitution from the best of his own field, and from the best of his own vineyard.

Arminian

A man who lets his animal loose to graze in another’s field or vineyard must repay from the best of his own produce. Arminian readers note how the law reaches into ordinary farm life: carelessness with one’s property that harms a neighbor carries responsibility, and the repayment must be generous, not grudging.

Reformed

Negligence that lets an animal eat a neighbor’s crop is answered with restitution from the best of the offender’s own field. Reformed readers see the eighth commandment applied to responsibility for what we own and control. The equity of the law still teaches that love of neighbor means guarding his livelihood.

Exodus 22:6

6“If fire breaks out, and catches in thorns so that the shocks of grain, or the standing grain, or the field are consumed; he who kindled the fire shall surely make restitution.

Arminian

Fire set in a field, such as when burning off brush, can spread through thorn hedges and destroy a neighbor’s grain. The one who kindled it must make full restitution, even though he did not mean to cause the damage. Arminian readers see accountability for the foreseeable results of one’s actions, not only for what one intended.

Reformed

The man who started the fire is liable for the loss, whether or not he meant harm. Reformed readers see a principle of care toward a neighbor’s property: if your act endangers him, you answer for it. The civil statute belongs to Israel’s farming life, but its equity of responsible conduct still applies.

Exodus 22:7

7“If a man delivers to his neighbor money or stuff to keep, and it is stolen out of the man’s house, if the thief is found, he shall pay double.

Arminian

Without banks, people left money or goods with a neighbor for safekeeping. If the goods are stolen and the thief is found, he repays double. Arminian readers see trust as a moral bond in community life; the law protects the depositor and puts the penalty on the thief rather than the honest keeper.

Reformed

The law of deposits guards a common practice of trust between neighbors. If a thief is caught, he bears the double penalty. Reformed readers note that this equity still shapes honest stewardship: those who hold another’s property hold it as a trust before God.

Exodus 22:8

8If the thief isn’t found, then the master of the house shall come near to God, to find out whether or not he has put his hand on his neighbor’s goods.

Arminian

If no thief is found, the keeper must “come near to God” to establish whether he took the goods himself. The Hebrew word here (’elohim) is usually rendered “God,” and the WEB keeps that; some older translations read “the judges.” Interpreters are divided over whether this means a sanctuary procedure or a hearing before judges acting in God’s name. Either way, Arminian readers see the case brought under God’s scrutiny, where honesty matters more than appearances.

Reformed

The keeper is brought “near to God,” most likely to a sanctuary or to judges acting under God’s authority, to clear himself; the WEB renders ’elohim as “God,” while some versions read “judges.” The uncertainty does not change the point. Reformed readers see human justice placed under the divine Judge, a principle they also find in Romans 13, where rulers serve as God’s ministers.

Exodus 22:9

9For every matter of trespass, whether it is for ox, for donkey, for sheep, for clothing, or for any kind of lost thing, about which one says, ‘This is mine,’ the cause of both parties shall come before God. He whom God condemns shall pay double to his neighbor.

Arminian

Every disputed claim of ownership, for an animal, clothing, or anything lost, goes “before God,” and the party God condemns repays double. How the verdict was reached is not stated; suggestions include an oath, a sacred lot, or the judges’ ruling. Arminian readers stress that the text leaves the procedure open and that the law treats false claims seriously as an offense against both God and neighbor.

Reformed

Disputed property claims come before God, and the guilty party (whether a false accuser or a dishonest keeper) repays double. Reformed readers see an equal penalty for false claims and for theft, which deters lying in court. The verse leaves open how the verdict was given, and Reformed commentators, like others, differ on whether judges or a sacred procedure are in view.

Exodus 22:10

10“If a man delivers to his neighbor a donkey, an ox, a sheep, or any animal to keep, and it dies or is injured, or driven away, no man seeing it;

Arminian

A man who keeps a neighbor’s animal may lose it to death, injury, or raiders when no one else is watching. The law addresses the hard case where no witness can settle what happened. Arminian readers note the law’s realism about uncertainty in human affairs.

Reformed

The case concerns animals held in trust that die, are hurt, or are driven off with no witness. Reformed readers note the law’s care to provide a just way forward when evidence fails, a mark of the wisdom of Israel’s judicial laws.

Exodus 22:11

11the oath of the LORD shall be between them both, he has not put his hand on his neighbor’s goods; and its owner shall accept it, and he shall not make restitution.

Arminian

With no witness, the keeper swears an oath by the LORD that he did not take the animal, and the owner must accept it without restitution. Arminian readers see the oath as a solemn appeal to God, who knows the truth, and a check against false accusation. The oath binds the conscience of the one who swears it.

Reformed

The “oath of the LORD” settles the dispute, and the owner accepts it. Reformed readers see a lawful oath as an act of worship that calls God to witness, which is why it ends the matter. Reformed confessions treat lawful oaths as permitted to believers on proper occasions, and this verse is one of the scriptural grounds for that.

Exodus 22:12

12But if it is stolen from him, the one who stole shall make restitution to its owner.

Arminian

If the animal is stolen while in the keeper’s care, restitution is owed to the owner. The WEB reads “the one who stole shall make restitution,” while many translations take the Hebrew to mean that the keeper himself makes it good, since theft from his care suggests a lapse in watching it. Either way, Arminian readers see the law distinguishing loss through theft from unavoidable misfortune.

Reformed

Restitution follows theft from the keeper’s care. The WEB names “the one who stole” as the payer; many translations read the keeper as the subject, liable because he should have guarded the animal. Reformed readers, on either reading, draw the equity of diligent and honest stewardship over what is entrusted to us.

Exodus 22:13

13If it is torn in pieces, let him bring it for evidence. He shall not make good that which was torn.

Arminian

If a wild animal tore the animal apart, the keeper brings the remains as evidence and owes nothing (compare Amos 3:12, where a shepherd rescues remnants from a lion). Arminian readers note that the law seeks evidence and fairness, not suspicion, and does not hold anyone liable for what he could not prevent.

Reformed

Physical evidence clears the keeper when a predator kills the animal. Reformed readers see the law guarding the innocent from unjust liability. Jacob’s complaint to Laban (Gen 31:39) shows how unfair it was when this standard was ignored.

Exodus 22:14

14“If a man borrows anything of his neighbor’s, and it is injured, or dies, its owner not being with it, he shall surely make restitution.

Arminian

A borrower whose borrowed animal is injured or dies while the owner is absent must make it good. He had the use and benefit of the animal, so he bears the risk. Arminian readers see a fair allocation of responsibility based on who controlled and benefited.

Reformed

The borrower who has an animal in his sole care bears full liability for its loss. Reformed readers see the law’s equity in fair dealing: one who takes the benefit takes the responsibility. Both traditions read this as plain civil wisdom rather than a doctrinal statement.

Exodus 22:15

15If its owner is with it, he shall not make it good. If it is a leased thing, it came for its lease.

Arminian

If the owner was present, the borrower is not liable, since the owner could have protected his own animal. A hired animal’s loss is covered by its rental fee. Arminian readers see the section close with balanced rules that keep neighbors from exploiting one another.

Reformed

The owner’s presence removes the borrower’s liability, and a rental fee covers the risk of a hired animal. Reformed readers note that the whole section aims at restored relationships and honest dealing, a pattern of justice that the moral law still requires though the specific statutes belonged to Israel.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
