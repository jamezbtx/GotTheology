Passage

Exodus 22

Verses 16–31 · WEB

The second half of Exodus 22 widens from property to covenant loyalty and mercy. It treats seduction and the bride-price, then sets three capital laws against sorcery, bestiality, and sacrifice to other gods. Then come the protection of the alien, the widow, and the fatherless, whose cry God promises to hear, and the bans on charging interest to the poor and on keeping a poor man’s cloak overnight. The chapter ends with reverence for God and rulers, prompt offerings of firstfruits and firstborn, and a call to holiness that forbids eating torn flesh. Israel’s laws for its own society rest on the character of a holy and gracious God.

Exodus 22:16

16“If a man entices a virgin who isn’t pledged to be married, and lies with her, he shall surely pay a dowry for her to be his wife.

Arminian

A man who seduces an unbetrothed virgin must pay the bride-price (mohar) and marry her. In Israel’s setting the law protects the young woman’s standing and future: he cannot use her and walk away. Arminian readers see the law holding a man responsible for his choices and limiting their harm to her, while noting that the statute addresses an ancient family economy, not modern ideals of marriage.

Reformed

The seducer must pay the bride-price and take her as his wife, so that the woman and her family are not left disgraced and unprovided for. Reformed readers read this as a judicial law for Israel’s society that expresses the seventh commandment and protects the vulnerable. Its equity still teaches that sexual wrong carries real obligations and must not be treated lightly.

Exodus 22:17

17If her father utterly refuses to give her to him, he shall pay money according to the dowry of virgins.

Arminian

The father may refuse the marriage, and the man still pays the full bride-price of a virgin. The father’s veto guards his daughter from a forced union with an unsuitable man, and the payment secures her future. Arminian readers note the law addresses a patriarchal setting while restraining its abuses (compare Deut 22:28–29).

Reformed

The father’s right to refuse shows the marriage is not automatic; the woman’s welfare and the family’s judgment come first. The seducer pays regardless. Reformed readers see protection for the weaker party within Israel’s family structure, read by its equity rather than as a pattern for modern marriage law.

Exodus 22:18

18“You shall not allow a sorceress to live.

Arminian

“You shall not allow a sorceress to live” sets a capital penalty for sorcery, which in Israel meant seeking power or knowledge from sources other than the LORD and threatened its covenant loyalty (Deut 18:10–12). Wesleyan and other classical Arminian readers hold that such penalties belonged to Israel’s theocratic civil law. Mainstream Arminian traditions do not apply them to modern states or the church. The church now answers spiritual sin with the word and church discipline (1 Cor 5:12–13), while still rejecting occult practice.

Reformed

The death penalty for sorcery protected the covenant nation’s exclusive loyalty to the LORD. Reformed confessions treat this as judicial law that expired with Israel’s polity except for its general equity. Most Reformed churches therefore do not apply it today, though a minority (theonomists) argue for continuing civil penalties. For the church, both traditions agree that the sword is not its tool; occult practice is met with repentance, discipline, and the gospel (Acts 19:18–19).

Exodus 22:19

19“Whoever has sex with an animal shall surely be put to death.

Arminian

Bestiality is a capital offense in Israel (compare Lev 18:23; 20:15–16), condemned as a violation of the created order. Arminian readers see the moral wrong as lasting, while the civil penalty belonged to Israel as a nation. The moral principle stands even though the penalty is not carried into the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the seventh commandment and the order of creation that sets humans apart from animals, which remains morally binding. The death penalty itself was Israel’s judicial law, and most Reformed teaching does not regard it as binding on modern states, though Reformed writers differ in how far its equity reaches.

Exodus 22:20

20“He who sacrifices to any god, except to the LORD only, shall be utterly destroyed.

Arminian

Sacrificing to any god but the LORD brings the sentence of being “utterly destroyed” (Hebrew cherem, devoted to destruction). In Israel this was treason against the covenant King and the first commandment. Arminian readers note that the church does not punish false worship with civil force; idolatry is met with the call to repent and turn to the living God (1 Thess 1:9).

Reformed

The first commandment stands behind this sentence: worship belongs to the LORD alone. Reformed readers see the moral principle as permanent while the civil sanction belonged to Israel’s theocracy. Earlier Reformed teaching gave magistrates a role in suppressing idolatry; many Reformed churches later revised their confessions to limit that role, an internal development worth noting honestly.

Exodus 22:21

21“You shall not wrong an alien or oppress him, for you were aliens in the land of Egypt.

Arminian

Israel must not wrong or oppress the alien (ger), the foreign resident without land or clan protection, because they themselves were aliens in Egypt. Arminian readers see memory of redemption shaping ethics: those who received mercy must show mercy. The command rests on what God did for them, not on what the alien deserves.

Reformed

The motive clause grounds the law in Israel’s history of deliverance: you know what oppression feels like. Reformed readers see grace preceding obligation, a pattern that runs through the law. Both traditions find the moral principle lasting and fulfilled in the call to love the stranger (Lev 19:34; Heb 13:2).

Exodus 22:22

22“You shall not take advantage of any widow or fatherless child.

Arminian

Widows and the fatherless had no adult male to defend them in court or work the land, so they were easy to exploit. God forbids taking advantage of them. Arminian readers see God’s special concern for the vulnerable as a defining mark of true religion (James 1:27).

Reformed

The law singles out the widow and the orphan as people God himself protects. Reformed readers see in this the moral law of love for neighbor, applied first to those with the least power. James 1:27 makes care for them a mark of pure religion.

Exodus 22:23

23If you take advantage of them at all, and they cry at all to me, I will surely hear their cry;

Arminian

If they are mistreated and cry to God, he will surely hear. The emphatic Hebrew construction stresses the certainty of God’s response. Arminian readers see a personal God who responds to the prayers of the oppressed and takes up their cause.

Reformed

God promises to hear the cry of the oppressed widow and orphan. Reformed readers see the LORD as the defender of the helpless, whose justice backs every protective law. Both traditions read the verse as a comfort to the afflicted and a warning to oppressors.

Exodus 22:24

24and my wrath will grow hot, and I will kill you with the sword; and your wives shall be widows, and your children fatherless.

Arminian

God’s wrath will burn against the oppressor, and the punishment fits the crime: his own wife will be a widow and his children fatherless. Arminian readers see God’s justice as personal and moral. Those who exploit the weak face the Judge himself, not only human courts.

Reformed

The penalty mirrors the sin: those who make widows and orphans suffer will leave their own families as widows and orphans. Reformed readers see divine justice upholding the law where human courts fail. The warning shows that God sees oppression even when no court acts.

Exodus 22:25

25“If you lend money to any of my people with you who is poor, you shall not be to him as a creditor. You shall not charge him interest.

Arminian

Lending to a poor fellow Israelite is to be an act of help, not a business deal: no creditor’s pressure and no interest. Arminian readers see the law guarding neighbors from exploitation in hardship. Christian traditions have long debated how this applies to commercial lending; the core is generosity toward those in need (Luke 6:35).

Reformed

The ban on interest concerns loans to the poor among God’s people, where profit would deepen their distress. Reformed writers from the Reformation onward have generally distinguished exploitative lending to the needy from ordinary commercial interest, and applied the general equity of this law: do not profit from a neighbor’s poverty.

Exodus 22:26

26If you take your neighbor’s garment as collateral, you shall restore it to him before the sun goes down,

Arminian

A garment taken as collateral must be returned before sunset. The pledge secured a loan, but the lender must not use it to deprive the borrower of what he needs. Arminian readers see mercy shaping even lawful commercial dealings.

Reformed

Even lawful security on a loan has limits: the cloak must come back by nightfall. Reformed readers see the law putting human need above a creditor’s rights. Its equity still shapes Christian conduct in business and debt.

Exodus 22:27

27for that is his only covering, it is his garment for his skin. What would he sleep in? It will happen, when he cries to me, that I will hear, for I am gracious.

Arminian

The cloak was the poor man’s only covering at night. If he cries to God, God will hear, “for I am gracious.” Arminian readers see this as the law reflecting God’s own character: his grace is the reason for Israel’s mercy, and his ear is open to the poor.

Reformed

The law appeals to God’s own nature: “I am gracious.” Reformed readers see God’s compassion as both the model and the motive for his people’s laws. The same God who hears the oppressed in verse 23 hears the poor man shivering at night.

Exodus 22:28

28“You shall not blaspheme God, nor curse a ruler of your people.

Arminian

Israel must not blaspheme God or curse a ruler of the people. Some translations render ’elohim here as “judges,” but the WEB reads “God,” and Paul cites the second clause in Acts 23:5. Arminian readers see reverence for God and respect for lawful authority held together; criticizing rulers honestly differs from cursing them.

Reformed

Reverence for God and respect for rulers belong together, since rulers hold authority under God (Rom 13:1). Paul’s apology in Acts 23:5 shows the second clause still guiding Christian speech. Reformed readers see an application of the third and fifth commandments; the WEB renders ’elohim as “God,” though some versions read “judges.”

Exodus 22:29

29“You shall not delay to offer from your harvest and from the outflow of your presses. “You shall give the firstborn of your sons to me.

Arminian

Israel must offer promptly from harvest and from the presses, and give the firstborn of their sons to the LORD. Firstborn sons were redeemed, not sacrificed (Exod 13:13; 34:20), a reminder that the LORD spared Israel’s firstborn at the Passover. Arminian readers see grateful worship: the first and best belong to God, and delay shows a divided heart.

Reformed

Firstfruits and firstborn acknowledge that everything belongs to the LORD. Firstborn sons were redeemed by payment (Exod 13:13; 34:20), pointing back to the Passover. Reformed readers classify these as ceremonial laws fulfilled in Christ, while their principle of giving God the first and best remains.

Exodus 22:30

30You shall do likewise with your cattle and with your sheep. It shall be with its mother seven days, then on the eighth day you shall give it to me.

Arminian

Firstborn cattle and sheep stay with their mother seven days and are given to God on the eighth (compare Lev 22:27). Arminian readers note the care for the animal in this timing. The sacrificial system belonged to the ceremonial law fulfilled in Christ, while gratitude to God remains.

Reformed

Firstborn animals stay seven days with the mother and are offered on the eighth, a timing that recalls the eighth day of circumcision (Gen 17:12). Reformed readers see a ceremonial ordinance now fulfilled in Christ, the firstborn over all creation (Col 1:15), with its lesson of devotion still standing.

Exodus 22:31

31“You shall be holy men to me, therefore you shall not eat any meat that is torn by animals in the field. You shall cast it to the dogs.

Arminian

Israel is to be a holy people, set apart to God, so they must not eat meat torn by wild animals, which had not been properly drained of blood (Lev 17:15). It goes to the dogs. Arminian readers see holiness reaching into daily life, while recognizing the food laws as ceremonial, set aside in Christ (Mark 7:19; Acts 10:15). The call to holiness remains (1 Pet 1:15–16).

Reformed

“You shall be holy men to me” explains the food law: Israel’s separation in diet marked them as the LORD’s. Reformed readers see this as ceremonial law fulfilled in Christ, who declared all foods clean (Mark 7:19). The call to a holy life set apart to God remains for the church (1 Pet 1:15–16).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
