Passage

James 4

Verses 11–17 · WEB

James applies humility to speech about neighbors and plans for tomorrow. Slander usurps God’s authority, while boastful merchants forget that their lives and activities depend on the Lord. Both traditions hear a call to obey known duties rather than claim control over other people or the future.

James 4:11

11Don’t speak against one another, brothers. He who speaks against a brother and judges his brother, speaks against the law and judges the law. But if you judge the law, you are not a doer of the law but a judge.

Arminian

Speaking against a brother puts the speaker above the law rather than under it. Arminian readers see slander as violating the love owed to a neighbor. Both traditions distinguish this proud condemnation from truthful correction offered in humility.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a warning against claiming authority over the law while refusing to obey it. Malicious judgment contradicts brotherly love. James does not excuse wrongdoing; he forbids using another’s faults to exalt oneself as judge.

James 4:12

12Only one is the lawgiver, who is able to save and to destroy. But who are you to judge another?

Arminian

God alone holds ultimate authority to give law, save, and destroy. Arminian readers see this as a check on presumptuous condemnation of neighbors. Accountability to God should foster humility rather than license to decide another person’s final standing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divine sovereignty exposing the arrogance of assuming God’s judgment seat. Human discernment remains necessary, but final judgment belongs to God. Both traditions reject speech that treats a neighbor as subject to our ultimate verdict.

James 4:13

13Come now, you who say, “Today or tomorrow let’s go into this city and spend a year there, trade, and make a profit.”

Arminian

James addresses plans for travel, trade, and profit that assume control of the future. Arminian readers see a warning against self-sufficient planning, not against commerce itself. Responsible intentions must remain open to God’s direction and the needs he reveals.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ordinary business plans becoming presumptuous when God is left out of account. Work and foresight are not condemned. Both traditions affirm planning that acknowledges dependence rather than treating continued life and success as guaranteed.

James 4:14

14Yet you don’t know what your life will be like tomorrow. For what is your life? For you are a vapor that appears for a little time and then vanishes away.

Arminian

Life is uncertain and brief, like a vapor. Arminian readers see human limits as a reason for humility and faithful action today. Neither profitable plans nor sincere intentions give anyone mastery over tomorrow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fragility of creaturely life exposing the folly of self-sufficiency. God sustains the life people so easily take for granted. Both traditions understand the image as a summons to dependence, not an excuse for fatalism.

James 4:15

15For you ought to say, “If the Lord wills, we will both live, and do this or that.”

Arminian

Planning should acknowledge that both continued life and action depend on the Lord. Arminian readers affirm providence while generally understanding God’s rule as allowing genuine creaturely choices. The point is humble dependence, not a formula that guarantees success.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly understand both life and planned action as encompassed by God’s sovereign decree. This does not cancel meaningful human planning or responsibility. James calls for a posture of dependence, not merely adding religious words to unchanged boasting.

Key contrast Both affirm providence and responsible planning. Classical Arminian readers generally allow libertarian creaturely choices within God’s governance; Reformed readers generally understand those choices as encompassed by God’s decree. James’s immediate call to humble dependence is common ground.

James 4:16

16But now you glory in your boasting. All such boasting is evil.

Arminian

Boasting about tomorrow turns plans into claims of self-sufficient power. Arminian readers see such arrogance as morally wrong, not simply unwise. Faithful planning gives thanks for present opportunities without claiming ownership of an unknown future.

Reformed

Reformed readers see boasting as denying in practice the dependence creatures owe their Maker. Success does not justify arrogant confidence. Both traditions reject presumption while encouraging diligent work entrusted to God’s care.

James 4:17

17To him therefore who knows to do good and doesn’t do it, to him it is sin.

Arminian

Knowing the good creates responsibility to do it. Arminian readers see omission as real sin: acknowledging dependence on God must lead to changed conduct. The principle reaches beyond avoiding obvious wrongdoing to acting faithfully on what one knows.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize sins of omission as well as commission. Knowledge of duty cannot substitute for obedience and increases accountability when ignored. In context, the planners must act on James’s correction rather than merely agree with it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
