Passage

James 4

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Following his contrast between earthly and heavenly wisdom, James traces quarrels to disordered desires and divided loyalty. His sharp rebuke of friendship with the world gives way to the promise of greater grace and a summons to repentance. Both traditions see humility before God as the alternative to selfish ambition, not a way of earning mercy.

James 4:1

1Where do wars and fightings among you come from? Don’t they come from your pleasures that war in your members?

Arminian

James traces communal conflict to desires fighting within people. Arminian readers see a call to examine and resist disordered pleasures rather than blame others alone. Both traditions connect outward quarrels with inward loyalties needing God’s grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see remaining sinful desires disrupting the community. James looks beneath the quarrel to the heart rather than treating conflict as merely a problem of circumstances. Repentance must address motives as well as conduct.

James 4:2

2You lust, and don’t have. You murder and covet, and can’t obtain. You fight and make war. You don’t have, because you don’t ask.

Arminian

Coveting breeds destructive rivalry, while failure to ask exposes neglected dependence on God. Arminian readers take the charge of murder seriously without assuming a specific unreported incident. Both traditions see prayer as incompatible with grasping at others’ expense.

Reformed

Reformed readers see frustrated desire escalating into grave wrongdoing. James’s reference to murder must not be softened into harmless irritation, though the precise situation is not supplied. Prayer expresses dependence rather than entitlement or self-sufficiency.

James 4:3

3You ask, and don’t receive, because you ask with wrong motives, so that you may spend it on your pleasures.

Arminian

Prayer aimed at financing selfish pleasure is wrongly directed. Arminian readers see motives as important to faithful asking; God is not obligated to satisfy disordered desires. Unanswered requests here call for examination rather than a technique for getting more.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God refusing to become an instrument of sinful ambition. Asking is not a guarantee that every desire will be fulfilled. Both traditions understand faithful prayer as seeking God’s good purposes rather than recruiting him to serve self-indulgence.

James 4:4

4You adulterers and adulteresses, don’t you know that friendship with the world is hostility toward God? Whoever therefore wants to be a friend of the world makes himself an enemy of God.

Arminian

The language of adultery portrays divided loyalty to God. Arminian readers see a serious warning to the community against choosing the world’s rebellious values. Friendship with the world here means allegiance opposed to God, not kindness toward unbelieving neighbors.

Reformed

Reformed readers see worldliness as a betrayal of loyalty owed to God. The warning calls professing believers to examine what governs their desires. Both traditions distinguish loving people in the world from adopting a pattern of life hostile to God.

James 4:5

5Or do you think that the Scripture says in vain, “The Spirit who lives in us yearns jealously”?

Arminian

The WEB wording points to the Spirit’s jealous yearning against divided loyalty. Arminian readers can understand this as God’s claim on those he indwells. The verse’s difficult wording permits other interpretations and should not be made to settle a distinctively Arminian doctrine.

Reformed

Reformed readers can likewise read the WEB wording as expressing God’s jealous claim on his people. The Spirit, the nature of the yearning, and the scriptural reference are interpreted differently by readers within both traditions. The shared emphasis remains undivided loyalty to God.

James 4:6

6But he gives more grace. Therefore it says, “God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble.”

Arminian

God answers human pride with opposition but offers greater grace to the humble. Arminian readers see repentance as a grace-enabled response, never a payment that earns mercy. The promise encourages sinners to return rather than despair at the preceding rebuke.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace as sufficient for the need James has exposed and as the source of genuine humility. God opposes self-exaltation while sustaining those who depend on him. Both traditions deny that humility makes a person independently deserving of grace.

James 4:7

7Be subject therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you.

Arminian

Submission to God and resistance to the devil belong together. Arminian readers see active, grace-enabled resistance rather than passive surrender to temptation. The promise encourages confidence in God, not reliance on one’s own spiritual strength.

Reformed

Reformed readers see submission and resistance as duties carried out through God’s enabling power. The devil is neither sovereign nor irresistible. Both traditions hear a practical command to reject evil while entrusting themselves to God’s authority.

James 4:8

8Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, you sinners. Purify your hearts, you double-minded.

Arminian

God promises nearness to those who draw near in repentance. Arminian readers emphasize a genuine response to enabling grace, expressed in cleansed conduct and an undivided heart. The invitation is not a claim that sinners initiate reconciliation without divine help.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that God’s grace awakens and sustains the repentance he commands. Drawing near is a real human action, not a substitute for God’s initiative. Clean hands and pure hearts describe outward and inward renewal together.

Key contrast Both affirm God’s prior grace and the duty to repent. Arminian readers stress a grace-enabled response that can be resisted; Reformed readers stress God’s effectual renewing work as the source of that response. The invitation itself is shared, not a denial of divine initiative by either tradition.

James 4:9

9Lament, mourn, and weep. Let your laughter be turned to mourning and your joy to gloom.

Arminian

James calls for grief over sin rather than carefree enjoyment of it. Arminian readers see sincere repentance involving changed affections as well as behavior. The command confronts complacency; it does not forbid all joy in God’s mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see godly sorrow replacing the laughter of self-satisfied worldliness. Mourning is not a work that earns pardon but an appropriate response to recognized sin. Both traditions distinguish repentance from despair and from a permanently joyless faith.

James 4:10

10Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and he will exalt you.

Arminian

Those who humble themselves before the Lord may trust him to lift them up. Arminian readers see a promise accompanying sincere, grace-enabled repentance. Exaltation is God’s gift, not a bargain for worldly status or control over its timing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God restoring the lowly through the grace that brings them to humility. The command calls for genuine action while leaving the outcome with the Lord. Both traditions contrast dependence on God with the self-promotion driving the quarrels.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
