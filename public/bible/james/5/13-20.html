Passage

James 5

Verses 13–20 · WEB

James ends with prayer: in suffering and joy, in sickness through the elders, in mutual confession, and in the example of Elijah. The letter closes with a charge to restore anyone who wanders from the truth. Both traditions share the call to prayer and pursuit; the final verses touch the real disagreement over perseverance and apostasy.

James 5:13

13Is any among you suffering? Let him pray. Is any cheerful? Let him sing praises.

Arminian

Suffering should lead to prayer; cheerfulness should lead to praise. Arminian readers see a simple pattern of bringing every circumstance into relationship with God, both in hardship and in joy.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree and note that the believer’s whole life, bad days and good, is to be lived before God. Prayer and praise are not reserved for crises or festivals. This verse has broad shared ground.

James 5:14

14Is any among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the assembly, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord;

Arminian

The sick are to call the elders, who pray and anoint with oil in the Lord’s name. Arminian readers generally see this as a practice of caring, prayerful ministry within the church. Whether the oil was medicinal, symbolic, or both is debated.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the elders’ pastoral role and the authority of “the name of the Lord,” not the oil itself, as the source of healing. Interpretations of the anointing vary widely across denominations, and this is not a point of Arminian–Reformed disagreement. Both stress that healing comes from God.

James 5:15

15and the prayer of faith will heal him who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up. If he has committed sins, he will be forgiven.

Arminian

The prayer of faith will heal the sick, the Lord will raise them up, and sins committed will be forgiven. Arminian readers see a strong encouragement to pray in faith, while recognizing that God may answer in different ways. The mention of sin does not mean all sickness is caused by sin, only that it can be.

Reformed

Reformed readers also stress that the Lord, not the prayer itself, raises the sick, and that faith-filled prayer submits to God’s will. Many note that the verse does not promise healing in every case. The link to forgiveness reminds the church to care for both body and soul.

James 5:16

16Confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed. The insistent prayer of a righteous person is powerfully effective.

Arminian

Confess sins to one another and pray for one another for healing; the earnest prayer of a righteous person is powerful. Arminian readers note that mutual confession builds honest community, and that prayer is a real means through which God works.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm that prayer is genuinely effective, while seeing its power as rooted in God’s sovereign choice to work through the prayers of his people. Mutual confession, not confession only to clergy, is the pattern. Both traditions take this verse as encouragement to pray boldly.

James 5:17

17Elijah was a man with a nature like ours, and he prayed earnestly that it might not rain, and it didn’t rain on the earth for three years and six months.

Arminian

Elijah was a man like us, yet he prayed and rain stopped for three and a half years. Arminian readers emphasize that Elijah’s ordinary humanity makes his prayer an example ordinary believers can follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Elijah was ordinary, and they note that his prayer aligned with God’s revealed word of judgment in 1 Kings. God answered according to his purposes. The common ground is that God uses the prayers of weak people.

James 5:18

18He prayed again, and the sky gave rain, and the earth produced its fruit.

Arminian

He prayed again and rain fell, and the earth produced its fruit. Arminian readers see prayer as part of how God brings both judgment and blessing, inviting believers to persevere in prayer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same pattern of God sending rain in his time, in answer to prayer, after the people turned back to him. Prayer and providence work together. This connects back to the farmer waiting for rain in verse 7.

James 5:19

19Brothers, if any among you wanders from the truth and someone turns him back,

Arminian

If any believer wanders from the truth and someone turns him back… Arminian readers note that James speaks of someone “among you,” which they take as evidence that a real believer can drift from the truth and is in genuine danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that people within the congregation can wander and that the church must pursue them. Many say that those who finally fall away were not truly regenerate, or that God uses such rescues to preserve his own. Both agree that restoring the wanderer is a holy duty.

Key contrast Both traditions take the wanderer as part of the church and both call for pursuit. Classical Arminians typically read this as evidence that a genuine believer can depart from the faith; Reformed readers typically hold that God preserves the regenerate, so either the wanderer was not truly converted or the restoration is God’s means of preserving him.

James 5:20

20let him know that he who turns a sinner from the error of his way will save a soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins.

Arminian

Whoever turns a sinner back will save a soul from death and cover many sins. Arminian readers often take “death” as eternal death and see the verse as showing that a wandering believer is in real danger of being lost, which is why restoration is urgent. Covering sins refers to forgiveness through repentance.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see eternal stakes, but understand rescue of the wanderer as one of the means God uses to keep his elect, so the warning and the rescue both serve perseverance. Some readers take “death” as physical death as discipline. Whether the soul saved and the sins covered belong to the wanderer or the restorer is debated, though most take them as the wanderer’s.

Key contrast Both see eternal stakes and urge rescue. Arminians usually read the saving of the soul from death as rescue from a real possibility of final apostasy; Reformed readers usually read the same rescue as a means through which God secures the perseverance of his people.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
