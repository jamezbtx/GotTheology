Passage

James 5

Verses 7–12 · WEB

Turning back to the believers, James urges patience until the Lord comes, like a farmer waiting for the rain. He warns against grumbling, points to the prophets and Job as examples of endurance, and calls for simple honesty in speech. Both traditions read this as practical encouragement for suffering Christians grounded in the Lord’s return and his compassion.

James 5:7

7Be patient therefore, brothers, until the coming of the Lord. Behold, the farmer waits for the precious fruit of the earth, being patient over it, until it receives the early and late rain.

Arminian

Because the Lord is coming, believers are to be patient, like a farmer waiting for the early and late rains. Arminian readers stress that patience is an active, chosen endurance. The farmer works and waits; believers persevere in faith and good works until the Lord returns.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the farmer as one who trusts God to send rain he cannot produce himself. Patience rests on God’s faithfulness to bring the harvest in his time. Both traditions agree that the Lord’s coming is the anchor of Christian endurance under injustice.

James 5:8

8You also be patient. Establish your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is at hand.

Arminian

James repeats the call to patience and adds, “Establish your hearts.” Arminian readers hear a real responsibility to strengthen one’s own resolve and keep faith steady, since the Lord’s coming is near.

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear a genuine command, while noting that elsewhere Scripture describes God as the one who establishes his people’s hearts. Human effort and divine strengthening work together rather than compete. Both see the nearness of the Lord as motive, not cause for speculation about dates.

James 5:9

9Don’t grumble, brothers, against one another, so that you won’t be judged. Behold, the judge stands at the door.

Arminian

Do not grumble against one another, lest you be judged; the judge stands at the door. Arminian readers note that suffering can turn believers against each other, and James warns that such resentment is itself accountable before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers similarly see this as a warning to believers that the Judge is near and sees inner attitudes. Pressure from outside must not produce bitterness inside the church. Shared ground is complete here: humility before the coming Judge should silence complaint.

James 5:10

10Take, brothers, for an example of suffering and of perseverance, the prophets who spoke in the name of the Lord.

Arminian

The prophets, who spoke in the Lord’s name, are examples of suffering and patience. Arminian readers see them as models to imitate, people who chose faithfulness even when it cost them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the prophets as examples of God sustaining his servants through opposition. Their endurance shows that suffering for God’s word is normal, not a sign that God has abandoned his people. Both traditions treat the prophets as encouragement to keep going.

James 5:11

11Behold, we call them blessed who endured. You have heard of the perseverance of Job and have seen the Lord in the outcome, and how the Lord is full of compassion and mercy.

Arminian

“We call them blessed who endured.” Job’s perseverance and the Lord’s purpose in the outcome reveal that God is full of compassion and mercy. Arminian readers emphasize Job’s faithful endurance and God’s kindness in the end, which encourages believers to persevere rather than give up.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress “the outcome of the Lord,” seeing God’s good purpose working through Job’s suffering, even when it was hidden. God was compassionate throughout, not only at the end. Both agree the verse comforts sufferers by pointing to God’s merciful character.

James 5:12

12But above all things, my brothers, don’t swear—not by heaven, or by the earth, or by any other oath; but let your “yes” be “yes”, and your “no”, “no”, so that you don’t fall into hypocrisy.

Arminian

Above all, do not swear oaths; let your yes be yes and your no be no. Arminian readers, like most Christians, connect this to Jesus’ teaching and read it as a call to plain, consistent honesty rather than verbal guarantees designed to manipulate.

Reformed

Reformed readers also link this to the Sermon on the Mount. Historically, most Reformed churches have understood the command as forbidding casual or evasive oaths, not solemn oaths before lawful authority, while some other Christian groups read it as forbidding all oaths. That debate cuts across traditions. The shared point is integrity of speech.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
