Passage

James 5

Verses 1–6 · WEB

James closes his letter with a prophetic woe against wealthy oppressors who hoard riches, withhold wages, and condemn the innocent. Echoing Israel’s prophets, he announces that their decaying wealth will testify against them and that the Lord of Armies hears the cries of the exploited. Arminian and Reformed readers share most of this ground: God defends the oppressed and judges unjust wealth.

James 5:1

1Come now, you rich, weep and howl for your miseries that are coming on you.

Arminian

James turns abruptly to the wealthy with prophetic language: weep and howl. Arminian readers hear the tone of Israel’s prophets, a warning meant to provoke repentance while there is still time. Coming miseries are real, but the summons to grieve implies that a different response is still possible.

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear the prophetic voice of judgment against oppressive wealth. Many note that James may be addressing rich outsiders who exploit the church rather than believers, which makes this more a declaration of God’s justice for the oppressed than a call to the congregation. Both readings take the coming judgment seriously.

James 5:2

2Your riches are corrupted and your garments are moth-eaten.

Arminian

Riches rot and clothing is moth-eaten. Arminian readers see James exposing the illusion of security in possessions. What the rich trust in is already decaying, so the warning urges them to stop placing hope in what cannot last.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same transience and connect it to Jesus’ teaching about treasure that moth and rust destroy. Wealth hoarded rather than used for good is already under the sentence of decay. The verse displays the vanity of anything that competes with God for trust.

James 5:3

3Your gold and your silver are corroded, and their corrosion will be for a testimony against you and will eat your flesh like fire. You have laid up your treasure in the last days.

Arminian

Corrosion becomes a witness against the hoarders and will consume them like fire. They stored treasure “in the last days,” when the end was near. Arminian readers stress responsibility: they had the means to do good and chose to hoard, and that choice will testify at judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the courtroom image: the rust itself is evidence. The “last days” have already begun with Christ’s coming, so hoarding is not only selfish but blind to the age they live in. Both traditions agree that how people use wealth reveals the heart and will be judged.

James 5:4

4Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you have kept back by fraud, cry out; and the cries of those who reaped have entered into the ears of the Lord of Armies.

Arminian

Withheld wages cry out, and the cries of harvesters reach the Lord of Armies. Arminian readers highlight God’s attentive justice toward the exploited. The title “Lord of Armies” assures the poor that a powerful judge hears them, and it warns employers that fraud against workers is sin against God.

Reformed

Reformed readers echo the Old Testament laws requiring prompt payment of laborers and the cry of Abel’s blood. God’s sovereign rule as Lord of Armies means no injustice escapes his notice. Shared ground is strong here: both see God as the defender of the oppressed and the judge of economic sin.

James 5:5

5You have lived in luxury on the earth, and taken your pleasure. You have nourished your hearts as in a day of slaughter.

Arminian

They lived in luxury and fattened their hearts “as in a day of slaughter,” like animals eating heedlessly before they are killed. Arminian readers see a sobering picture of self-indulgence that ignores coming judgment, a lifestyle that could have been turned toward generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the image the same way: indulgence prepares the indulgent for judgment as cattle are fattened for the slaughter. The point is not that enjoyment is evil, but that luxury gained by oppression and pursued without regard for God is self-destruction.

James 5:6

6You have condemned and you have murdered the righteous one. He doesn’t resist you.

Arminian

They condemned and murdered “the righteous one,” who does not resist. Most interpreters in both traditions take this as the innocent poor generally, crushed through courts and economic power, though some ancient and modern readers have heard an allusion to Christ. Arminian readers note the moral weight: the powerful chose violence against the defenseless.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise usually read the righteous one as the oppressed believer or innocent poor, while acknowledging possible echoes of Christ, the righteous sufferer who did not resist. The verse’s exact referent is debated within both traditions rather than between them. Either way, God sees innocent suffering and will vindicate it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
