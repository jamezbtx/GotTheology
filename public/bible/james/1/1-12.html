Passage

James 1

Verses 1–12 · WEB

James greets scattered believers and at once calls them to count trials as joy, because tested faith produces endurance and maturity. Those who lack wisdom are to ask a generous God in undivided faith, not double-mindedly. Poor and rich alike are reset by the gospel, and the one who endures testing receives the crown of life promised to those who love God. Arminian and Reformed readers share most of this ground; they differ mainly on how the promise to those who endure relates to perseverance.

James 1:1

1James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ, to the twelve tribes which are in the Dispersion: Greetings.

Arminian

James calls himself a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ, setting Jesus beside God as Lord. Arminian readers note the humility of a leader who claims no rank but service, writing to Jewish believers scattered abroad, the twelve tribes in dispersion, as the people of God facing life away from home.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same high Christology in a plain greeting: James serves God and the Lord Jesus Christ together. The twelve tribes language presents the church as the renewed Israel of God, scattered yet one people under the Messiah.

James 1:2

2Count it all joy, my brothers, when you fall into various temptations,

Arminian

Count it all joy in various trials. Arminian readers stress that joy here is a chosen outlook grounded in what God is doing, not a denial of pain. Believers are called to respond to hardship in faith rather than resentment.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear joy rooted in God’s purpose behind trials. The word can mean testing or temptation; here outward trials are in view. Joy is possible because hardship is not random but serves God’s good work in His people.

James 1:3

3knowing that the testing of your faith produces endurance.

Arminian

Testing faith produces endurance. Arminian readers see faith that is real and active, strengthened as it keeps trusting under pressure. Testing reveals and develops what grace has begun, and believers cooperate by holding on.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God refining genuine faith. Testing does not create faith but proves and strengthens it, producing steadfastness. The process is God’s work in those who truly believe.

James 1:4

4Let endurance have its perfect work, that you may be perfect and complete, lacking in nothing.

Arminian

Let endurance finish its work so believers become mature and complete. Arminian readers, especially in the Wesleyan stream, hear a real call to growing wholeness in love. Maturity is pursued, not assumed.

Reformed

Reformed readers read perfect and complete as maturity, not sinless perfection in this life. Endurance is allowed to finish its work as God sanctifies His people progressively, a process completed only in glory.

James 1:5

5But if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all liberally and without reproach, and it will be given to him.

Arminian

God gives wisdom to all liberally and without reproach. Arminian readers rejoice in this open invitation: anyone lacking wisdom may ask and receive. God’s generosity is not grudging or limited to a few.

Reformed

Reformed readers also delight in God’s generous giving. Wisdom here is practical skill for living faithfully in trials. God does not scold those who ask; He gives freely to His children who come to Him.

James 1:6

6But let him ask in faith, without any doubting, for he who doubts is like a wave of the sea, driven by the wind and tossed.

Arminian

Ask in faith without doubting. Arminian readers stress that faith here is wholehearted trust, not a technique for getting results. The doubter is tossed like a wave because loyalty is divided.

Reformed

Reformed readers read doubting as a divided heart that wavers between God and the world, not ordinary struggles with assurance. Faith rests on God’s character, and the wave image shows how unstable life becomes without that anchor.

James 1:7

7For that man shouldn’t think that he will receive anything from the Lord.

Arminian

Such a person should not expect to receive anything from the Lord. Arminian readers see a real warning: prayer that half-trusts God and half-trusts other things is not the prayer of faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree the warning targets divided loyalty. God is not stingy, but He does not reward double-minded prayer that treats Him as one option among many.

James 1:8

8He is a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways.

Arminian

The double-minded man is unstable in all his ways. Arminian readers note that the instability spreads from prayer into the whole life. Grace calls for an undivided heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers see double-mindedness as a heart not wholly given to God. The theme returns in James 4:8, where the double-minded are called to purify their hearts.

James 1:9

9Let the brother in humble circumstances glory in his high position;

Arminian

The lowly brother should boast in his exaltation. Arminian readers see the gospel reversing social status: the poor believer has a high place in God’s kingdom. Worth comes from belonging to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same reversal. The poor believer’s exaltation is his standing in Christ and his share in the kingdom (compare 2:5). His dignity rests on God’s grace, not his circumstances.

James 1:10

10and the rich, in that he is made humble, because like the flower in the grass, he will pass away.

Arminian

The rich should boast in being made low, since they will fade like a flower. Arminian readers hear a call to humility: earthly wealth is fragile and gives no standing before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note debate over whether the rich here are believers called to humility or outsiders warned of judgment. Either way, wealth is passing, and the only lasting boast is in the Lord.

James 1:11

11For the sun arises with the scorching wind and withers the grass; and the flower in it falls, and the beauty of its appearance perishes. So the rich man will also fade away in his pursuits.

Arminian

The scorching sun withers grass and flower; so the rich man fades in his pursuits. Arminian readers see a vivid picture of how quickly wealth and status vanish, echoing Isaiah 40.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear Isaiah 40:6–8 behind the image: all flesh is grass, but the word of the Lord endures. Earthly glory fades, so believers anchor their hope in what God has promised.

James 1:12

12Blessed is a person who endures temptation, for when he has been approved, he will receive the crown of life which the Lord promised to those who love him.

Arminian

Blessed is the one who endures testing; he will receive the crown of life promised to those who love God. Arminian readers stress that the promise is tied to continuing to love God and endure. Perseverance is a real call, and believers who keep faith receive the crown.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the crown as God’s promised reward to all who truly love Him. Endurance is the mark of genuine faith, and God Himself preserves His people so that they will endure.

Key contrast Both affirm that those who endure receive the crown of life. Arminian readers usually stress perseverance as a responsibility believers can neglect. Reformed readers usually stress perseverance as the sure fruit of God’s preserving grace in true believers.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
