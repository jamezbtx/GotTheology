Passage

James 1

Verses 13–18 · WEB

James turns from outward trials to inner temptation. God tempts no one; sin grows from human desire and ends in death. By contrast, every good gift comes from the unchanging Father of lights, who by His own will gave believers new birth through the word of truth. Both traditions agree God is never the author of sin; they differ on how God’s will relates to human response in the new birth.

James 1:13

13Let no man say when he is tempted, “I am tempted by God,” for God can’t be tempted by evil, and he himself tempts no one.

Arminian

No one should say God is tempting them, because God cannot be tempted by evil and tempts no one. Arminian readers see this as a strong statement that God is never the source of sin. Human choices, not God’s design, explain moral failure.

Reformed

Reformed readers fully agree that God is not the author of sin and tempts no one. Classic Reformed confessions affirm God’s sovereign providence while denying that He causes or approves evil. James guards against blaming God for our own desires.

Key contrast Both deny that God tempts anyone to sin. Arminian readers often see this as a reason to reject any divine decree that renders sin certain. Reformed readers hold together God’s comprehensive providence and His freedom from authoring sin, often admitting mystery.

James 1:14

14But each one is tempted when he is drawn away by his own lust and enticed.

Arminian

Each person is tempted when drawn away by his own desire. Arminian readers stress personal responsibility: temptation works through our own desires, and we are accountable for following them.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the source of temptation is internal desire. Sinful desire drags and entices, and the fault lies with the human heart, not God.

James 1:15

15Then the lust, when it has conceived, bears sin. The sin, when it is full grown, produces death.

Arminian

Desire conceives sin, and full-grown sin brings death. Arminian readers see a sobering progression that believers must resist early. The warning carries real stakes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the deadly life cycle of sin: desire gives birth to sin, and sin produces death. The image contrasts with God giving new birth in verse 18.

James 1:16

16Don’t be deceived, my beloved brothers.

Arminian

Do not be deceived, beloved brothers. Arminian readers see James warning his readers not to misplace blame or underestimate sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers read this as a hinge: do not be deceived about God’s character. He is not the source of evil but the giver of every good gift.

James 1:17

17Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights, with whom can be no variation nor turning shadow.

Arminian

Every good and perfect gift comes from the Father of lights, who does not change. Arminian readers rejoice in God’s steady goodness. All good things, including grace that enables faith, come from Him.

Reformed

Reformed readers delight in God’s unchanging nature. He is the source of every good, including the new life described next. His goodness does not shift like shadows.

James 1:18

18Of his own will he gave birth to us by the word of truth, that we should be a kind of first fruits of his creatures.

Arminian

Of his own will God gave us birth through the word of truth. Arminian readers see God’s gracious initiative in new birth through the gospel. They usually hold that this new birth comes as people respond in faith to the word, enabled by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here God’s sovereign will in regeneration. New birth comes by His decision and power through the word, making believers first fruits of a renewed creation.

Key contrast Both affirm that new birth comes from God’s will through the word. Arminian readers usually understand regeneration as received through grace-enabled faith. Reformed readers usually understand it as God’s sovereign act that produces faith.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
