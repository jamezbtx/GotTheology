Passage

James 2

Verses 14–26 · WEB

James asks whether faith without works can save. Using examples of needy believers, demons who believe, Abraham offering Isaac, and Rahab protecting the spies, he shows that faith without works is dead. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that no one is saved by meritorious works and that real faith produces obedience. They largely agree that James and Paul address different questions, though they nuance justification and its relation to faith differently. James is not opposing Paul; he opposes a dead, merely verbal faith.

James 2:14

14What good is it, my brothers, if a man says he has faith, but has no works? Can faith save him?

Arminian

What good is it if someone says he has faith but has no works? Arminian readers stress the word says: James critiques a claim to faith that produces nothing. Such faith cannot save.

Reformed

Reformed readers also stress says. The question is about professed faith that lacks the fruit of real faith. Dead faith cannot save because it is not the trust that unites to Christ.

Key contrast Both deny that a fruitless profession saves. Arminian readers often emphasize that faith must remain living and active. Reformed readers often emphasize that true faith always produces works and that its absence shows faith was never genuine.

James 2:15

15And if a brother or sister is naked and in lack of daily food,

Arminian

A brother or sister lacks clothing and daily food. Arminian readers see a concrete test of faith in a neighbor’s need.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same scenario. Faith that ignores the needy fails the basic call to love.

James 2:16

16and one of you tells them, “Go in peace. Be warmed and filled;” yet you didn’t give them the things the body needs, what good is it?

Arminian

Saying “go in peace” without meeting needs is useless. Arminian readers see words without deeds as empty religion.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Empty blessing illustrates empty faith.

James 2:17

17Even so faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself.

Arminian

Faith without works is dead. Arminian readers see works as the life of true faith. Faith that stops acting dies.

Reformed

Reformed readers see works as the evidence of living faith. Faith that never produces works was never alive.

James 2:18

18Yes, a man will say, “You have faith, and I have works.” Show me your faith without works, and I will show you my faith by my works.

Arminian

Show me faith without works, and I will show faith by works. Arminian readers note the debate about where the quotation ends, but the point is clear: faith is shown through deeds.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same difficulty with punctuation. James’s point stands: genuine faith is visible in works.

James 2:19

19You believe that God is one. You do well. The demons also believe—and shudder.

Arminian

Even demons believe God is one and shudder. Arminian readers stress that mere agreement with truth is not saving faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Saving faith includes knowledge, assent, and trust; demons have the first two without trust.

James 2:20

20But do you want to know, vain man, that faith apart from works is dead?

Arminian

Do you want to know that faith apart from works is dead? Arminian readers see James pressing the point against a hollow claim. The WEB here follows manuscripts reading dead; many critical editions read useless or barren, with the same force.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same rhetorical push. Whether the word is dead or useless, faith that produces no works accomplishes nothing and is not the trust that receives Christ.

James 2:21

21Wasn’t Abraham our father justified by works, in that he offered up Isaac his son on the altar?

Arminian

Abraham was justified by works when he offered Isaac. Arminian readers see justification here as God’s vindication of Abraham’s faith through obedience. They agree no works earn salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers read justified here as demonstrated or vindicated righteousness, not the initial declaration of Genesis 15. Abraham’s works show his faith was real.

Key contrast Both deny that Abraham earned God’s acceptance. Arminian readers often describe justification by works here as God’s confirmation of a living faith. Reformed readers usually distinguish initial justification by faith from the demonstration of that faith by works.

James 2:22

22You see that faith worked with his works, and by works faith was perfected.

Arminian

Faith worked with his works, and by works faith was perfected. Arminian readers see faith and works cooperating, with obedience bringing faith to maturity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see works perfecting faith in the sense of completing and displaying it. Faith produces works; works add no merit.

James 2:23

23So the Scripture was fulfilled which says, “Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him as righteousness,” and he was called the friend of God.

Arminian

Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him as righteousness. Arminian readers see Genesis 15:6 fulfilled as Abraham’s faith proved itself in obedience, and they note the title friend of God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Genesis 15:6 comes years before the offering of Isaac in Genesis 22. Abraham was counted righteous by faith, and his later obedience fulfilled that word by showing his faith was real.

James 2:24

24You see then that by works a man is justified, and not only by faith.

Arminian

A man is justified by works, and not only by faith. Arminian readers stress that James rejects a faith that stands alone, not faith itself. No one is justified by meritorious works, and James is not contradicting Paul.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly hold that James and Paul use justified with different emphases: Paul addresses how sinners are accepted before God, while James addresses how genuine faith is shown and vindicated. Some Reformed interpreters connect James’s point to the final judgment. Both apostles reject dead faith.

Key contrast Both reject merely verbal faith and any idea of earning salvation. Arminian readers more often describe ongoing faith working through love as necessary for final salvation. Reformed readers more often distinguish justification by faith alone from works as its necessary fruit and evidence.

James 2:25

25In the same way, wasn’t Rahab the prostitute also justified by works when she received the messengers and sent them out another way?

Arminian

Rahab was justified by works in sheltering the spies. Arminian readers see her action as faith expressed in risky obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Hebrews 11:31 praising her faith. Her deeds show that her faith was real.

James 2:26

26For as the body apart from the spirit is dead, even so faith apart from works is dead.

Arminian

As the body without the spirit is dead, faith without works is dead. Arminian readers see faith requiring active obedience to remain alive.

Reformed

Reformed readers see works as the visible life of faith. A body without breath is a corpse; faith without works is no faith.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
