Passage

James 2

Verses 1–13 · WEB

James confronts favoritism in the assembly. Treating the rich man with honor and the poor man with contempt contradicts faith in the glorious Lord Jesus. God has chosen the poor to be rich in faith, and the royal law of love exposes partiality as sin. The law is a unity, and believers should speak and act as those judged by the law of freedom, for mercy triumphs over judgment. Both traditions share James’s ethic; they differ mainly in how they read God’s choice of the poor.

James 2:1

1My brothers, don’t hold the faith of our glorious Lord Jesus Christ with partiality.

Arminian

Do not hold the faith of our glorious Lord Jesus Christ with partiality. Arminian readers see favoritism as incompatible with faith in Christ. The glorious Lord welcomes all, so His people must too.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same contradiction. Faith in Christ’s glory relativizes all earthly status, so partiality denies the gospel.

James 2:2

2For if a man with a gold ring, in fine clothing, comes into your synagogue, and a poor man in filthy clothing also comes in,

Arminian

A rich man with a gold ring and a poor man in filthy clothes enter the assembly. Arminian readers see a concrete scene that tests whether faith shapes practice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the word synagogue for the gathering, likely reflecting Jewish-Christian congregations. The scene exposes how easily appearances shape treatment.

James 2:3

3and you pay special attention to him who wears the fine clothing and say, “Sit here in a good place;” and you tell the poor man, “Stand there,” or “Sit by my footstool”

Arminian

Honoring the fine clothing and sidelining the poor man. Arminian readers see a familiar sin of social favoritism, real then and now.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same sin. Seating and treatment reveal what we value, often more than our confessions do.

James 2:4

4haven’t you shown partiality among yourselves, and become judges with evil thoughts?

Arminian

Such partiality makes believers judges with evil thoughts. Arminian readers stress that favoritism is not trivial but a corrupt judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree: partiality puts us in God’s place as judges, and with wrong motives.

James 2:5

5Listen, my beloved brothers. Didn’t God choose those who are poor in this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the Kingdom which he promised to those who love him?

Arminian

God chose the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom. Arminian readers often read this as God’s choice of a group: He has favored the poor, and they receive the kingdom through faith. Election here is seen as God’s choosing of the lowly who believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign election of many who are poor. God’s choice is not based on worldly status, and faith itself is His gift to those He has chosen.

Key contrast Both see God’s gracious preference for the poor and reject status as a basis for His favor. Arminian readers often read the choice as corporate, focused on a class who believe. Reformed readers often read it as God’s choice of persons, with faith as the fruit of election.

James 2:6

6But you have dishonored the poor man. Don’t the rich oppress you and personally drag you before the courts?

Arminian

You dishonored the poor, though the rich oppress you. Arminian readers see the irony: the church honors those who mistreat it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same irony. Favoring the powerful contradicts both God’s choice and the church’s own experience.

James 2:7

7Don’t they blaspheme the honorable name by which you are called?

Arminian

They blaspheme the honorable name by which you are called. Arminian readers see the name of Christ invoked over believers being dishonored by the rich.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the name may refer to Christ’s name called over believers, perhaps at baptism. Favoring blasphemers dishonors that name.

James 2:8

8However, if you fulfill the royal law according to the Scripture, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself,” you do well.

Arminian

Fulfilling the royal law, love your neighbor as yourself, is doing well. Arminian readers see love as the heart of God’s law and the measure of obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the royal law as the King’s law summarized in love. Favoritism fails that law, and love fulfills it.

James 2:9

9But if you show partiality, you commit sin, being convicted by the law as transgressors.

Arminian

Partiality is sin, and the law convicts as transgressors. Arminian readers stress that favoritism is a real violation, not a minor flaw.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. The law exposes favoritism as sin, showing the need for grace and for real change.

James 2:10

10For whoever keeps the whole law, and yet stumbles in one point, he has become guilty of all.

Arminian

Stumbling in one point makes one guilty of all. Arminian readers see the law’s unity: one cannot pick and choose commandments.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law’s unity and the seriousness of sin. One breach makes a person a lawbreaker, which drives sinners to rely on Christ rather than their record.

James 2:11

11For he who said, “Do not commit adultery,” also said, “Do not commit murder.” Now if you do not commit adultery but do murder, you have become a transgressor of the law.

Arminian

The same God said not to commit adultery and not to murder. Arminian readers see that God’s authority stands behind every command.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree: because one Lawgiver speaks every command, breaking one breaks faith with Him.

James 2:12

12So speak and so do as men who are to be judged by the law of freedom.

Arminian

Speak and act as those judged by the law of freedom. Arminian readers see accountability: believers’ lives matter at judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law of freedom as the law believers delight in through grace. Judgment according to works is affirmed, while acceptance rests on Christ.

James 2:13

13For judgment is without mercy to him who has shown no mercy. Mercy triumphs over judgment.

Arminian

Judgment is merciless to the merciless, but mercy triumphs over judgment. Arminian readers see a real warning and a hope: mercy shown reflects mercy received.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Mercy toward others is evidence of having received mercy, and God’s mercy prevails over judgment for His people.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
