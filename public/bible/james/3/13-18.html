Passage

James 3

Verses 13–18 · WEB

James tests claims to wisdom by their conduct and fruit, continuing his concern about speech and influence within the community. Bitter rivalry produces disorder, while wisdom from above produces purity, mercy, and peace. Both traditions recognize that true understanding is displayed in a gentle life rather than ambitious self-promotion.

James 3:13

13Who is wise and understanding among you? Let him show by his good conduct that his deeds are done in gentleness of wisdom.

Arminian

Wisdom must be displayed in good conduct marked by gentleness. Arminian readers see a call to practice the wisdom God gives rather than merely claim understanding. Both traditions test spiritual wisdom by character, not verbal brilliance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see true wisdom bearing visible fruit in humble deeds. Knowledge without gentleness fails James’s test. The question challenges especially those seeking influence to show the character appropriate to teaching and leadership.

James 3:14

14But if you have bitter jealousy and selfish ambition in your heart, don’t boast and don’t lie against the truth.

Arminian

Bitter jealousy and selfish ambition contradict a claim to wisdom. Arminian readers see a warning to acknowledge and repent of these motives rather than defend them. Boasting cannot turn rivalry into faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the heart’s motives exposed beneath impressive claims. Selfish ambition must not be disguised as zeal for truth. Both traditions affirm that honesty about inward sin is necessary for a healthy community.

James 3:15

15This wisdom is not that which comes down from above, but is earthly, sensual, and demonic.

Arminian

Wisdom driven by rivalry does not come from God. Arminian readers see earthly, sensual, and demonic as a forceful diagnosis of its source and direction. Believers must reject counsel that makes self-interest look spiritually wise.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom detached from God as incapable of producing the character James commends. Its demonic character appears in its opposition to God’s peace. Both traditions reject measuring wisdom by worldly success or persuasive power.

James 3:16

16For where jealousy and selfish ambition are, there is confusion and every evil deed.

Arminian

Jealousy and ambition generate disorder and harmful conduct. Arminian readers see inward attitudes taking social form, making repentance a community concern. Resisting rivalry protects neighbors as well as one’s own spiritual life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sinful desires producing recognizable fruit in the congregation. Disorder is not evidence of spiritual vitality merely because participants claim strong convictions. Both traditions call leaders to confront rivalry rather than reward it.

James 3:17

17But the wisdom that is from above is first pure, then peaceful, gentle, reasonable, full of mercy and good fruits, without partiality, and without hypocrisy.

Arminian

Wisdom from above is recognized by purity, peace, gentleness, mercy, and integrity. Arminian readers see God’s gift taking shape in responsive obedience. Being reasonable means openness to correction, not abandoning faithfulness for the sake of approval.

Reformed

Reformed readers see these qualities as the fruit of God-given wisdom, not achievements that purchase his favor. Purity and peace belong together. Both traditions resist a false choice between firm conviction and merciful, impartial treatment of others.

James 3:18

18Now the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace.

Arminian

Peacemakers sow toward a harvest of righteousness. Arminian readers see grace-enabled action shaping the community’s life over time. Peace is not passive avoidance of conflict but a way of pursuing what is right without selfish rivalry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see righteousness flourishing through the peaceful conduct God’s wisdom produces. The harvest image encourages patient faithfulness rather than coercive ambition. Both traditions recognize peacemaking as a practical expression of heavenly wisdom.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
