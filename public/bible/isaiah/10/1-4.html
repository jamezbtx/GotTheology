Passage

Isaiah 10

Verses 1–4 · WEB · Voices

The last in a series of woes targets officials who write unjust laws to rob widows, orphans, and the poor. When the day of punishment comes, their wealth will offer no refuge. The chapter’s refrain returns: God’s anger has not turned away, and his hand is still stretched out.

Isaiah 10:1

1Woe to those who decree unrighteous decrees, and to the writers who write oppressive decrees

Arminian

Woe to those who decree unrighteous decrees, and to the writers who write oppressive decrees. Wesley: magistrates who make unjust laws and give unjust sentences; grievous things that vex their subjects.

“Woe”—Israel’s bench. They still write it. The poor of v. 2 are this people’s poor.

Reformed

Calvin: he comes to particulars so hypocrisy cannot hide. Not two classes of scribes, but princes and magistrates whose decrees approach robbery. Those on the highest rank imagine they are exempt and not bound to give account; they will be first punished.

The governors of 9:15–16 under a first woe. Common ground: decree, iniquitous, writers, oppression.

Isaiah 10:2

2to deprive the needy of justice, and to rob the poor among my people of their rights, that widows may be their plunder, and that they may make the fatherless their prey!

Arminian

To deprive the needy of justice, and to rob the poor among my people of their rights, that widows may be their plunder, and that they may make the fatherless their prey. Wesley: judgment—from obtaining a just sentence.

A theft in a court. Widows and fatherless are still His concern (9:17).

Reformed

Calvin: keep the poor from their cause. Magistrates ought to have assisted them more, yet indulge more contemptuously in oppressing them. The Lord takes peculiar care of the poor (Ex 22:23; Deut 24:15) and will not let this pass unpunished. He is their protector (Ps 68:5). The powerful are warned: no avenger now seen, still He will avenge.

The poor are his, and the day of reckoning is his. On this both agree: needy, justice, poor of my people, widows, fatherless.

Isaiah 10:3

3What will you do in the day of visitation, and in the desolation which will come from afar? To whom will you flee for help? Where will you leave your wealth?

Arminian

What will you do in the day of visitation, and in the desolation which will come from afar? To whom will you flee for help? Where will you leave your wealth? Wesley: from far—the Assyrians; Israelites were secure, in amity with them after weakening Judah. Glory—your wealth, to be kept safe for your use.

“What”—a question to a secure court. They still have nowhere to park the glory they wrote for themselves.

Reformed

Calvin: princes careless, intoxicated by prosperity. God has fixed a time. From afar: they had beaten neighbors and allied with a powerful nation. Visitation is judgment—He visits in mercy and in judgment. In vain they rely on resources. Judges appointed to defend the poor: they will have judgment without mercy who showed no mercy (Jas 2:13). Where deposit your glory: where find a hiding-place to preserve it? If princes fall, what of the lowest? No one has reason to flatter himself.

The day is fixed. Held in common: day of punishment, ruin from afar, flee for help, wealth.

Isaiah 10:4

4They will only bow down under the prisoners, and will fall under the slain. For all this his anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still.

Arminian

They will only bow down under the prisoners, and will fall under the slain. For all this his anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still. Wesley: without me—without my favour and help, which you have forfeited. Shall bow down—notwithstanding all your succours.

The refrain of 9:12, 17, 21 comes again: his hand is stretched out still. Arminian readers note that the help they lost was help they had refused, and the outstretched hand remains a threat and an invitation at once.

Reformed

Calvin: without me they shall fall—they have revolted from God. The cause of all distresses is to forsake the fountain of life. Ironical: they vaunt of being forsaken as if nothing were more pleasant than distance from Him. Or: no hiding-place but among the bound and the slain. Wrath not yet apparent; more frightful punishments reserved. Nothing more desirable than sincere repentance, that we may obtain pardon.

“Still”—they left the fountain; the hand is not done. Held in common: prisoners, slain, anger not turned away, hand stretched out still.

Key contrast Both confess the woe on those who write oppression, and that after the ruin from afar His hand is stretched out still (10:1, 4). Israel’s statutes and Assyria from far: the day is fixed. Arminians hear help forfeited—without His favour they bow, though they might have had it (Wesley). Reformed commentators hear revolt from the fountain of life: calamity is His absence, and more punishments remain till they are moved to sincere repentance (Calvin).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on unjust statutes, a day of visitation from afar, and a hand still stretched out, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Woe—unto those magistrates who make unjust laws, and give unjust sentences. Grievousness—unjust decrees as cause grief and vexation to their subjects. Judgment—from obtaining a just sentence. From far—from the Assyrians. The Israelites, having weakened the Jews and being in amity with the Assyrians, were secure. Glory—your wealth. Without me—without my favour and help, which you have forfeited. Shall bow down—notwithstanding all your succours.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 10:1–4)

Reformed Notable voices

“Woe to them that decree: he attacks princes and magistrates who oppressed by unjust and tyrannical decrees approaching robbery. Those elevated imagine they are exempt and not bound to give account; they will be the first that are punished. To keep back the poor from judgment: magistrates ought to have assisted them more than others. The Lord takes peculiar care of the poor and will not allow oppression inflicted on them to pass unpunished. What will you do in the day of visitation? God has fixed a time for judgment. Visitation is judgment: He visits in mercy and in judgment. From afar: He can shake heaven and earth from east to west. They will have judgment without mercy who have showed no mercy. Without me they shall fall: the cause of their destruction is that they have revolted from God. The cause of all our distresses is to forsake the fountain of life. For all this his anger is not turned away: a dreadful destruction; He will find out more frightful punishments. Nothing is more truly desirable than to be moved by a sincere feeling of repentance, that we may obtain pardon.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 10:1–4)
