Passage

Isaiah 14

Verses 28–32 · ESV · Voices

In the year that King Ahaz died came this oracle: rejoice not, O Philistia, all of you, that the rod that struck you is broken, for from the serpent’s root will come forth an adder, and its fruit will be a flying fiery serpent. The firstborn of the poor will graze, and the needy lie down in safety; but I will kill your root with famine, and your remnant it will slay. Wail, O gate; cry out, O city; melt in fear, O Philistia, all of you! For smoke comes out of the north, and there is no straggler in his ranks. What will one answer the messengers of the nation? The LORD has founded Zion, and in her the afflicted of his people find refuge.

Isaiah 14:28

28In the year that King Ahaz died came this oracle:

Arminian

Wesley: This burden—this following burdensome prophecy concerning the Philistines, who in Ahaz’s time, made an inroad into Judah, and took divers of their cities. In the year that King Ahaz died came this oracle. Shared: historic Philistia, in the year Ahaz died.

Arminian pastoral: the warning is dated. Ahaz’s death is a year in Judah’s kings, not a headline. Do not turn Philistia into a modern state. The oracle stands as a real word in that year.

Reformed

Calvin: here the fifteenth chapter ought to have begun; the Prophet enters on a new subject and passes to the Philistines, near neighbors who cherished deadly hostility against the Jews. So long as Ahaz lived, the Philistines were victorious; after his death they became still more courageous, because Hezekiah seemed to have neither shrewdness nor authority. Isaiah has not the Philistines so much in his view as the godly, whom he wishes to comfort.

Reformed pastoral: the year King Ahaz died is the Lord’s date. Shared: historic Philistia, not a twenty-first-century map. The rod and the north are still to come; they are his appointment.

Isaiah 14:29

29Rejoice not, O Philistia, all of you, that the rod that struck you is broken, for from the serpent’s root will come forth an adder, and its fruit will be a flying fiery serpent.

Arminian

Wesley: Of him—most understand this of Uzziah, who did then much mischief. But he was dead thirty-two years before this time, and therefore their joy for his death was long since past. Others understand it of Ahaz: but he was so far from smiting them, that he was smitten by them. We may understand this of the royal race of Judah, whose rod might be said to be broken, because that scepter was come into the hands of slothful princes. A serpent—from the root of David shall come Hezekiah, who, like a serpent, shall sting thee to death, as he did.

Arminian pastoral: rejoice not is a real call to historic Philistia. The adder from the serpent’s root is Wesley’s Hezekiah, a worse successor than the slothful princes, not a newspaper decoder. Shared: do not rejoice, Philistia.

Reformed

Calvin: some think that by the broken rod is meant King Ahaz, but that view is unfounded; for in all his battles with the Philistines he was vanquished. It must therefore be referred to Uzziah, and yet I would not limit it even to him, but would refer it to the whole body of the Jewish people. I differ from others who view the adder and the fiery serpent as applying to Hezekiah only. The favor began with Hezekiah, but belongs to the Jews as to one body. The Church will always rise again. The godly are hurtful to the wicked, though in themselves harmless.

Reformed pastoral: the adder from the root is the Lord’s appointment. Shared: rejoice not, O Philistia. Historic Judah and Philistia, not a millennial camp fight.

Key contrast Both hear Philistia told not to rejoice that the rod is broken, and both refuse to make Ahaz the smiter—he was smitten by them. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s royal race of Judah in slothful hands, and from David’s root Hezekiah, who like a serpent shall sting Philistia to death. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: the rod is Uzziah and the Jewish people as one body; the adder and fiery serpent are not Hezekiah only, and the Church will always rise again.

Isaiah 14:30

30And the firstborn of the poor will graze, and the needy lie down in safety; but I will kill your root with famine, and your remnant it will slay.

Arminian

Wesley: The poor—the people of the Jews, who are brought to extreme poverty. The title of first-born is given to persons eminently poor. Feed—shall have plenty of provisions. Kill—I will utterly destroy thee both root and branch. And the firstborn of the poor will graze, and the needy lie down in safety.

Arminian pastoral: Zion’s poor graze because they trust. Plenty of provisions is a real safety in the land, not a slogan. Shared: the firstborn of the poor will graze. Historic Jews and Philistia, not a modern state.

Reformed

Calvin: he calls them the first-born of the poor as being eminent for their wretchedness. The Lord will deliver them from such misery. He compares his people to sheep. Two things the Lord here promises: pastures, and safety and protection. I will kill thy root with famine: he compares the Philistines to a tree which strikes its roots so deep that we would think it cannot be rooted out. He will slay thy remnant: commonly viewed as referring to Hezekiah, but I prefer to extend it to the whole body.

Reformed pastoral: the killing of the root is the Lord’s appointment. Shared: the needy lie down in safety. Historic Philistia, not a twenty-first-century map.

Key contrast Both hear Judah’s poor grazing in safety and Philistia’s root killed with famine. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s eminently poor Jews given plenty, and the remnant slain as utter destruction root and branch under Hezekiah’s sting. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: the remnant is not Hezekiah only but the whole body, and the deep-rooted tree is dried up by the Lord’s appointment.

Isaiah 14:31

31Wail, O gate; cry out, O city; melt in fear, O Philistia, all of you! For smoke comes out of the north, and there is no straggler in his ranks.

Arminian

Wesley: Gate—the gate is put for the city. City—city is here put collectively for their cities. The north—from Chaldea. A smoak—a grievous judgment and calamity. Times—when God’s appointed time shall come, not one of all that numerous army shall desert his colours, or lag behind the rest.

Arminian pastoral: wail is a real call. Smoke from the north is Wesley’s historic Chaldean host, not a newspaper decoder. Shared: melt in fear, O Philistia, all of you. Do not copy the wailing.

Reformed

Calvin: we may understand smoke to mean fire, for the smoke appears before the fire burns. By the north we may understand the Assyrians as well as the Jews; yet I prefer to interpret it as referring to the Jews themselves. No one shall be alone on his appointed day: when God shall have determined to ruin the land of the Philistines, all will be ready for battle.

Reformed pastoral: the north-coming host is his appointment. Shared: smoke comes out of the north, and there is no straggler in his ranks. Historic army, not a millennial decoder.

Key contrast Both hear a historic army, smoke from the north, no straggler in the ranks—not a newspaper. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s Chaldea, a grievous judgment, not one of that numerous army lagging behind when God’s time comes. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: he prefers the Jews themselves as that north, though he would not argue against the Assyrians; when the Lord has determined the ruin, all are ready for battle.

Isaiah 14:32

32What will one answer the messengers of the nation? “The LORD has founded Zion, and in her the afflicted of his people find refuge.”

Arminian

Wesley: What—what shall a Jew say to the people of other nations, who shall enquire concerning the state of Zion, when not only the Philistines, but even the Jews themselves, shall fall by the hands of the same enemy? That—although Zion at present be in a very distressed condition, yet she stands upon a firm foundation, and God who first founded her, will restore her, and his poor despised people shall resort to her, as to a strong refuge.

Arminian pastoral: the afflicted find refuge because they trust. Shared: the LORD has founded Zion. Historic Zion, not a culture-war speech.

Reformed

Calvin: that the Lord hath founded Zion. The destruction of the land of the Philistines will be a signal proof of God’s compassion towards his people. The foundation is nothing else than God’s gracious adoption; that foundation does not consist of lime or stones, but of the gracious promises of eternal life. He does not mean that the hope of believers will be placed in Zion as we hope in God, but that the inhabitants of Zion shall dwell in a safe and quiet place. He calls them poor, that we may not think that we are exempted from ordinary calamities, though we are under God’s protection.

Reformed pastoral: the LORD has founded Zion; that founding is his appointment. Shared: in her the afflicted of his people find refuge. Historic Zion, not America.

Key contrast Both confess that the LORD has founded Zion, and in her the afflicted of his people find refuge—shared, not a rapture fight. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s distressed Zion still standing on a firm foundation: God who first founded her will restore her, and his poor despised people shall resort to her as to a strong refuge. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: the foundation is gracious adoption, not lime or stones; Philistia’s fall is a signal proof of his compassion, and the poor are not exempt from calamity though they are under his protection.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the oracle in the year King Ahaz died, Philistia told not to rejoice that the rod is broken, smoke out of the north, and Zion founded as the LORD’s refuge—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Isaiah 14:28–32.

Arminian Notable voices

“This burden—This following burdensome prophecy concerning the Philistines, who in Ahaz’s time, made an inroad into Judah, and took divers of their cities. Others understand it of Ahaz: but he was so far from smiting them, that he was smitten by them. We may understand this of the royal race of Judah, who had been a terrible scourge to them, whose rod might be said to be broken, because that scepter was come into the hands of slothful princes. A serpent—From the root of David shall come Hezekiah, who, like a serpent, shall sting thee to death, as he did. The poor—The people of the Jews, who are brought to extreme poverty. Feed—Shall have plenty of provisions. Kill—I will utterly destroy thee both root and branch. Gate—The gate is put for the city. The north—From Chaldea. A smoak—A grievous judgment and calamity. Times—When God’s appointed time shall come, not one of all that numerous army shall desert his colours, or lag behind the rest. What—What shall a Jew say to the people of other nations, who shall enquire concerning the state of Zion, when not only the Philistines, but even the Jews themselves, shall fall by the hands of the same enemy? That—Although Zion at present be in a very distressed condition, yet she stands upon a firm foundation, and God who first founded her, will restore her, and his poor despised people shall resort to her, as to a strong refuge.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament, Isaiah 14

Reformed Notable voices

“In the year that King Ahaz died. Here the fifteenth chapter ought to have begun; the Prophet enters on a new subject and passes to the Philistines, who, being the near neighbors of the Jews, cherished deadly hostility against them. So long as Ahaz lived, the Philistines were victorious; after his death they became still more courageous, because Hezekiah, the new king, had neither shrewdness, nor authority, nor wisdom. Isaiah has not the Philistines so much in his view as the godly, whom he wishes to comfort. Rejoice not, thou whole Philistia. Some think that by the broken rod is meant King Ahaz, but that view is unfounded; for in all his battles with the Philistines he was vanquished. It must therefore be referred to Uzziah, and yet I would not choose to limit it even to him, but would at the same time refer it to the whole body of the Jewish people. Out of the adder’s root shall spring a cockatrice. I differ from others who view the adder and the fiery serpent as applying to Hezekiah only. Though that opinion derives great plausibility from Hezekiah holding all that belonged to the Philistines as far as Gaza, yet the Prophet intended that this promise should extend farther. The favor began with Hezekiah, but belongs to the Jews as to one body. The Church will always rise again. The names of cockatrice and fiery serpent do not imply reproach; the godly are hurtful to the wicked, though in themselves harmless. The first-born of the poor shall feed: he calls them the first-born of the poor as being eminent for their wretchedness. He compares his people to sheep. Two things the Lord here promises: pastures, and safety and protection. I will kill thy root with famine: he compares the Philistines to a tree which strikes its roots so deep that we would think it cannot be rooted out. By the north we may understand the Assyrians as well as the Jews; yet I prefer to interpret it as referring to the Jews themselves. Smoke may mean fire, for the smoke appears before the fire burns. When God shall have determined to ruin the land of the Philistines, all will be ready for battle. That the Lord hath founded Zion: the destruction of the land of the Philistines will be a signal proof of God’s compassion towards his people. The foundation is nothing else than God’s gracious adoption; that foundation does not consist of lime or stones, but of the gracious promises of eternal life. He calls them poor, that we may not think that we are exempted from ordinary calamities, though we are under God’s protection.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah 14:28–32
