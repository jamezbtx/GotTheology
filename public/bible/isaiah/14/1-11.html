Passage

Isaiah 14

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

The LORD has compassion on Jacob and again chooses Israel, setting them in their own land; sojourners join them, and they possess their former captors in the LORD’s land. After rest from pain, turmoil, and hard service they take up a taunt against the king of Babylon: the oppressor has ceased, the staff of the wicked is broken; the earth is quiet and the cedars of Lebanon rejoice; Sheol stirs the shades of dead kings to greet him, and his pomp is brought down to maggots and worms.

Isaiah 14:1

1For the LORD will have compassion on Jacob and will again choose Israel, and will set them in their own land, and sojourners will join them and will attach themselves to the house of Jacob.

Arminian

Wesley: Chuse—will renew his choice of them; for he had rejected them. Compassion on Jacob; Israel set in their own land; sojourners join and attach themselves to the house of Jacob. Historic restoration after Babylon, not a twenty-first-century map.

Arminian pastoral: he had rejected them in the exile; he renews the choice. The mercy is a real word over Jacob’s return, not a newspaper and not a millennial decoder.

Reformed

Calvin: God will destroy Babylon, because he will have compassion on Israel, whom he cannot despise or reject. All things are directed so that those whom he has elected may be saved. Election is eternal; he never repents of his choice. When he chastises his people, this has the appearance of rejecting them; he is said to choose when he confirms his election.

Reformed pastoral: compassion is why Babylon falls. The“again choose” is God making adoption visible after the exile looked like casting off. Historic Israel under his hand, not a modern state.

Key contrast Both hear compassion on Jacob and a people set in their own land—historic restoration after Babylon, not a modern map. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s renewed choice: he had rejected them, and now chooses again. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: election is eternal; he never repents of his choice;“will yet choose” is God confirming what he never dropped, when chastisement had looked like rejection.

Isaiah 14:2

2And the peoples will take them and bring them to their place, and the house of Israel will possess them in the LORD’s land as male and female slaves. They will take captive those who were their captors, and rule over those who oppressed them.

Arminian

Wesley: Rule—which they literally did, after their return into their own land. But this was more eminently verified in a spiritual sense, in the days of the gospel. Peoples bring them home; captors become captives in the LORD’s land.

Arminian pastoral: the literal return is real; the gospel sense is further, not a forced end-times chart. Historic homecoming, then the gospel’s wider rule. Do not turn male and female slaves into a culture-war speech.

Reformed

Calvin: the return from Babylon was only a slight foretaste of those things accomplished by Christ. Foreign nations become willing companions and even servants. These statements must be referred to the dominion and yoke of Christ, not to a government of an outward nature, such as the Jews falsely imagine. He also hears the calling of the Gentiles, the two peoples one body.

Reformed pastoral: Israel possesses them in the LORD’s land because Christ subdues the nations by the word. Shared: captors become captives. Not a newspaper and not an earthly Jewish empire.

Key contrast Both hear the peoples bringing Israel home and captors becoming captives. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s literal rule after the return, then a more eminent gospel sense. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: Ezra was a taste; the possession is the dominion and yoke of Christ, not a government of an outward nature.

Isaiah 14:3

3When the LORD has given you rest from your pain and turmoil and the hard service with which you were made to serve,

Arminian

Wesley is silent on this verse; nearest: they return to their own land, and the taunt waits until rest is given. Pain, turmoil, hard service: the Babylonian yoke, not a modern labor slogan.

Arminian pastoral: rest is given so they can take up the song. The hard service was real. Hear it as mercy after exile, not as a decoder for another age.

Reformed

Calvin: he adds a confirmation of the former promises. By a variety of expressions he removes doubt. He expressly mentions the yoke and bondage, that they might know the Lord would take away these obstructions whenever he pleased. He also hears a further application: God can take away any hard yoke, even the bondage he names under Antichrist.

Reformed pastoral: rest from pain is the Lord’s gift, not luck. The deliverance of historic Israel is the pattern; Calvin’s further word does not move the verse off Babylon’s yoke.

Isaiah 14:4

4you will take up this taunt against the king of Babylon: “How the oppressor has ceased, the insolent fury ceased!”

Arminian

Wesley: Golden city—as they used to call themselves; which therefore he expresses here in a word of their own language. The taunt is against the king of Babylon. How the oppressor has ceased; the insolent fury ceased.

Arminian pastoral: shared: this mashal is aimed at Babylon’s king, not at a modern tyrant and not at a millennial cipher. The song is the taunt already begun against that king.

Reformed

Calvin: the term mashal denotes sayings weighty and remarkable. How throws it into astonishment and ridicule. It might be thought incredible that Babylon, so furnished, should be overturned. The ruin will become a proverb. He hears the city covetous of gold. The saying is against the king of Babylon.

Reformed pastoral: the taunt is the Lord’s gift after rest. Both camps: king of Babylon. The oppressor named here is that historic king.

Isaiah 14:5

5The LORD has broken the staff of the wicked, the scepter of rulers,

Arminian

Wesley is silent on this verse; nearest: the golden city ceased, the oppressor’s staff is done. The LORD has broken the staff of the wicked, the scepter of rulers—Babylon’s, not a modern state’s.

Arminian pastoral: the broken staff is historic judgment. The warning was a real call while the scepter still struck. Not a culture-war speech.

Reformed

Calvin: it did not happen at random or through the blind violence of fortune. The Lord hath broken the staff of the wicked and the sceptre of the rulers. No imperial power can support unjust tyranny. Ascribe it to the providence of God, not to chance.

Reformed pastoral: the LORD breaks what he allowed for a time. Wicked rule is not self-securing. Historic Babylon’s scepter, not a headline.

Isaiah 14:6

6that struck the peoples in wrath with unceasing blows, that ruled the nations in anger with unrelenting persecution.

Arminian

Wesley: Anger—with rigour and not with clemency. None—neither the Babylonians themselves, nor their confederates. Unceasing blows, unrelenting persecution: that king’s manner against the nations.

Arminian pastoral: the wrath was chosen cruelty under a historic scepter. The warning stood. Do not copy the blows; do not aim them at a living people.

Reformed

Calvin: the people had been struck with an incurable stroke; there was no limit to the violence; they had rioted with impunity in unbounded licentiousness. God will not spare tyrants, though he wink at them for a time. The same destruction awaits them as befell Babylon.

Reformed pastoral: unrelenting persecution meets a righteous Judge. The LORD who broke the staff had seen the blows. Historic nations under Babylon, not a millennial camp fight.

Isaiah 14:7

7The whole earth is at rest and quiet; they break forth into singing.

Arminian

Wesley: The earth—the subjects of that vast empire, who groaned under their cruel bondage. They break forth into singing because the oppressor has ceased.

Arminian pastoral: rest and quiet are for those who groaned. Historic subjects of that empire, not a twenty-first-century map. Singing is relief, not a slogan.

Reformed

Calvin: tyrants are hated by the whole world. When they are dead or ruined, all men break forth into joy, and express the feelings they formerly entertained and dissembled through fear. Then do their hatred and spite burst forth.

Reformed pastoral: the whole earth at rest is the Lord’s doing. Secret groans were heard. Shared: the earth sings because Babylon’s king is down.

Isaiah 14:8

8The cypresses rejoice at you, the cedars of Lebanon, saying, “Since you were laid low, no woodcutter comes up against us.”

Arminian

Wesley: The trees—which were felled for the service of her pride and luxury, but now are suffered to stand. Cypresses and cedars of Lebanon: no woodcutter comes up against us.

Arminian pastoral: Lebanon’s timber served Babylon’s pride. The trees’ joy is poetry for a real halt in the cutting, not a chart of another age.

Reformed

Calvin: even the dumb creatures, the fir-trees and the cedars, congratulate themselves. Personification: since the tyrant is dead, they will now stand gladly and at ease. The Heavenly Judge cannot endure tyrants; though unhappy men are silent under their sway, the Lord listens to their secret groans.

Reformed pastoral: no woodcutter because the LORD laid the king low. Shared: cedars of Lebanon against Babylon’s king, not a newspaper.

Isaiah 14:9

9Sheol beneath is stirred up to meet you when you come; it rouses the shades to greet you, all who were leaders of the earth; it raises from their thrones all who were kings of the nations.

Arminian

Wesley: Thrones—from their graves, which he seems to call their thrones by way of irony: the only thrones now left to them. Thrones both paved and covered with worms, instead of their former thrones, adorned with gold and precious stones. Sheol is stirred to meet the king of Babylon.

Arminian pastoral: the shades are dead kings greeting Babylon’s king. The taunt already begun is still against that monarch. Historic Sheol-poetry for a fallen king, not a millennial decoder.

Reformed

Calvin: as he had attributed gladness to the trees, so by a similar figure he attributes speech to the dead. The whole passage is ironical. At the approach of kings the people tremble; here the dead go forth to meet this tyrant with such honor as he deserves. Not only the living but the dead will rejoice at his death.

Reformed pastoral: Sheol stirred is mock ceremony for Babylon’s king. Shared: the oppressor is brought down to Sheol. Calvin and Wesley both hear the king.

Isaiah 14:10

10All of them will answer and say to you: “You too have become as weak as we! You have become like us!”

Arminian

Wesley is silent on this verse; nearest: thrones of graves and worms; the shades greet the king. “You too have become as weak as we! You have become like us!” The mighty king is leveled with dead men.

Arminian pastoral: weakness is the punch of the taunt. Historic kings of the nations say it to Babylon’s king. Not a millennial decoder and not a culture-war speech.

Reformed

Calvin: these are taunts with which the dead jeer the tyrant who has joined them. Tyrants are blinded by their greatness and do not think that they are mortal; they even make themselves to be half-gods. After death it is made known they shared the condition of all mortals. Art thou become like unto us?

Reformed pastoral: death acknowledges how small the king is. Shared: “like us” is Sheol’s greeting to the king of Babylon. Calvin’s half-gods is that king’s pride under the grave.

Isaiah 14:11

11Your pomp is brought down to Sheol, the sound of your harps; maggots are laid as a bed beneath you, and worms are your covers.

Arminian

Wesley: Thy pomp—all thy glory is buried with thee. Viols—all thy musical instruments, which were much used in Babylon, and were doubtless used in Belshazzar’s solemn feasts, at which time the city was taken; to which possibly the prophet here alludes. The worm—instead of those stately carpets upon which thou didst frequently tread.

Arminian pastoral: maggots for a bed, worms for covers: the king of Babylon’s end. Belshazzar’s feast is Wesley’s historic hook, not a newspaper. Pomp is buried with him.

Reformed

Calvin: he mentions royal pomp that the change may be more attentively considered. That pomp could not prevent him from being reduced to the same level with other men. Musical instruments: the luxuries that cause kings to forget death. The dead say jestingly: the worms serve thee for tapestry, and the worm for a coverlet. Foolish confidence of men who, intoxicated with enjoyments, forget themselves.

Reformed pastoral: pomp brought down to Sheol is the Lord’s doing. Shared: the oppressor is brought down to Sheol. Worms are the coverlet of that king.
