Isaiah 37

Verses 1–13 · ESV · Voices

When King Hezekiah heard it, he tore his clothes and covered himself with sackcloth and went into the house of the LORD; sends Eliakim, Shebna, and the elders to Isaiah; remnant; Isaiah’s word, do not be afraid; Rabshakeh returns; Sennacherib hears of Tirhakah; letter: gods of the nations. The unit stays with that historic 8th-century going into the house of the LORD—as Wesley and Calvin actually read—not a newspaper, not a millennial camp fight.

Isaiah 37:1

1As soon as King Hezekiah heard it, he tore his clothes and covered himself with sackcloth and went into the house of the LORD.

Arminian

Wesley: rent his cloaths—great men must not think it any disparagement to them, to sympathize with the injured honour of the great God. Hezekiah mourns and sends to Isaiah to pray for them. Sackcloth into the house of the LORD is still that sympathy after Rabshakeh’s wall-speech.

Arminian reading: they might yet have torn the clothes at that report and gone into the house. Historic Hezekiah, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another king to make “went into the house of the LORD” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: when Jerusalem appears to be completely ruined, Hezekiah, being bereft of earthly assistance, betakes himself to the protection of God, and thus acknowledges that there is no other remedy for heavy distresses. He immediately betook himself to the temple in the same manner as to a place of safety. He likewise adds the outward expressions of repentance, the rending of the clothes and wearing sackcloth; adversity does not fall out to us by chance, but is the method by which God arouses us to repentance. Sackcloth and ashes will be of little avail, if they be not preceded by the inward feelings of the heart.

Reformed note: the going in is still that historic house of the LORD. Calvin will not let the rending become a newspaper of later kings.

Key contrast Both keep Hezekiah’s rending and sackcloth as that historic going into the house of the LORD, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads the torn clothes as no disparagement to great men: they sympathize with the injured honour of the great God. Calvin reads the same rending as repentance: adversity is the method by which God arouses us to repentance; Hezekiah does not murmur or bring forward his good deeds; sackcloth is of little avail if not preceded by the inward feelings of the heart. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 37:2

2And he sent Eliakim, who was over the household, and Shebna the secretary, and the senior priests, covered with sackcloth, to the prophet Isaiah the son of Amoz.

Arminian

Wesley: Hezekiah mourns and sends to Isaiah to pray for them, ver. 1-5. Eliakim, Shebna, and the elders of the priests covered with sackcloth go to Isaiah after the house of the LORD in v. 1. The history is, almost in the same words contained 2 Kings 19.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have sent the household and the priests in that hour. Historic 8th-century embassy to the prophet is already named. The church is not to stretch “sent Eliakim” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: this message was not intended merely to invite Isaiah to join with him in lamentation, but to request some consolation from his doctrine. And indeed to no purpose shall prayers be poured into the air, if they do not rest on the word of God. It was therefore a proof of sincere piety in Hezekiah, that, while he was earnestly employed in prayer, he at the same time added a confirmation of his hope, that he might not yield to temptation. He follows the method appointed by God, when he wishes to hear God speaking by the mouth of the Prophet.

Reformed accent: the sending is still that historic word sought from Isaiah. Calvin will not let Eliakim and Shebna become a newspaper of later envoys.

Isaiah 37:3

3They said to him, “Thus says Hezekiah, ‘This day is a day of distress, of rebuke, and of disgrace; children have come to the point of birth, and there is no strength to bring them forth.

Arminian

Wesley: the children—we are like a poor travailing woman in great extremity, having no strength left to help herself, and to bring forth her infant into the world. We have attempted to deliver ourselves from the Assyrian yoke; and had carried on that work to some maturity, and as we thought, brought it to the birth; but now we have no might to finish. We have begun an happy reformation, and are hindered by this insolent Assyrian, from bringing it to perfection.

Arminian reading: they might yet have named the birth and the missing strength without calling the day a later chart. Historic distress, rebuke, and disgrace at Jerusalem, 8th century, are already named. The church is not to hunt another labour to make “no strength to bring them forth” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin’s sending of v. 2 still holds: the message was not intended merely to invite Isaiah to join in lamentation, but to request consolation from his doctrine. The day of distress, of rebuke, and of disgrace is still that embassy’s word after sackcloth in v. 1–2. Unbelievers are exceedingly noisy in their prayers, and yet they flee from God by despising or disregarding his promises.

Reformed note: the birth-proverb stays that historic extremity under Assyria. Calvin will not let “children have come to the point of birth” become a newspaper of later pains.

Isaiah 37:4

4It may be that the LORD your God will hear the words of the Rabshakeh, whom his master the king of Assyria has sent to mock the living God, and will rebuke the words that the LORD your God has heard; therefore lift up your prayer for the remnant that is left.’”

Arminian

Wesley: for the remnant—for Judah, which is but a remnant, now the ten tribes are gone: for Jerusalem, which is but a remnant, now the defenced cities of Judah are taken. Lift up thy prayer is still that remnant after the birth-proverb of v. 3.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have asked prayer for Judah left after the cities fell. Historic remnant under Sennacherib, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to stretch “the remnant that is left” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: this word perhaps, therefore, does not imply doubt, but is equivalent to an expression which we commonly use, if it be possible, when we venture to hope and promise to ourselves something. Hezekiah was convinced that nothing is hidden from God; he only asks whether God determines to call in question the blasphemy of this filthy dog; he wishes God to hear effectually. When he desires that prayer should be offered for the remnant of the people that was left, this circumstance was fitted powerfully to move the Lord; the nearer we appear to be to destruction, so much the more warmly and earnestly ought we to implore that he would render assistance to us.

Reformed accent: “it may be” is still that historic plea, not an open question in God. Calvin will not let the remnant become a newspaper of later leftovers.

Key contrast Both keep the remnant as that historic leftover under Assyria, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads the remnant as Judah, now the ten tribes are gone, and Jerusalem, now the defenced cities of Judah are taken. Calvin reads “perhaps” as not implying doubt—Hezekiah asks that God hear effectually the blasphemy of Rabshakeh—and the remnant as extremity fitted to move the Lord. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. Neither makes “it may be” an open-theist gap in God’s knowledge.

Isaiah 37:5

5When the servants of King Hezekiah came to Isaiah,

Arminian

Wesley’s sending still holds: Hezekiah mourns and sends to Isaiah to pray for them, ver. 1-5. The servants of King Hezekiah came to Isaiah after the remnant-plea of v. 4, as in the Kings parallel.

Arminian reading: they might yet have come to the prophet in that hour. Historic servants of Hezekiah, 8th century, are already named. The church is not to hunt another arrival to make “came to Isaiah” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: as the Prophet formerly related that the pious king had no other refuge than to consult the mouth of the Lord, so he now shews that he did not consult in vain; for he received the consolation which he desired. Instructed by this example, if we seek relief from him by pouring our cares and anxieties into the bosom of God, our hope shall never be disappointed.

Reformed note: the coming is still that historic consult of Isaiah. Calvin will not let the servants become a newspaper of later courts.

Isaiah 37:6

6Isaiah said to them, “Say to your master, ‘Thus says the LORD: Do not be afraid because of the words that you have heard, with which the young men of the king of Assyria have reviled me.

Arminian

Wesley: he comforts them, ver. 6, 7. “Do not be afraid” is still that comfort after the servants came in v. 5. The history is inserted here to explain and confirm some of the foregoing predictions.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have heard “do not be afraid” as the LORD’s word to that master. Historic word against the Assyrian young men, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to stretch “do not be afraid” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: Isaiah begins by saying that he gives the reply in the name of God. Fear not: when he bids him “not fear,” he exhorts Hezekiah to be of a courageous or, at least, a calm disposition; all who trust in God, and expect from him deliverance from their distresses, rise superior to all fears by the exercise of patience. He plainly declares that God conducts his own cause which he has undertaken to defend. By calling them servants, he presents in a stronger light the baseness of the action.

Reformed accent: the comfort is still that historic “thus says the LORD.” Calvin will not let “do not be afraid” become a newspaper of later nerves.

Isaiah 37:7

7Behold, I will put a spirit in him, so that he shall hear a rumor and return to his own land, and I will make him fall by the sword in his own land.’”

Arminian

Wesley’s comfort of v. 6 still holds: he comforts them, ver. 6, 7. The rumour and the sword in his own land are still that comfort after “do not be afraid.”

Arminian reading: they might yet have heard a rumor named and a sword in Assyria’s land, not a later chart. Historic 8th-century word against Sennacherib is already named. The church is not to hunt another spirit to make “he shall hear a rumor” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: it is a highly appropriate metaphor that there is in the hand of God a wind or whirlwind to drive Sennacherib in another direction. To compare wicked men to straw or chaff is a mode of expression frequently employed in Scripture, because God easily drives them wherever he thinks proper. Others translate it, “I will put my Spirit in him,” as if the Prophet were speaking of a secret influence of the heart; but that is a forced interpretation. This is the wind by the raising of which Sennacherib was suddenly driven away; for a report which he heard about the kings of Egypt and Ethiopia constrained him to return to his own country. He who subdued other men’s cities and kingdoms shall not be able to defend his own country, but shall be destroyed and perish in it.

Reformed note: the spirit and the rumor stay that historic driving of the tyrant. Calvin will not let the wind become a newspaper of later campaigns.

Isaiah 37:8

8The Rabshakeh returned, and found the king of Assyria fighting against Libnah, for he had heard that the king had left Lachish.

Arminian

Wesley: returned—to the king, to give him an account of the treaty; leaving behind him the army under the other commanders. Rabshakeh at Libnah, Lachish left, is still that return after Isaiah’s word in v. 6–7.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have seen the envoy go back and the army left. Historic Libnah and Lachish, 8th century, are already named. The church is not to stretch “the Rabshakeh returned” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: he now declares how Rabshakeh, without doing anything, returned to his king, not to the same place where he had left him; for he understood that he had raised the siege of Lachish, and had departed into Egypt for the purpose of attacking Libnah. Though God restrained the violence of the tyrant by a new war, in order to give some relief to the Jews, yet he did not wish to conquer the tyrant by the hand of man, but only to shew openly and, as it were, to display on a theater his unconquerable pride.

Reformed accent: the return is still that historic empty going back. Calvin will not let Libnah become a newspaper of later forts.

Key contrast Both keep Rabshakeh’s return as that historic move from Lachish toward Libnah, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads the return as to the king, to give him an account of the treaty, leaving behind him the army under the other commanders. Calvin reads it as without doing anything: God restrained the tyrant by a new war to give the Jews some relief, yet did not wish to conquer him by the hand of man, but to display his unconquerable pride. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 37:9

9Now the king heard concerning Tirhakah king of Cush, “He has set out to fight against you.” And when he heard it, he sent messengers to Hezekiah, saying,

Arminian

Wesley: Sennacherib called away against the king of Ethiopia, sends a blasphemous letter to Hezekiah, ver. 8-13. Tirhakah king of Cush is still that calling away after Libnah in v. 8.

Arminian reading: they might yet have heard Ethiopia named and still seen the messengers go to Hezekiah. Historic Tirhakah, 8th century, is already named. The church is not to hunt another Cush to make “he has set out to fight against you” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: from what follows we may conjecture the reason why the king of Assyria suddenly departed from Judea; for the kings of Egypt and Ethiopia had formed a league with each other against Sennacherib. These kings did not intend to provide for the safety of Judea at their own loss, but looked to themselves. The king of Assyria, being involved in so hazardous a war, sends messengers to Hezekiah, to induce him by terrors and threatenings to surrender. His rage and cruelty, indeed, are not abated, but are restrained so that they can do no harm.

Reformed note: Tirhakah stays that historic Ethiopian march. Calvin will not let the rumor become a newspaper of later Africa.

Isaiah 37:10

10“Thus shall you speak to Hezekiah king of Judah: ‘Do not let your God in whom you trust deceive you by promising that Jerusalem will not be given into the hand of the king of Assyria.

Arminian

Wesley’s blasphemous letter of vv. 8–13 still holds: Sennacherib called away against the king of Ethiopia, sends a blasphemous letter to Hezekiah. “Do not let your God in whom you trust deceive you” is still that letter after Tirhakah in v. 9.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have refused to call the LORD a deceiver. Historic promise that Jerusalem would not be given, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to stretch “do not let your God deceive you” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: how shocking is this blasphemy, to speak of God the Author of truth, and to accuse him of falsehood and deceit, as if he actually imposed on his people! What is left to God when his truth is taken away, for nothing is more absolutely his own? These promises, therefore, the tyrant meets by this blasphemy,—“Let not thy God deceive thee.” And thus he exalts himself against God, as if God were not sufficiently powerful to defend Jerusalem, and as if his own power were greater, not only than all the power of men, but even than the power of God himself.

Reformed accent: the charge of deceit is still that historic letter to Hezekiah. Calvin will not let “your God…deceive you” become a newspaper of later boasts.

Isaiah 37:11

11Behold, you have heard what the kings of Assyria have done to all lands, devoting them to destruction. And shall you be delivered?

Arminian

Wesley’s letter still holds: Sennacherib sends a blasphemous letter to Hezekiah. “Shall you be delivered?” after all lands devoted to destruction is still that letter after the deceit-charge of v. 10.

Arminian reading: they might yet have heard the lands named and not weighed Jerusalem with them. Historic kings of Assyria, 8th century, are already named. The church is not to hunt another destruction to make “and shall you be delivered?” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: here we ought to observe a twofold comparison; for he compares Hezekiah to other kings of Judah who preceded him, because he was inferior to them, and yet they were vanquished by the kings of Assyria; and Sennacherib, on the other hand, having obtained greater power than all the rest, is more daring and insolent. The other comparison is that of the kings of Assyria, and Sennacherib himself, with the idols of the nations; for if the idols could not protect the nations that adored them, consequently neither will the God of Israel defend the nation by which he is adored.

Reformed note: the lands devoted stay those historic Assyrian falls. Calvin will not let “shall you be delivered?” be weighed with idols, nor pasted onto a newspaper.

Isaiah 37:12

12Have the gods of the nations delivered them, the nations that my fathers destroyed, Gozan, Haran, Rezeph, and the people of Eden who were in Telassar?

Arminian

Wesley: Sennacherib called away against the king of Ethiopia, sends a blasphemous letter to Hezekiah, ver. 8-13. Gozan, Haran, Rezeph, and the people of Eden who were in Telassar are still that letter’s list after the lands of v. 11. The history is, almost in the same words contained 2 Kings 19.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have seen fallen towns and not called the LORD one more name. Historic east of Jerusalem, 8th century, is already named. The church is not to stretch “gods of the nations” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: Gozan. This place is mentioned in 2 Kings 17:6, and 18:11. We may infer that it was a town in Media; it is enough that, with regard to Jerusalem, it lay in an easterly direction. Haran is often mentioned in Scripture; it is more generally believed to have been in Mesopotamia, and this is confirmed by the journeyings of Abraham. He endeavors to prove this by examples, because he has vanquished nations which were under the protection of other gods, and draws an argument from the power of his ancestors,—“They conquered the gods of other nations, and I am far superior to my ancestors; therefore the God of Israel will not conquer me.”

Reformed accent: Gozan and Haran stay those historic eastward falls. Calvin will not let the list become a newspaper of later empires.

Isaiah 37:13

13Where is the king of Hamath, the king of Arpad, the king of the city of Sepharvaim, the king of Hena, or the king of Ivvah?’”

Arminian

Wesley’s blasphemous letter still holds: Sennacherib sends a blasphemous letter to Hezekiah, ver. 8-13. Where is the king of Hamath, Arpad, Sepharvaim, Hena, or Ivvah?—still that letter after Gozan and Telassar in v. 12, as in the Kings parallel.

Arminian close of the unit: they might yet have seen those kings gone and kept Jerusalem’s God uncompared. Historic Hamath and Sepharvaim, 8th century, are already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another Ivvah to make “where is the king” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: thus do wicked men commonly exalt themselves more and more in prosperity, so that at length they forget that they are men, and not only claim for themselves, but even think that they surpass, Divine Majesty. Setting aside all distinction between right and wrong, satisfied with the mere power of doing injury, they glory in their own crimes and those of their ancestors. If Satan taunt us with the destruction of many nations, we must attend to the difference of our condition; because, although we are liable to similar calamities, still we have assured hope of our salvation, of which they are destitute.

Reformed close: Hamath, Arpad, Sepharvaim, Hena, and Ivvah stay those historic vanished kings. Calvin will not let “where is the king” pass as one more idol-proof, nor as a later newspaper.
