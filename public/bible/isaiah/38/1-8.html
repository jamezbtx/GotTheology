Passage

Isaiah 38

Verses 1–8 · ESV · Voices

In those days Hezekiah became sick and was at the point of death; set your house in order; he weeps; the LORD heard; fifteen years; the sign of the shadow going back ten steps. The unit stays with that historic 8th-century illness, prayer, added years, and sign on the dial of Ahaz—as Wesley and Calvin actually read—not a newspaper, not a millennial camp fight.

Isaiah 38:1

1In those days Hezekiah became sick and was at the point of death. And Isaiah the prophet the son of Amoz came to him, and said to him, “Thus says the LORD: Set your house in order, for you shall die, you shall not recover.”

Arminian

Wesley: Hezekiah in his sickness receives from Isaiah a message of death, ver. 1. Those days—in the year of the Assyrian invasion. Set—make thy will, and settle the affairs of thy family and kingdom. Not live—such threatenings, though absolutely expressed, have often secret conditions.

Arminian reading: they might yet have set the house in order under that death-word, not a later chart. Historic sickness of Hezekiah, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another sickbed to make “set your house in order” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: in those days was Hezekiah sick unto death. The pious king was seized with a mortal disease and despaired of life; he suffered dreadful agony from a warning of his death, as if in a hostile manner God had thundered on his head from heaven. More probable that he was attacked after the siege had been raised; after one ship-wreck, another. Give charge concerning thy house: his chief care ought to be, not about testaments and heirs, but about promoting the salvation of those whom the Lord has committed to his charge. Thou shalt die, and shalt not live: that Hezekiah may not look around him as if he were uncertain, he is twice informed that he must die.

Reformed note: the sickness stays that historic illness of this king. Calvin will not let “set your house in order” become a newspaper of later wills.

Key contrast Both keep the sickness as that historic 8th-century illness of Hezekiah, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley dates those days to the year of the Assyrian invasion, and reads “set your house in order” as make thy will and settle the affairs of thy family and kingdom; the death-word, though absolutely expressed, has often secret conditions. Calvin thinks it more probable that the disease came after the siege had been raised: after having scarcely reached the shore from one ship-wreck, he suddenly fell into another; and the chief care in giving charge concerning the house is not testaments and heirs but the salvation of those committed to his charge. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. Neither makes the sickbed a later headline.

Isaiah 38:2

2Then Hezekiah turned his face to the wall and prayed to the LORD,

Arminian

Wesley: turned his face—as he lay in his bed. He could not retire to his closet, but he retired as well as he could, turned from the company, to converse with God. By prayer, ver. 2, 3. hath his life lengthened.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have turned from the company to that wall, not a later closet. Historic prayer of the sick king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to stretch “turned his face to the wall” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: then Hezekiah turned his face to the wall. He does not break out into rage or indignation like unbelievers, but bears this affliction patiently. He does not debate with God, as if he had already endured enough of distresses from enemies. Overwhelmed by shame and grief, as if he shunned the face of men, he summons up his energy, and turns wholly to God, so as to rely entirely upon him; in this manner he sharpens his eagerness for prayer. Yet it is a remarkable pattern of piety, that, when he has received the sentence of death, he does not cease to call upon God.

Reformed accent: the wall stays that historic turning of this king. Calvin will not let the prayer become a newspaper of later rooms.

Isaiah 38:3

3and said, “Please, O LORD, remember how I have walked before you in faithfulness and with a whole heart, and have done what is good in your sight.” And Hezekiah wept bitterly.

Arminian

Wesley: in truth—sincerely with an honest mind. I am not conscious to myself of any gross exorbitances, for which thou usest to shorten mens days. Wept—for that horror of death which is and was common to men, especially, in the times of the Old Testament, when the grace of God in Christ was not so fully manifested, as now it is: and, for the distracted condition in which the church and state were then likely to be left, through the uncertainty of the succession to the crown.

Arminian reading: they might yet have wept for that horror and that succession, not a later chart. Historic bitter weeping of Hezekiah, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another tear to make “Hezekiah wept bitterly” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: I beseech thee, Jehovah. He appears here to expostulate with God, and to remonstrate with him about his own past life, as if he were undeservedly distressed; but the case is far otherwise. He does not plead his merits against God, or remonstrate with him as if he were unjustly punished, but fortifies himself against a sore temptation, that he may not think that God is angry with him for correcting the vices and removing the corruptions which prevailed throughout the whole of his kingdom, and especially in regard to religion. Tears: not that in themselves tears procure favor, or have any power of appeasing God, but because they distinguish sincere prayers from those which are offered in a careless manner.

Reformed note: the walking and the tears stay that historic prayer. Calvin will not let the whole heart become a newspaper of later merits, nor an open-theist gap in which God learned the walk by the weeping.

Key contrast Both keep the bitter weeping as that historic prayer of Hezekiah, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads “in truth” as sincerely with an honest mind: I am not conscious to myself of any gross exorbitances for which thou usest to shorten mens days; he wept for the horror of death common in the Old Testament, when the grace of God in Christ was not so fully manifested, and for the distracted condition of church and state through the uncertainty of the succession. Calvin denies that Hezekiah expostulates or pleads merits against God: he fortifies himself against the temptation that the Lord had cast him off for correcting the vices of the kingdom; tears distinguish sincere prayers, and do not of themselves procure favor. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. Neither makes the tears an open-theist briefing of a God who had not known the prayer.

Isaiah 38:4

4Then the word of the LORD came to Isaiah:

Arminian

Wesley: by prayer, ver. 2, 3. hath his life lengthened; the sun goeth backward for a sign thereof, ver. 4-8. Court—of the king’s palace. This is noted to shew God’s great readiness to hear the prayers of his children—still that word after the weeping of v. 3, as in the Kings parallel.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have heard the word come so readily after that prayer. Historic word to Isaiah, 8th century, is already named. The church is not to stretch “the word of the LORD came to Isaiah” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: then came the word of Jehovah. Isaiah had departed, leaving the sting, as the saying is, in the wound. The glad tidings of life were not brought until, after long and severe struggles, he perceived that he was utterly ruined; the favor of God does not all at once shine on them, but is purposely delayed till they are sincerely humbled. While death was threatened against Hezekiah, still God had not decreed it, but determined in this manner to put to the test the faith of Hezekiah. We must suppose a condition to be implied in that threatening; God threatened the death of Hezekiah, because he was unwilling that Hezekiah should die.

Reformed accent: the word stays that historic return of Isaiah. Calvin will not let the coming of the word become a newspaper of later dispatches, nor a change of purpose in God.

Isaiah 38:5

5“Go and say to Hezekiah, Thus says the LORD, the God of David your father: I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears. Behold, I will add fifteen years to your life.

Arminian

Wesley: God of David—I am mindful of my promise made to David and his house, and will make it good in thy person. Court—this is noted to shew God’s great readiness to hear the prayers of his children. Fifteen years—we have not an instance of any other, who was told before-hand just how long, he should live. God has wisely kept us at uncertainties, that we may be always ready.

Arminian reading: they might yet have heard those fifteen years as that word to this king, not a later chart. Historic hearing of the prayer, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another term of years to make “I will add fifteen years” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: thus saith Jehovah the God of David thy father. From a regard to his covenant which he made with David, he is inclined to mercy. I have heard thy prayer: we must not imagine that prayers are meritorious; in giving freely what he freely promised, he adds this as the crowning excellence of his kindness. I have seen thy tears: not that in themselves tears procure favor. I add to thy days fifteen years: this might appear to be absurd, for we were created on the condition of not being able to pass, by a single moment, the limit marked out for us, as Job says, Thou hast appointed his bounds which he cannot pass; but what is said about an extended period must be understood to refer to the views of Hezekiah, who had been excluded from the hope of life, and must have justly reckoned to be gain what was afterwards added, as if he had been raised up from the grave to a second life.

Reformed note: the fifteen years stay that historic addition to this king’s days. Calvin will not let the added years become a newspaper of later calendars, nor an open-theist change of an unknown future.

Key contrast Both keep the fifteen years as that historic word to this king, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley notes we have not an instance of any other who was told beforehand just how long he should live; God has wisely kept us at uncertainties, that we may be always ready; and he is mindful of his promise made to David and his house, and will make it good in thy person. Calvin will not have the added years pass the limit marked out: Job’s bounds which he cannot pass still stand; the extended period is from the views of Hezekiah, who had been excluded from the hope of life and must reckon the addition as gain, as if raised from the grave to a second life; prayers are not meritorious; God gives freely what he freely promised. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. Neither treats the fifteen years as God changing an unknown future.

Isaiah 38:6

6I will deliver you and this city out of the hand of the king of Assyria, and will defend this city.

Arminian

Wesley’s lengthening of vv. 4–8 still holds: I will deliver thee and this city out of the hand of the king of Assyria; and I will defend this city—still that word with the fifteen years of v. 5, as in the Kings parallel. Those days remain the year of the Assyrian invasion.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have heard this city defended with that life. Historic defense of Jerusalem, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another city to make “I will defend this city” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: and I will deliver thee. Those who think that Hezekiah was sick during the time of the siege found an argument on this, that otherwise this promise would appear to be superfluous. But there is little force in that reasoning; for the Assyrian might have recruited his forces, and mustered a fresh army, at a later period. That promise is far from being superfluous, because along with life it promises protection from the enemy, against whom he would not otherwise have been secured, and may be regarded as an enlargement and increase of that blessing which the Lord promised to Hezekiah.

Reformed accent: the defense stays that historic word with the life. Calvin will not let this city become a newspaper of later sieges.

Isaiah 38:7

7“This shall be the sign to you from the LORD, that the LORD will do this thing that he has promised:

Arminian

Wesley: by prayer hath his life lengthened; the sun goeth backward for a sign thereof, ver. 4-8. The sign that the LORD will do this thing is still that word after the defense of v. 6, as in the Kings parallel.

Arminian reading: they might yet have taken that sign as the pledge of this promise, not a later chart. Historic sign to Hezekiah, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to stretch “this shall be the sign” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: and this shall be a sign to thee. The sacred history relates that Hezekiah asked a sign from the Lord, and that it was granted to him. We must not accuse him of unbelief, because his faith was weak; for we shall not find any person who ever had faith which was perfect and complete in every respect. In seeking some assistance to support his weakness, he cannot be blamed on that account; having embraced the promise made to him by the Prophet, he shews his confidence in God by seeking a remedy for distrust. Believers never rushed forward at random to ask signs, but were guided by a secret and peculiar influence of the Spirit.

Reformed note: the sign stays that historic pledge of this promise. Calvin will not let the asking become a newspaper of later tokens.

Isaiah 38:8

8Behold, I will make the shadow cast by the declining sun on the dial of Ahaz turn back ten steps.” So the sun turned back on the dial the ten steps by which it had declined.

Arminian

Wesley: the sun goeth backward for a sign thereof, ver. 4-8. Go down—in an instant: for that motion of the sun is natural for the kind of it, though miraculous for the swiftness of it; but the other would be both ways miraculous. Degrees—these degrees were lines in the dial: but whether each of these lines or degrees noted an hour, or half an hour, or a quarter of an hour, is uncertain. But the sun itself went back, and the shadow with it.

Arminian close of the unit: they might yet have seen that shadow go back ten steps on the dial of Ahaz. Historic sign, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another dial to make “the shadow…turn back ten steps” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: lo, I bring back the shadow of degrees. This sign bears a resemblance to the event itself, as all other signs generally do; for it is as if he had said, As it is in my power to change the hours of the day, and to make the sun go backwards, so it is in my power to lengthen thy life. As to the shadow not going back as many degrees as there were years added to his life, that was impossible, because there were not more than twelve degrees on the sundial; we need not give ourselves any uneasiness about the number; it is enough that there is a manifest correspondence and resemblance. On the sundial of Ahaz: here the Jews make fables according to their custom; there is no history of this, and it is entirely destitute not only of evidence but of probability; nor is there anything said here about the death of Ahaz, but about the sundial which he had made.

Reformed close: the ten steps stay that historic turning on Ahaz’s dial. Calvin will not let the shadow become a newspaper of later clocks.

Key contrast Both keep the shadow going back ten steps as that historic sign on the dial of Ahaz, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads the backward motion as both ways miraculous (the downward motion is natural for the kind of it, though miraculous for swiftness); the sun itself went back, and the shadow with it; the degrees were lines in the dial, whether hour or half-hour or quarter is uncertain. Calvin reads the sign as bearing a resemblance to the event: as it is in my power to change the hours of the day and make the sun go backwards, so it is in my power to lengthen thy life; we need not be uneasy that the shadow did not go back as many degrees as years added, there being not more than twelve degrees on the sundial; he refuses the Jewish fable that Ahaz’s death-day was shortened. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. Neither makes the dial a later headline.

For my own part, I look upon it as a more probable conjecture, that he was attacked by this disease after the siege had been raised; after having scarcely reached the shore from one ship-wreck, he suddenly fell into another.
