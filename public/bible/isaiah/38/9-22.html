Isaiah 38

Verses 9–22 · ESV · Voices

A writing of Hezekiah king of Judah, after he had been sick and had recovered: Sheol; rest of my years; like a weaver; like a lion; the LORD was ready to save me; we will sing to the stringed instruments; fig cake; what is the sign. The unit stays with that historic psalm of the recovered king, 8th century—as Wesley and Calvin actually read—not a newspaper, not a millennial camp fight.

Isaiah 38:9

9A writing of Hezekiah king of Judah, after he had been sick and had recovered from his sickness:

Arminian

Wesley: His song of praise to God, ver. 9-20. The writing of Hezekiah king of Judah, when he had been sick, and was recovered of his sickness—still that song after the recovery, not a later sheet.

Arminian reading: they might yet have written that song after that sickness, not a later chart. Historic psalm of the recovered king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another writing to make “a writing of Hezekiah” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: the writing of Hezekiah. Though sacred history gives no account of this writing, yet it deserves to be recorded; Hezekiah was unwilling to pass in silence, or to bury in forgetfulness, so remarkable a blessing; this song may be regarded as a public record. This is a very melancholy song; for it contains complaints rather than prayers; he was oppressed by so great perplexity that he was weary with groaning, and sunk in lamentations.

Reformed note: the writing stays that historic psalm of this king. Calvin will not let the recovered king’s writing become a newspaper of later memoirs.

Key contrast Both keep the writing as that historic psalm of the recovered king, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley names it his song of praise to God, ver. 9-20, the writing of Hezekiah when he had been sick and was recovered. Calvin calls it a very melancholy song, for it contains complaints rather than prayers; Hezekiah was unwilling to bury so remarkable a blessing in forgetfulness, and the song may be regarded as a public record, yet he was weary with groaning and sunk in lamentations. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. Neither makes the writing a later headline.

Isaiah 38:10

10I said, In the middle of my days I must depart; I am consigned to the gates of Sheol for the rest of my years.

Arminian

Wesley: His song of praise to God, ver. 9-20, still holds: I said in the cutting off of my days, I shall go to the gates of the grave: I am deprived of the residue of my years—still that word of Sheol and the rest of his years in the recovered king’s psalm.

Arminian reading: they might yet have named the gates of Sheol and the residue of those years, not a later chart. Historic consignment of this king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another Sheol to make “the rest of my years” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: I said in the cutting off of my days. This is a very melancholy song; he believed that an angry and offended God had broken off the course of his life; he does not merely say that his life is cut short by a violent disease, but recognises that undoubted judgment of God as the cause of the cutting off. The residue of the years: those years had been taken from him which he might have lived, if it had been the good pleasure of God.

Reformed note: Sheol and the rest of the years stay that historic cutting off of this king. Calvin will not let the gates of Sheol become a newspaper of later graves.

Isaiah 38:11

11I said, I shall not see the LORD, the LORD in the land of the living; I shall look on man no more among the inhabitants of the world.

Arminian

Wesley: His song of praise to God, ver. 9-20, still holds: I said, I shall not see the LORD, even the LORD, in the land of the living; I shall behold man no more with the inhabitants of the world—still that word after the gates of Sheol in v. 10.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have feared not to see the LORD in the land of the living, not a later chart. Historic dread of this king, 8th century, is already named. The church is not to stretch “I shall not see the LORD” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: I said, I shall not see God. The message of God’s vengeance affected him as if he had been deprived of God’s fatherly love; if he was unworthy of beholding the sun, how could he hope for what was of higher value? Not that hope was altogether effaced from his mind, but having his attention fixed on the curse of God, he cannot so soon rise to heaven. We were created and born on the express condition that we should devote ourselves to the knowledge of God.

Reformed accent: not seeing the LORD stays that historic cry of this king. Calvin will not let the land of the living become a newspaper of later countries.

Isaiah 38:12

12My dwelling is plucked up and removed from me like a shepherd’s tent; like a weaver I have rolled up my life; he cuts me off from the loom; from day to night you bring me to an end;

Arminian

Wesley: His song of praise to God, ver. 9-20, still holds: mine age is departed, and is removed from me as a shepherd’s tent: I have cut off like a weaver my life: he will cut me off with pining sickness: from day even to night wilt thou make an end of me—still that weaver and that tent in the recovered king’s psalm.

Arminian reading: they might yet have rolled up that life like a weaver, not a later chart. Historic dwelling of this king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another loom to make “like a weaver” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: my dwelling is departed. After he had dwelt at ease in a royal palace, his lot was changed, just as if a shepherd’s tent were pitched for two days in one field and afterwards removed to another. I have cut off, as a weaver, my life: I have cut this thread for myself, so that I alone am the cause of my death. And yet it is not without reason that he soon afterwards ascribes to God what he had acknowledged to have proceeded from himself; although we give to God grounds for dealing severely with us, yet he is the judge who inflicts punishment. From day even to night: in a short space of time he was brought down; he consumes men by the breath of a moment.

Reformed note: the weaver stays that historic rolling up of this king’s life. Calvin will not let the shepherd’s tent become a newspaper of later camps.

Isaiah 38:13

13I calmed myself until morning; like a lion he breaks all my bones; from day to night you bring me to an end.

Arminian

Wesley: I reckon—When I could not rest all the night even 'till morning, my thoughts were presaging that God would instantly break me to pieces, and the like thoughts followed me from morning 'till evening.

Arminian reading: they might yet have reckoned through that night like a lion, not a later chart. Historic night of this king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another lion to make “like a lion he breaks all my bones” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: I reckoned till the dawn. From this verse it may be inferred that Hezekiah labored two days at least under the disease; though he reached the dawn, still through constant tossings he was hastening to death. As a lion, so hath he broken my bones. The comparison of God to a lion ought not to be reckoned strange, though God is naturally gracious, merciful, and kind; we cannot be aware of that gentleness when we have provoked him by our crimes. Thus must the godly be sometimes terrified by the judgment of God, that they may be more powerfully excited to desire his favor.

Reformed note: the lion stays that historic breaking of this king. Calvin will not let the bones become a newspaper of later nights.

Key contrast Both keep the lion as that historic breaking of this king, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads “I reckon” as the night he could not rest even till morning: his thoughts were presaging that God would instantly break him to pieces, and the like thoughts followed from morning till evening. Calvin infers that Hezekiah labored two days at least under the disease; the lion is God, who has broken his bones, and the comparison is not strange once we have provoked him by our crimes. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. Neither makes the lion a later headline.

Isaiah 38:14

14Like a swallow or a crane I chirp; I moan like a dove. My eyes are weary with looking upward. O Lord, I am oppressed; be my pledge of safety!

Arminian

Wesley: His song of praise to God, ver. 9-20, still holds: like a crane or a swallow, so did I chatter: I did mourn as a dove: mine eyes fail with looking upward: O LORD, I am oppressed; undertake for me—still that chirp after the lion of v. 13.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have chirped like a swallow and moaned like a dove, not a later chart. Historic oppression of this king, 8th century, is already named. The church is not to stretch “be my pledge of safety” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: as a crane, or a swallow. Hezekiah cannot satisfy himself in explaining the severity of his anguish; he was reduced so low that he could not utter an articulate voice, but muttered some confused sound, like persons almost at the point of death. Still it is certain that this indistinct sound of the voice is nevertheless heard by God. My eyes were lifted up on high: although his eyes were nearly worn out with weakness, yet he did not cease to lift up his eyes to heaven. O Lord, it hath oppressed me; comfort me: he asks comfort from God, that he may not sink under the violence of disease.

Reformed accent: the swallow and the crane stay that historic chatter of this king. Calvin will not let the pledge become a newspaper of later sureties.

Isaiah 38:15

15What shall I say? For he has spoken to me, and he himself has done it. I walk slowly all my years because of the bitterness of my soul.

Arminian

Wesley: His song of praise to God, ver. 9-20, still holds: what shall I say? he hath both spoken unto me, and himself hath done it: I shall go softly all my years in the bitterness of my soul—still that word after the oppression of v. 14.

Arminian reading: they might yet have walked slowly all those years in that bitterness, not a later chart. Historic bitterness of this king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another walk to make “what shall I say” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: what shall I say? This is generally supposed to be an exclamation of joy, as if he congratulated himself on having already obtained his wish. But I think differently. Hezekiah appears to proceed in his complaints; life and death are in his hand; it is useless for me to argue or contend with him. God hath actually fulfilled what he had threatened by his word. Hezekiah enjoins silence on himself on this ground, that it is useless to contend with God. I shall walk trembling all my life: a trembling and feeble step; Hezekiah had been reduced to so great weakness that he despaired of ever afterwards recovering his former strength.

Reformed note: “what shall I say” stays that historic complaint of this king. Calvin will not let the walk become a newspaper of later steps.

Isaiah 38:16

16O Lord, by these things men live, and in all these is the life of my spirit. Oh restore me to health and make me live!

Arminian

Wesley: By these—By thy promises, and thy performance of them; therefore it is not strange, that one word of God hath brought me back from the jaws of death.

Arminian reading: they might yet have lived by those promises and that performance, not a later chart. Historic recovery of this king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another word to make “by these things men live” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: O Lord, even to all who shall live after them. I rather think that Hezekiah’s meaning was this: O Lord, whosoever shall live beyond those years, to them also will the life of my spirit be known; this favor will be acknowledged not only by the men of that age, but also by posterity. And didst cause me to sleep, and didst make me alive: he magnifies the greatness of the favor; by the word sleep he means death; he compares this mortal disease to death; it will be reckoned to be a kind of resurrection.

Reformed note: “by these things” stays that historic recovery of this king. Calvin will not let the making alive become a newspaper of later years.

Key contrast Both keep “by these things” as that historic recovery of this king, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads “by these” as by thy promises, and thy performance of them; therefore it is not strange that one word of God hath brought him back from the jaws of death. Calvin reads the same clause as the lengthened years known to those who shall live after them: this favor acknowledged not only by that age but by posterity; sleep means death, and the making alive is a kind of resurrection. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. Neither makes “by these things” a later headline.

Isaiah 38:17

17Behold, it was for my welfare that I had great bitterness; but in love you have delivered my life from the pit of destruction, for you have cast all my sins behind your back.

Arminian

Wesley: His song of praise to God, ver. 9-20, still holds: behold, for peace I had great bitterness: but thou hast in love to my soul delivered it from the pit of corruption: for thou hast cast all my sins behind thy back—still that welfare after the life of v. 16.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have had that bitterness for welfare, and those sins cast behind the back, not a later chart. Historic deliverance of this king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another pit to make “you have cast all my sins behind your back” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: lo, in peace my bitterness was bitter. The disease seized him suddenly while he enjoyed ease and quietness; I think that Hezekiah fell into this disease after the defeat of Sennacherib. Thou hast cast behind thy back all my sins: he looks at something higher than the cure of the body; he had been guilty before God, but by his grace had been forgiven; he reckons it of higher value that he has been reconciled to God than a hundred or a thousand lives. Hezekiah perceived the cause of his distress, that is, his sins; and when he had received the forgiveness of them, he knew that punishment also ceased and was remitted. Thou hast cast behind thy back: the remembrance of them is altogether effaced.

Reformed note: the sins cast behind the back stay that historic pardon of this king. Calvin will not let the pit of destruction become a newspaper of later graves.

Isaiah 38:18

18For Sheol does not thank you; death does not praise you; those who go down to the pit do not hope for your faithfulness.

Arminian

Wesley: Praise—The dead are not capable of glorifying thy name among men upon earth. They cannot expect nor receive the accomplishment of thy promised goodness in the land of the living.

Arminian reading: they might yet have named Sheol as unable to thank, not a later chart. Historic Sheol of this king’s psalm, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another pit to make “Sheol does not thank you” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: for hell shall not confess thee. He declares that the highest and most desirable advantage that life can yield to him is that he will praise God. Yet Hezekiah appears to employ language which is too exclusive; for the death of believers declares the glory of God not less than their life, and, being after death perfectly united to God, they do not cease to proclaim his praises along with the angels. The pious king, when he had been struck by God’s wrath, grieved that by his sins he had excluded himself from life; they who are ruined and lost will have no ground of thanksgiving. The saints, when they spoke in this manner, did not consider what kind of condition awaited them after death, but looked only at the end for which they were created and preserved in the world.

Reformed note: Sheol’s silence stays that historic line of the recovered king’s psalm. Calvin will not let the pit become a newspaper of later graves.

Key contrast Both keep Sheol’s silence as that historic line of the recovered king’s psalm, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads it straight: the dead are not capable of glorifying thy name among men upon earth; they cannot expect nor receive the accomplishment of thy promised goodness in the land of the living. Calvin says Hezekiah appears to employ language which is too exclusive, for the death of believers declares the glory of God not less than their life; Hezekiah spoke under grief, thinking death the punishment of sins, and did not then consider the condition after death. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. Neither makes Sheol a later headline.

Isaiah 38:19

19The living, the living, he thanks you, as I do this day; the father makes known to the children your faithfulness.

Arminian

Wesley: His song of praise to God, ver. 9-20, still holds: the living, the living, he shall praise thee, as I do this day: the father to the children shall make known thy truth—still that thanks after Sheol’s silence in v. 18.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have thanked as the living this day, and the father made known to the children, not a later chart. Historic thanks of this king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another father to make “the father makes known to the children” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: the living, the living, he shall confess thee. He does not include all men without exception; for many live who yet extinguish the glory of God by their ingratitude. But he simply declares that men, so long as God supports them in this life, may justly be regarded as the lawful heralds of his glory. As I do this day: he will be one of the witnesses of the glory of God, not only to the men of his own age, but also to posterity. The father shall make known to the sons thy truth: children are given to men on the express condition that every man, by instructing his children, shall endeavor to transmit the name of God to posterity.

Reformed accent: the living who thank stay that historic witness of this king. Calvin will not let the father and the children become a newspaper of later households.

Isaiah 38:20

20The LORD will save me, and we will play my music on stringed instruments all the days of our lives, at the house of the LORD.

Arminian

Wesley: Was ready—Was a present help. Therefore we will sing my songs to the stringed instruments all the days of our life in the house of the LORD—still that present help after the living of v. 19.

Arminian reading: they might yet have sung those songs on the stringed instruments in that house, not a later chart. Historic saving of this king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another harp to make “we will play my music on stringed instruments” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: Jehovah to save me. He acknowledges that he was delivered, not by the aid or industry of men, but solely by the kindness of God; he contrasts God’s keeping with the death to which he had been sentenced; having formerly dreaded him as a severe judge, he now avows him as his deliverer, and leaps with joy. And we will sing our songs: he not only prepares himself for singing in token of gratitude, but also calls on others to join and accompany him, and on this account mentions the Temple; much more then was the king bound to bring others to unite with him in thanksgiving. All the days of our life: not only for one day or for one year, but as long as he shall live.

Reformed note: the stringed instruments stay that historic song in the house of the LORD. Calvin will not let the saving become a newspaper of later choirs.

Isaiah 38:21

21Now Isaiah had said, “Let them take a cake of figs and apply it to the boil, that he may recover.”

Arminian

Wesley: His song of praise to God, ver. 9-20, is followed by the lump of figs as in the Kings parallel: let them take a lump of figs, and lay it for a plaister upon the boil, and he shall recover—still that cake after the stringed instruments of v. 20.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have laid that cake of figs on that boil, not a later chart. Historic plaster of this king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another boil to make “a cake of figs” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: and Isaiah said. Isaiah now relates what was the remedy which he prescribed to Hezekiah. Some think that it was not a remedy, because figs are dangerous and hurtful to boils, but that the king was taught by this sign that the cure proceeded from nothing else than from the favor of God alone. But since figs are employed even by our own physicians for maturing a pustule, it is possible that the Lord, who had given a promise, gave also a medicine; although the Lord does not need secondary means, yet he makes use of them whenever he thinks proper. And the value of the promise is not lessened by this medicine, which without the word would have been vain and useless.

Reformed close: the cake of figs stays that historic plaster on this king’s boil. Calvin will not let the figs become a newspaper of later salves.

Isaiah 38:22

22Hezekiah also had said, “What is the sign that I shall go up to the house of the LORD?”

Arminian

Wesley: Shalt go—To give me solemn praise for this mercy. Hezekiah also had said, What is the sign that I shall go up to the house of the LORD?—still that sign after the cake of figs in v. 21, as in the Kings parallel.

Arminian close of the unit: they might yet have asked what is the sign that he shall go up to that house, not a later chart. Historic question of this king, 8th century, is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another sign to make “what is the sign” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: now, Hezekiah had said. It is more probable that in this passage the order has been reversed, as frequently takes place with Hebrew writers, and that what was spoken last is related first. Isaiah did not at the beginning say that Hezekiah had asked a sign, though the sacred history attests it; and therefore he adds what he had left out at the proper place. That I shall go up: he means that it will be his chief object throughout his whole life to celebrate the name of God; he did not desire life for the sake of living at ease and enjoying pleasure, but in order to defend the honor of God and the purity of his worship.

Reformed close: the sign stays that historic question of this king. Calvin will not let “what is the sign” become a newspaper of later tokens.
