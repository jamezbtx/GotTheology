Passage

Isaiah 30

Verses 18–26 · ESV · Voices

Therefore the Lord waits to be gracious: eighth-century Judah, after the Egypt plan and the thousand fleeing, is told he will exalt himself to show mercy. A people shall dwell in Zion, in Jerusalem; the Teacher no longer hidden; bread of adversity still, then a word behind, idols defiled, rain and harvest, moon like the sun when he binds the breach. The unit stays in Isaiah’s Judah—not a newspaper of later nations.

Isaiah 30:18

18Therefore the Lord waits to be gracious to you, and therefore he exalts himself to show mercy to you. For the Lord is a God of justice; blessed are all those who wait for him.

Arminian

Wesley: wait—patiently expect your repentance. Exalted—he will work gloriously. Judgment—or mercy. That wait—in his way, with faith and patience. The Lord waiting is still mercy over the Judah that would not return in v. 15, not a later delay.

Arminian pastoral: he waits for their repentance; they might yet wait for him in his way. The blessing is named in Isaiah’s hearing. The church is not to hunt another century to make “wait to be gracious” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: therefore will Jehovah wait. The Prophet now adds consolation; he intended to soothe their minds, and encourage them to hope for better things. He contrasts this waiting with the excessive haste against which he spoke at the beginning of the chapter. The Lord will not render like for like, and will not in that manner hasten to punish them. Jehovah is a God of judgment: God exercises moderation in inflicting punishment, because he is inclined to mercy. Blessed are all that wait for him.

Reformed accent: he is not like us; he does not act with bustling or hurry, otherwise at every moment we must perish. The waiting is the Lord’s over historic Judah after the Egypt haste, not a newspaper of later delays.

Key contrast Both keep the Lord waiting to be gracious to historic Judah after the Egypt plan, not a newspaper. Wesley reads the wait as the Lord patiently expecting their repentance, judgment as mercy, the blessed as those who wait in his way with faith and patience. Calvin reads the wait as God himself refusing like-for-like haste: a God of judgment meaning moderation in chastening, not severity that would ruin those whose salvation he promotes. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 30:19

19For a people shall dwell in Zion, in Jerusalem; you shall weep no more. He will surely be gracious to you at the sound of your cry. As soon as he hears it, he answers you.

Arminian

Wesley: shall dwell—after a set time, they shall return to Jerusalem, and have a fixed abode. This was in part accomplished upon their return from Babylon; but more fully in the times of the gospel, when many of them were, and the whole body of them shall be brought into Christ's church. The people in Zion are still that Judah after the wait of v. 18.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet cry. Wesley’s Babylon return is already enough; the gospel gathering of that people is not a millennial chart and not a newspaper of later states. The church is not to stretch “Zion” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: surely the people in Zion shall dwell in Jerusalem. The people will indeed be afflicted, but will at length return to Zion. Weeping thou shalt not weep: this mourning shall not be perpetual. At the voice of thy cry: if we do not ask pardon from God, we are altogether unworthy of his mercy. He will answer thee: this means nothing else than that he will give evidence of his kindness and aid; the Lord answers, not by word, but by deed.

Reformed note: Jerusalem shall again be populous, because God had chosen it to be his sanctuary. The cry is Zion’s in that generation, not a newspaper pasted onto “weep no more.”

Isaiah 30:20

20And though the Lord give you the bread of adversity and the water of affliction, yet your Teacher will not hide himself anymore, but your eyes shall see your Teacher.

Arminian

Wesley’s heading for vv. 18–26 is still God’s mercies to his people. He does not gloss this verse. The bread of adversity sits between the cry answered in v. 19 and the shepherd-word of v. 21: mercy is promised, and the Teacher is no longer hidden from that people.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet see the Teacher. Adversity bread is still Zion’s ration in that generation. The church is not to stretch “bread of adversity” into a newspaper of later famines.

Reformed

Calvin: when the Lord shall have given you the bread of anguish and the water of affliction, then will the Lord bless you; he will change your condition for the better. The word moreh is viewed by some commentators as meaning a teacher. But this does not agree with the context; as the ignorant multitude was more deeply affected by the want of food, Isaiah gives them a taste of God's fatherly kindness under the emblem of abundance of food. By bread and water he means extreme want; instead of this famine he will send plenty. This is what he means by the word rain.

Reformed accent: this had a literal reference to a country whose fertility depended on heaven. The church reads Judah’s scarcity turned, not a later code for “Teacher.”

Isaiah 30:21

21And your ears shall hear a word behind you, saying, “This is the way, walk in it,” when you turn to the right or when you turn to the left.

Arminian

Wesley: shall hear—thou shalt hear the voice of God's word and spirit. Behind thee—a metaphor borrowed from shepherds, who use to follow their sheep, and recall them when they go out of the way. The word behind is still for Zion’s people after the Teacher is seen.

Arminian reading: they might yet walk in it. Right and left are still that generation’s turns. The church is not to rename “the way” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: then shall thine ears hear. The chief ground of gladness is when God restores pure and sound doctrine; he also promises that he would give us ears, for otherwise he would speak to the deaf. He compares God to a schoolmaster, who places the children before his eyes. By the right and left hand he means all the actions of human life, whatever they are, so that in all that we undertake we may have God for our guide, provided only that we do not turn aside from the path which he points out to us.

Reformed pastoral: they who follow God as their guide will be in no danger of going astray. The word behind is Judah’s in that hour, not a newspaper of later paths.

Key contrast Both keep the word behind as guidance for historic Judah after the hidden Teacher, not a newspaper. Wesley reads a shepherd following the sheep, recalling them by God’s word and spirit when they go out of the way. Calvin reads a schoolmaster with the children before his eyes, and takes right and left as all the actions of life, not a license to leave the path. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 30:22

22Then you will defile your carved idols overlaid with silver and your gold-plated metal images. You will scatter them as unclean things. You will say to them, “Be gone!”

Arminian

Wesley: defile—to shew your contempt of it. Covering—the leaves or plates wherewith their images were frequently covered. Ornament—it was a costly and glorious robe. The idols are still Judah’s silver and gold in that hour.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet say Be gone. Scattering the unclean is a real act in historic Zion. The church is not to hunt later statues to make “defile” true.

Reformed

Calvin: then shall you profane the covering. They will bid adieu to their errors, and devote their minds to the pure worship of God; they who are truly converted to God detest and abhor them. When he speaks of the silver and gold of the graven images, he means that no loss or damage prevents believers from abhorring the worship of idols. We ought to prefer the worship of God to everything else.

Reformed note: nothing can be so valuable that it ought not to be reckoned worthless when it comes into competition with restoring the worship of God. The church hears Judah’s Be gone, not a later headline for “idols.”

Isaiah 30:23

23And he will give rain for the seed with which you sow the ground, and bread, the produce of the ground, which will be rich and plenteous. In that day your livestock will graze in large pastures,

Arminian

Wesley: bread—which shall be the fruit of thy own land and labour: and excellent for quality, which is called, fat, and abundant for quantity. Rain and harvest are still Judah’s ground after the idols are gone.

Arminian reading: they might yet sow. Fat bread is the reverse of adversity’s loaf in v. 20. The church is not to stretch “rain for the seed” into a newspaper of later harvests.

Reformed

Calvin: then will he give rain to thy seed. The fruit of repentance is that he receives converted persons into favor, and bestows his blessing on them. It is in vain for men to toil in cultivating their fields, if the Lord do not send rain from heaven. Let us not ascribe barrenness and famine to any other causes than to our own fault; by our iniquities we shut the bosom of the earth.

Reformed accent: if his kindness overflows even on the cattle, how much more on men. The rain is over historic Judah’s seed, not a newspaper pasted onto “plenteous.”

Isaiah 30:24

24and the oxen and the donkeys that work the ground will eat seasoned fodder, which has been winnowed with shovel and fork.

Arminian

Wesley: clean provender—there should be such plenty of corn, that the very beasts, instead of straw, should eat corn; and that not in the ear, or with the straw, but the pure grain. The oxen are still those that work Judah’s ground.

Arminian pastoral: plenty for the beasts is mercy overflowing that farm. The church is not to rename “seasoned fodder” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: thine oxen also. When he promises that the oxen and the asses shall eat abundant and clean provender, this is a repetition of the preceding verse. This passage is taken from the Law, that we may learn to discern in the sickness and death of cattle the indignation of God, and to desire more earnestly to be reconciled to him. Brute beasts suffer hunger along with their masters, and have a share in the bestowal of favor when God is reconciled to men.

Reformed note: the winnowed fodder stays with that reconciled farm in Isaiah’s century, not a later cipher for “oxen.”

Isaiah 30:25

25And on every lofty mountain and every high hill there will be brooks running with water, in the day of the great slaughter, when the towers fall.

Arminian

Wesley: hill—which is commonly dry and barren. In the day—when God shall destroy the enemies of his people. The towers—the mighty potentates, who fought against God's people. Brooks on high hills are still the Lord’s gift in that slaughter day over Judah’s foes.

Arminian close of the hills: they might yet see water on the dry hill. Towers falling are potentates against that people, not a later skyline. The church is not to hunt another siege to make “great slaughter” true.

Reformed

Calvin: when the prophets describe the kingdom of Christ, they commonly draw metaphors from the ordinary life of men. On every high hill there shall be streams: water is not plentiful on the peaks of the mountains, which are exceedingly dry; yet the Lord promises that it shall be so. By the day of slaughter is denoted that God will keep his people safe against the violence of enemies. It is generally thought that he now speaks of the defeat of Sennacherib; but when I examine it more closely, I am more disposed to view this as referring to the destruction of Babylon; although a vast multitude was slain when Sennacherib was put to flight, yet still the people were not delivered.

Reformed pastoral: let us not be terrified at their power; neither their troops nor their bulwarks will hinder them from falling into the hands of God. The slaughter is still over Judah’s enemies in that history, not a newspaper of later towers.

Isaiah 30:26

26Moreover, the light of the moon will be as the light of the sun, and the light of the sun will be sevenfold, as the light of seven days, in the day when the Lord binds up the brokenness of his people, and heals the wounds inflicted by his blow.

Arminian

Wesley: sevenfold—as if the light of seven days were combined together in one. Healeth—when God shall effectually cure the wounds of his people, making Israel and Judah to be one, and making Jew and Gentile to be one fold under one shepherd. The moon like the sun is still the Lord’s binding of that people’s breach.

Arminian close of the range: Wesley’s one fold is the gospel shepherd, not a millennial chart and not a newspaper of later nations. They might yet be healed. Wesley and Calvin both keep this light as the cure of Judah’s wound; this is not an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. The church is not to stretch “sevenfold” into a later sky.

Reformed

Calvin: the light of the moon shall be as the light of the sun. The Lord will go beyond the course of nature in this kindness. It never happened that the brightness of the sun was increased, unless when the sun stood still in the days of Joshua, and when, for the sake of Hezekiah, the dial went backward; but on this occasion nothing is said about those miracles. He shews what will be the condition of the godly under the reign of Christ; he speaks of happiness in which ungodly men can have no share. When the Lord shall have bound up the breach of his people: the Lord will be like a physician to heal their wounds; the people must be crushed and bruised in such a manner as to be reduced almost to nothing.

Reformed close: if we think the Lord deals harshly with us, let us call to remembrance that he will bind up our wounds. Calvin and Wesley agree the Lord waits to be gracious and then heals the blow; this sevenfold light is already enough to wait and to return. No millennial dispute is in this comment.

Key contrast Both keep the sevenfold light as the Lord’s healing of historic Judah’s wound, not a newspaper. Wesley reads the healing as making Israel and Judah one, and Jew and Gentile one fold under one shepherd. Calvin refuses the old sun-miracles as the sense, and reads extraordinary brightness under Christ’s reign as a special favor to believers after severe bruising, which the ungodly cannot share. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.
