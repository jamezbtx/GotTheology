Passage

Isaiah 30

Verses 27–33 · ESV · Voices

Behold, the name of the Lord comes from afar: eighth-century Judah, after the wait to be gracious, is told of burning anger, thick rising smoke, a stream to the neck, a song as at a holy feast, then Asshur terror-stricken at his voice. Every stroke of the appointed staff; Topheth prepared for the king, kindled like sulfur. The unit stays with historic Assyria against Isaiah’s Judah—not a newspaper of later nations.

Isaiah 30:27

27Behold, the name of the Lord comes from afar, burning with his anger, and in thick rising smoke; his lips are full of fury, and his tongue is like a devouring fire;

Arminian

Wesley: behold—here he gives them an earnest of those greater mercies in times to come, by assuring them of the approaching destruction of the Assyrian forces. The name—the Lord himself. From far—from a remote place: even from heaven. Heavy—he will inflict heavy judgments upon them. Indignation—he hath pronounced a severe sentence against them, and will give command for the execution of it. The name from afar is still the Lord himself against historic Asshur, not a later headline.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet behold. Burning anger is the Lord’s sentence on that army, already pronounced. Wesley’s earnest is the Assyrian fall as pledge of mercies still named in Isaiah’s hearing. The church is not to hunt another empire to make “from afar” true.

Reformed

Calvin: behold the name of the Lord cometh. He threatens the destruction of the Assyrians, who were at that time the chief enemies of the Church. By the name of God he unquestionably means God himself. Ungodly men, when they do not perceive the hand of God, think that he is at a great distance, and mock at the confidence of believers as groundless. God, whom they thought to be at a great distance, will come, or rather, has already come, and is at hand. To the ungodly he holds out nothing but fear and terror. His burden is heavy: the Lord will bring with him dreadful calamities, which the ungodly will not be able to endure.

Reformed accent: they shall learn that the sound which proceedeth from the sacred name of God is not idle thunder. The coming is against historic Asshur in Isaiah’s century, not a newspaper of later smoke.

Key contrast Both keep the name coming against historic Asshur, not a newspaper. Wesley reads from far as a remote place, even from heaven: the Lord himself coming to destroy the Assyrian forces as an earnest of mercies. Calvin reads from afar as the speech of unbelievers who think God is distant; the Prophet shews he has already come and is at hand. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 30:28

28his breath is like an overflowing stream that reaches up to the neck; to sift the nations with the sieve of destruction, and to place on the jaws of the peoples a bridle that leads astray.

Arminian

Wesley: his breath—God's anger. A stream—coming from him as vehemently, as a mighty torrent of waters. To sift—to shake and scatter, as it were with a sieve. The nations—the Assyrian army, which was made up of several nations. With—not with an ordinary sieve, which casteth away the chaff only, but with a sieve, which should shake them so long and so vehemently, as to cast away altogether. A bridle—God will over-rule them by his powerful providence. To err—whereas other bridles guide into the right way, this shall turn them out of the way, by giving them up to their own foolish counsels, which shall bring them to certain ruin.

Arminian reading: they might yet see Asshur given up to his own counsel. The sieve is still that multinational host at Judah’s neck. The church is not to stretch “bridle that leads astray” into a newspaper of later peoples.

Reformed

Calvin: the Church will indeed be chastised, but yet the Assyrians shall utterly perish; they will be plunged into the deep by the Spirit of God. He will shake the Assyrians with a sieve of vanity, a useless sieve, intended not to preserve, but to destroy; for, in another sense, the Lord is wont to sift his own people also, so as to gather them like good grain into the barn. A bridle causing to err: the Lord commonly restrains his own people by a bridle in order to bring them to obedience; he restrains wicked men in such a manner as to cast them down headlong to destruction.

Reformed note: the Lord chastises both, but in different ways—the children of God, that they may be purified and preserved—and the reprobate, that they may be cast down headlong and destroyed. The stream to the neck is still Asshur’s, not a later cipher.

Key contrast Both keep the sieve and bridle on the Assyrian host, not a newspaper. Wesley reads a sieve that casts the multinational army away altogether, and a bridle that turns them out of the way by giving them up to their own foolish counsels. Calvin reads a useless sieve meant to destroy, not to preserve as when the Lord sifts his own people for the barn, and a bridle that casts the wicked headlong while the same figure brings his children to obedience. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 30:29

29You shall have a song as in the night when a holy feast is kept, and gladness of heart, as when one sets out to the sound of the flute to go to the mountain of the Lord, to the Rock of Israel.

Arminian

Wesley: a song—you shall have songs of praise. The night—he seems to have a particular respect to the solemnity of the passover, in which they spent some considerable part of the night in rejoicing, and singing psalms before the Lord. As when—like the joy of one that is going up to the solemn feasts with musick. The song is still Judah’s after Asshur is sifted, not a later anthem.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet sing. Wesley’s passover night is already enough: historic Zion going up to the mountain. The church is not to rename “holy feast” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: all the punishments which he threatened against the Assyrians shall tend to the advantage of the Church; the Lord punishes the outrages committed against his people not less severely than if they had been committed against himself. This song will be sacred, and compared to a holy solemnity, in order to excite believers to thankfulness; they shall not dance, as irreligious men do, but shall raise and fix their eyes on God. He calls it a song of the night, because the Jews began the day at sunset. By the mountain he means the temple which was built on the mountain. He calls God the Mighty One of Israel, because it was by his assistance that they had been redeemed and preserved.

Reformed pastoral: in future they will not be safe in any other way than by placing their hope in God alone. The flute and the Rock stay with that generation’s feast, not a newspaper pasted onto “gladness of heart.”

Isaiah 30:30

30And the Lord will cause his majestic voice to be heard and the descending blow of his arm to be seen, in furious anger and a flame of devouring fire, with a cloudburst and storm and hailstones.

Arminian

Wesley: his voice—his thunder, metaphorically taken for some terrible judgment. The lightning—upon the Assyrian. With—with great wrath; which is signified by heaping so many words of the same signification together. The voice and arm are still against that host, after Judah’s song.

Arminian close of the storm: they might yet see the blow. Hailstones are the Lord’s in Isaiah’s century. The church is not to hunt another cloudburst to make “majestic voice” true.

Reformed

Calvin: the destruction shall be such that men will be constrained to hear the voice of God; that is, to acknowledge his judgment, and to confess that this calamity hath proceeded from him, as if he had spoken openly. The event will be so manifest, that there shall be no one who does not understand that this calamity proceeded from the mouth, that is, from the decree of God. Whatever he has decreed he likewise executes, and his hand can never be separated from his mouth. We must not dream, as the Rabbins do, that the Assyrians were struck by a thunderbolt, for their conjecture is excessively frivolous; the Prophet, by these comparisons, describes the judgment of God.

Reformed accent: though they were heavily afflicted, a far more dreadful judgment would soon fall on their enemies. The storm is still over historic Asshur, not a later sky.

Key contrast Both keep the voice and arm against historic Asshur, not a newspaper. Wesley reads his voice as thunder, metaphorically taken for some terrible judgment, the lightning upon the Assyrian. Calvin refuses the Rabbins’ thunderbolt as the sense, and reads the voice as the decree whose work quickly follows, his hand never separated from his mouth. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 30:31

31The Assyrians will be terror-stricken at the voice of the Lord, when he strikes with his rod.

Arminian

Wesley’s heading for these verses is still the approaching destruction of the Assyrian forces. He does not gloss this verse. The Assyrians terror-stricken at the voice sit between the hailstones of v. 30 and the founded rod of v. 32: Asshur is beaten by that voice, not by Egypt’s horses.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet hear that the name from afar is enough. The rod is still the Lord’s on historic Asshur. The church is not to hunt a later empire to make “the Assyrians” true.

Reformed

Calvin: surely by the voice of Jehovah. He added this for two reasons; first, to shew why the Assyrian must be bruised; since he was cruel and savage to others, it is proper that the same measure which he meted should be measured to him again. The second reason is, because the power of the Assyrian king appeared to be so great that he could not fall. Although he was fortified on every hand, yet by the voice of God alone he shall be bruised. In order to destroy them, the Lord will have no need of any other arms than his own voice; by the slightest expression of his will he will lay them low.

Reformed note: the Prophet intends to withdraw the minds of believers from earthly means, that they may not inquire how it shall be done, but may be satisfied with the bare promise of God. Asshur falls in that history, not in a newspaper of later rods.

Isaiah 30:32

32And every stroke of the appointed staff that the Lord lays on them will be to the sound of tambourines and lyres. Battling with brandished arm, he will fight with them.

Arminian

Wesley: the rod—Hebrew. the founded rod, the judgment of God, called a founded rod, because it was firmly established, by God's immutable purpose. Him—upon the Assyrian. With harps—their destruction shall be celebrated by God's people, with joy and musick, and songs of praise. Of shaking—or, shaking of the hand, of which kind of shaking this Hebrew word is constantly used. God will fight against them, and destroy them by his own hand. With it—with the army of the Assyrians. The staff is still on that host, to Judah’s music.

Arminian reading: they might yet keep feast while the founded rod falls. Immutable purpose here is the Lord’s settled stroke on Asshur, not a later score. The church is not to rename “tambourines and lyres” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: the Assyrians will in vain try every method of escaping from the hand of God; wherever they go, whether they attempt to go forward or to turn back, the hand of God shall pursue them. That wound is so deeply fixed that it cannot be shaken off or removed. In like manner, the weight of God's wrath lies on the reprobate, and holds them weighed down to the end. With tabrets and harps: the issue of the battle will not be doubtful; as soon as God determines to go forth to fight, he already holds the victory in his hand. Tabrets and harps are expressive of the joy of conquerors, when they shout aloud and chant the song of victory.

Reformed pastoral: we, too, are chastened by the hand of God, but the wounds do not always last; our grief is turned into joy. The appointed staff stays with historic Asshur, not a later cipher for “brandished arm.”

Isaiah 30:33

33For a burning place has long been prepared; indeed, for the king it is made ready, its pyre made deep and wide, with fire and wood in abundance; the breath of the Lord, like a stream of sulfur, kindles it.

Arminian

Wesley: Tophet—this was a place near Jerusalem, in which the idolatrous Israelites used to offer up their children to Moloch. It may be put, for any place of torment; and particularly it is put for hell. For the king—for the king of Assyria. Fire—he alludes to the ancient custom, of burning sacrifices, and particularly of burning children to Moloch. The breath—the immediate hand of God, or his word of anger. Brimstone—he seems to allude to that shower of fire and brimstone, Gen. xix, 24. The burning place is still prepared for that king, not a later headline.

Arminian close of the range: they might yet fear the breath that kindles it. Wesley’s Tophet near Jerusalem for the king of Assyria is already enough; his “particularly hell” is not a millennial chart and not a newspaper of later nations. Wesley and Calvin both keep this fire as the Lord’s against Asshur’s king; this is not an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. The church is not to stretch “pyre” into a later sky.

Reformed

Calvin: for Tophet is ordained. The Prophet goes on to threaten the vengeance of God, and says that not only a temporary calamity, but also everlasting destruction awaits the wicked; for hell is prepared for them, and not merely for persons of ordinary rank, but likewise for the king himself and the nobles. By Tophet he unquestionably means Hell; not that we must fancy to ourselves some place in which the wicked are shut up, as in a prison, after their death; but it denotes their miserable condition and excruciating torments. Since yesterday: of old, since the beginning of the world, the Lord hath determined what punishments he shall inflict on them. Yea, for the king it is prepared: not even kings, who are supposed to enjoy some peculiar privilege, are exempted from this punishment. He speaks of the punishments of the life to come, in addition to the distresses which they suffer in this life.

Reformed close: let us not judge of the lot of the wicked according to outward appearances; let us wait for the Lord, who in due time will execute his righteous judgment. Calvin and Wesley agree the breath kindles a fire prepared for Asshur’s king; this Topheth is already enough without a later newspaper. No millennial dispute is in this comment.

Key contrast Both keep Topheth as the Lord’s burning against the king of historic Asshur, not a newspaper. Wesley starts from the valley near Jerusalem where children were offered to Moloch, allows it to stand for any torment and particularly hell, and names the king of Assyria; the breath is the immediate hand of God, the brimstone an allusion to Genesis 19. Calvin takes Tophet to mean Hell without fancying a prison after death, everlasting destruction not a temporary calamity only, determined from the beginning of the world, and not even kings exempt. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.
