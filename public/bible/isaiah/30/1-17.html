Passage

Isaiah 30

Verses 1–17 · ESV · Voices

Ah, stubborn children: eighth-century Judah, under Assyria’s shadow, takes counsel and goes down to Egypt without asking at the Lord’s mouth. Pharaoh’s protection turns to shame; the beasts of the Negeb carry treasure through trouble and anguish to a people that cannot profit; Egypt’s help is worthless, Rahab who sits still. The seers are told not to see, the prophets to speak smooth things; returning and rest were offered, and they would not. A thousand shall flee at the threat of one. The unit stays in Isaiah’s Judah—not a newspaper of later nations.

Isaiah 30:1

1“Ah, stubborn children,” declares the LORD, “who carry out a plan, but not mine, and who make an alliance, but not of my Spirit, that they may add sin to sin;

Arminian

Wesley: the rebellious—the Jews. Take counsel—that consult together. Cover—that seek protection. But not—not such as by my spirit, speaking in my word, I have required them to do. That they may add—that unto all their sins, they may add distrust of my power and mercy, and put confidence in an arm of flesh. The stubborn children are Judah in that hour, carrying out a plan that is not the Lord’s.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have asked at his mouth. The covering they seek is still Pharaoh’s shadow, not a later headline. The church is not to hunt another century to make “stubborn children” true.

Reformed

Calvin: the Prophet exclaims against the Jews, because, when they were hard pressed by the Assyrians, they fled to Egypt for help. God had strictly forbidden them to enter into any alliance with the Egyptians. That they may cover a covering: the words relate to counsels held secretly and by stealth, by which they cunningly endeavored to deceive the prophets. They add sin to sin: after having brought the Assyrians into Judea, they wished to drive them out by the help of the Egyptians.

Reformed accent: whatever is undertaken without the word of God must be improper and wicked. The covering is Judah’s own secret counsel in Isaiah’s century, not a newspaper pasted onto “alliance.”

Key contrast Both keep the woe on historic Judah’s Egypt plan, not a newspaper. Wesley reads “cover” as seeking protection—an arm of flesh instead of the covering God’s Spirit required. Calvin reads “cover a covering” as secret counsels held by stealth to deceive the prophets and escape the eyes of God. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 30:2

2who set out to go down to Egypt, without asking for my direction, to take refuge in the protection of Pharaoh and to seek shelter in the shadow of Egypt!

Arminian

Wesley: asked—either by the priests or prophets. The going down is still Judah’s embassy, not a later march. They set out without the mouth God had given them in priest and prophet.

Arminian reading: they might yet have asked. Refuge in Pharaoh is a will that would not wait on the Lord in that generation. The church is not to stretch “Egypt” into a newspaper of later states.

Reformed

Calvin: they walk that they may go down into Egypt without asking at the mouth of God, and even in the face of his prohibition. Strengthening themselves with the strength of Pharaoh: they placed confidence in the forces of the Egyptians. They showed that they cared little about the power of God, and did not greatly trust in him.

Reformed note: Isaiah contrasts Pharaoh with God; the creatures enter into contest with him when men place their hearts in them. The shadow is Egypt’s in Hezekiah’s century, not a later cipher.

Isaiah 30:3

3Therefore shall the protection of Pharaoh turn to your shame, and the shelter in the shadow of Egypt to your humiliation.

Arminian

Wesley’s heading for vv. 1–7 is still the prophet threatens the people for their confidence in Egypt. The protection they sought in v. 2 turns here to shame; the shadow they chose becomes humiliation. The shame is Judah’s in that hour.

Arminian pastoral: they might have left Pharaoh’s shelter while the word still stood. Confidence in an arm of flesh is not forced. The church is not to rename “shadow of Egypt” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: but to you shall the strength of Pharaoh be shame. You think that you gain much protection from Pharaoh, but it will yield you reproach and disgrace. The shadow of Egypt, by which you hoped to be covered, will make you blush for shame. Nothing acquired by wicked and unlawful methods can be of advantage to any person.

Reformed accent: the end of the wicked who despise the word is ruin, even when for a time everything succeeds to their wish. The church hears Judah’s shame under Assyria, not a newspaper of later humiliations.

Isaiah 30:4

4For though his officials are at Zoan and his envoys reach Hanes,

Arminian

Wesley: his princes—the princes of Judah. Hanes—an eminent city of Egypt. Officials and envoys are Judah’s own, sent south in that century, not a later corps.

Arminian note: the princes might yet have stayed. Zoan and Hanes are real Egyptian cities on a real embassy. The church is not to hunt another map to make “Hanes” true.

Reformed

Calvin: the Jews obtained Egyptian aid with great labor and expense. They had to perform long and painful journeys, to endure much toil, and to expend vast sums of money, in order to arrive, loaded with presents, at the most distant cities of Egypt. On this embassy were sent not persons of mean rank but princes and nobles. They did not need to go far to seek God; those wretched persons despised God, and chose rather to run to the very ends of the world than to receive the assistance which was offered to them.

Reformed pastoral: the contrast is already in the promise, This is my rest. Judah’s nobles in Zoan are enough. The verse stays with that embassy, not a later headline.

Isaiah 30:5

5everyone comes to shame through a people that cannot profit them, that brings neither help nor profit, but shame and disgrace.”

Arminian

Wesley: they—both the messengers, and they who sent them. Everyone is still that embassy and the court that dispatched it. Egypt cannot profit them; the disgrace is already named.

Arminian close of the mission: they might have turned back before the shame stuck. Help that is not the Lord’s is not help. The church is not to stretch “a people that cannot profit” into a newspaper of later nations.

Reformed

Calvin: they shall all be ashamed of a people that will not profit them. He threatens not only that the Egyptians will prove false, but that even though they endeavor to the utmost to fulfill the promises which they have made, still they will be of no use. Events are in the hand of God; they will profit nothing without his blessing.

Reformed accent: a nation so powerful could yield no assistance when the Lord did not make it sure. The church reads Judah’s empty profit in Isaiah’s hearing, not a later code for “shame.”

Isaiah 30:6

6An oracle on the beasts of the Negeb. Through a land of trouble and anguish, from where come the lioness and the lion, the adder and the flying fiery serpent, they carry their riches on the backs of donkeys, and their treasures on the humps of camels, to a people that cannot profit them.

Arminian

Wesley: the burden—the treasures, which were carried upon asses or camels, into Egypt, which lay southward from Judea. The land of trouble—Egypt, so called prophetically. From whence—this may be understood properly, but withal, seems to design the craft and cruelty of that people. They—the Jews. Their riches—to procure their assistance. Bunches—upon the backs. The caravan is still Judah’s, south through the Negeb.

Arminian pastoral: treasure on donkeys is a real payment for a real alliance. They might have kept the riches at home. The church is not to rename “beasts of the Negeb” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: he ridicules the enormous cost and the prodigious inconveniences which they endured; at so high a price did they purchase their destruction. He mentions the south because they journeyed through a southern region, Egypt being situated to the south of Judea. He addresses the beasts of burden to pour contempt on men, because it was in vain to speak to them. Though the people imagined that there was uninterrupted prosperity in Egypt, it would be a land of anguish and affliction even to the brute animals.

Reformed note: lions and fiery serpents are the Lord’s extra stroke on a road they would not quit. The oracle stays with that southern journey, not a newspaper pasted onto “Negeb.”

Isaiah 30:7

7Egypt’s help is worthless and empty; therefore I have called her “Rahab who sits still.”

Arminian

Wesley: to her—to Jerusalem or Judah. Sit still—it is safer and better for them to sit quietly at home, seeking to me for help. Rahab who sits still is still the word over that Egypt embassy: help worthless, Judah told to stay.

Arminian reading: sitting still is a real offer to historic Judah. They might yet have sat at home. The church is not to stretch “Rahab” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: surely the Egyptians are vanity; the strength of Egypt will do them no good, even though he be earnest and employ all his forces. Therefore have I cried to her: I consider this to refer to God himself, who complains that his distinct warnings produced no effect, and that his exhortation to them to sit still was intended to meet the troubles he foresaw. To sit still means to remain and to stay at home. They did not think that God’s protection was sufficient if they had not also the Egyptians on their side.

Reformed accent: they who do not give sufficient honor to God have their hearts agitated by unbelief, so that they tremble and never find peace. The cry is to Jerusalem in that century, not a newspaper of later Rahabs.

Isaiah 30:8

8And now, go, write it before them on a tablet and inscribe it in a book, that it may be for the time to come as a witness forever.

Arminian

Wesley: write—this warning. Before—in their presence. Note it—so this was to be written twice over, once in a table, to be hanged up in some public place, that all present might read it; and again, in a book, that it might be kept for the use of posterity. The time to come—as a witness for me and against them. The tablet is still Isaiah’s, before that people.

Arminian pastoral: the record is a sincere warning they might still have heard. Posterity is to learn, not to invent a later embassy. The church is not to hunt another age to make “witness forever” true.

Reformed

Calvin: after having convicted the Jews of manifest unbelief, he means that it should be attested and sealed by permanent records, that posterity may know how obstinate and rebellious that nation was, and how justly the Lord punished them. When any prediction was remarkable, the Lord commanded that it should be written in larger characters. Prophecies were not written merely for the men of a single age; their children ought to know that they ought not to imitate their fathers.

Reformed note: the tablet irritates that generation in their sight. The church reads a real witness against Judah’s Egypt counsel, not a later code for “book.”

Isaiah 30:9

9For they are a rebellious people, lying children, children unwilling to hear the instruction of the LORD;

Arminian

Wesley’s heading for vv. 8–11 is still contempt of God’s word. The rebellious of v. 1 are named again as lying children, unwilling to hear. The instruction refused is the Lord’s in that generation.

Arminian close of the charge: they might yet have heard. Lying children are Judah’s own, not a later guild. The church is not to stretch “unwilling” into a newspaper of later nations.

Reformed

Calvin: the perverseness of this nation is desperate, because they cannot submit to be restrained by any doctrine. They boasted that they were the holy and elect seed of Abraham, as if God’s adoption had been a veil to cover the grossest crimes. Who refuse to hear the law of Jehovah: the source of all evils is contempt of the word, which discovers their wickedness and their contempt of God himself.

Reformed accent: it is idle to pretend that they worship God when they are disobedient to his word. The church hears Isaiah’s lying children, not a later headline.

Isaiah 30:10

10who say to the seers, “Do not see,” and to the prophets, “Do not prophesy to us what is right; speak to us smooth things, prophesy illusions,

Arminian

Wesley’s heading still governs: contempt of God’s word. The seers are told not to see; the prophets, not to speak what is right. Smooth things are the request of that court that would not hear instruction.

Arminian pastoral: they might have borne the harsh message. Asking for illusions is a will, not a fate. The church is not to rename “seers” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: who say to the seers, See not. He does not relate the words of wicked men as if they openly made use of these words, but describes the state of the fact and their actual dispositions. They were singularly cunning hypocrites, who boasted of worshipping God. They desire smooth things; they were ready to receive flatterers with unbounded applause, and would willingly have allowed their ears to be tickled in the name of God.

Reformed note: not one of them was so impudent as to say that he wished to be deceived; they denied that what Isaiah told them was the word of God. The church reads Judah’s demand for smoothness, not a newspaper of later prophets.

Isaiah 30:11

11leave the way, turn aside from the path, let us hear no more about the Holy One of Israel.”

Arminian

Wesley: cause, &c.—do not trouble us with harsh messages from God. Leaving the way is still that people’s word against the Holy One of Israel, not a later slogan.

Arminian reading: they might yet have stayed in the path. The request to hear no more is eighth-century Judah’s. The church is not to hunt another century to make “Holy One of Israel” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: when the prophets are set aside, the Lord is also rejected and set aside. Cause the Holy One of Israel to depart: God does not spare or flatter their vices, but acts the part of a good and skillful physician. Men desire to be flattered, and cannot patiently endure that God should threaten them. The Prophet joins dislike of heavenly doctrine and hatred of uprightness.

Reformed pastoral: they who set the word aside act as if they denied that he is God. The path left is Isaiah’s, in that house, not a later cipher.

Isaiah 30:12

12Therefore thus says the Holy One of Israel, “Because you despise this word and trust in oppression and perverseness and rely on them,

Arminian

Wesley: and trust—in the wealth which you have gotten by oppression, and in your perverse course of sending to Egypt for help. The Holy One they would hear no more now names the despising. The word despised is still the rest offered to that Judah.

Arminian note: they might have left the wealth and the embassy. Perverseness is a course they chose. The church is not to stretch “oppression” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: because you have disdained this word. He expresses their contempt more strongly by the word disdain. For my own part, I do not view oppression as referring to riches gained by unlawful methods, but rather to that rebelliousness in which that nation insolently indulged. By these two words he intended to express the presumption of wicked men, by which they fiercely and wantonly rose up against God.

Reformed accent: they thought they would speedily overcome his power by their fierceness. The disdain is Judah’s in Isaiah’s hearing, not a newspaper pasted onto “oppression.”

Key contrast Both hear the Holy One answer Judah’s demand for smooth things, not a newspaper. Wesley reads the trust as wealth gotten by oppression and the perverse course of sending to Egypt. Calvin expressly refuses riches gained by unlawful methods, and takes oppression as that nation’s insolent rebelliousness against God. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 30:13

13therefore this iniquity shall be to you like a breach in a high wall, bulging out and about to collapse, whose breaking comes suddenly, in an instant;

Arminian

Wesley: this iniquity—of trusting to Egypt, shall be like a wall which is high, but swelling forth in some parts, which, upon the least accident, falls down suddenly. The bulge is still that embassy’s sin, not a later crack.

Arminian pastoral: they might have come off the wall before the instant. High and swelling is Judah’s confidence in Pharaoh. The church is not to rename “breach” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: he compares wicked men to a wall that is rent, or that bulges out. As the swelling out of a wall threatens the ruin of it, so the haughtiness and insolence of wicked men are a sign and very sure proof of their approaching ruin; the more they are puffed up without any solid value, the more readily do they throw themselves down headlong.

Reformed note: for a time the Lord permits wicked men to swell and utter their big words, that at length by their swelling they may bring upon themselves ruin. The wall is Judah’s in that century, not a later headline.

Isaiah 30:14

14and its breaking is like that of a potter’s vessel that is smashed so ruthlessly that among its fragments not a shard is found with which to take fire from the hearth, or to dip up water out of the cistern.”

Arminian

Wesley: he—God. The smashing is the Lord’s, as sudden as the bulge of v. 13. No shard left for fire or water: the vessel is Judah’s Egypt-trust, broken in that generation.

Arminian close of the wall: they might have been a vessel still whole. Ruthless breaking is not a code. The church is not to stretch “potter’s vessel” into a newspaper of later states.

Reformed

Calvin: when a wall has fallen, some traces of the ruin are still to be seen, and the stones of it may be applied to use; but here they who are puffed up with obstinacy against God shall perish in such a manner that they cannot be restored, and all that is left of them shall be utterly useless. He employs the metaphor of a potter’s vessel, the broken fragments of which cannot be repaired or put together.

Reformed accent: nothing is more difficult than to root out the false confidence from their hearts. The church hears a real smash of that obstinacy, not a later cipher for “shard.”

Isaiah 30:15

15For thus said the Lord GOD, the Holy One of Israel, “In returning and rest you shall be saved; in quietness and in trust shall be your strength.” But you were unwilling,

Arminian

Wesley: in returning—to God. Quietness—in sitting still, and quieting your minds. Confidence—placed upon me, and my promises. The Holy One of Israel still offers what Rahab who sits still had already named: stay, return, rest. They were unwilling.

Arminian pastoral: returning is a real call they might have obeyed. Strength in quietness is given, not forced. The verse stays with eighth-century Judah, not a millennial chart and not a newspaper of later nations.

Reformed

Calvin: some render shubah repentance; others render it rest, and I am more disposed to adopt that rendering. In rest and quietness shall you be safe. He enjoins the people to be of a quiet disposition, and next promises that their salvation shall be certain. The people do not believe this promise, and consequently they do not obey the command. Unbelief alone produces that uneasiness. In like manner had Moses long before addressed them, You shall be silent, and the Lord will fight for you.

Reformed accent: faith cannot exist without the promises, and as soon as the promises have been embraced, souls that were restless are made calm. The rest refused is Judah’s in that hour, not a later headline.

Key contrast Both keep returning and rest as the Holy One’s word to historic Judah, not a newspaper. Wesley reads returning as to God, quietness as sitting still and quieting the mind, confidence placed upon God and his promises. Calvin prefers rest rather than repentance as the sense of shubah: a quiet disposition, salvation certain if they rely fully upon him. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 30:16

16and you said, “No! We will flee upon horses”; therefore you shall flee away; and, “We will ride upon swift steeds”; therefore your pursuers shall be swift.

Arminian

Wesley’s heading for vv. 12–17 is still wherefore they shall be destroyed. The No is the unwillingness of v. 15 made vocal: horses instead of rest, swift steeds instead of quiet trust. The flight is Judah’s own word turning back on them.

Arminian reading: they might have said Yes to returning. Horses are Egypt’s gift and Judah’s boast in that century. The church is not to hunt a later cavalry to make “flee upon horses” true.

Reformed

Calvin: we will flee on horses; therefore shall you flee. He shews how they refused to wait calmly for the salvation of the Lord; for they chose rather to flee to the Egyptians. In the first of these clauses, to flee means to escape, and in the second it means to take flight. You shall certainly flee, not to find a place of refuge, but to turn your back and to be pursued by horses swifter than yours.

Reformed note: by the distinct reply, No, he shews how obstinately they refused the prophets’ advice. Isaiah does not affirm that they will receive no assistance from Egypt, but that the Lord will thwart it. The church reads that No in Isaiah’s Judah, not a newspaper of later steeds.

Isaiah 30:17

17A thousand shall flee at the threat of one; at the threat of five you shall flee, till you are left like a flagstaff on the top of a mountain, like a signal on a hill.

Arminian

Wesley: till—till you be destroyed, and but a few of you left. A thousand fleeing at the threat of one is the rest they would not have, now turned to panic. The flagstaff is still Judah after the Egypt alliance, not a later ensign.

Arminian close of the range: they might have been saved in returning. Few left is the harvest of No. Wesley and Calvin both keep this flight in Isaiah’s century; this is not an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. The church is not to stretch “a thousand shall flee” into a newspaper of later nations.

Reformed

Calvin: a thousand, as one, shall flee at the rebuke of one. Because the Jews, on account of their vast numbers, relied on their forces, the Lord will break and take away their courage. We shall have just as much courage as the Lord shall give us. It is the result of the righteous vengeance of God that we are terrified by men, when he could not prevail upon us to fear him. God needs not extensive preparations to chastise us; if he lift up but a finger, we are undone. Till they have been reduced to the last extremity: as a mast of a ship on the top of a mountain, as a banner on a hill—so few that all that remains shall be an indication of very great ruin.

Reformed close: this great multitude dazzles the eyes; there will be such decrease that they shall no longer have the face of a people. Calvin and Wesley agree the Holy One offered rest and they would not; this leftover pole is already enough to fear and to return. No millennial dispute is in this comment.
