Passage

Isaiah 36

Verses 11–22 · WEB · Voices

Hezekiah’s officials ask the Rabshakeh to speak Aramaic so the people on the wall won’t understand. He refuses and shouts in Hebrew, urging the people not to trust Hezekiah or the LORD and offering them surrender and deportation dressed up as peace. The people stay silent as the king ordered, and the officials return with their clothes torn.

Isaiah 36:11

11Then Eliakim, Shebna and Joah said to Rabshakeh, “Please speak to your servants in Aramaic, for we understand it. Don’t speak to us in the Jews’ language in the hearing of the people who are on the wall.”

Arminian

Wesley: he sends Rabshakeh, who by his blasphemous persuasions tempts Hezekiah to despair, and the people to revolt.

The officials ask Rabshakeh to speak Aramaic, the diplomatic language, so the people on the wall won’t understand. They fear the effect of his words on morale.

Reformed

Calvin: this circumstance again shews how deeply Hezekiah was depressed, when by his ambassador he entreats so humbly the servant of his enemy. They request that he will not speak in this manner in the presence of the people; because it is difficult to restrain a people naturally giddy and fickle. These ambassadors therefore gain nothing from Rabshakeh; when he is entreated, he grows worse.

Isaiah 36:12

12But Rabshakeh said, “Has my master sent me only to your master and to you, to speak these words, and not to the men who sit on the wall, who will eat their own dung and drink their own urine with you?”

Arminian

Wesley: to the men—to tell them to what extremities and miseries he will force them.

The crude threat is aimed at the soldiers on the wall: the siege will reduce them to eating their own waste. Rabshakeh wants the people to feel hopeless before a battle is fought.

Reformed

Calvin: hence we see the fierceness and insolence of the enemy. Rabshakeh was not sent for the purpose of offering any conditions of peace, but rather to obtain an unconditional surrender, and to strike the people with alarm; that they will perish through hunger, and will be compelled to eat and drink what is revolting.

Isaiah 36:13

13Then Rabshakeh stood, and called out with a loud voice in the Jews’ language, and said, “Hear the words of the great king, the king of Assyria!

Arminian

Wesley: Jews language—the tradition of the Jews is, that Rabshaketh was an apostate Jew. If so, his ignorance of the God of Israel was the less excusable, and his enmity the less strange: for apostates are usually the most bitter and spiteful enemies.

Rabshakeh shifts to Hebrew so the people on the wall will hear. Wesley mentions the tradition that he was an apostate Jew, which would make his blasphemy all the less excusable.

Reformed

Calvin: it was, indeed, exceedingly shocking that the holy language, which had been consecrated to the mysteries of heavenly wisdom, was profaned and prostituted to wicked blasphemies. No enemies are more destructive than those who speak the same language as ourselves. When the Prophet says that he stood, he expresses the fierceness and insolence of the wicked man.

Key contrast Both keep the loud Hebrew cry as wall-speech. Wesley reads the Jews’ language through the tradition that Rabshaketh was an apostate Jew; if so, his ignorance of the God of Israel was the less excusable, and his enmity the less strange, for apostates are usually the most bitter and spiteful enemies. Calvin reads the same cry as profanation: the holy language consecrated to the mysteries of heavenly wisdom was prostituted to wicked blasphemies; no enemies are more destructive than those who speak the same language as ourselves.

Isaiah 36:14

14The king says, ‘Don’t let Hezekiah deceive you; for he will not be able to deliver you.

Arminian

The appeal goes over the king’s head to the people: don’t let Hezekiah deceive you. It is an attempt to divide the city from its king before a single arrow is shot.

Reformed

Calvin: while he claims for his master the name of king, he speaks of Hezekiah as a private individual, without adding any title. He goes on to utter impudent calumnies against him, and at the same time vomits out his venom against God himself; for he calls it imposture and deception for Hezekiah to rely on his favor. Rabshakeh’s assertion, that they cannot be delivered by the hand of Hezekiah, is indeed true, unless God assist.

Isaiah 36:15

15Don’t let Hezekiah make you trust in the LORD, saying, “The LORD will surely deliver us. This city won’t be given into the hand of the king of Assyria.”’

Arminian

Wesley: he sends Rabshakeh, who by his blasphemous persuasions tempts Hezekiah to despair, and the people to revolt.

The enemy’s target is trust in the LORD. Rabshakeh cannot breach the wall, so he aims at the confidence behind it.

Reformed

Calvin: he quotes the exhortation by which Hezekiah encouraged the people, and speaks lightly of it as an idle and unfounded speech. Wicked men, though they assert the power of God, treat it with contempt. We must adhere firmly to the promises, that, hoping against hope, we may trust in God, and not suffer ourselves to be drawn away from him by any allurements.

Isaiah 36:16

16Don’t listen to Hezekiah, for the king of Assyria says, ‘Make your peace with me, and come out to me; and each of you eat from his vine, and each one from his fig tree, and each one of you drink the waters of his own cistern;

Arminian

Wesley: a present—upon which terms, I will give you no disturbance; but quietly suffer each of you to enjoy his own possession.

The offer sounds generous—vine, fig tree, and cistern—but it is terms of surrender. Wesley reads it as a promise of quiet on condition that they abandon their king.

Reformed

Calvin: while he labors to turn away the hearts of the people from Hezekiah, he at the same time invites them to pleasures, that they may forget God. To make a blessing is to conduct themselves in a friendly manner. He makes use of the word blessing as a cloak to that bondage which was in itself hateful. To revolt from a pious king, whom God had appointed, and who was a type of Christ, was more wretched and miserable than anything else that could befall them.

Calvin will not let the “blessing” pass as peace.

Key contrast Both keep the vine, fig, and cistern as that offer at the wall. Wesley reads the present as terms on which he will give no disturbance, but quietly suffer each to enjoy his own possession. Calvin reads “make a blessing” as a cloak to bondage: to revolt from a pious king, whom God had appointed, and who was a type of Christ, was more wretched than anything else, and could not take place without denying God.

Isaiah 36:17

17until I come and take you away to a land like your own land, a land of grain and new wine, a land of bread and vineyards.

Arminian

Wesley (on v. 16): upon which terms, I will give you no disturbance; but quietly suffer each of you to enjoy his own possession.

The fine print follows: deportation to “a land like your own.” Assyria’s peace means exile.

Reformed

Calvin: he now adds another condition far harder than the former; for he declares that peace cannot be made with Sennacherib in any other way than by the people going into banishment. This was nothing else than to abandon the worship of God and degenerate into superstition, and voluntarily to quit the inheritance which God had given them. He says that the country is equally fertile and productive; yet he cunningly passes by what ought above all other things to be valued by them, the worship of God, the temple, the kingdom.

Isaiah 36:18

18Beware lest Hezekiah persuade you, saying, “The LORD will deliver us.” Have any of the gods of the nations delivered their lands from the hand of the king of Assyria?

Arminian

Wesley: is not—thus boldly he speaks of the things which he understood not, judging of the great God, by their petty gods.

The argument from history—no other god saved its land—assumes the LORD is one god among many. Wesley calls it boldness about things Rabshakeh did not understand.

Reformed

Calvin: formerly he boasted that he was God’s servant, and that God had sent him to destroy Judea; but now he openly insults God himself. The wicked Rabshakeh bursts forth with greater violence, and boasts that he will gain the victory over God himself. Have any of the gods of the nations rescued their land? He does not hesitate to compare himself to the living God.

Calvin will not let “the LORD will deliver us” be weighed with idols.

Isaiah 36:19

19Where are the gods of Hamath and Arpad? Where are the gods of Sepharvaim? Have they delivered Samaria from my hand?

Arminian

Hamath, Arpad, and Sepharvaim had all fallen, and so had Samaria. The list is meant to frighten, but it ranks the living God with idols.

Reformed

Calvin: he mentions chiefly the neighboring cities, the destruction of which might affect them more deeply on account of their being better known to the Jews. I have no doubt that these places belonged to Syria and Israel; as if he had said, Look at these two kingdoms subdued, which were presided over by their gods as their guardians. Will your God resist me?

Isaiah 36:20

20Who are they among all the gods of these countries that have delivered their country out of my hand, that the LORD should deliver Jerusalem out of my hand?’”

Arminian

Wesley (on v. 18): thus boldly he speaks of the things which he understood not, judging of the great God, by their petty gods.

Rabshakeh’s final question sets the LORD among the gods of conquered nations. That comparison is exactly what God will answer in chapter 37.

Reformed

Calvin: this insolence of ungodly men arises from their not understanding that God punishes the sins of men when they suffer any adversity. They venture to make a comparison between them and God himself. It is our duty to acknowledge that God inflicts punishment by the hand of wicked men, who may be regarded as the instruments of God’s anger. If wicked men are more powerful, let us not think that the arm of God is broken.

Calvin refuses to let “the LORD should deliver Jerusalem” pass as one more idol.

Isaiah 36:21

21But they remained silent, and said nothing in reply, for the king’s commandment was, “Don’t answer him.”

Arminian

Wesley: he sends Rabshakeh, who by his blasphemous persuasions tempts Hezekiah to despair, and the people to revolt.

The people obey Hezekiah’s order and say nothing. Silence refuses to argue with blasphemy on its own terms.

Reformed

Calvin: ambassadors were sent to soothe the enemy; when they are unsuccessful they are enjoined to be silent, that they might not provoke that savage beast. It rather refers to those who guarded the walls, who, though they were sharply piqued by the taunts of the enemy, yet were not provoked to quarrels, because they obeyed the king’s command. It is no despicable courage, even when we have it not in our power to utter a syllable, still not to shrink or flinch, but to remain quietly in our place.

Isaiah 36:22

22Then Eliakim the son of Hilkiah, who was over the household, and Shebna the scribe, and Joah, the son of Asaph the recorder, came to Hezekiah with their clothes torn, and told him the words of Rabshakeh.

Arminian

The officials follow Hezekiah’s order not to answer (v. 21) and bring the report home with torn clothes. Grief, not argument, is the fitting response to blasphemy.

Reformed

Calvin: we now see that Eliakim and the other ambassadors were not silent as if they either approved of the impiety of Rabshakeh, or through dread of danger connived at such blasphemies; for they tear their garments. I except Shebna, who was destitute of piety, and was only driven by shame to assume the dress of mourning along with others as a matter of form.

Calvin does not let the three look like one piety.

Key contrast Both keep the torn clothes as that return from the wall. Wesley reads the three as the same embassy: Rabshakeh’s blasphemous persuasions tempt Hezekiah to despair and the people to revolt, and Eliakim, Shebna, and Joah come with clothes torn and tell the words. Calvin excepts Shebna: Eliakim and the others tear their garments as offended at the slanders; Shebna was destitute of piety, and was only driven by shame to assume the dress of mourning as a matter of form.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Voices from both camps

Classic Arminian and Reformed comment on Isaiah 36:11–22, in the writers’ own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“He sends Rabshakeh, who by his blasphemous persuasions tempts Hezekiah to despair, and the people to revolt. The history related here, and in the three following chapters, is, almost in the same words contained 2 Kings 18, 19, 20. It is inserted here, to explain and confirm some of the foregoing predictions. To the men—To tell them to what extremities and miseries he will force them. Jews language—The tradition of the Jews is, that Rabshaketh was an apostate Jew. If so, his ignorance of the God of Israel was the less excusable, and his enmity the less strange: for apostates are usually the most bitter and spiteful enemies. A present—Upon which terms, I will give you no disturbance; but quietly suffer each of you to enjoy his own possession. Thus saith—But what are the greatest men when they come to compare with God, or when God comes to contend with them? Is not—Thus boldly he speaks of the things which he understood not, judging of the great God, by their petty gods.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 36:11–22; 2 Kgs 18:26–37)
