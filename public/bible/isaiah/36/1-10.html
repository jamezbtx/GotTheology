Passage

Isaiah 36

Verses 1–10 · ESV · Voices

In the fourteenth year of King Hezekiah, Sennacherib king of Assyria came up against all the fortified cities of Judah and took them; the Rabshakeh from Lachish stood by the conduit of the upper pool on the highway to the Washer's Field; Eliakim, Shebna, and Joah came out; trust in Egypt that broken reed; high places Hezekiah removed; two thousand horses; the LORD said, Go up against this land and destroy it. The unit stays with that historic 8th/7th-century embassy—as Wesley and Calvin actually read—not a newspaper, not a millennial camp fight.

Isaiah 36:1

1In the fourteenth year of King Hezekiah, Sennacherib king of Assyria came up against all the fortified cities of Judah and took them.

Arminian

Wesley: Sennacherib invades Judah. The history related here is, almost in the same words contained 2 Kings 18, 19, 20. It is inserted here, to explain and confirm some of the foregoing predictions. Them—many of them; universal particles being frequently so used both in scripture, and other authors; and this success God gave him; to lift him up to his own greater and more shameful destruction: to humble and chastise his own people for their manifold sins, and, to gain an eminent opportunity to advance his own honour by that miraculous deliverance which he designed for his people.

Arminian reading: they might yet have seen that the taking was many, not a blank of every wall. Historic fourteenth year of Hezekiah is already named, 8th/7th century. This is not an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. The church is not to hunt another campaign to make “Sennacherib came up” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: in this and the following chapter the Prophet relates a remarkable history, which may be regarded as the seal of his doctrine. Not without reason does he specify the time; for at that time Hezekiah had restored the worship of God. If we judge of this calamity according to the perception of the flesh, we shall think that God was unjust in permitting his servant to be reduced to such extremities; the Lord did not punish Hezekiah for carelessness, pleasures, or luxury; God therefore intended to try his faith and patience. Yet the people were justly punished for their iniquities; although true religion flourished as to external worship, yet their life was not changed for the better.

Reformed note: the taking is still that historic siege after the reforms. Calvin will not let the fourteenth year become a newspaper or a chart of a later camp.

Key contrast Both keep Sennacherib’s coming as historic in Hezekiah’s fourteenth year, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads “all” as many of them, universal particles so used; God gave the success to lift Assyria toward a more shameful destruction, to humble and chastise his own people for their manifold sins, and to gain an eminent opportunity to advance his own honour by the miraculous deliverance he designed. Calvin reads the narrative as the seal of Isaiah’s doctrine; Hezekiah’s piety is tried, not punished for superstition; the people, not the reforming king, are chastised for unchanged lives. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 36:2

2And the king of Assyria sent the Rabshakeh from Lachish to King Hezekiah at Jerusalem, with a great army. And he stood by the conduit of the upper pool on the highway to the Washer's Field.

Arminian

Wesley: sent—having received the money, upon which he agreed to depart from Hezekiah and his land, he breaks his faith with Hezekiah, thereby justifying his revolt, and preparing the way for his own destruction. Rabshakeh at the upper pool is still that broken faith after the taking of v. 1.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have seen the envoy as faith broken, not as a later headline. Historic Lachish and the conduit are already named. The church is not to hunt another pool to make “the Rabshakeh” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: then the king of Assyria sent Rabshakeh. The order of the narrative may here have been altered; historical connection is frequently disturbed; the Scriptures frequently make use of a figure of speech in which a part is taken for the whole. It appeared that the king of Assyria had brought the whole of Judea under his dominion, because nearly all that remained was Jerusalem alone, in which Hezekiah was shut up.

Reformed accent: the conduit is still that historic upper pool. The great army stays with Lachish’s siege, not a newspaper pasted onto “Rabshakeh.”

Isaiah 36:3

3And there came out to him Eliakim the son of Hilkiah, who was over the household, and Shebna the secretary, and Joah the son of Asaph, the recorder.

Arminian

Wesley’s sending of v. 2 still holds: he sends Rabshakeh, who by his blasphemous persuasions tempts Hezekiah to despair, and the people to revolt. Eliakim, Shebna, and Joah come out as in the Kings parallel; the history is inserted here to explain and confirm some of the foregoing predictions.

Arminian reading: they might yet have gone out to that wall. Historic household, secretary, and recorder are already named. This is not a millennial chart. The church is not to stretch three names into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: Eliakim was formerly mentioned. It was he to whom the Lord promised that he would give him the chief power in the kingdom after the banishment of Shebna. It now appears as if that promise had failed, when he is sent to an enemy as a suppliant. Besides, the godly king had such a scarcity of good men, that, along with Eliakim, he was compelled to send Shebna, whom he knew well to be deceitful and treacherous.

Reformed note: the three are still that embassy at the pool. Calvin will not let Isa 22’s promise look failed because Eliakim walks out as a suppliant, nor turn the names into a newspaper.

Isaiah 36:4

4And the Rabshakeh said to them, “Say to Hezekiah, ‘Thus says the great king, the king of Assyria: On what do you rest this trust of yours?

Arminian

Wesley: thus saith—but what are the greatest men when they come to compare with God, or when God comes to contend with them? The great king’s trust-question is still that blasphemous persuasion after the three come out in v. 3.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have measured that “great king” against God. Historic Assyria at the wall is already named. The church is not to hunt another throne to make “on what do you rest this trust” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: say now to Hezekiah. The three ambassadors were not only repulsed, but disdainfully treated by the tyrant’s delegate. Thus saith the great king: he calls the king of Assyria great, because by his power he eclipsed all others, so that he stood alone in his lofty rank. By these thunderbolts of words Hezekiah might have been overthrown and subdued.

Reformed accent: the trust asked is still Hezekiah’s at that wall. The “great king” stays a historic taunt, not a newspaper of later empires.

Isaiah 36:5

5Do you think that mere words are strategy and power for war? In whom do you now trust, that you have rebelled against me?

Arminian

Wesley’s thus saith of v. 4 still holds: what are the greatest men when they come to compare with God, or when God comes to contend with them? Mere words and the charge of revolt are still that blasphemous persuasion tempting Hezekiah to despair.

Arminian reading: they might yet have heard the taunt as a man’s mouth, not as the last word. Historic rebellion-charge against Hezekiah is already named. This is not an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. The church is not to stretch “mere words” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: I have said (only a word of the lips). He ridicules Hezekiah, as if he fed the expectation of the people by empty boasting, while he was not provided with military preparations. He perfectly understands, he claims, what it is on which Hezekiah places his chief reliance, namely, on words and eloquence; but it is not by these that war can be begun or carried on.

Reformed note: the mere words are still that historic scoff at the wall. Calvin will not let the taunt become a newspaper of later speeches.

Isaiah 36:6

6Behold, you are trusting in Egypt, that broken reed of a staff, which will pierce the hand of any man who leans on it. Such is Pharaoh king of Egypt to all who trust in him.

Arminian

Wesley: this broken reed—whoever trusts in man, leans on a broken reed: but God is the rock of ages. Egypt is still that staff after the trust-question of vv. 4–5.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have leaned on the rock of ages, not on Pharaoh. Historic Egypt as a pierced reed is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to hunt another Nile to make “broken reed” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: behold, thou hast trusted in that broken staff of reed. He slanderously and falsely accuses the pious king of this vain confidence. Yet God justly rewarded a rebellious and disobedient people by allowing this filthy dog to reproach them with their wicked revolt. The hope of receiving aid from the Egyptians is not only false and unfounded, but pernicious.

Reformed accent: the reed is still that historic Egypt. Calvin will not let the slander of Hezekiah stand as if the pious king had rested there, nor paste the reed onto a later headline.

Key contrast Both keep Egypt as that historic broken reed at the wall, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads a pastoral rule: whoever trusts in man leans on a broken reed; but God is the rock of ages. Calvin reads the same reed as a slander of Hezekiah: the pious king did not rest on Egypt; Rabshakeh falsely accuses him, though the people deserved the taunt for their revolt. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 36:7

7But if you say to me, “We trust in the LORD our God,” is it not he whose high places and altars Hezekiah has removed, saying to Judah and to Jerusalem, “You shall worship before this altar”?

Arminian

Wesley: is not—thus boldly he speaks of the things which he understood not, judging of the great God, by their petty gods; and of God's worship by the vain fancies of the Heathens, who measured piety by the multitude of altars. The charge against the high places is still that misunderstanding after the reed of v. 6.

Arminian reading: they might yet have kept one altar and not called it a crime. Historic reform of Hezekiah is already named. The church is not to stretch “high places” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: this slander Satan attempted not only to wound the heart of the king. Every person must plainly see how slanderous is that charge; for Hezekiah had taken away false gods and superstitious worship, which God abhors. We must stand out with unshaken faith against such ungodly speeches, by shewing that what they call the worship of God is not his worship.

Reformed note: the one altar is still that historic purge. Calvin and Wesley both keep Rabshakeh ignorant of the LORD’s worship, not a newspaper of later shrines.

Isaiah 36:8

8Come now, make a wager with my master the king of Assyria: I will give you two thousand horses, if you are able on your part to set riders on them.

Arminian

Wesley’s broken reed of v. 6 still holds: whoever trusts in man, leans on a broken reed: but God is the rock of ages. Two thousand horses are still that blasphemous persuasion tempting Hezekiah to despair, after the altar-slander of v. 7.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have refused the wager and leaned on the rock. Historic cavalry-taunt at the wall is already named. The church is not to hunt another stable to make “two thousand horses” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: now come, give a hostage. He concludes that there will be nothing better for Hezekiah than to lay aside the intention of carrying on war and to surrender. This is ironical language. If I shall give thee two thousand horses, thou wilt not find among all thy people men to ride on them.

Reformed accent: the horses are still that ironic dare from Lachish’s camp. The wager stays a historic taunt, not a newspaper pasted onto “riders.”

Isaiah 36:9

9How then can you repulse a single captain among the least of my master's servants, when you trust in Egypt for chariots and for horsemen?

Arminian

Wesley’s reed of v. 6 still holds: whoever trusts in man, leans on a broken reed: but God is the rock of ages. One captain and Egypt’s chariots are still that same staff after the wager of v. 8.

Arminian reading: they might yet have measured the least captain against the rock of ages. Historic Egypt-trust at the wall is already named. This is not a millennial camp fight. The church is not to stretch “a single captain” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: and how dost thou despise? Hezekiah is so far from being able to endure the presence of his king, that he ought not to be compared to the very smallest of his captains. Although Sennacherib does not yet appear before them with his whole army, Rabshakeh boasts that his lieutenants are sufficiently powerful, so that Hezekiah ought not to hesitate to make submission.

Reformed note: the least captain is still that historic boast while the king sits at Lachish. Calvin will not let the taunt become a newspaper of later cavalry.

Isaiah 36:10

10Moreover, is it without the LORD that I have come up against this land to destroy it? The LORD said to me, “Go up against this land and destroy it.”’”

Arminian

Wesley: am I—he neither owned God's word, nor regarded his providence; but he forged this, to strike a terror into Hezekiah and the people. “The LORD said” is still that forgery after Egypt’s chariots in v. 9, closing the first speech.

Arminian close of the unit: they might yet have heard a forged word and not taken it as the LORD’s. Historic claim at the wall, 8th/7th century, is already named. The church is not to stretch “the LORD said to me” into a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: and now have I come up without Jehovah? He alleges that Hezekiah has not to contend with a mortal man, but with God himself. Satan assumes the character of God himself, and is transformed into an angel of light. We ought therefore to distinguish wisely when God speaks, and when, on the other hand, his name is falsely assumed by men. An upright conscience ought to be like a brazen wall, that, imitating Hezekiah, we may stand unshaken.

Reformed close: the claim is still that historic theft of the Name at the pool. Calvin will not let “the LORD said” pass as God’s mouth, nor as a later newspaper.

Key contrast Both keep “the LORD said, Go up against this land” as Rabshakeh’s word at that wall, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads a forgery: he neither owned God's word, nor regarded his providence; he forged this to strike a terror into Hezekiah and the people. Calvin reads a subtler theft of the Name: Satan assumes the character of God and is transformed into an angel of light; we must distinguish when God speaks from when men falsely assume his name. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 36:1–10; 2 Kgs 18:13–25)

Reformed Notable voices

“It happened in the fourteenth year. In this and the following chapter the Prophet relates a remarkable history, which may be regarded as the seal of his doctrine, in which he predicted the calamities that would befall his nation, and at the same time promised that God would be merciful to them, and would drive back the Assyrians and defend Jerusalem and the Holy Land. Not without reason does he specify the time; for at that time Hezekiah had restored the worship of God throughout the whole of his dominions. While superstitions were removed and the Temple cleansed, Judea is invaded by the king of Assyria; Jerusalem alone is left, and in that city Hezekiah was shut up as in a prison. If we judge of this calamity according to the perception of the flesh, we shall think that God was unjust in permitting his servant to be reduced to such extremities; the Lord did not punish Hezekiah for carelessness, pleasures, or luxury, and much less for superstitions; God therefore intended to try his faith and patience. Yet the people were justly punished for their iniquities; although true religion flourished as to external worship, yet their life was not changed for the better. Then the king of Assyria sent Rabshakeh. Historical connection is frequently disturbed; the Scriptures frequently make use of a figure of speech in which a part is taken for the whole. Say now to Hezekiah: in order to terrify Hezekiah he calls the king of Assyria great; by these thunderbolts of words Hezekiah might have been overthrown. I have said (only a word of the lips): he ridicules Hezekiah, as if he fed the expectation of the people by empty boasting. Behold, thou hast trusted in that broken staff of reed: he slanderously and falsely accuses the pious king of this vain confidence; yet God justly rewarded a rebellious and disobedient people by allowing this filthy dog to reproach them with their wicked revolt. And if thou shalt say to me, We trust in Jehovah our God: this slander Satan attempted to wound the heart of the king; Hezekiah had taken away false gods and superstitious worship, which God abhors. Now come, give a hostage: this is ironical language. And how dost thou despise: Hezekiah ought not to be compared to the smallest of his captains. And now have I come up without Jehovah? He alleges that Hezekiah has not to contend with a mortal man, but with God himself. Satan assumes the character of God himself, and is transformed into an angel of light. We ought therefore to distinguish wisely when God speaks, and when, on the other hand, his name is falsely assumed by men. An upright conscience ought to be like a brazen wall to us, that, imitating the example of Hezekiah, we may stand unshaken against such accusations and slanders.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 36:1–10)
