Passage

Isaiah 20

Verses 1–6 · ESV · Voices

In the year that the commander in chief, sent by Sargon king of Assyria, came to Ashdod and captured it, the LORD told Isaiah to loose the sackcloth from his waist and walk naked and barefoot. Three years as a sign and portent against Egypt and Cush: so the king of Assyria shall lead away Egyptian captives and Cushite exiles, young and old, naked and barefoot, buttocks uncovered. Then they shall be dismayed because of Cush their hope and Egypt their boast; the inhabitants of this coastland will say, this is what has happened to those in whom we hoped to be delivered from the king of Assyria. The unit stays with historic Ashdod, Sargon, Egypt, and Cush in the Assyria age—as Wesley and Calvin actually read—not a newspaper of later states, not a millennial camp fight.

Isaiah 20:1

1In the year that the commander in chief, who was sent by Sargon the king of Assyria, came to Ashdod and fought against it and captured it—

Arminian

Wesley: Sargon—Sennacherib, who, before he came to Jerusalem, came up against and took all the walled cities of Judah, of which Ashdod might be reckoned one, as being in the tribe of Judah. Shared: Ashdod taken in that year.

Arminian pastoral: the date is a real year under historic Assyria. Do not turn Tartan at Ashdod into a newspaper. Philistine Ashdod, Sargon’s captain, not a twenty-first-century siege.

Reformed

Calvin: in the year that Tartan came to Ashdod. He shews that Israel will be put to shame by this chastisement of the Egyptians, because they placed their confidence in Egypt. He now joins Ethiopia. Sacred history informs us that Tartan was one of Sennacherib’s captains, which constrains us to acknowledge that this Sargon was Sennacherib, who had two names. The ten tribes had been led into captivity; Tartan was besieging Ashdod; nothing was now left for the Jews but to call foreign nations to their aid.

Reformed pastoral: the capture is dated so the vain hope can be named. Shared: Ashdod taken by Assyria. Historic Philistia, not a modern state.

Isaiah 20:2

2at that time the LORD spoke by Isaiah the son of Amoz, saying, “Go, and loose the sackcloth from your waist and take off your sandals from your feet,” and he did so, walking naked and barefoot.

Arminian

Wesley: Sackcloth—which he wore in token of his grief for the calamities that were already come upon Israel, and were coming upon Judah. Naked—not wholly naked, but without his upper garment, as slaves and prisoners used to do, whose posture he was to represent. Bare-foot—after the manner of mourners and captives. Shared: the prophet walks the sign.

Arminian pastoral: the sign is a real walk in historic Judah. Do not restage nakedness as spectacle, and do not aim it at a modern people.

Reformed

Calvin: go and loose the sackcloth from thy loins. In order to confirm this prophecy by the use of a symbol, he is commanded to walk naked and barefoot. The Lord spoke by Isaiah: the prophet is God’s servant in the sign, as he will be called in v. 3. He did so: obedience to the word, that Judah might see Egypt’s future in a man of Judah.

Reformed note: the stripped prophet is still that year’s sign. Shared: sackcloth loosed, sandals off. Historic Isaiah, not a newspaper.

Isaiah 20:3

3Then the LORD said, “As my servant Isaiah has walked naked and barefoot for three years as a sign and a portent against Egypt and Cush,

Arminian

Wesley: Three years—not constantly, but when he went abroad among the people, to whom this was appointed for a sign. A sign—when this judgment should come, namely, three years after this prophecy. Shared: three years against Egypt and Cush.

Arminian pastoral: the counted years are that age’s clock, not a twenty-first-century date scheme. Historic Egypt and Ethiopia, not a millennial chart.

Reformed

Calvin: three years. Why for such a period? Because that was the time granted to the Egyptians and Ethiopians till they should be carried into captivity. As my servant Isaiah has walked: the sign is the prophet’s own body, a portent against those two nations on whom Judah leaned.

Reformed pastoral: the three years are the Lord’s appointment. Shared: a sign and portent against Egypt and Cush. Historic walk, not a newspaper decoder.

Key contrast Both hear three years as a sign against historic Egypt and Cush, not a modern countdown. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s manner: not constantly, but when he went abroad; the judgment three years after the prophecy. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: three years were the time granted those nations until captivity; the servant’s body is the portent while Judah still wants their help.

Isaiah 20:4

4so shall the king of Assyria lead away the Egyptian captives and the Cushite exiles, both the young and the old, naked and barefoot, with buttocks uncovered, the nakedness of Egypt.

Arminian

Wesley: Uncovered—having their garments cut off by the middle. Shared: young and old led as the prophet walked.

Arminian pastoral: the shame is Egypt’s and Cush’s under historic Assyria. Do not hunt another column of captives to make “buttocks uncovered” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: the captivity of Egypt and the removal of Ethiopia. So shall the king of Assyria lead away the Egyptian captives and the Ethiopian exiles, naked and barefoot, with their buttocks uncovered, to the shame of Egypt. The sign in the prophet’s flesh is the thing itself: the hope of Judah stripped.

Reformed note: the led-away are still those two nations. Shared: naked and barefoot, young and old. Historic Assyria, not a later empire pasted onto the verse.

Isaiah 20:5

5Then they shall be dismayed and ashamed because of Cush their hope and of Egypt their boast.

Arminian

Wesley: They—all that shall trust to them. But under this general expression the Israelites seem to be principally intended. Shared: Cush the hope, Egypt the boast, then shame.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have ceased to boast in Egypt. Historic Israel and Judah leaning on the Nile, not a culture-war speech. Do not baptize a foreign court as refuge.

Reformed

Calvin: and they shall be afraid. He now shews for whose benefit he had foretold these things: that the Jews might not rely on Egypt and Ethiopia. They shall be ashamed of Ethiopia their expectation, and of Egypt their glory. The Lord takes away the props that they may learn to rest on him alone.

Reformed pastoral: dismay is mercy’s lesson. Shared: ashamed of Cush and Egypt. Historic vain hope, not a newspaper.

Isaiah 20:6

6And the inhabitants of this coastland will say in that day, ‘Behold, this is what has happened to those in whom we hoped and to whom we fled for help to be delivered from the king of Assyria! And we, how shall we escape?’”

Arminian

Wesley: Of the country—of this land, in which the prophet was, and to whose inhabitants these words were uttered. Such—so vain is our hope placed upon such a people as are unable to deliver themselves. Shared: how shall we escape?

Arminian close: the coastland’s question is still Judah’s in that year. Historic flight to Egypt against Sargon, not a twenty-first-century alliance. The warning still calls them off the Nile.

Reformed

Calvin: lo, what is become of our expectation? He calls them expectation, or lurking, because the Jews looked toward them and hid themselves under their shadow. The inhabitant of this isle shall say in that day: so vain is the hope placed upon such a people as are unable to deliver themselves. And we, how shall we escape? When the props fall, they see that they must deal with the king of Assyria before God, not behind Egypt.

Reformed close: the coastland is still that land’s. Shared: those in whom we hoped cannot save. Historic Assyria, Egypt, and Cush—already enough without a later newspaper.

Reformed Notable voices

“In the year that Tartan came to Ashdod. In the preceding chapter Isaiah prophesied about the calamity which threatened Egypt; he now shews that Israel will be put to shame by this chastisement of the Egyptians, because they placed their confidence in Egypt. He now joins Ethiopia. Sacred history informs us that Tartan was one of Sennacherib’s captains, which constrains us to acknowledge that this Sargon was Sennacherib, who had two names. The ten tribes had been led into captivity; Judea appeared almost to be utterly ruined; Tartan was besieging Ashdod. Nothing was now left for the Jews but to call foreign nations to their aid. In the mean time, Isaiah is sent by God to declare that their expectation is vain in relying on the Egyptians. Go and loose the sackcloth from thy loins: in order to confirm this prophecy by the use of a symbol. Three years: that was the time granted to the Egyptians and Ethiopians till they should be carried into captivity. And they shall be afraid and ashamed of Ethiopia their expectation, and of Egypt their glory. Lo, what is become of our expectation?
