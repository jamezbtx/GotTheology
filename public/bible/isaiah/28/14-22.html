Passage

Isaiah 28

Verses 14–22 · ESV · Voices

Isaiah turns from Ephraim’s refused rest to the scoffers who rule Jerusalem: they have made a covenant with death, and the Lord answers with a tested stone in Zion. The unit stays in eighth-century Judah—an overwhelming scourge, a short bed, Perazim and Gibeon—not a newspaper of later nations.

Isaiah 28:14

14Therefore hear the word of the LORD, you scoffers, who rule this people in Jerusalem!

Arminian

Wesley’s heading for these verses is their mock at God’s threatening. Hear is spoken to scornful men who actually rule this people in Jerusalem—Judah’s court, not a cipher. The word comes after the rest they would not hear (v. 12); scoffing is the next will, not a stage direction.

Arminian pastoral: rulers can still hear. The charge is eighth-century Jerusalem, priests’ sister-city now answering in jeers. Grace that spared Judah after Ephraim’s fall (vv. 5–6) is a sincere call; they mock it. The church is not to hunt a later capital to make “scoffers” true.

Reformed

Calvin: he addresses not ordinary men but rulers and governors, addicted to sophistry and deceit, who think that by jeers and cunning they can escape the judgment of God. Their guilt is aggravated because they inhabit the very sanctuary of God and infect with their pollution God’s chosen people.

Reformed accent: office is no shelter. Instruction would be of little use; the prophet threatens. Jerusalem’s nobles are the scoffers of that generation. The church reads a real court under the word, not a later headline pasted onto “this people which is in Jerusalem.”

Isaiah 28:15

15Because you have said, “We have made a covenant with death, and with Sheol we have an agreement, when the overwhelming whip passes through it will not come to us, for we have made lies our refuge, and in falsehood we have taken shelter”;

Arminian

Wesley: said—in your hearts. We are as safe from death, and hell, or the grave, as if they had entered into covenant with us. The scourge is the judgment of God through the land; for—we shall secure ourselves by lying and dissimulation. The treaty is a heart-boast, not a document God countersigns.

Arminian reading: they chose the lie as refuge. The overwhelming whip is the storm already named (v. 2), coming through Judah’s land in that century. Creaturely cunning does not cancel the scourge. The church is to refuse both despair and the habit of renaming this covenant every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: they never broke out into such childish boasting; the prophet looked at their feelings and aims, not their pretexts. Whoever flatters himself and his vices, and fearlessly despises God’s threatenings, declares that he has entered into a league with death. Carnal presumption forgets the judgment of God and takes lying and deceit for a hiding-place.

Reformed note: the compact is imaginary. Hypocrites think jeers can escape the arm of God. The scourge is still the Lord’s flood against Jerusalem’s unbelief. The verse does not need a later newspaper; the hiding-place is already named falsehood in Isaiah’s hearing.

Key contrast Both hear a false safety against the scourge. Wesley reads the covenant as their heart-speech: as safe from death and hell as if they had a treaty, secured by lying and dissimulation. Calvin says they never uttered those offensive words; the prophet names their feelings—an imaginary compact of carnal presumption.

Isaiah 28:16

16therefore thus says the Lord GOD, “Behold, I am the one who has laid as a foundation in Zion, a stone, a tested stone, a precious cornerstone, of a sure foundation: ‘Whoever believes will not be in haste.’

Arminian

Wesley: because your refuges are so vain and deceitful, I will direct you to a surer refuge, which God hath laid in Zion. I lay—I have promised it, and will, in the fulness of time, perform it. In Zion—in my church. A stone—the Messiah. Tried—approved as every way sufficient. Corner-stone—uniting the several parts of the building. Sure—upon whom you may securely rest. He that believeth this promise shall not hastily catch at any way of escaping danger, but shall patiently wait upon God in his way, till he deliver him.

Classical Arminians hear the same Christ Wesley names. The stone is laid for Jerusalem’s scoffers in that century; belief is a living wait, not a scramble back into the covenant with death. The church rests on that tried stone. Neither Wesley nor the verse maps a millennial fight; the Messiah is the refuge they would not take.

Reformed

Calvin: Behold, I lay in Zion a stone, a stone of trial. He calls it a corner-stone because it supports the whole weight of the building—a fundamental foundation, not one of many. As another foundation cannot be laid, so on it alone must the whole Church rest and be built. The Apostles and evangelists shew that this stone is Christ. He that believeth shall not make haste: they who believe will have peace and serenity of mind, and shall not wander in uncertainty or hasten to seek other remedies.

Reformed accent: God alone founds the church. Calvin takes the tried stone actively—a trier by which the house is squared. Christ is Zion’s stone while Judah’s scoffers still ran to lies. No millennial dispute is in this comment; the harbour is the truth of the Lord, already enough.

Key contrast Both land on the same christology: the stone is the Messiah / Christ, the sure foundation of the church in Zion. Wesley stresses a promised stone performed in the fullness of time, and a believer who will not hastily catch at escape but wait for deliverance. Calvin stresses a trying stone that squares the house, and a believer who will not make haste because faith already has peace. Neither comment invents a millennial fight.

Isaiah 28:17

17And I will make justice the line, and righteousness the plumb line; and hail will sweep away the refuge of lies, and waters will overwhelm the shelter.”

Arminian

Wesley: plummet—I will execute just judgment as by a line and plummet, with exactness and care; I will severely punish, and utterly destroy all who reject that stone. The line was used not only in erecting buildings but in pulling them down: the parts marked out for demolition. Hail and waters are the same storm as v. 2, now aimed at the liar’s shelter in Jerusalem.

Arminian note: rejecting the cornerstone is a real refusal, and the plumb line is exact, not arbitrary. The refuge of lies can still be left; they will not. The verse stays with Judah’s hiding-place in that century. Faith measures by the stone already named, not by a later chart of hail.

Reformed

Calvin: the Lord administers equal judgment when he restores the Church, trying everything by a rule. Hail shall sweep away the reliance of falsehood, and the flood will penetrate the hiding-places of thoughtless indifference. Hypocrites, with all their boasting, shall nevertheless perish, even though the Lord preserve the Church.

Reformed pastoral: the line that builds on Christ is the line that pulls down the lie. The church is not the scoffers’ shelter; it is what remains when hail takes the refuge. Jerusalem’s falsehood is the thing swept—not a modern headline made to fit “waters.”

Isaiah 28:18

18Then your covenant with death will be annulled, and your agreement with Sheol will not stand; when the overwhelming scourge passes through, you will be beaten down by it.

Arminian

The treaty of v. 15 is cancelled in the same land it was boasted. Wesley’s “as safe from death and hell as if they had entered into covenant” is answered: the agreement will not stand. The overflowing scourge passes through, and they are trodden—Judah’s scoffers under the same kind of flood that took Ephraim’s crown.

Arminian close of the boast: a covenant they never had authority to sign cannot bind the scourge. Beaten down is the fruit of lying refuge, not a puppet-fall. The church hears eighth-century Jerusalem losing its deal with death, not a later nation slotted into “Sheol.”

Reformed

Calvin: he now threatens that the Lord will arouse them from their sleep, however profound, and will annul their imaginary compacts. That peace which the wicked enjoy while they slumber in their sins will not be perpetual; they shall be compelled to acknowledge that God is their judge. When they wish to enjoy repose, they shall be seized and agitated by strange terrors.

Reformed accent: the compact was never valid. God breaks what they only imagined. The scourge is his, and the treading is already in the word for Jerusalem’s unbelief. The church is not to hunt a later prey to make the beating-down true.

Isaiah 28:19

19As often as it passes through it will take you; for morning by morning it will pass through, by day and by night; and it will be sheer terror to understand the message.

Arminian

Wesley: it shall not only come to you, but abide upon you; when it hath passed over you, it shall return again, morning after morning, and follow you day and night without the least respite. The report—so dreadful shall the judgment be, that it shall strike you with horror when you only hear the rumour of it.

Arminian pastoral: the scoffers who would not hear rest (v. 12) will hear the rumour and shake. Repeated passing is repeated mercy refused, then repeated stroke in their own days. The message is still intelligible; terror is what understanding becomes once the scourge is in the land. This is Judah’s morning, not a later timetable.

Reformed

Calvin: destruction of the reprobate is close at hand, though they promise themselves everlasting happiness. The scourge will be constant and daily. Against believers the wrath of God is momentary; against unbelievers it is eternal, for it never ceases to pursue them. Terror alone shall cause them to understand the report: chastisements will teach what the word did not, and knowledge so late will leave no room for repentance.

Reformed note: Pharaoh and Esau are Calvin’s warnings that late terror is not always repentance. The church reads a people in Jerusalem who would not be instructed until the rumour itself was horror—without turning “morning by morning” into a later chart.

Key contrast Both hear a returning scourge and a report that horrifies. Wesley applies the unrespite stroke and the rumour’s terror to the mockers in the land. Calvin distinguishes: wrath against believers is momentary, against the reprobate eternal; terror may teach the doctrine too late for repentance.

Isaiah 28:20

20For the bed is too short to stretch oneself on, and the covering too narrow to wrap oneself in.

Arminian

Wesley: those lying refuges to which you trust will not be able to give you that protection which you expect from them; no more than a man can stretch himself upon a bed that is too short for him. The covenant with death is a cot that will not fit the body when the scourge arrives.

Arminian reading: they chose the bed. Stretching and wrapping are what the lie cannot do. Eighth-century Judah’s alliances and falsehoods are too small for the storm. The church is not to lengthen this bed into a parable of later empires; the covering was already too narrow in Jerusalem.

Reformed

Calvin: he compares the reprobate, pressed down by the hand of God, to those concealed in a short and narrow bed, where instead of rest they feel sharp pains. The Jews will be shut up; the bed given to man for rest will be an instrument of torture. If they seek a covering, it will be too short; vengeance pursues them on all sides.

Reformed pastoral: God drags them forth from hiding-places and hems them in. The short bed is Jerusalem’s false peace under the word. Comfort will not be found in the shelter hail already named; the church fears that confinement, not a later cartoon of “covering.”

Isaiah 28:21

21For the LORD will rise up as on Mount Perazim; as in the Valley of Gibeon he will be roused; to do his deed—strange is his deed! and to work his work—alien is his work!

Arminian

Wesley: Perazim—where he fought against the Philistines (2 Sam. 5:20). Gibeon—where he fought against the Canaanites (Josh. 10:10) and afterwards against the Philistines (1 Chron. 14:16). Strange work—this work of bringing total destruction upon Israel was contrary to the benignity of his own nature, and to the usual way of dealing with his people.

Arminian note: the old victories are real places, now turned against the scoffers in Judah. The strangeness is that the Lord who burst out for David now rises against his own. That is this generation’s alien work—not a newspaper of later wars. They could have taken the stone; they kept the covenant with death.

Reformed

Calvin quotes the same Perazim and Gibeon, where the Lord displayed his arm in defense of his people. For his own part he considers “strange” to mean simply what is uncommon or wonderful. He willingly admits the contrast: the Lord formerly performed miracles to save his people; he will now perform them in order to destroy that people, reckoning the wicked Israelites with the Philistines and Canaanites.

Reformed accent: God rises up when we think him idle. The strange act is Judah’s own judgment in Isaiah’s century, patterned on old battles, not a later map. The church is to fear the God who can use Perazim against Jerusalem as easily as for it.

Key contrast Both keep Perazim and Gibeon as real old battles now aimed at the scoffers. Wesley takes the strange work as contrary to the benignity of God’s nature and his usual dealing with his people. Calvin prefers “strange” as uncommon or wonderful, while granting that the Lord now fights against the people he once saved.

Isaiah 28:22

22Now therefore do not scoff, lest your bonds be made strong; for I have heard a decree of destruction from the Lord GOD of hosts against the whole land.

Arminian

Wesley: lest thereby you make the judgments of God sure and unavoidable. For—God hath assured me that he will utterly destroy the people of Israel. The mockers of v. 14 are warned once more: jeers tighten the bands; the determined consumption is already heard.

Arminian close of the range: “do not scoff” is a real imperative to Jerusalem’s rulers. Bonds grow strong by their continuing mock, not by a decree they could never have refused. The whole land is Judah’s land under the scourge of this chapter. The stone still stands (v. 16); they may yet cease from the covenant with death. The verse is eighth-century warning, not a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: as the fox fallen into a snare fastens the knot more firmly by trying to escape, so wicked men by disobedience entangle themselves more and more. Be ye not mockers. He has heard a consumption determined upon the whole earth—or, if better, limited to Judea. Dreadful destruction is not distant; the day is fixed.

Reformed close: resistance does not loosen the chain. The prophet brings nothing but what God revealed. The church hears a decree already spoken over the land of the scoffers. Christ remains the tried stone; the mockers’ bonds are of their unbelief in that generation, not a puzzle for later nations.
