Passage

Isaiah 28

Verses 1–13 · ESV · Voices

In the eighth century Isaiah opens a woe on Ephraim’s proud crown—Samaria, drunk on wine and prosperity—then turns the same charge on Judah’s priests and prophets, who would not hear the rest God offered. The unit stays in that generation: precept upon precept, a foreign tongue, and a fall backward; it is not a newspaper of later nations.

Isaiah 28:1

1Ah, the proud crown of the drunkards of Ephraim, and the fading flower of its glorious beauty, which is on the head of the rich valley of those overcome with wine!

Arminian

Wesley: that proud and insolent kingdom; drunkards, having many excellent vines, were much exposed to this sin; Ephraim is the kingdom of the ten tribes. The head is Samaria, seated upon a mountain, head of the kingdom and of the fat valleys that ring it. The woe is eighth-century: a city and a people overcome with wine, not a cipher for later states.

Classical Arminians hear a real charge against real drinkers. Prosperity and the vine did not force the stagger; they exposed a will that would not stay sober. The fading flower is Samaria’s beauty while it still sits above the valley. The church is to refuse both despair and the habit of renaming Ephraim every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: the anger of the Lord will quickly overtake, first, Israel, and afterwards the Jews; the ten tribes had not yet been led into captivity. Crown here is the wicked confidence that proceeds from excess of riches. He calls them drunkards because, intoxicated by prosperity, they dreaded no adversity and thought themselves not even subject to God.

Reformed accent: abundance and fullness produce cruelty and pride. The fading flower is already named as fading before the storm of v. 2 arrives. The church’s warning is not a map of modern nations but the Lord’s curse on wealth used without moderation in Samaria’s own generation.

Isaiah 28:2

2Behold, the Lord has one who is mighty and strong; like a storm of hail, a destroying tempest, like a storm of mighty, overflowing waters, he casts down to the earth with his hand.

Arminian

Wesley: a strong one—the king of Assyria. Shall cast down—the crown of pride. The hand—by the hand of God, which shall strengthen him in this work. Hail, tempest, and overflowing waters are the campaign that will actually throw Samaria down, not a symbol to be reassigned at will.

Arminian reading: the Assyrian is a responsible conqueror, and the Lord’s hand is still the Lord’s. Strengthening a king for judgment does not cancel the king’s violence or Ephraim’s guilt. “Behold” is for that generation that would not dread adversity. Faith watches Assyria without baptizing every later empire as this storm.

Reformed

Calvin allows that this may refer to the Assyrians, ready at God’s command, yet prefers to take it without a named substantive—a staff or instrument by which the Lord will cast them down from lofty pride. The metaphor of the fading flower continues: nothing is more destructive to flowers than hail. Wicked men are not moved by threatenings, so the prophet shows he does not conjecture at random.

Reformed note: the glory of the Israelites will be laid low as one throws down a drunk man with the hand. The instrument is the Lord’s whether or not a king’s name is supplied. The church is to fear the hand that casts down, not to master a later newspaper key to “mighty and strong.”

Key contrast Both read a real overthrow of Ephraim’s crown in that generation. Wesley names the mighty one as the king of Assyria, strengthened by God’s hand. Calvin allows the Assyrians but prefers to leave the agent unnamed—a staff or instrument in the Lord’s own grip.

Isaiah 28:3

3The proud crown of the drunkards of Ephraim will be trodden underfoot;

Arminian

Wesley: the expression is emphatical; the crown which was upon their own heads shall be trodden under the feet of others; and they, whose drunkenness made them fall to the ground, shall be trodden down there. The joke of the feast becomes the fact of the siege.

Arminian pastoral: falling in drink is still a fall they chose; being trodden is the sequel they would not hear. The verse stays with Ephraim’s crown, not a later capital. The people of God are to see how pride and wine end in the dust of that valley, not to hunt a modern “Ephraim” underfoot.

Reformed

Calvin: the same statement as v. 2 is confirmed. The glory and splendor of the Israelites will be laid low. What sat as a wreath on the head of the fat valley will be a thing for feet.

Reformed accent: the treading is the Lord’s sentence already spoken, not chance of war. The church is consoled that a crown which thought itself beyond all danger is already underfoot in the word. Samaria’s pride does not outrun the hail.

Isaiah 28:4

4and the fading flower of its glorious beauty, which is on the head of the rich valley, will be like a first-ripe fig before the summer: when someone sees it, he swallows it as soon as it is in his hand.

Arminian

Wesley: as soon as a man sees it he plucks it off and devours it as soon as he can get it into his hand. So shall it be with Ephraim’s glory, which his enemies shall devour greedily. The first-ripe fig is Samaria’s beauty: short, sweet to the taker, gone.

Arminian note: greedy swallowing is a real army’s haste, not a code. The flower is still on the head of the rich valley when the hand closes. Creaturely appetite does the devouring; the Lord had said it would. The church is not to stretch “first-ripe fig” into a chart of later summers.

Reformed

Calvin: he inculcates the same truth on minds blinded by prosperity. First-ripe fruits last but a short time and cannot be preserved; they are quickly eaten. The happiness of the Israelites will be of that sort—flourishing prosperity not of long duration, swallowed in a moment.

Reformed pastoral: by ingratitude men prevent the goodness bestowed on them from reaching maturity. We abuse his blessings and corrupt them by wickedness. The hasty fruit is Ephraim’s, and the same pattern warns every rich valley: what will not ripen in repentance is eaten at once.

Isaiah 28:5

5In that day the LORD of hosts will be a crown of glory, and a diadem of beauty, to the remnant of his people,

Arminian

Wesley: in that day—when the kingdom of Israel shall be destroyed. A diadem—God shall give eminent glory and beauty to the kingdom of Judah. The true crown replaces the drunkards’ wreath; Judah is spared for a time to see her sister’s fall and to wear what Samaria would not.

Arminian reading: remnant here is Judah still in the land, a real kingdom after Ephraim’s ruin, called to honor the Lord as its crown. Grace is given; it can still be refused (vv. 7–13). The verse is eighth-century consolation for Judah after Ephraim’s ruin, not a chart of later ages.

Reformed

Calvin: after speaking of Israel he passes to Judah and shows that amidst severe vengeance there will still be room for compassion. Although ten tribes perished, the Lord will preserve some remnant, which he will consecrate to himself. Calvin will not extend this indiscriminately to all the Jews, but to the elect who were wonderfully rescued from death.

Reformed accent: in God alone there is enough glory to supply all earthly defects. The Church is never so disfigured that the Lord does not adorn it. New splendor is promised when the multitude is diminished, that believers may not lose courage at the calamity at hand. The remnant is His consecration, not Judah’s mere survival as a block.

Key contrast Both hear a true crown after Ephraim falls. Wesley applies the diadem to the kingdom of Judah as such. Calvin will not extend the promise indiscriminately to all the Jews, but to the elect remnant the Lord consecrates to himself.

Isaiah 28:6

6and a spirit of justice to him who sits in judgment, and strength to those who turn back the battle at the gate.

Arminian

Wesley: to them who not only drive their enemies from their land, but pursue them into their own lands, and besiege them in their own cities. Justice on the bench and strength at the gate are gifts for actual officers and soldiers in Judah’s generation.

Arminian pastoral: the spirit of justice is real help for a judge who must still sit and a defender who must still fight. Grace does not turn the gate into a metaphor. The remnant of v. 5 is to use what God gives in court and in war, not to treat “that day” as a later newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: God adorns that remnant by the true art of civil government, which consists chiefly of counsel and strength. It is God who gives both. Magistrates will not be able to rule, and generals will not be able to repel enemies, unless the Lord directs them.

Reformed note: wisdom on the judgment seat and force at the gate are not Judah’s native surplus; they are the Lord’s. The church’s safety after Samaria falls is that God himself supplies what the fading crown never was.

Isaiah 28:7

7These also reel with wine and stagger with strong drink; the priest and the prophet reel with strong drink, they are swallowed by wine, they stagger with strong drink, they reel in vision, they stumble in giving judgment.

Arminian

Wesley: Judah is guilty of the same sins with Israel, therefore they also must expect the same calamities. The prophet—the teachers, who should have been patterns of sobriety to the people. They err—the prophets miscarry in their sacred employment. Stumble—the priests mistake in pronouncing the sentence of the law, which was their duty.

Arminian reading takes the wine as wine. Priests and prophets really reel, and so vision and judgment really stumble. The spared sister repeats the drunkard’s sin. Grace that left Judah standing after Ephraim’s fall is a sincere call to sobriety, not a decree that Judah cannot fall. The charge is drunken ministry in that century.

Reformed

Calvin returns to irreligious despisers who were Jews in name only. He speaks of wine and strong drink metaphorically: they were like drunk men because they wanted knowledge and sound understanding—though he does not deny that continued drunkenness brutalizes. The most grievous thing: not only the common people were drunk, but the priests who ought to have held out the light, and the prophets erred in vision and judgment.

Reformed accent: office is no shelter. Those who forsake God cannot plead a splendid title. The example of Israel did not make Judah better. The church is to see that God may spare a remnant and still find its priests blind—without turning this verse into a later polemic.

Key contrast Both indict Judah’s priests and prophets after Ephraim’s woe. Wesley reads ordinary drunkenness: teachers who should have been patterns of sobriety miscarry, and priests mistake the sentence of the law. Calvin takes wine here chiefly as metaphor for want of knowledge and sound understanding, while still granting that excess stupefies.

Isaiah 28:8

8For all tables are full of filthy vomit, with no space left.

Arminian

Tables full, no space left: the feast has nowhere clean to set a cup. The priests and prophets of v. 7 have turned the table of sacred duty into a drunkard’s board. Wesley’s “same sins with Israel” is still the frame; Judah’s uncleanness is public, not a private slip.

Arminian note: vomit is the fruit of a will that would not stop drinking. The verse is shameless eighth-century Judah, not a cartoon of later churches or nations. The call is to rise from the table while the Lord still speaks rest (v. 12).

Reformed

Calvin pursues the metaphor and draws a picture of what happens to men given up to drunkenness: they forget shame and shrink from nothing disgraceful. Under this figure Isaiah describes the whole life of the people as shameful beyond endurance—no sincerity or uprightness left. If we look at their life, no part is free from enormities; doctrine itself stinks as if polluted by vomiting and filth.

Reformed pastoral: the prophet intended to express that nothing clean remained. The church reads a people who would not be instructed by a sister’s fall. The table is Judah’s in that day; the word does not need a later headline to be true.

Isaiah 28:9

9“To whom will he teach knowledge, and to whom will he explain the message? Those who are weaned from the milk, those taken from the breast?

Arminian

Wesley: He—God. Who is there among this people that are willing to be taught the knowledge of God? A minister may as soon teach an infant as these men. The sneer may be theirs against the prophet’s simple lessons, or the prophet’s grief over hearers who will not be weaned into sense; either way the people refuse knowledge.

Arminian pastoral: willingness is the question. God sincerely teaches; they will not be taught. Infancy here is stubborn dullness, not humility. The message can be explained; the breast is an indictment. Faith still owes the hearing they will not give.

Reformed

Calvin: the disease is incurable; God has tried every method without effect. He compares them to very young infants whom it would be a waste of time to teach. This is not the infancy of 1 Peter 2:2, but men so stupefied by vice that they pay no more regard to heavenly doctrine than if they had no understanding.

Reformed note: to teach doctrine is useless among stupid and senseless persons, children not in malice but in understanding. Since they are unfit for receiving doctrine, God cannot be accused of undue severity if he resolve not to thunder in their ears any longer. The remnant of vv. 5–6 is not this sneer.

Isaiah 28:10

10For it is precept upon precept, precept upon precept, line upon line, line upon line, here a little, there a little.”

Arminian

Wesley: they must be taught like little children, because of their great dullness. Line—one line of the book after another, as children are taught to read. The singsong is either their mock of Isaiah’s ABC, or the method their dullness required. Either way the word came in small pieces they would not assemble.

Arminian reading: repetition is mercy before it is mockery. Precept upon precept is a sincere pedagogy. They could have learned line by line; they used the little bits as a joke. The church is to receive the slow lesson, not to treat “here a little” as a puzzle for later ages.

Reformed

Calvin: the Lord complains of spending labor to no purpose, as if one were to teach children who must have elementary instructions repeated and quickly forget them. He intended to express a repetition constant, unceasing, and full of annoyance. Instruction upon instruction: the same words again, here a little, there a little.

Reformed accent: קו is not a builder’s slow wall but a rule for children. God accommodated himself to their capacity; they still would not learn. The church hears patience already spent in Judah’s hearing, not a coded timetable.

Isaiah 28:11

11For by people of strange lips and with a foreign tongue the LORD will speak to this people,

Arminian

Wesley: another tongue—by people of a strange language, whom he shall bring among them, seeing they will not hear him speaking, by his prophets, in their own language. The next sermon is Assyrian in the gate. What they mocked as baby-talk will be replaced by a speech they cannot bargain with.

Arminian note: the foreign tongue is a real invader’s mouth in that century. They could have heard Isaiah; they would not. The Lord still speaks, now by a people they did not choose. This is not a newspaper of later tongues; it is the eight-century sequel to refused rest.

Reformed

Calvin views these words as relating to God, who became a barbarian to a people without understanding. By their own fault they made God, who formed our tongues, appear to be a stammerer. He does not as yet threaten them, but lays the blame on their indolence: they shut their eyes and derived no advantage from heavenly doctrine. Their infatuation in not hearing God is compared to a prodigy.

Reformed accent: strange lips are first the judgment of unused ears. A people who would not hear clear Hebrew will find the word a confused noise. The church is warned that neglected preaching turns the speaker into a stammerer before a later army ever arrives.

Isaiah 28:12

12to whom he has said, “This is rest; give rest to the weary; and this is repose”; yet they would not hear.

Arminian

Wesley: this—this doctrine. The rest—the only way in which you will find rest. The offer is plain and refused. Weary Judah is told where repose sits; “they would not hear” is a real will, not a stage direction.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere rest. God said it; they would not. The doctrine of trust, not Samaria’s wine and not a later alliance, was the rest. The verse stays with hearers in Isaiah’s generation. Grace enabled the hearing it required; they still would not. The tragedy here is refused repose.

Reformed

Calvin: the reason God appears to the Jews to be a barbarian is that they had not ears. It was to no purpose that the Lord offered them rest. Words were spoken to the deaf. Their wickedness was doubly inexcusable in refusing rest offered to them, which all men naturally desire. He invites all men to a blessed rest; unbelievers voluntarily draw down wretched uneasiness.

Reformed pastoral: nothing is clearer than the doctrine of God; it is vain to plead excuse. This rest is prepared for the weary who groan under the burden. All who seek rest any other way, running beyond the word, must be subjected to torture or wretched uncertainty. The Papists of Calvin’s illustration aside, the text’s weary are Judah’s weary who would not hear.

Isaiah 28:13

13And the word of the LORD will be to them precept upon precept, precept upon precept, line upon line, line upon line, here a little, there a little, that they may go, and fall backward, and be broken, and snared, and taken.

Arminian

Wesley: as this method has been used and was necessary, so it still shall be. As they were children in understanding, they shall still continue to be such; they shall be ever learning, and never come to the knowledge of the truth. This will be the event or consequence of their sin: they will fall backward, the worst and most dangerous way of falling, and so be broken to pieces.

Arminian close of the range: the same little lines that could have taught them become the path they trip on. Falling backward is the fruit of not hearing rest (v. 12), not a puppet-fall. Snared and taken is Assyria’s net in their own century. The weary were told the rest; they would not hear; they go and fall.

Reformed

Calvin: having spoken of voluntary stupidity, he now threatens its punishment. God will strike them with such bewilderment that they shall be totally deprived of the benefit of saving doctrine and perceive in it nothing but an empty sound. The word is not taken from them, but they are deprived of sound judgment, blind amidst the clearest light. Thus God blinds and hardens the reprobate more and more on account of their disobedience.

Reformed close: they shall fall backward, and be broken and snared. It will be impossible that evil shall not befall those who do not keep the path God pointed out. We need a twofold grace: that God would shine by his word, and that he would open understandings and dispose hearts to obedience. The snare is already in the word for Judah’s unbelief; the church is not to hunt a later prey to make the taking true.

Key contrast Both see the ABC of v. 10 become a trap. Wesley reads the fall as the event and consequence of their sin: they remain children in understanding, ever learning and never arriving, and so fall backward. Calvin reads a judicial hardening of the reprobate: God strikes them with bewilderment so that the word remains and yet they are blind in its light.

Arminian Notable voices

John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 28:1–13)

“He shews that the anger of the Lord will quickly overtake, first, Israel, and afterwards the Jews. Amidst this severe vengeance there will still be room for compassion; although ten tribes perished, the Lord will preserve some remnant, which he will consecrate to himself. It was to no purpose that the Lord offered to them rest. God will strike them with such bewilderment that they shall be totally deprived of the benefit of saving doctrine. Thus God blinds and hardens the reprobate more and more on account of their disobedience. They shall fall backward, and be broken and snared.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 28:1–13)
