Passage

Isaiah 50

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

The LORD asks Judah for a bill of divorce and names no bankrupt creditor: they were sold for iniquities, the mother sent away for transgressions. The Servant’s ear is opened; he is not rebellious, gives his back to those who strike, and is not put to shame; those who walk in darkness must trust the LORD, while torch-kindlers lie down in torment.

Isaiah 50:1

1Thus says the LORD: “Where is your mother’s certificate of divorce, with which I sent her away? Or which of my creditors is it to whom I have sold you? Behold, for your iniquities you were sold, and for your transgressions your mother was sent away.”

Arminian

Thus says the LORD: Where is your mother’s certificate of divorce, with which I sent her away? Or which of my creditors is it to whom I have sold you? Behold, for your iniquities you were sold, and for your transgressions your mother was sent away. Wesley: The scope of this and the next chapter is to vindicate God’s justice and to convince the Jews that they were the causes of their own calamities. Behold—you can blame none but yourselves and your own sins, for all your captivities and miseries.

Arminian hearing: Judah is not sold because the LORD is bankrupt; the missing bill is the point. The sending-away is real consequence of iniquities, not a feint. Shared: no creditor; sold for iniquities; mother sent away for transgressions. Historic Judah, not a later-age chart. The dereliction of the Jews is not because the Servant of 49 cannot save.

Reformed

Where is that bill of divorcement? Calvin: the Lord shews that he is not the author of the divorce, but that the people went away by their own fault and followed their lusts, so that they had utterly broken the bond of marriage. Who is the creditor to whom I sold you? You cannot bring this reproach against me; on account of your transgressions you have been sold and reduced to slavery. Did the Lord divorce his heritage? Did he make void the covenant? Certainly not; when he did not bestow upon them his wonted favor, it was a kind of divorce or rejection.

Reformed hearing: two contrasts—the wife is divorced by the husband’s fault or because she is unchaste; children are sold for the father’s poverty or by their own fault. Shared: not sold because he is bankrupt; sold for iniquities. Present want of favor looks like divorce; the Husband did not cancel the bond.

Key contrast Both refuse a bankrupt LORD and lay the sale on Judah’s iniquities. Wesley takes the sending-away as real calamity they may blame on none but themselves. Calvin asks whether the Lord made void the covenant and answers Certainly not: the divorce is the appearance when wonted favor is withheld; the people, not the Husband, broke the bond.

Isaiah 50:2

2Why, when I came, was there no man; why, when I called, was there no one to answer? Is my hand shortened, that it cannot redeem? Or have I no power to deliver? Behold, by my rebuke I dry up the sea, I make the rivers a desert; their fish stink for lack of water and die of thirst.

Arminian

Why, when I came, was there no man; why, when I called, was there no one to answer? Is my hand shortened, that it cannot redeem? Or have I no power to deliver? Behold, by my rebuke I dry up the sea, I make the rivers a desert. Wesley: Wherefore—the general accusation delivered in the last words he now proves by particular instances. When—when I, first by my prophets, came to call them to repentance. No man—that complied with my call. To answer—to come at my call. Is my hand—what is the reason of this contempt? Is it because you think I am either unwilling or unable to save you? A wilderness—as dry and fit for travelling as a wilderness.

Arminian call: the prophets’ summons is a real call to repentance; none complied. The hand is not shortened. Shared: he came; none answered; sea dried at his rebuke. Those who stayed away did not stay because he could not redeem, but because they would not come. No millennial fight; the point is historic refusal of a real call.

Reformed

Why did I come? Calvin: still it is not he who prevents them from being immediately set at liberty; they grow old and rot in their distresses, for the Lord was ready to assist them if they had not rejected his grace and kindness. He approaches by the preaching of the Word and by tokens of fatherly kindness. It is an instance of extreme ingratitude to refuse to accept the grace of God which is freely offered. By shortening hath my hand been shortened? Who would venture to plead against God that his power was diminished? Their sins were the hinderance.

Reformed call: freely offered grace refused; both the beginning and the progress of the evil arise from the people. Shared: he came; none answered; the hand is not short. Egypt’s sea is the proof he can still deliver. Calvin’s offer is not a sneer at Wesley’s prophetic call; both hear a real coming they would not meet.

Isaiah 50:3

3I clothe the heavens with blackness and make sackcloth their covering.

Arminian

I clothe the heavens with blackness and make sackcloth their covering. Wesley: I clothe—when it is necessary to save my people, I cover them with thick and dark clouds black as sackcloth, Rev. vi, 12.

Arminian covering: the same power that can darken the heavens is not the bankrupt creditor of v. 1. Shared: heavens clothed; sackcloth covering. Not a culture fight about skies; the point is the LORD who can still save. The light promised to the nations in 49:6 is not cancelled by this blackness.

Reformed

I clothe the heavens with blackness. Calvin: he mentions that thick darkness which was spread over all Egypt during the space of three days. The heaven was clothed as with a mourning dress. God, who had so miraculously assisted the fathers, was prevented by their ingratitude from granting relief to the miseries which now oppressed them.

Reformed covering: Egypt’s darkness is the token that power is not the lack. Shared: blackness; sackcloth. Ingratitude, not a shortened hand, is why relief delays. The same LORD who led out of Egypt still has a Servant whose word is light.

Isaiah 50:4

4The Lord GOD has given me the tongue of those who are taught, that I may know how to sustain with a word him who is weary. Morning by morning he awakens; he awakens my ear to hear as those who are taught.

Arminian

The Lord GOD has given me the tongue of those who are taught, that I may know how to sustain with a word him who is weary. Morning by morning he awakens; he awakens my ear to hear as those who are taught. Wesley: Given me—this and the following passages may be in some sort understood of the prophet Isaiah, but they are far more evidently and eminently verified in Christ, and indeed seem to be meant directly of him. The tongue—all ability of speaking plainly, and convincingly, and persuasively. Weary—burdened with the sense of his deplorable condition. Wakeneth—me, from time to time, and continually. To hear—he by his Divine power assists me to the practice of all his commands and my duties, with all attention and diligence.

Arminian Servant: the speaker is Christ, the taught one whose word sustains the weary. Shared: tongue given; ear wakened; the weary helped. The light for the nations in 49:6 now has a morning ear. No forced Isa 53 fork here; Wesley lands on the taught Christ, not on a later atonement chart.

Reformed

The Lord Jehovah. Calvin: after twice convicting them of guilt he adds consolation; this passage is commonly explained so as to relate to Christ, as if it had not been applicable to the Prophet. For my own part, I have no doubt that Isaiah comes forward as one who represents all the servants of God, not only those who were from the beginning, but those who should come afterwards. Hath given me the tongue of the learned, that I may know a word in season to the weary. No man is fit to teach who has not first been qualified by God; all things necessary for discharging their office are gifts of the Holy Spirit.

Reformed Servant: Christ is not denied; the Head includes the members who comfort the faint. Shared: tongue of the taught; weary sustained; morning by morning. The chief duty of the word is to soothe wretched men. None are good teachers but those who have been good scholars.

Key contrast Both hear Christ in the taught tongue that sustains the weary. Wesley takes the verses as in some sort of Isaiah, yet far more evidently and eminently verified in Christ, and indeed meant directly of him. Calvin will not treat the beating that follows as proof the text is Christ’s alone: Isaiah represents all the servants of God, then and later, organs of the Spirit.

Isaiah 50:5

5The Lord GOD has opened my ear, and I was not rebellious; I turned not backward.

Arminian

The Lord GOD has opened my ear, and I was not rebellious; I turned not backward. Wesley has no separate lemma here; the head-note still governs: he was obedient in that work, and God is present with him. The opened ear of v. 4 now answers as non-rebellion.

Arminian obedience: Christ does not turn back. The call is real; the Servant’s yes is real. Shared: ear opened; not rebellious; not turned backward. Carry the Servant-block: the same Christ who is light in 49:6 is here the one who hears and does not refuse. No millennial chart of later ears.

Reformed

The Lord Jehovah hath opened mine ear. Calvin: the opening of the ear refers not only to doctrine but to the whole calling; the Lord opens the ear not only when he declares his will, but when he powerfully affects a man’s heart and moves him to render obedience. And I was not rebellious: he undertakes nothing at random, but, being fully convinced of God’s calling, he discharges the office of a teacher, though it is laborious and difficult, because he is ready to obey.

Reformed obedience: Spirit-moved hearing, not a bare announcement. Shared: ear opened; not rebellious. The Head’s servants are made willing. Calvin’s “whosoever hath heard and learned from the Father cometh to me” is the same Christ both camps name, not a second gospel.

Isaiah 50:6

6I gave my back to those who strike, and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard; I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting.

Arminian

I gave my back to those who strike, and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard; I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting. Wesley: I gave—I patiently yielded up myself to those who smote me.

Arminian yielding: the taught Christ of v. 4 actually gives his back. This is obedience, not a forced Isa 53 atonement fork; Wesley does not go there on this verse. Shared: back, cheeks, face not hidden. Historic smiters; the Servant is not rebellious. No culture war about shame; the point is the patient yield of the one meant directly of Christ.

Reformed

I exposed my body to the smiters. Calvin: the ministers of the word cannot perform their office faithfully without being exposed to a contest with the world, and even without being fiercely assailed on all sides. My face I did not hide from shame and spitting: out of the very bosom of the Church there always spring up wicked men who insolently attack the prophets. While this doctrine belongs to all believers, it belongs especially to the teachers of the word, who ought to go before others as standard-bearers.

Reformed yielding: the Head’s servants share the spitting; Calvin names the same slanders as were directed against Christ and the Apostles, without turning the verse into a 53-style atonement argument. Shared: back given; face not hid.

Key contrast Both hear a real back given to smiters and a face not hidden. Wesley, having said the stretch is meant directly of Christ, hears patient self-yielding to those who smote him. Calvin hears the condition of faithful ministers—and of all believers—fiercely assailed, teachers as standard-bearers; he will not treat the rods as proof the speaker can only be Christ in isolation from his servants.

Isaiah 50:7

7But the Lord GOD helps me; therefore I have not been disgraced; therefore I have set my face like a flint, and I know that I shall not be put to shame.

Arminian

But the Lord GOD helps me; therefore I have not been disgraced; therefore I have set my face like a flint, and I know that I shall not be put to shame. Wesley has no separate lemma; the head-note still governs: God is present with him. Help is why the flint-face holds.

Arminian flint: the Servant knows he shall not be put to shame because the LORD helps, not because a party won. Shared: help; not disgraced; face like flint. The obedient Christ of vv. 4–6 now stands. No millennial chart of later shames; the point is the present God.

Reformed

For the Lord Jehovah will help me. Calvin: so great courage comes from God’s assistance; he is fortified against all the attacks of the world. I have set my face as a flint: the servants of God, being so shamefully treated, must have sunk had they not withstood them with a forehead of stone. It is not enough that God is willing to help us, if we do not also feel it; confidence is demanded. I shall not be confounded: special assistance is promised to godly teachers and ministers of the word.

Reformed flint: the Head’s ministers turn wholly to God. Shared: help; flint; not ashamed. Those who tremble at the contest, Calvin says, were never duly qualified—not a sneer at Wesley’s present God, but the same help felt.

Isaiah 50:8

8He who vindicates me is near. Who will contend with me? Let us stand up together. Who is my adversary? Let him come near to me.

Arminian

He who vindicates me is near. Who will contend with me? Let us stand up together. Who is my adversary? Let him come near to me. Wesley: Justifieth—God will clear up my righteousness, and shew by many and mighty signs and wonders, that I lived and died his faithful servant. Let him come—I am conscious of mine own innocency, and I know that God will give sentence for me.

Arminian vindication: Christ’s innocency is public; he lived and died the faithful Servant. Shared: the vindicator is near; adversaries may come. Not a culture court; the point is the LORD who sentences for the obedient Servant. The light of 49:6 is not a condemned light.

Reformed

He is near that justifieth me. Calvin: the Prophet mentions nothing peculiar to himself, but testifies what the Lord will always be toward faithful ministers. That any one may make this protestation, his conscience must be pure; the promise belongs only to those who have been called by God and who sincerely perform their duty. Let us stand together: so great is the force of truth that it does not dread the light of day. God, whose cause we plead, is our Judge, and will at length acquit us.

Reformed vindication: the near Justifier is the same Lord who helps in v. 7. Shared: he who justifies is near; stand together. Calvin will say in v. 9 that this block is the ministry of the Word, not a second reading of universal salvation forced back onto the Servant’s innocency.

Isaiah 50:9

9Behold, the Lord GOD helps me; who will declare me guilty? Behold, all of them will wear out like a garment; the moth will eat them up.

Arminian

Behold, the Lord GOD helps me; who will declare me guilty? Behold, all of them will wear out like a garment; the moth will eat them up. Wesley: They—mine accusers and enemies. The moth—shall be cut off and consumed by a secret curse.

Arminian moth: accusers of the faithful Servant wear out. Who will declare him guilty? The help of v. 7 answers. Shared: Lord GOD helps; moth eats them. No forced 53-fork; Wesley does not turn “guilty” into a later atonement chart. The Servant who is light remains uncondemned.

Reformed

Who is he that condemneth me? Calvin: Paul appears to allude to this passage—“It is God that justifieth; who shall condemn?” We may safely have recourse to the judgment-seat of God when we are well assured that we have obtained his righteousness by free grace through Christ. But here Isaiah handles a different subject; for he does not speak of the universal salvation of men, but of the ministry of the Word, which the Lord will defend against the attacks of wicked men. All who fight with the Word of God shall fall and vanish away; like garments they perish, consumed by worms.

Reformed moth: fierce dogs that bark at godly teachers fade, however high their authority. Shared: help; none shall condemn; moth-eaten garments. Calvin’s free-grace aside is the gospel already in the Servant, not a second gospel; the verse itself he keeps as the Word defended.

Key contrast Both hear help against those who would declare the speaker guilty. Wesley hears accusers of Christ cut off by a secret curse, the moth eating those who fought the faithful Servant. Calvin grants Paul’s Romans use—righteousness by free grace through Christ—then refuses to read that universal salvation back into this verse: here it is the ministry of the Word the Lord will not suffer to be overwhelmed.

Isaiah 50:10

10Who among you fears the LORD and obeys the voice of his servant? Let him who walks in darkness and has no light trust in the name of the LORD and rely on his God.

Arminian

Who among you fears the LORD and obeys the voice of his servant? Let him who walks in darkness and has no light trust in the name of the LORD and rely on his God. Wesley: The voice—of Christ, who is called God’s servant, by way of eminency and to intimate that though he was God, yet he would take upon himself the form of a servant. In darkness—not in sin, but in misery, that lives in a disconsolate and calamitous condition. No light—no comfort. Trust—let him fix his faith and hope in the mercy, and faithfulness, of the Lord, declared in his word, and in his interest in God, who by the mediation of this servant is reconciled to him and made his God.

Arminian darkness: misery, not a sin-ledger; the call is to trust the name and stay upon God. Shared: fear the LORD; obey the Servant’s voice; walk in darkness; trust. The Servant of 49 is here the voice to obey. Those who have no light are told to trust, not to kindle v. 11’s torches.

Reformed

Who is among you that feareth the Lord? Calvin: he directs his discourse to believers, that they may suffer themselves to be guided by the Word of God. Their number is small; yet he addresses them separately, that they may detach themselves from the mixed crowd. Let him hear the voice of his servant: God does not wish to be heard but by the voice of his ministers. By darkness he means not the ignorance or blindness of the human understanding, but the afflictions by which the children of God are almost always overwhelmed. If they have walked in darkness, they shall at length enjoy the light of the Lord.

Reformed darkness: affliction of the children of God, matching Wesley’s misery-not-sin. Shared: fear; hear the servant; trust the name. The remnant must detach from the crowd and become obedient to that Word. The light they will enjoy is the LORD’s, not a later scheme of ages.

Key contrast Both hear darkness as misery and affliction, not as sin, and both send the fearful to trust the LORD. Wesley takes “the voice of his servant” as Christ’s—God’s servant by eminency, who took the form of a servant—and the trust as interest in God who by this Servant’s mediation is reconciled. Calvin takes the voice as the ministers God employs; he will not be heard but by them; the small remnant must detach from the crowd and become obedient to that Word.

Isaiah 50:11

11Behold, all you who kindle a fire, who equip yourselves with burning torches! Walk by the light of your fire, and by the torches that you have kindled! This you have from my hand: you shall lie down in torment.

Arminian

Behold, all you who kindle a fire, who equip yourselves with burning torches! Walk by the light of your fire, and by the torches that you have kindled! This you have from my hand: you shall lie down in torment. Wesley: All ye—you that reject the light which God hath set up, and seek for comfort and safety, by your own inventions. Walk—use your utmost endeavours to get comfort from these devices. This—this shall be the fruit of all, you shall receive nothing but vexation and misery.

Arminian torches: the light God set up is the Servant’s voice in v. 10; own inventions cannot replace it. Shared: kindle a fire; walk by it; from his hand; lie down. No culture war about who holds a torch; the point is rejecting the light already given. Those who perish do not perish because no light was set up, but because they would not trust it.

Reformed

Lo, all of you kindle a fire. Calvin: he upbraids them with choosing to kindle for themselves their own light, instead of drawing near to the light of God—the consolation of the Word versus empty words of comfort. They rejected this light in order to kindle another, and shall be consumed by this light as by a conflagration. Walk in the light of your fire: ironical permission denoting disappointment. From my hand: men who have forsaken the light of the Word, and who seek consolation from some other quarter, shall miserably perish.

Reformed torches: own light is not the Servant’s light. Shared: fire kindled; lie down in sorrow. The nations’ light from 49:6 is still the LORD’s; sparks of our making do not guide. No dispensational chart; the fork is God’s light versus ours.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on a bill of divorce, a people sold for iniquities not for the LORD’s bankruptcy, a Servant’s opened ear and unhidden face, and those who walk in darkness versus the torches they kindle—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Isaiah 50:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“Thus saith the Lord—the scope of this and the next chapter is to vindicate God’s justice and to convince the Jews that they were the causes of their own calamities. Behold—you can blame none but yourselves and your own sins, for all your captivities and miseries. When I, first by my prophets, came to call them to repentance. No man—that complied with my call. Is my hand—what is the reason of this contempt? Is it because you think I am either unwilling or unable to save you? Given me—this and the following passages may be in some sort understood of the prophet Isaiah, but they are far more evidently and eminently verified in Christ, and indeed seem to be meant directly of him. The tongue—all ability of speaking plainly, and convincingly, and persuasively. I gave—I patiently yielded up myself to those who smote me. Justifieth—God will clear up my righteousness, and shew by many and mighty signs and wonders, that I lived and died his faithful servant. The voice—of Christ, who is called God’s servant, by way of eminency and to intimate that though he was God, yet he would take upon himself the form of a servant. In darkness—not in sin, but in misery. Trust—let him fix his faith and hope in the mercy, and faithfulness, of the Lord, who by the mediation of this servant is reconciled to him and made his God. All ye—you that reject the light which God hath set up, and seek for comfort and safety, by your own inventions.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 50:1–11)

Reformed Notable voices

“Where is that bill of divorcement? The Lord shews that he is not the author of the divorce, but that the people went away by their own fault. Did the Lord divorce his heritage? Did he make void the covenant? Certainly not; when he did not bestow upon them his wonted favor, it was a kind of divorce or rejection. Lo, for your iniquities ye have been sold. Why did I come? The Lord was ready to assist them, if they had not rejected his grace and kindness; it is extreme ingratitude to refuse to accept the grace of God which is freely offered. This passage is commonly explained so as to relate to Christ; for my own part, I have no doubt that Isaiah comes forward as one who represents all the servants of God. Hath given me the tongue of the learned, that I may know a word in season to the weary. The Lord opens the ear when he powerfully affects a man’s heart and moves him to render obedience. I exposed my body to the smiters: the ministers of the word cannot perform their office faithfully without being fiercely assailed. He is near that justifieth me: here Isaiah handles not the universal salvation of men, but the ministry of the Word. Who is among you that feareth the Lord? Let him hear the voice of his servant; God does not wish to be heard but by the voice of his ministers. By darkness he means not ignorance, but the afflictions by which the children of God are almost always overwhelmed. Lo, all of you kindle a fire: they chose their own light instead of the light of God; men who have forsaken the light of the Word shall miserably perish.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 50:1–11)
