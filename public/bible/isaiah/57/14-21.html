Passage

Isaiah 57

Verses 14–21 · ESV · Voices

After the idolatry of 57:1–13, a voice commands the way to be built up. The High and Holy One dwells with the contrite, will not contend forever, heals the backslider, and creates “Peace, peace” for the far and the near. The wicked remain a tossing sea: no peace for them.

Isaiah 57:14

14And it shall be said, “Build up, build up, prepare the way, remove every obstruction from my people's way.”

Arminian

Wesley: God will raise up a man who shall say with authority and efficacy. Cast up—make causeways for safe passage, and remove all that may hinder their return.

Arminian hope: the road home is commanded, not merely wished. Obstacles can be taken up. “My people’s way” is restoration language—return from exile, and, in the gospel’s width, a path cleared for whoever will walk it under grace.

Reformed

Calvin: lest the land-promise of v. 13 look ridiculous once they are driven out, a second promise is added—they shall be brought back. He meets the doubt of a long banishment. The words agree with Isaiah 40:1–4. He addresses Cyrus and Darius, whose minds the Lord inspired to open the path.

Reformed comfort: if the Lord determines to bring back his people, there must be a road. Every bar is removed because he has promised the return and is their leader in the journey.

Isaiah 57:15

15For thus says the One who is high and lifted up, who inhabits eternity, whose name is Holy: “I dwell in the high and holy place, and also with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit, to revive the spirit of the lowly, and to revive the heart of the contrite.

Arminian

The High and Holy One is not too high for the crushed. He dwells in two places at once: eternity, and the lowly heart. Revival is the purpose—spirit and heart made alive again.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere nearness: whoever is contrite may find this Guest. Height does not cancel welcome. The same holiness that judged the high beds of vv. 7–8 now stoops to revive.

Reformed

Calvin: God is actually in heaven, yet does not overlook human affairs. He is anxious about the salvation of men and dwells with the afflicted (Ps 138:6). He is unlike us—no shadow of change—and will perform what he promised. Wicked men, though oppressed, remain fierce; hearts must be lowly if they expect assistance.

Reformed confession: he descends even to the lifeless to breathe new life. Twice the lowly spirit is named, so that the promises belong to the meek who lay aside haughtiness, not to the unbroken.

Isaiah 57:16

16For I will not contend forever, nor will I always be angry; for the spirit would grow faint before me, and the breath of life that I made.

Arminian

Wesley: I will not proceed to the utmost severity with sinful men. Anger has a term; the Maker remembers the breath he made.

Arminian pastoral: wrath is real and so is its limit. Faintness of spirit is a reason God himself names. The door of healing (v. 18) is not slammed by an endless lawsuit. Sinners may still turn while he is not always angry.

Reformed

Calvin: amidst captivity they thought God an enemy. He shows punishments will be short; he will make abatement of what he might demand. “Angry” is spoken according to our flesh, to lead to repentance, yet we must not be swallowed by despair as if he would never be reconciled.

Reformed accent: he deals gently because he perceives our weakness (Ps 103:13–14; 78:38–39). He will not try his strength against breath that vanishes. The Church, not the reprobate in their hardness, is especially directed to this promise.

Isaiah 57:17

17Because of the iniquity of his unjust gain I was angry, I struck him; I hid my face and was angry, but he went on backsliding in the way of his own heart.

Arminian

Wesley: covetousness, of which the Jews were eminently guilty, comprehends all the sins for which God contended. Yet he was not reformed, but trespassed more and more.

Arminian gravity: hiding and striking did not create new holiness. Backsliding in the way of his own heart is a chosen continuance. Discipline can be refused; that refusal is why healing in v. 18 is sheer mercy, not a wage for finally improving.

Reformed

Calvin: obstinate wickedness; no complaint of immoderate cruelty. Lust or covetousness—the source of evils (1 Tim 6:10)—or every sinful desire. God punishes not only outward crimes but wicked dispositions. “I hid myself” is according to our opinion, when we imagine him an enemy.

Reformed note: the people were in no degree made better; violent remedies could not bring them back. They were desperate. The next verse will therefore magnify mercy, not their reform.

Isaiah 57:18

18I have seen his ways, but I will heal him; I will lead him and restore comfort to him and his mourners,

Arminian

Wesley: mourners—those humbled under God’s hand, who mourn in Zion for their own and others’ sins. Healing is promised to seen ways, not hidden ones.

Classical Arminians: “I have seen” does not end in abandonment. Lead and restore are verbs of grace that meet the humbled. Comfort is for mourners, not for the still-defiant of vv. 3–13. The invitation to be among those mourners remains sincere.

Reformed

Calvin magnifies mercy: gracious to a people obstinate and rebellious, he anticipates them by grace. Chastisements could not drive them to repentance without the Spirit; they would have grown harder. “I have seen his ways, and will heal him”—an image of mankind, diseased almost beyond hope, then healed and kept.

Reformed sequence: healing, then consolation. Those not dissatisfied with their vices can look for nothing but wrath. The mourners are the few good men who groaned under wrath while others indulged.

Isaiah 57:19

19creating the fruit of the lips. Peace, peace, to the far and to the near,” says the Lord, “and I will heal him.

Arminian

Wesley: I create—by my almighty power. Peace not wrought by men’s hands, but by God’s lips. Doubling signifies certainty and abundance. Far off—Gentiles far from God, as well as Jews near (Ps 148:14).

Arminian gospel: peace is spoken to both distances. The house of prayer for all peoples (56:7) and this double peace belong together. Far and near may receive what God creates; healing is held out as a real word, not a private code.

Reformed

Calvin: he will cause them to hear glad tidings of peace; the ministry later belongs to apostles as ambassadors of reconciliation (2 Cor 5:20). Repetition expresses certainty and continuance. Captives “far off” thought it belonged only to those at home; he says they shall share the grace.

Reformed fulfillment: Paul glances here when Christ “brought peace to them that are near, and to them that are far off” (Eph 2:17). Jews cast off resembled Gentiles; the gift of peace and healing is God’s, not the Church’s achievement.

Key contrast Both preach created peace to far and near. Arminians typically stress a sincere word of peace the far as well as the near may receive; Reformed readers typically stress God creating that peace by his lips and actually gathering both distances in Christ.

Isaiah 57:20

20But the wicked are like the tossing sea; for it cannot be quiet, and its waters toss up mire and dirt.

Arminian

Wesley: their minds are restless, hurried with lusts and passions, with guilt, and the dread of divine vengeance.

Arminian warning: the sea’s tossing is the inner life of those who will not take the peace just offered. Quiet is possible in the healing of v. 18; remaining a tossing sea is not a fate assigned before the word is heard. Mire is what a refused peace leaves behind.

Reformed

Calvin: good men shall have peace; the wicked continual war. They cannot share this promise by false use of God’s name. They wish ease and a lulled conscience, but until reconciled they are at war within. The sea is troubled even without a storm; billows fight each other.

Reformed image: conscience is the most agonizing executioner. The wicked are terrified by their own guilt. No middle course: accept this peace or be miserably distressed.

Isaiah 57:21

21There is no peace,” says my God, “for the wicked.”

Arminian

Wesley: though they may have prosperity, they have no share in this inward, spiritual, everlasting peace.

Arminian close: “my God” still speaks a true no. Prosperity is not peace. The sentence is a warning meant to be heard in time—the same God who heals mourners (v. 18) will not call unrest peace. Historic Israel’s wicked, and every later reader who stays a tossing sea, stand under one word.

Reformed

Calvin: in vain the reprobate seek peace; God threatens war. They cannot acknowledge him while they reject his prophet. The prophet declares he has a command to declare perpetual war against them.

Reformed close: God is the only author of peace. This threatening tears from them their dearest pleasures. Without reconciliation, conscience will never cease to annoy. The High and Holy One who dwells with the contrite is the same God who withholds peace from the wicked.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the contrite, healing, and peace to the far and the near—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Isaiah 57:14–21.

Arminian Notable voices

“Cast up—Make causeways… and remove all things which may hinder them in their return. I will not proceed to the utmost severity with sinful men. Mourners—To those who are humbled under God’s hand, that mourn in Zion… Peace—That peace which is not wrought by mens hands, but only by God’s lips or word… Far off—To the Gentiles who are far from God, as well as to the Jews… There is no peace… they have no share in this inward, and spiritual, and everlasting peace.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 57:14, 16, 18–19, 21)

Reformed Notable voices

“I dwell in the high and holy place, and with him who is of a contrite and humble spirit, to revive the spirit of the humble. Because not for ever will I strive… he takes into account our weakness. I have seen his ways, and will heal him… he is gracious to that people, though obstinate. Peace, peace to him that is far off, and to him that is near… Paul appears to have glanced at this passage (Eph 2:17). There is no peace to the wicked.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 57:14–21)
