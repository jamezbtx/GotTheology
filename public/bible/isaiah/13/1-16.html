Passage

Isaiah 13

Verses 1–16 · ESV · Voices

Isaiah the son of Amoz sees the oracle concerning Babylon: a signal on a bare hill, the LORD of hosts mustering a host from a distant land, the day of the LORD near as destruction from the Almighty. Hands go feeble and hearts melt; the land is made a desolation and its sinners destroyed from it. Sun, moon, and stars go dark in the overthrow of proud Babylon; people become rarer than gold; fugitives flee like a hunted gazelle; the sword and the atrocities of ancient siege fall on the city.

Isaiah 13:1

1The oracle concerning Babylon which Isaiah the son of Amoz saw.

Arminian

The oracle is a burden concerning Babylon—the city and empire which Wesley names as fallen by Cyrus. This title is commonly given to sad prophecies, grievous to them on whom they are laid. Isaiah sees a real word against a real empire, not a cipher for a modern map.

Arminian pastoral: the warning is a real call. Babylon’s pomp is chosen arrogance under a historic judgment. The son of Amoz is not decoding a twenty-first-century newspaper; he is laying God’s burden on proud Babylon.

Reformed

Calvin: there is nothing of which it is more difficult to convince men than that the providence of God governs this world. This word makes known to the godly that the Lord appoints all calamities and afflictions, that every one may suffer the punishment of his own sin. It was difficult to believe a monarchy so flourishing could be overturned.

Reformed pastoral: the oracle is not fortune. Historic Babylon falls by the secret and wonderful purpose of God. Sovereignty here is not a culture-war speech; it is the Lord ruling while an empire boasts.

Key contrast Both hear a burden against historic Babylon, not a modern state. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s sad prophecy of the city and empire fallen by Cyrus—a real warning laid on chosen pride. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: the Lord appoints the calamity, and providence, not chance, overturns the empire.

Isaiah 13:2

2On a bare hill raise a signal; cry aloud to them; wave the hand for them to enter the gates of the nobles.

Arminian

Wesley: a banner to gather soldiers together; a mountain whence it may be discerned at a considerable distance. He seems to intimate that their enemies should come from the mountainous country of Media. Shake—beckon to them with your hand, that they may go and fight against Babylon, and take it, and so enter in to the palaces of the king and his princes.

Arminian pastoral: the signal is a public summons toward Media, not toward a modern superpower. Cry aloud is a real call into historic gates. Babylon’s nobles are named because their pride is about to be entered.

Reformed

Calvin: a private man here commands the Medes and Persians, assembles armies, orders a banner to be lifted up. This should lead us to consider the majesty of God, in whose name he spoke. They will be ready at the bidding of God, because they are moved by his secret influence.

Reformed pastoral: the prophet’s cry is God’s. When God has spoken about what shall happen, we ought to entertain no doubt concerning it. The road into the gates of the nobles will be plain when he gives the signal.

Isaiah 13:3

3I myself have commanded my consecrated ones, and have summoned my mighty men to execute my anger, my proudly exulting ones.

Arminian

Wesley: sanctified ones—the Medes and Persians, so called because they were set apart by God for this holy work of executing his just vengeance. Mighty ones—those whom I have made mighty for this work. Highness—in my glory, in the doing of that work which tends to the advancement of my glory. Tho’ the Medes had no regard to God, but only to their own ends.

Arminian pastoral: God sets them apart for vengeance; their own ends are still theirs. They are not holy in motive; they are consecrated as instruments. Babylon’s judgment is just, and the warning still calls the proud to humble themselves before the anger they chose to provoke.

Reformed

Calvin: he calls the Medes and Persians sanctified ones, those whom he has prepared. He does not throw down men at random on the earth; he regulates and controls the violent passions of the reprobate. They are set apart and prepared to execute his will, though they had no such intention. Though prompted by their own ambition, pride, and cruelty, God directed them, without knowing it, to execute his judgment.

Reformed pastoral: “I myself have commanded” is the LORD’s decree. Even pagan mighty men serve as weapons of his indignation. The host is summoned; the anger is his.

Key contrast Both hear Medes and Persians as God’s instruments though they do not aim at God. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s “own ends”—set apart for just vengeance, yet still pursuing their own campaign. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: God directs them without their knowing, and even the passions of the reprobate are ruled to execute his judgment.

Isaiah 13:4

4The sound of a tumult is on the mountains as of a great multitude! The sound of an uproar of kingdoms, of nations gathering together! The LORD of hosts is mustering a host for battle.

Arminian

Wesley: nations—the Medes and Persians and other nations which served under them in this war. The sound is of a real host gathering against Babylon, not of a riddle about another continent.

Arminian pastoral: God summons the host, and the uproar is meant to be heard. Kingdoms gather; the warning is a real call. Proud Babylon is told that the LORD of hosts is mustering—so the arrogant may still wail while there is time.

Reformed

Calvin: the Lord of hosts mustereth the host of the battle; the various nations are moved by God’s direction, and they do nothing but according to his command, as if some earthly general were to draw up his forces.

Reformed pastoral: the tumult on the mountains is mustered, not accidental. Even pagan instruments serve his decree. The LORD of hosts is not a spectator of the noise.

Isaiah 13:5

5They come from a distant land, from the end of the heavens, the LORD and the weapons of his indignation, to destroy the whole land.

Arminian

Wesley: they come from the ends of the earth under heaven, which is not to be understood strictly. The weapons—the Medes and Persians, who were but a rod in God’s hand, and the instruments of his anger. Land—of Babylon.

Arminian pastoral: distant land, end of the heavens: far, not everywhere, and not a modern map. The land to be destroyed is Babylon’s. The rod is real; so is the anger Babylon earned by chosen pomp.

Reformed

Calvin: the operations of war do not spring up at random from the earth; God rules on high. Armed men are nothing else than the weapons of his indignation. They will come from a distant country to overturn the monarchy of Babylon. There is no reason why we should promise to ourselves a lasting and prosperous condition.

Reformed pastoral: the LORD and the weapons of his indignation come together. Pagan armies remain vessels of anger in his hand, to destroy the whole land of that empire.

Isaiah 13:6

6Wail, for the day of the LORD is near; as destruction from the Almighty it will come!

Arminian

Wesley is silent on this verse; nearest: he calls the burden a sad prophecy laid on Babylon, and later heaps words “to signify the extremity of his anger.” Wail, for the day of the LORD is near—against that empire, as destruction from the Almighty.

Arminian pastoral: the warning is a real call to howl, not a license to decode modern headlines. The day of the LORD here is historic judgment on proud Babylon, and the pattern of God’s day whenever he arises against chosen arrogance.

Reformed

Calvin: he calls it the day of the Lord, because when the Lord delays his judgment, he appears to cease from the discharge of his office. He has his own appointed time. The inhabitants of Babylon will have good reason not only to cry but to howl; they shall learn by their own destruction how appropriately God is called Shaddai, strong and powerful to destroy.

Reformed pastoral: delay is not absence. Destruction from the Almighty will come at his time against Babylon. The LORD of hosts musters the day as surely as he musters the host.

Isaiah 13:7

7Therefore all hands will be feeble, and every human heart will melt.

Arminian

Wesley, on the panic of the taking: amazed—to see so impregnable a city as Babylon, so easily and unexpectedly taken. Hands go feeble and hearts melt because the burden has reached the body.

Arminian pastoral: pride that felt impregnable can still go slack. The melting is honest terror under a real warning. Babylon’s chosen pomp does not keep a pulse steady when God is against it.

Reformed

Calvin: the power of the Lord to destroy the inhabitants of Babylon will be so great that they shall have no means of withstanding his anger. It is in the power of God to soften hearts, and to crush, loosen, or enfeeble hands or arms. How vain is that confidence which we place in outward resources.

Reformed pastoral: melting hearts are the Lord’s doing, not a mood. Fortifications cannot hold a people God has made faint. The day of the LORD unmakes courage on the spot.

Isaiah 13:8

8They will be dismayed: pangs and agony will seize them; they will be in anguish like a woman in labor. They will look aghast at one another; their faces will be aflame.

Arminian

Wesley (listed with the faint hands of v. 7): flames—Hebrew faces of flame, inflamed with rage and torment. Amazed—to see so impregnable a city so easily and unexpectedly taken. Pangs like labor are the body’s confession that the day has arrived.

Arminian pastoral: terror is honest; it can still be a call to humble oneself. Do not make a spectacle of Babylon’s anguish. Faces aflame are the fruit of chosen arrogance under judgment, not a scene to enjoy or to aim at a modern people.

Reformed

Calvin: pangs and sorrows shall take hold of them. The comparison of a woman that travaileth denotes not only the intensity of the grief, but likewise the suddenness. Faces of flames: when we are in agony the face glows. This kind of terror, for which there was no apparent cause, the ancients called a panic; it proceeded from God.

Reformed pastoral: the labor-pains are from God. Sudden, severe, and aimed at a city that thought itself safe. The LORD of hosts can unmake a warrior’s face without a visible blow.

Isaiah 13:9

9Behold, the day of the LORD comes, cruel, with wrath and fierce anger, to make the land a desolation and to destroy its sinners from it.

Arminian

Wesley: behold—divers words are heaped together, to signify the extremity of his anger. Cruel, wrath, fierce anger, desolation, sinners destroyed from the land: the heap is for Babylon’s day.

Arminian pastoral: the day of the LORD here is historic judgment on Babylon’s sinners, not a chart for decoding headlines. The warning remains a real call: put away chosen pomp before the land is made a desolation.

Reformed

Calvin: the day of the Lord is at hand to terrify those who are at ease. Why is it called cruel? What could wicked men imagine to be in God but the utmost severity? He shall destroy the sinners thereof out of it—not all men without distinction, but the ungodly and wicked who inhabited Babylon.

Reformed pastoral: “cruel” is how the ungodly meet the Judge. The land made desolate is Babylon’s. God comes as a severe Judge to those at ease in their sin; the godly hear consolation that the oppressor will not sit unpunished.

Isaiah 13:10

10For the stars of the heavens and their constellations will not give their light; the sun will be dark at its rising, and the moon will not shed its light.

Arminian

Wesley: constellations—which consist of many stars, and therefore give a greater sight. Darkened—all things shall look darkly and dismally; men shall have no comfort or hope. Going forth—as soon as he rises. As soon as they have any appearance or hope of amendment, they shall be instantly disappointed.

Arminian pastoral: sun, moon, and stars fail as the empire’s lights go out. Follow Wesley: this is political night and lost hope, not a forced end-times chart. When God hides comfort from proud Babylon, amendment dies at sunrise.

Reformed

Calvin: the prophets are accustomed to add to their threatenings extravagant modes of speaking, which place the anger of God before our eyes; yet the expressions do not go beyond the dreadful nature of what took place. When the sun and moon and stars shine, God may be said to cheer us; the darkness conveys that God, by hiding his face, cast those with whom he was angry into the darkness of sorrow.

Reformed pastoral: cosmic language is prophetic weight for a political overthrow under God’s hidden face. Follow Calvin: the figures are strong because the judgment is. They are not a newspaper decoder, and they are not a millennial camp fight.

Isaiah 13:11

11I will punish the world for its evil, and the wicked for their iniquity; I will put an end to the pomp of the arrogant, and lay low the pompous pride of the ruthless.

Arminian

Wesley: the world—the Babylonish empire, which is called the world, as the Roman empire afterwards was, because it was extended to a great part of the world. Evil, iniquity, pomp, pride: the world in view is that empire’s reach, not a twenty-first-century map.

Arminian pastoral: the pomp of the arrogant is chosen arrogance under judgment. The warning is a real call to put the pomp down. God lays low the ruthless; he does not hand us a slogan against a modern nation.

Reformed

Calvin: he does not speak of the whole world; but as Babylon was the seat of the most powerful of all monarchies, he gives to it on that account the name of the world. I will cause the arrogancy of the proud to cease. God cannot endure any arrogancy. Chiefly by their pride was the wrath of God kindled.

Reformed pastoral: the LORD lays low the pompous pride of the ruthless. Pride is the match that kindles wrath; the decree will put an end to it. Even this “world” is Babylon’s world under his hand.

Isaiah 13:12

12I will make people more rare than fine gold, and mankind than the gold of Ophir.

Arminian

Wesley: more precious—the city and nation shall be so depopulated. People rarer than fine gold is the cost of Babylon’s chosen pride, spoken of that city and nation.

Arminian pastoral: depopulation is not a metaphor to weaponize. It is the grief of a judged empire. Hear the warning; do not turn scarcity of lives into a culture-war speech.

Reformed

Calvin: he describes how cruel and savage will be the war carried on against Babylon. The enemies will be so eager to shed blood that it will be impossible to rescue a man out of their hands at any price; they will choose rather to kill than to accept a ransom.

Reformed pastoral: men priced above Ophir because so few remain. The LORD has said it of Babylon. Sovereignty here is judgment on a ruthless empire, not a permit to prize the slaughter.

Isaiah 13:13

13Therefore I will make the heavens tremble, and the earth will be shaken out of its place, at the wrath of the LORD of hosts in the day of his fierce anger.

Arminian

Wesley: therefore—a poetical and prophetical description of great horrors and confusions, as if heaven and earth were about to meet together. Follow him: the sky-language is poetry for horror, not a chart of the sky.

Arminian pastoral: the day of his fierce anger is Babylon’s day, and the pattern of every day when God arises against pride. The warning still calls the arrogant to wail rather than to decode the heavens.

Reformed

Calvin: another figure, not only to terrify the wicked, but to afford consolation to the godly. Though heaven and earth be moved, that the ungodly may be shaken and destroyed, nevertheless this will take place. The Lord will move both heaven and earth rather than not cast them down headlong.

Reformed pastoral: at the wrath of the LORD of hosts the earth is not secure for tyrants. The shake is his, in the day of his fierce anger. Cosmic tremor here serves the historic overthrow, not a headline.

Isaiah 13:14

14And like a hunted gazelle, or like sheep with none to gather them, each will turn to his own people, and each will flee to his own land.

Arminian

Wesley: it—Babylon. A roe—fearful in itself, especially when it is pursued by the hunter. A sheep—in a most forlorn condition. Every man—those soldiers of other nations whom she had hired to assist her.

Arminian pastoral: hired troops flee to their own lands. Babylon cannot keep the flock she bought. Fear is a real summons; each turns away because the city they served is under the hunter.

Reformed

Calvin: auxiliary troops will be of no avail to the Babylonians. They will all be like roes, which are timorous creatures, and like scattered sheep, so that they will neither repair to their standards nor preserve any order. The hearts of men are in the hand of God in such a manner that those who formerly boasted of being bold become effeminate.

Reformed pastoral: God undoes courage as easily as he gives it. Foreign help scatters because he has mustered a different host. The gazelle runs on his decree, not on luck.

Isaiah 13:15

15Whoever is found will be thrust through, and whoever is caught will fall by the sword.

Arminian

Wesley: found—in Babylon, at the taking of it. Whoever is found, whoever is caught: the sword is at the historic taking, not a parable aimed at a modern street.

Arminian pastoral: be honest—this is war-judgment. Do not sensationalize it. Do not copy it. Do not turn a Babylonian siege into a speech against a living people. The warning still calls the proud to repent of the pomp that brought the sword.

Reformed

Calvin: none shall escape from Babylon; all who shall be there shall perish. Xenophon relates that, by the command of Cyrus, they slew every one that they met in the beginning of the night. The prediction extends farther; that slaughter was only the forerunner of others.

Reformed pastoral: the thrust-through is the taking of the city under God’s judgment. The LORD of hosts mustered the host; he is not a spectator of the sword. Name the fall without relish, and without making the invaders’ cruelty a model.

Isaiah 13:16

16Their infants will be dashed in pieces before their eyes; their houses will be plundered and their wives ravished.

Arminian

Wesley is silent on this verse; nearest: found in Babylon at the taking of it, and later, they shall have no pity on the fruit of the womb. The prophet tells the truth of ancient siege: infants dashed, houses plundered, wives ravished.

Arminian pastoral: we do not defend copying the violence. We do not use it as culture-war fuel. We grieve, and we hear a warning against Babylon’s chosen pomp that brought this day. Honesty without sensationalism is the only pastoral tone this verse will bear.

Reformed

Calvin: he draws a picture of extreme cruelty. It is the utmost pitch of ferocity exercised by an invading army, when no age is spared, and infants, whose age makes it impossible for them to defend themselves, are slain in the sight of their parents. To the same purpose is the plundering of houses and the ravishing of wives, when enemies have forgotten all humanity. The Lord, though he is not cruel, makes use of agents who are so cruel; he acts righteously even by the agency of wicked men, and is not stained with their wickedness.

Reformed pastoral: name the horror without relish. God judges historic Babylon; he is not stained by the invaders’ crimes. This is not a permit to imitate them. War-judgment is spoken so the godly would know the oppressor would not sit unpunished—not so we would learn their ferocity.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the oracle against Babylon, the day of the LORD, and the LORD of hosts mustering a host—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Isaiah 13:1–16.

Arminian Notable voices

“The burden—This title is commonly given to sad prophecies, which indeed are grievous burdens to them on whom they are laid. Babylon—Of the city and empire of Babylon by Cyrus. Sanctified ones—The Medes and Persians, so called, because they were set apart by God, for this holy work of executing his just vengeance. Mighty ones—Those whom I have made mighty for this work. Tho’ the Medes had no regard to God, but only to their own ends. The weapons—The Medes and Persians, who were but a rod in God’s hand, and the instruments of his anger. Land—Of Babylon. Darkened—All things shall look darkly and dismally; men shall have no comfort or hope. The world—The Babylonish empire, which is called the world, as the Roman empire afterwards was, because it was extended to a great part of the world. Therefore—A poetical and prophetical description of great horrors and confusions, as if heaven and earth were about to meet together.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament, Isaiah 13

Reformed Notable voices

“There is nothing of which it is more difficult to convince men than that the providence of God governs this world. He calls the Medes and Persians sanctified ones, set apart and prepared to execute his will, though they had no such intention. Though they were prompted to battle by their own ambition, pride, and cruelty, yet God directed them, without knowing it, to execute his judgment. The Lord of hosts mustereth the host of the battle; the various nations are moved by God’s direction, and they do nothing but according to his command. Armed men are nothing else than the weapons of his indignation. He calls it the day of the Lord, because when the Lord delays his judgment, he appears to cease from the discharge of his office. As Babylon was the seat of the most powerful of all monarchies, he gives to it on that account the name of the world. God cannot endure any arrogancy. The prophets are accustomed to add to their threatenings extravagant modes of speaking, which place the anger of God, as it were, before our eyes. He draws a picture of extreme cruelty. The Lord acts righteously even by the agency of wicked men, and is not stained with their wickedness.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah 13:1–16
