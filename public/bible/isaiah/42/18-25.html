Passage

Isaiah 42

Verses 18–25 · ESV · Voices

After the new song and the warrior of 42:10–17, the address turns: Hear, you deaf, and look, you blind, that you may see. The servant and messenger here are not the Christ-Servant of 42:1–9 but historic Judah—those who had the Law, saw many things, and would not observe. The LORD was pleased, for his righteousness’ sake, to magnify his law and make it glorious; yet this is a people plundered and looted, trapped in holes, spoil with none to say, “Restore!” Who gave Jacob to the looter? The LORD, against whom they sinned; he poured the heat of his anger, and they did not take it to heart.

Isaiah 42:18

18Hear, you deaf, and look, you blind, that you may see!

Arminian

Hear, you deaf, and look, you blind, that you may see! Wesley: Hear—O you, whosoever you are, who resist this clear light.

Arminian hearing: the summons is to whoever resists light already given. Shared: deafness is willful, not a lack of light. Historic Judah under the word, not a newspaper crowd. Do not invent open theism: the light is already clear; they shut their eyes. The servant of v. 19 will name who this deafness belongs to.

Reformed

O ye deaf, hear. Calvin: a different sense of blind and deaf than v. 16—those who shut their eyes in the midst of light, deaf who refuse to hear. Directly the Jews (Isaiah 6:9; Matthew 13:13), in some measure Gentiles. Fault wholly with men: nothing hinders but that they are deaf and blind; accompanied by malice and ingratitude.

Reformed hearing: the blindness is malice in the light, not darkness from want of revelation. Shared with Wesley: they resist what is already clear. Skip a mild fork on “whosoever” versus “especially the Jews”: both still address historic hearers who will not look. Match v. 16 of the prior unit only to mark the contrast—there God leads the blind; here they refuse to see.

Isaiah 42:19

19Who is blind but my servant, or deaf as my messenger whom I send? Who is blind as my dedicated one, or blind as the servant of the LORD?

Arminian

Who is blind but my servant, or deaf as my messenger whom I send? Who is blind as my dedicated one, or blind as the servant of the LORD? Wesley: My servant—the Jews, who will not receive their Messiah. Messenger—my messengers, singular for plural: the priests and other teachers appointed to instruct my people. The Lord’s servant—the most eminent teachers and rulers of the Jews, called and obliged to be the Lord’s servants in a special manner.

Shared identity, not a fork: this servant is historic Judah, the Jews who will not receive their Messiah—not the Christ-Servant of 42:1–9. Wesley and Calvin agree on that reading. The messenger is the priests and teachers; the dedicated one is the eminent rulers who ought to have seen. Historic Jews under the Law, not a living nation as modern proxy. Do not invent a millennial Israel as the camp split.

Reformed

Who is blind but my servant? Calvin: not a retort against the prophets; not Isaiah; not Christ—that has nothing to do with the meaning. Jews who had the Law and so ought to have seen more clearly; then priests occupying the highest rank whose office was to interpret the Law (Malachi 2:7). Servant = the people; messenger = the priests. Perfect = those who ought to have been perfect, who had a perfect rule of righteousness. Nearer to God and higher rank = less excusable.

Reformed hearing: Calvin expressly refuses Isaiah and Christ as this servant. Shared with Wesley: the servant is the Jews; the messenger is the priests. No keyContrast on identity. The Christ-Servant of 42:1–9 is another figure; this unit indicts those who had the Law and shut their eyes. Higher rank makes them less, not more, excusable.

Isaiah 42:20

20He sees many things, but does not observe them; his ears are open, but he does not hear.

Arminian

He sees many things, but does not observe them; his ears are open, but he does not hear. Wesley: Heareth not—thou dost not seriously consider the plain word, and the wonderful works of God.

Arminian hearing: the fault is not lack of revelation but failure to consider word and works already given. Shared: eyes and ears are open; observation is refused. Historic Judah who saw many things, not a newspaper. Match v. 19: the same servant who had the Law still will not hear.

Reformed

Seeing many things. Calvin: double blindness. Explains the nature of this blindness. Jews by wicked contempt quenched God’s light. Double condemnation versus Gentiles.

Reformed hearing: they had light and quenched it; that is why their condemnation is heavier than the Gentiles’. Shared with Wesley: the word and works were plain; they would not consider. Skip a fork: both camps refuse to make this a deity who had not spoken. The blindness of v. 18 is here shown as contempt, not ignorance.

Isaiah 42:21

21The LORD was pleased, for his righteousness’ sake, to magnify his law and make it glorious.

Arminian

The LORD was pleased, for his righteousness’ sake, to magnify his law and make it glorious. Wesley: Well pleased—although God might justly destroy thee suddenly, yet he will patiently wait for thy repentance, that he may be gracious; and that not for thy sake, but for the glory of his own faithfulness, in fulfilling that covenant which he made with thy pious progenitors. Magnify—he will maintain the honour of his law, and therefore is not forward to destroy you, who profess the true religion, lest his law should upon that occasion be exposed to contempt.

Arminian hearing: patience waits for repentance; the motive is covenant-faithfulness to the progenitors, not Judah’s desert. Shared: the law is magnified for the LORD’s righteousness, not theirs. Do not invent open theism: Wesley’s God might justly destroy and chooses to wait—he is not discovering whether they will repent. Historic Israel under the Law, not a living proxy.

Reformed

The Lord is well pleased. Calvin: it was not God who prevented a prosperous life. The Lord is willing, disposed to deliver his people, for magnifying his Law and extolling his righteousness. Not induced by anyone else than himself. “On account of his righteousness” excludes everything men could bring; no merit. This reason applied especially to the Jews. They shewed themselves unworthy; by obduracy they made their wounds incurable.

Reformed hearing: deliverance would have been from his own righteousness alone; they closed the door by hardness. Shared with Wesley: not for their sake, but for his righteousness and the law’s honor. The fork is accent: Wesley stresses patient waiting for repentance and the law’s public honor among those who profess it; Calvin stresses no merit and wounds made incurable. Match the hub: God rules; human desert is excluded.

Key contrast Arminian readings, with Wesley, take the LORD’s pleasure as patience: although he might justly destroy suddenly, he waits for repentance that he may be gracious—not for their sake, but for the glory of his faithfulness to the covenant with pious progenitors; he is not forward to destroy those who profess the true religion, lest the law be exposed to contempt. Reformed readings, with Calvin, take the LORD as willing to deliver his people for magnifying his Law and extolling his own righteousness; “on account of his righteousness” excludes everything men could bring—no merit; they showed themselves unworthy and by obduracy made their wounds incurable. Shared: the LORD’s righteousness and the honor of his law, not human desert. Neither comment is a millennial fight or an open-theist pause.

Isaiah 42:22

22But this is a people plundered and looted; they are all of them trapped in holes and hidden in prisons; they have become plunder with none to rescue, spoil with none to say, “Restore!”

Arminian

But this is a people plundered and looted; they are all of them trapped in holes and hidden in prisons; they have become plunder with none to rescue, spoil with none to say, “Restore!” Wesley: But—notwithstanding this respect which God hath to his people, he hath severely scourged you for your sins. Hid—taken in snares made by their own hands, and by God’s just judgment cast into dungeons and prisons. None—none afforded them help.

Arminian hearing: the scourge is just, and the snares are their own as well as his judgment. Shared: plundered Judah is not an accident. Historic holes and prisons of the exile, not a newspaper. Match v. 21: the same LORD who was pleased to magnify the law has now scourged this people.

Reformed

But this people. Calvin: through their own fault miserable. They reject God who would otherwise have been inclined to do good. Spoil without hope of deliverance because they rejected the grace offered. If the Church is ruinous, ascribe it to our iniquities.

Reformed hearing: misery is their fault; God was inclined to good and they refused the grace. Shared with Wesley: scourged for sins; none to restore. Skip a fork: both refuse chance and both refuse to blame God as unwilling. Historic plunder of Jacob, not a living nation’s headline. The next verses will name who gave them up.

Isaiah 42:23

23Who among you will give ear to this, will attend and listen for the time to come?

Arminian

Who among you will give ear to this, will attend and listen for the time to come? Wesley has no separate note on this verse. Paraphrase from the nearest: the Hear of v. 18 still stands—O you who resist this clear light—and v. 25 will say they were secure and stupid under God’s judgments. Who will give ear is that same summons after the scourge of v. 22.

Arminian hearing: the question is real; some may yet attend for the time to come. Shared: the warning is given; stupidity is possible. Historic hearers of Isaiah’s word, not a newspaper. Do not invent a unique Wesley sentence here.

Reformed

Who among you. Calvin: Jews so stupid they will not see even when warned. Who shall hearken for the time to come—who, subdued by afflictions, repents though late.

Reformed hearing: the question hunts for a late repentant, subdued by the afflictions already poured. Shared with Wesley’s Hear of v. 18 and the stupidity of v. 25: they have been warned. No keyContrast: Calvin’s “repents though late” is not an Arminian/Reformed fork on ability; both still call them to give ear. Match v. 22: the plundered people are now asked who will listen.

Isaiah 42:24

24Who gave up Jacob to the looter, and Israel to the plunderers? Was it not the LORD, against whom we have sinned, in whose ways they would not walk, and whose law they would not obey?

Arminian

Who gave up Jacob to the looter, and Israel to the plunderers? Was it not the LORD, against whom we have sinned, in whose ways they would not walk, and whose law they would not obey? Wesley has no extra note; the verse itself governs: it was the LORD, against whom we have sinned. His v. 22 still stands—severely scourged you for your sins—and his v. 25 will name the fury poured.

Arminian hearing: Jacob was given up because they sinned, would not walk, would not obey. Shared sovereignty: God is the author of the spoil, not chance. Do not invent open theism: the LORD who gave them up is not a deity surprised by Judah’s deafness. Historic Jacob and Israel, not a living nation as modern proxy.

Reformed

Who gave Jacob for a prey? Calvin: God is the author of these judgments, not chance. Because we have sinned against him. Two things: no evil happens but from the Lord; we suffer no evil but for a just cause, because we have sinned. They would not walk in his ways: the Prophet changes person and excludes himself from their number (contempt and rejection of God).

Reformed hearing: the looter is not luck; the cause is sin. Shared with Wesley and with the verse: the LORD gave Jacob up, against whom we have sinned. No keyContrast: both camps confess shared sovereignty and just cause. Calvin’s change of person (we have sinned; they would not walk) marks the prophet’s distance from their contempt, not a fork on whether God ruled the plunder.

Isaiah 42:25

25So he poured on him the heat of his anger and the might of battle; it set him on fire all around, but he did not understand; it burned him up, but he did not take it to heart.

Arminian

So he poured on him the heat of his anger and the might of battle; it set him on fire all around, but he did not understand; it burned him up, but he did not take it to heart. Wesley: Fury—most grievous judgments. Yet—they were secure and stupid under God’s judgments.

Arminian hearing: the fire was poured, and they remained stupid. Shared: God poured the heat; they did not take it to heart. Do not invent open theism: the poured fury is the LORD’s, not a deity surprised by Judah’s deafness. Historic Jacob under judgment, not a newspaper. Match vv. 18 and 24: the same deaf servant, given up for sin, still will not hear.

Reformed

Poured upon him fury. Calvin: metaphor of floodgates and war. God rushes forth like an armed enemy. He gave no heed; laid it not to heart. If this thought came into our minds—“God is judge and hath justly punished us”—we should immediately repent.

Reformed hearing: the war-metaphor is God himself as armed enemy; the tragedy is they laid it not to heart. Shared with Wesley: grievous judgments; they were stupid under them. No keyContrast: both refuse chance and both refuse a surprised deity. Calvin’s “we should immediately repent” is the pastoral use of the same fire, not an Arminian/Reformed millennial fight.
