Passage

Isaiah 42

Verses 10–17 · ESV · Voices

After the Servant-song of 42:1–9, the coastlands are called to sing a new song: praise from the end of the earth, from sea and desert, Kedar and Sela, mountains and islands. The LORD goes out like a mighty man, stirring zeal like a man of war; he has held his peace a long time, and now cries out like a woman in labor. Mountains and hills are laid waste, rivers turned to islands; he leads the blind in a way they do not know, darkness into light, and he does not forsake them. Those who trust in carved idols, who say to metal images, “You are our gods,” are turned back and utterly put to shame.

Isaiah 42:10

10Sing to the LORD a new song, his praise from the end of the earth, you who go down to the sea, and all that fills it, the coastlands and their inhabitants.

Arminian

Sing to the LORD a new song, his praise from the end of the earth, you who go down to the sea, and all that fills it, the coastlands and their inhabitants. Wesley: Sing—upon this new and great occasion, the salvation of the world by Christ. From the end—all nations from one end of the earth to another. Ye—you that go by sea carry these glad tidings from Judea, where Christ was born, and lived, and died, and published the gospel, unto the remotest parts of the earth.

Arminian hearing: the new song is Christ’s salvation, carried from Judea to the ends. Shared christology: Wesley and Calvin both name Christ as the subject of the song—salvation of the world by him, not a later-age chart. Historic Isaiah: seafarers and coastlands, not a newspaper. Do not invent a millennial scheme as the Arminian/Reformed fork.

Reformed

Sing to Jehovah. Calvin: gratitude; behold the prophecy as accomplished. Subject of the song: Christ revealed to the world, sent to restore the Church and as it were renew the whole world. New song: excellent, not ordinary; extraordinary grace of God revealed in Christ. All nations; Christ sent to all. Cannot be sung but by renewed men; needs the Spirit.

Reformed hearing: the song is already treated as done, and only the renewed can sing it. Shared with Wesley: Christ revealed; all nations; a new song of extraordinary grace, not an ordinary hymn. Skip a fork here: both camps refuse to make the new song a Jewish monopoly or a millennial timetable. The Spirit’s necessity is how Calvin hears the singing, not a cue to deny that the praise is from the end of the earth.

Isaiah 42:11

11Let the desert and its cities lift up their voice, the villages that Kedar inhabits; let the habitants of Sela sing for joy, let them shout from the top of the mountains.

Arminian

Let the desert and its cities lift up their voice, the villages that Kedar inhabits; let the habitants of Sela sing for joy, let them shout from the top of the mountains. Wesley: The wilderness—those parts of the world which are now desolate and forsaken of God, and barren of all good fruits. Kedar—the Arabians: a Heathen and barbarous people, put for all nations. Mountains—who are commonly more savage and ignorant than others.

Arminian hearing: desolate places and Heathen Kedar stand for all nations now brought into the song. Shared: Arabia as a part for the whole earth; grace reaches rude and barbarous peoples. Historic Kedar and Sela, not a living map. Do not invent a millennial desert-bloom as the camp split.

Reformed

Desert and its cities. Calvin: west sea and east desert and Arabia as the known map, a part for the whole earth. Kedar: Scenite Arabians in tents; rude and barbarous nations the grace reaches. From rising to setting of the sun these praises shall be heard.

Reformed hearing: Isaiah’s known map (sea, desert, Arabia) is a figure for the whole earth, sunrise to sunset. Shared with Wesley: Kedar names Heathen nations the gospel actually reaches. They agree enough: no fork. The shout from the mountains is the same new song of v. 10, not a later-age chart.

Isaiah 42:12

12Let them give glory to the LORD, and declare his praise in the coastlands.

Arminian

Let them give glory to the LORD, and declare his praise in the coastlands. Wesley: The islands—in the remotest parts of the world, as well as in Arabia, which was near to them.

Arminian hearing: remote islands and near Arabia alike give Jehovah glory. Shared: the new song of vv. 10–11 ends in praise declared, not muttered. Historic coastlands of Isaiah’s map, not a newspaper. Glory belongs to the LORD; the idols of v. 17 will get none of it.

Reformed

Give glory to Jehovah. Calvin: celebrate this redemption with a cheerful voice. Not lukewarm; those who have tasted grace cannot mutter.

Reformed hearing: tasted grace sings; lukewarm muttering is not this praise. Shared with Wesley: glory to the LORD in the remotest parts. Skip a fork: both camps put the praise on Jehovah’s redemption, not on images. Match the hub: the new song of Christ in v. 10 is what the coastlands declare.

Isaiah 42:13

13The LORD goes out like a mighty man, like a man of war he stirs up his zeal; he cries out, he shouts aloud, he shows himself mighty against his foes.

Arminian

The LORD goes out like a mighty man, like a man of war he stirs up his zeal; he cries out, he shouts aloud, he shows himself mighty against his foes. Wesley: Go forth—to battle. Stir up—his strength and anger against the obstinate enemies of his Son and gospel. Roar—as a lion upon his prey, and as soldiers when they begin the battle.

Arminian hearing: the warrior’s zeal is against obstinate enemies of the Son and the gospel. Shared: God himself goes forth; the strength is his, not ours. Historic Isaiah’s man of war, not a living campaign. Do not invent a dispensational versus amillennial fight: Wesley’s foes of the Son are the same LORD Calvin’s giant comes forth to assist his people against.

Reformed

Jehovah like a giant. Calvin: ascribes strength so believers rely on him when he seems to delay. Go forth: come forth publicly to give assistance. Like a warrior with burning indignation against enemies. Accommodates to our capacity. Needed through the captivity almost till the coming of Christ.

Reformed hearing: the giant-speech is so delayed hearts will still rely on him; the figure is accommodated to our capacity. Shared with Wesley: God goes forth as a warrior against enemies of his work. They agree enough: no keyContrast. Calvin’s span from captivity toward Christ’s coming is historic redemption, not a millennial chart against Wesley.

Isaiah 42:14

14For a long time I have held my peace; I have kept still and restrained myself; now I will cry out like a woman in labor; I will gasp and pant.

Arminian

For a long time I have held my peace; I have kept still and restrained myself; now I will cry out like a woman in labor; I will gasp and pant. Wesley: Long—I have for many ages suffered the devil and his servants to prevail in the world, but now I will bring forth and accomplish that glorious work which I have long conceived in my mind; yea, I will suddenly destroy the incorrigible enemies of my truth.

Arminian hearing: the long peace was patience while the devil prevailed; the labor is the conceived work now brought forth, and sudden ruin of the incorrigible. Shared: God was silent and now acts. Do not invent open theism: Wesley’s God had the work long conceived in his mind—he was restrained, not ignorant. Historic gospel-work after long delay, not a newspaper.

Reformed

I have kept silence. Calvin: temptation when God delays and the wicked carry themselves high. Long time: so hearts not languish; also rebukes unbelievers who trust his forbearance. Like a woman in labor: astonishing warmth of love and tenderness; redemption a kind of birth, the grave serving as a womb. Then adds lion and giant so the mother-metaphor not degrade majesty.

Reformed hearing: delay is a pastoral trial, not absence; the labor-cry is love’s heat, and the giant of v. 13 keeps the mother-speech from lowering majesty. Shared with Wesley: long restraint, then the work brought forth. No keyContrast: both hear a real silence broken, not a deity discovering what to do. Unbelievers who trust the forbearance are rebuked; hearts of believers are kept from languishing.

Isaiah 42:15

15I will lay waste mountains and hills, and dry up all their vegetation; I will turn the rivers into islands, and dry up the pools.

Arminian

I will lay waste mountains and hills, and dry up all their vegetation; I will turn the rivers into islands, and dry up the pools. Wesley: Hills—my most lofty and flourishing enemies. Dry up—I will remove all impediments out of the way.

Arminian hearing: lofty enemies are the hills; drying is the clearing of the path for the work of vv. 13–14. Shared: God himself removes what stands in the way. Historic Isaiah’s mountains and rivers, not a living war-map. Do not invent a millennial terraforming as the camp split.

Reformed

Reduce mountains and hills to a wilderness. Calvin: defenses and military forces of the wicked shall not prevent God from setting his people at liberty. Do not need allegory of cities and herbage. Power not limited to natural means.

Reformed hearing: the waste is real power against the wicked’s defenses; Calvin refuses a forced allegory of cities and plants, and will not limit God to ordinary means. Shared with Wesley: impediments and enemies cannot stop the deliverance. They agree enough: no fork. Match v. 13: the warrior who went forth now levels what blocked his people.

Isaiah 42:16

16And I will lead the blind in a way that they do not know, in paths that they have not known I will guide them. I will turn the darkness before them into light, the rough places into level ground. These are the things I do, and I do not forsake them.

Arminian

And I will lead the blind in a way that they do not know, in paths that they have not known I will guide them. I will turn the darkness before them into light, the rough places into level ground. These are the things I do, and I do not forsake them. Wesley: The blind—the Gentiles. By a way—the way of truth, hitherto hidden from them; I will take away all hindrances; direct them in the right way; enlighten their dark minds, and rectify their perverse wills and affections, until I have brought them to the end of their journey.

Arminian hearing: the blind are the Gentiles; the unknown way is the way of truth; minds enlightened and wills rectified until the journey’s end. Shared: God leads; darkness becomes light; he does not forsake them. Historic gospel to the nations after the warrior-work, not a newspaper. The fork with Calvin is who “the blind” are, not whether God leads.

Reformed

I will lead the blind. Calvin: those whose affairs are so difficult, intricate, disordered that they see no means of escape. When we have no way open, that is the time to hope in the Lord. Better to be blind persons guided by the hand of God than by excessive sagacity to form labyrinths. Darkness into light, crooked straight. Promised to believers alone who entrust themselves to God and have known their blindness. Not to worldly wise men who wish to see in spite of him.

Reformed hearing: blindness is the distressed believer’s confession, not a map-label for Gentiles as such; hope begins when no way is open, and the worldly-wise who insist on seeing are outside the promise. Shared with Wesley: God leads; darkness into light; he does not forsake. Match the hub: the same LORD of vv. 13–15 now guides. Do not invent a millennial path; the fork is the identity of the blind.

Key contrast Arminian readings, with Wesley, take the blind as the Gentiles: the way of truth hitherto hidden from them; God takes away hindrances, enlightens dark minds, and rectifies perverse wills and affections until he has brought them to the end of their journey. Reformed readings, with Calvin, take the blind as those whose affairs are so disordered they see no escape—believers who must become blind enough to be led by God’s hand, not worldly-wise men who wish to see in spite of him; the promise is to those who entrust themselves to God and have known their blindness. Shared: God leads; darkness becomes light; rough places are leveled; he does not forsake them. Neither comment is a millennial fight.

Isaiah 42:17

17They are turned back and utterly put to shame, who trust in carved idols, who say to metal images, “You are our gods.”

Arminian

They are turned back and utterly put to shame, who trust in carved idols, who say to metal images, “You are our gods.” Wesley has no separate note on this verse. His song still governs: the new song of Christ’s salvation (v. 10), glory given to the LORD in the coastlands (v. 12), and the warrior who will suddenly destroy the incorrigible enemies of his truth (v. 14). Shame of those who trust graven images follows that song; do not invent a unique Wesley sentence here.

Arminian hearing: idol-trust is put to shame as the new song’s reverse. Shared polemic: glory not to images. Historic carved gods of Isaiah’s world, not a culture-war on art. Match v. 12: praise is declared to the LORD; those who say to metal, “You are our gods,” are turned back.

Reformed

They shall be driven back. Calvin: distinguishes worshippers of God from worshippers of idols. Those who place hope in idols shall perish; those who trust in the word of God are certain of salvation. “Ye are our gods”: they ascribe the power of God to images. No man will be saved but he who trusts in God alone.

Reformed hearing: the verse splits two hopes—idols perish, the word saves; images are given what belongs to God. Shared with Wesley’s song: glory not to carved gods. No keyContrast: both camps run the same polemic. Calvin’s “trust in God alone” is the worshipper distinguished from the image, not a cue to invent a later-age chart.
