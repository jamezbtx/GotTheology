Passage

Isaiah 23

Verses 1–18 · WEB · Voices

The last of the oracles against the nations targets Tyre, the Phoenician trading capital whose ships reached Tarshish. Her collapse sends shock waves through the ports of the Mediterranean and Egypt. The LORD of Armies planned it to humble pride in wealth. After seventy years Tyre will trade again, but her profits will be dedicated to the LORD.

Isaiah 23:1

1The burden of Tyre. Howl, you ships of Tarshish! For it is laid waste, so that there is no house, no entering in. From the land of Kittim it is revealed to them.

Arminian

Wesley: Of Tyre—the prophecy of the heavy calamity and destruction of Tyre. Tyre was destroyed first by Nebuchadnezzar, and afterwards by Alexander; this prophecy seemed directly to respect the former, with some reference to the latter; after seventy years Tyre should recover some glory before her second destruction. Howl—howling ascribed by a known figure. No house—not an house left, nor merchants that go into it. Chittim—eminent for shipping; it may be put for all countries which traded with her. Both traditions agree: ships of Tarshish wail; no house or harbor.

Tyre, not a twenty-first-century port.

Reformed

Calvin: the burden of Tyre. Tyre was very wealthy, celebrated for commerce and colonies: Carthage, Utica, Leptis, Cadiz. Isaiah threatens destruction because it had been hostile to the people of God (Ezekiel 26:2); God testifies fatherly regard by opposing her enemies. Some think of Alexander; the argument from Chittim has little force. Howl, ye ships of Tarshish; for it is laid waste, so that there is no house, no entering in: from the land of Chittim it is revealed to them.

The ships of Tarshish are told to howl.

Key contrast Both hear Tyre wasted and Tarshish howling. Arminians hear Wesley’s two strokes: Nebuchadnezzar first, Alexander later, with seventy years of recovery between. Reformed readers follow Calvin’s further word: the cause is hostility to God’s people; he will not rest the oracle on Alexander because of the name Chittim.

Isaiah 23:2

2Be still, you inhabitants of the coast, you whom the merchants of Sidon that pass over the sea have replenished.

Arminian

Wesley: Be still—Hebrew. Be silent, boast no more of thy wealth and power. The isle—of Tyre, which was an island, ’till Alexander joined it to the continent. That pass—that are a sea-faring people. Replenished—with manners, and commodities. Sidon’s merchants filled the coast.

Silence replaces boast.

Reformed

Calvin: be still, ye inhabitants of the isle, thou whom the merchants of Zidon, that pass over the sea, have replenished. The filled coast is told to be silent; trade cannot speak when the harbour is gone.

Be still.

Isaiah 23:3

3On great waters, the seed of the Shihor, the harvest of the Nile, was her revenue. She was the market of nations.

Arminian

Wesley’s chapter summary: by the sea, the seed of Sihor, the corn of Egypt wherewith Egypt abounded; Sihor is the Nile; the harvest from the inundation; a mart of nations. Nile grain as Tyre’s revenue.

Egypt-to-Tyre corn, not a modern shipping lane.

Reformed

Calvin: and by great waters the seed of Sihor, the harvest of the river, is her revenue; and she is a mart of nations. Tyre lived on Egypt’s grain as if it grew at home. The mart is the nations’ meeting-place, now to howl.

Shared: merchant of the nations.

Isaiah 23:4

4Be ashamed, Sidon; for the sea has spoken, the stronghold of the sea, saying, “I have not travailed, nor given birth, neither have I nourished young men, nor brought up virgins.”

Arminian

Wesley: Zidon—Zidon was a great city near Tyre, strongly united to her by commerce and league, and called by some the mother of Tyre. The sea—that part of the sea in which Tyre was. The strength—Tyre might be called the strength of the sea, because it defendeth that part of the sea from piracies. I travel not—I, who was so fruitful that I sent forth colonies (of which Carthage was one), am now barren and desolate. Sidon ashamed; the sea barren.

Mother-Sidon weeps a childless sea.

Reformed

Calvin: be thou ashamed, O Zidon; for the sea hath spoken, even the strength of the sea. The fortress of the waters confesses barrenness: no labor, no birth, no young men or virgins reared. The mother-port is put to shame by the empty harbour.

Shared: I have not given birth.

Isaiah 23:5

5When the report comes to Egypt, they will be in anguish at the report of Tyre.

Arminian

Wesley’s mart of v. 3 still binds Egypt to Tyre: the corn of Egypt was her revenue; when Tyre falls, Egypt feels the rumor.

Trade-pain, not a twenty-first-century market story.

Reformed

Calvin: as at the report concerning Egypt, so shall they be sorely pained at the report of Tyre. Egypt, bound to that mart by grain, will labor in pain when the harbour dies. The rumor travels the same waters that carried Shihor’s seed.

Held in common: report of Tyre.

Isaiah 23:6

6Pass over to Tarshish! Wail, you inhabitants of the coast!

Arminian

Wesley (on v. 1): tarry no longer; flee through thy territories into other countries. Held in common: cross to Tarshish; wail.

Flight to Tarshish is coast-people’s, not a modern evacuation headline.

Reformed

Calvin: pass ye over to Tarshish; howl, ye inhabitants of the isle. The same ships that traded now carry fugitives. Howling is the new commerce.

The inhabitants of the coast are told to wail.

Isaiah 23:7

7Is this your joyous city, whose antiquity is of ancient days, whose feet carried her far away to travel?

Arminian

Wesley: Antiquity—being built before Joshua’s time, Josh. Xix, 29. Her feet—whereas before, like a delicate lady, she would not set her foot to the ground, but used to be carried in stately chariots. To sojourn—to seek for new habitations. Held in common: ancient, exultant, now walking into exile.

Feet that settled colonies now sojourn.

Reformed

Calvin: is this your joyous city, whose antiquity is of ancient days? Her own feet shall carry her afar off to sojourn. Age and colonies do not keep her. The lady walks because the harbour is gone.

The old city is still Tyre. Common to both: origin from days of old; feet to sojourn.

Isaiah 23:8

8Who has planned this against Tyre, the giver of crowns, whose merchants are princes, whose traders are the honorable of the earth?

Arminian

Wesley: Who—this is the word of God, and not of man. The crowning city—which was a royal city, and carried away the crown from all other cities. Princes—equal to princes for wealth, and power, and reputation. On this both agree: who purposed this against the crowning city?

Merchants as princes are Tyre’s. The question waits for v. 9.

Reformed

Calvin: who hath taken this counsel against Tyre, the crowning city, whose merchants are princes, whose traffickers are the honourable of the earth? The question is posed so that the LORD of hosts, not chance or Alexander alone, may be named in the next verse.

Held in common: merchants were princes.

Isaiah 23:9

9The LORD of Armies has planned it, to stain the pride of all glory, to bring into contempt all the honorable of the earth.

Arminian

Wesley: The Lord—this is the Lord’s own doing. To stain—God’s design is by this example to abase the pride of all the potentates of the earth. Both affirm: the LORD of hosts purposed it.

Pride of glory is stained in Tyre as a lesson to all.

Reformed

Calvin: the Lord of hosts hath purposed it, to stain the pride of all glory, and to bring into contempt all the honourable of the earth. The counsel of v. 8 is his. He stains, so that no mart may think itself too high.

To dishonor the honored.

Isaiah 23:10

10Pass through your land like the Nile, daughter of Tarshish. There is no restraint any more.

Arminian

Wesley: Pass through—tarry no longer in thy own territories, but flee through them, into other countries, for safety and relief. As a river—swiftly, lest you be prevented. Tarshish—O Tyre, which might well be called daughter of Tarshish, that is, of the sea, because it was an island, born of the sea and nourished by it. Where they meet: no girdle left; flow like the Nile.

The unrestrained crossing is flight, not a trade boom.

Reformed

Calvin: pass through thy land as a river, O daughter of Tarshish: there is no more strength. When the girdle of the harbour is gone, the people pour out like a flood. Daughter of Tarshish: the sea-born city has no belt left.

Held in common: cross like the Nile.

Isaiah 23:11

11He has stretched out his hand over the sea. He has shaken the kingdoms. The LORD has ordered the destruction of Canaan’s strongholds.

Arminian

Wesley: He—the Lord. Shook—Hebrew. He made the kingdoms to tremble; the neighbouring and confederate kingdoms, who might justly quake at her fall. A commandment—hath put this design into the hearts of her enemies, and given them courage to attempt, and strength to execute it. Common to both: hand over the sea; Canaan’s strongholds doomed.

The sea-hand is the Lord’s.

Reformed

Calvin: he stretched out his hand over the sea, he shook the kingdoms: the Lord hath given a commandment against the merchant city, to destroy the strongholds thereof. The same hand that rides the cloud over Egypt (ch. 19) now lies on Tyre’s waters. Kingdoms shake because he commanded.

On this both agree: shaken kingdoms.

Isaiah 23:12

12He said, “You shall rejoice no more, you oppressed virgin daughter of Sidon. Arise, pass over to Kittim. Even there you will have no rest.”

Arminian

Wesley: Virgin—so he calls her, because she had hitherto never born the yoke of a conquering enemy. Zidon—Tyre may be called the daughter of Zidon, because she was first built and possessed by a colony of the Zidonians. No rest—thither thine enemies shall pursue thee, and there shall they overtake thee. Common to both: no more exulting; Cyprus gives no rest.

Even Chittim cannot hide her.

Reformed

Calvin: and he said, Thou shalt no more rejoice, O thou oppressed virgin, daughter of Zidon: arise, pass over to Chittim; there also shalt thou have no rest. The virgin-city has never been yoked; now she is oppressed. Flight to Cyprus repeats the howl of v. 1 and still fails.

Both traditions agree: virgin daughter of Sidon.

Isaiah 23:13

13Behold, the land of the Chaldeans. This people didn’t exist. The Assyrians founded it for those who dwell in the wilderness. They set up their towers. They overthrew its palaces. They made it a ruin.

Arminian

Wesley: Behold—thou Tyrians, cast your eyes upon the Chaldeans or Babylonians; who tho’ now flourishing, grow far more glorious, yet shall certainly be brought to utter ruin. This people—the Chaldeans at first were not a people, not formed into any commonwealth, ’till Nimrod built Babel, Gen. x, 9, 10, now the head of the Chaldean monarchy; which he built for those people who then lived in tents. He—the Lord. To ruin—will infallibly bring that great empire to ruin. He speaks of a future thing as if it were already past.

Look at Chaldea’s rise and ruin, then do not trust Tyre’s walls.

Reformed

Calvin: behold the land of the Chaldeans; this people was not; Ashur founded it for the inhabitants of the wilderness; he brought it to ruin. The Chaldeans had no name; they were descended from the Assyrians (Genesis 10:11). Ashur collected wanderers into cities; then the daughter devoured the mother. What has this to do with Tyre? Tyre will be overthrown by the Assyrians and Chaldeans. If the Chaldeans could conquer Assyria, why should Tyre rely on her riches?

Babylon’s land was founded by Assyria and will still fall to ruin.

Key contrast Both hear Chaldea raised from nothing and used against Tyre. Arminians follow Wesley’s Nimrod and Babel, then the Lord’s future ruin of that empire spoken as past. Reformed readers commonly follow Calvin’s further word: Assyria founded the wandering Chaldeans; the daughter ate the mother; therefore Tyre should not trust riches against both.

Isaiah 23:14

14Howl, you ships of Tarshish, for your stronghold is laid waste!

Arminian

Wesley: Your strength—the city of Tyre, where you found safety and wealth. Both traditions agree: howl again; the stronghold is gone.

The inclusio with v. 1 is Tarshish’s.

Reformed

Calvin: howl, ye ships of Tarshish. He repeats what he formerly said; the Cilicians constantly traded with Tyre. He bids their ships howl, because when that harbour is shut up, merchants are amazed. He calls that harbour their strength.

The wasted stronghold is still Tyre. Held in common: wail, ships of Tarshish.

Isaiah 23:15

15It will come to pass in that day that Tyre will be forgotten seventy years, according to the days of one king. After the end of seventy years it will be to Tyre like in the song of the prostitute.

Arminian

Wesley: Forgotten—neglected and forsaken. Seventy years—during the time of the Jewish captivity in Babylon. Tyre was taken by Nebuchadnezzar, Jer. Xxvii, 3, 8 Ezek xxvi, 7, a little after the taking of Jerusalem and was restored by the favour of the Persian monarchs after the return of the Jews. One king—one royal race of Nebuchadnezzar, including his son, and his son’s son, in whom his family and kingdom were to expire. Sing—she shall by degrees return to her former traffick, as harlots use to entice men by lascivious songs.

Seventy years are that captivity’s clock, not a twenty-first-century date scheme.

Reformed

Calvin: Tyre shall be forgotten seventy years, according to the days of one king. After the taking, he declares how long the calamity shall endure. By being forgotten he means no merchandise. The days of a king are put for the age of a man, generally seventy years (Psalm 90:10); he mentioned a king because Tyre had a king and reckoned time by a king’s life. Tyre shall have a song like that of a harlot: merchandise compared to that occupation because of fraud, not because trade itself is condemned.

Agreed on both sides: forgotten, then a harlot’s song.

Key contrast Both hear seventy years of Tyre forgotten, then a return to trade, not a modern countdown. Arminians follow Wesley’s years as the Jewish captivity, Tyre taken by Nebuchadnezzar and restored under Persia, one king as Nebuchadnezzar’s royal race. Reformed interpreters typically side with Calvin’s further word: seventy as a man’s age, reckoned by Tyre’s own king; the harlot-song as fraudulent merchandise, not a condemnation of trade as such.

Isaiah 23:16

16Take a harp; go about the city, you prostitute that has been forgotten. Make sweet melody. Sing many songs, that you may be remembered.

Arminian

Wesley: Go about—as harlots use to do. Thou harlot—so he calls Tyre, because she enticed the merchants to deal with her by various artifices, and even by dishonest practices, and because of the great and general uncleanness which was committed in it. Both traditions hear: harp and songs to be remembered.

The forgotten harlot is Tyre’s trade. Do not copy the melody.

Reformed

Calvin: take a harp, go about the city, thou harlot that hast been forgotten; make sweet melody, sing many songs, that thou mayest be remembered. He compares Tyre to a harlot grown old, forsaken, yet unable to forget her gain, going about with songs. By harp and sweet melody he means the tricks of merchants. He does not exhort Tyre to restore herself this way; he proceeds with his prophecy.

On this both agree: forgotten prostitute, many songs.

Isaiah 23:17

17It will happen after the end of seventy years that the LORD will visit Tyre. She will return to her wages, and will play the prostitute with all the kingdoms of the world on the surface of the earth.

Arminian

Wesley: Visit—in mercy. Her hire—the Hebrew word properly signifies, the hire of an harlot. Fornication—shall trade promiscuously with people of all nations, as harlots entertain all comers. Common to both: seventy years ended; the LORD visits; trade resumes.

Mercy’s visit does not yet equal holiness; that waits for v. 18.

Reformed

Calvin: Jehovah will visit Tyre. Although the Lord will afflict Tyre so that she appears ruined, yet she will obtain mercy, rising out of her ruins. Such restoration is justly ascribed to the favor of God; otherwise, as Malachi says of Edom, men would build and the Lord would pull down. Though he is a severe judge, he leaves room for compassion. She shall return to her hire, and shall commit fornication with all the kingdoms of the world: trade resumes its old arts until holiness claims the wages in v. 18.

The visit is still mercy on Tyre. Seventy years, then hire.

Isaiah 23:18

18Her merchandise and her wages will be holiness to the LORD. It will not be treasured nor laid up; for her merchandise will be for those who dwell before the LORD, to eat sufficiently, and for durable clothing.

Arminian

Wesley: Holiness—this is a prophecy concerning the conversion of the Tyrians to the true religion. Laid up—either out of covetousness, or for their pride and luxury, as they formerly did; but now they shall freely lay it out upon pious and charitable uses. Shall be—for the support and encouragement of the ministers of holy things, who shall teach the good knowledge of the Lord. Although this does not exclude, but rather imply their liberality in contributing to the necessities of all Christians. Held in common: merchandise made holy; food and fine clothing for those who live before the LORD.

Tyre’s wages go to Zion’s servants. Gospel mercy.

Reformed

Calvin: her merchandise and her hire shall be holiness to the Lord: it shall not be treasured nor laid up; for them that dwell before the Lord, to eat sufficiently, and for durable clothing. We are said to dwell before God; through Christ we approach nearer than the Levites. What is bestowed on the poor is consecrated to God; with such sacrifices he is well pleased. Our alms are so highly applauded, and our hands, as well as our gift, are consecrated to God.

Shared with Wesley: not hoarded, given to those who dwell before the LORD.

Voices from both camps

Classic Arminian and Reformed comment on Isaiah 23:1–18, in the writers’ own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Of Tyre—The prophecy of the heavy calamity and destruction of Tyre. Tyre was, according to this prophecy, destroyed; first by Nebuchadnezzar, and afterwards by Alexander the great. And tho’ this prophecy seemed directly to respect the former destruction, yet it seems to have some reference to the latter also; only it is intimated, that after seventy years, Tyre should recover some former power and glory, before her second and final destruction. Howl—To which howling and lamenting is ascribed by a known figure. No house—So effectually wasted, that there is not an house left in it. Chittim—An eminent place for shipping and trading; it may here be put for all other countries which traded with her. Be still—Hebrew. be silent, boast no more of thy wealth and power. The isle—Of Tyre, which was an island, ’till Alexander joined it to the continent. Zidon—A great city near Tyre, called by some the mother of Tyre. Waters—By the sea, the seed of Sihor, the corn of Egypt. A mart—A place to which all nations resort for traffick. Antiquity—Being built before Joshua’s time, Josh. xix, 29. Who—This is the word of God, and not of man. The crowning city—Which was a royal city, and carried away the crown from all other cities. The Lord—This is the Lord’s own doing. To stain—God’s design is by this example to abase the pride of all the potentates of the earth. Forgotten—Neglected and forsaken. Seventy years—During the time of the Jewish captivity in Babylon. Tyre was taken by Nebuchadnezzar a little after the taking of Jerusalem and was restored by the favour of the Persian monarchs after the return of the Jews. One king—One royal race of Nebuchadnezzar. Sing—She shall by degrees return to her former traffick. Visit—In mercy. Her hire—The hire of an harlot. Holiness—This is a prophecy concerning the conversion of the Tyrians to the true religion. Laid up—Now they shall freely lay it out upon pious and charitable uses, for the support of the ministers of holy things.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 23:1–18)

Reformed Notable voices

“The burden of Tyre. Tyre was very wealthy, and highly celebrated, both on account of the variety and extent of its commercial intercourse with all nations, and on account of the flourishing colonies which sprang from it: Carthage, Utica, Leptis, Cadiz. Isaiah threatens its destruction, because it had been hostile to the people of God, as we may infer from Ezekiel; it was the design of the Prophet to shew that God testifies his fatherly regard to his people by opposing all her enemies. Some think that this refers to the storming of Tyre by Alexander; the argument from Chittim has little force. Howl, ye ships of Tarshish; for it is laid waste. Be still, ye inhabitants of the isle, whom the merchants of Zidon have replenished. By great waters the seed of Sihor, the harvest of the river, is her revenue; she is a mart of nations. Be thou ashamed, O Zidon. Who hath taken this counsel against Tyre, the crowning city? The Lord of hosts hath purposed it, to stain the pride of all glory. He stretched out his hand over the sea. Behold the land of the Chaldeans; this people was not; Ashur founded it; he brought it to ruin: Tyre will be overthrown by the Assyrians and Chaldeans. Howl, ye ships of Tarshish. It shall come to pass in that day that Tyre shall be forgotten seventy years, according to the days of one king: the days of a king are put for the age of a man, generally seventy years. Tyre shall have a song like that of a harlot. Jehovah will visit Tyre: though she appear ruined, she will obtain mercy and be restored. Her merchandise and her hire shall be holiness to the Lord: it shall not be treasured nor laid up; for them that dwell before the Lord, that they may eat till they are satisfied. What is bestowed on the poor is consecrated to God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 23:1–18)
