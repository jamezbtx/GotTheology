Passage

Isaiah 12

Verses 1–6 · ESV · Voices

You will say in that day: I will give thanks, O Lord; though you were angry with me, your anger turned away, that you might comfort me. God is my salvation; I will trust and not be afraid. The Lord God is my strength and my song; he has become my salvation. With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation. In that day: give thanks, call upon his name, make known his deeds among the peoples. Sing, for he has done gloriously; let this be known in all the earth. Shout, inhabitant of Zion: great in your midst is the Holy One of Israel.

Isaiah 12:1

1You will say in that day: “I will give thanks to you, O Lord, for though you were angry with me, your anger turned away, that you might comfort me.

Arminian

You will say in that day: I will give thanks to you, O Lord, for though you were angry with me, your anger turned away, that you might comfort me. Wesley: in that day—when the reduction of Israel and conversion of the Gentiles is fulfilled (11:10–16).

Arminian that day: historic remnant first, then the nations. A real thanks after a real anger. They still say it. The stretched-out hand of 9–10 has turned. Not a millennial camp fight: Wesley names Israel’s reduction and the Gentiles’ conversion, the gospel after the history.

Reformed

Calvin: the song seals the promise, put in their mouth so they will not think they are deluded. He addresses the whole people as one. Believers first acknowledge guilt, then ascribe relief to mercy. He was satisfied with moderate chastisement. Anger of short duration (Ps 30:5). Punishment ended not because we paid what we deserved, but because through fatherly love He spares our weakness. This confession belongs properly to the godly and elect. Wrath against the ungodly is perpetual; their chastisements are forerunners of everlasting destruction; no consolation is promised them. The godly are trained to repentance, that they may not perish with the world (1 Cor 11:32).

Reformed that day: the elect’s song after the rod. Shared: that day, thanks, angry with me, anger turned away, comfort.

Key contrast Both confess that in that day they thank the Lord whose anger turned away to comfort (12:1). Historic then gospel, not a newspaper, and not a millennial camp fight. Arminians typically hear the day when Israel is reduced and the Gentiles converted, a people who may then praise (Wesley). Reformed readers typically hear a confession that belongs to the godly and elect: wrath short for them, perpetual for the ungodly; they were not billed and paid, but spared by fatherly love (Calvin).

Isaiah 12:2

2“Behold, God is my salvation; I will trust, and will not be afraid; for the Lord God is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation.”

Arminian

Behold, God is my salvation; I will trust, and will not be afraid; for the Lord God is my strength and my song, and he has become my salvation. Wesley: my salvation hath not been brought to pass by man, but by the almighty power of God.

Arminian behold: a real trust after the anger turned. Strength and song are still His. Not by man: they still must trust. Historic Zion, then the gospel’s song.

Reformed

Calvin: knowledge from experience: the brightness of God’s countenance, once hidden, now they can point to it. Confidence proceeds from faith. We have made good progress in faith when endued with this confidence. He is not a part of our strength but our complete strength. None sincerely sing but those convinced of their weakness, who seek strength from God alone. He will become my salvation: not a single day’s benefit, but till the coming of Christ, and to the end.

Reformed behold: faith’s calm after the rod. Shared: God is my salvation, trust, not afraid, strength, song, become my salvation.

Isaiah 12:3

3With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation.

Arminian

With joy you will draw water from the wells of salvation. Wesley: thirsty souls filled with Divine graces and comforts, which you may draw from God in the use of gospel-ordinances.

Arminian draw: a real well, a real drawing. Gospel ordinances are still the bucket. They still come thirsty. Historic wells, then the church’s means.

Reformed

Calvin: salvation set before you as a running fountain. Water as what life most needs. Empty of everything good, we are dry land (Ps 143:6). That fountain is Christ; out of His fullness we all draw (John 1:16). Whenever we feel our want we go directly to Him.

Reformed draw: the well is a Person. Shared: joy, draw water, wells of salvation. Wesley names the ordinances; Calvin names Christ. The same well.

Isaiah 12:4

4And you will say in that day: “Give thanks to the Lord, call upon his name, make known his deeds among the peoples, proclaim that his name is exalted.

Arminian

And you will say in that day: Give thanks to the Lord, call upon his name, make known his deeds among the peoples, proclaim that his name is exalted. The song goes public. One mouth becomes many.

Arminian among the peoples: historic remnant telling the nations, as 11:10’s signal. They still call and still declare. Not a newspaper missions chart.

Reformed

Calvin: not only sing individually but excite others. One soul and one mouth (Rom 15:6). Call upon his name: the whole of worship under that head; our confidence is placed in God. Make known among the peoples: not in one corner. A forerunner of the preaching of the gospel. After Christ was manifested they ought to have been heralds through every country.

Reformed among the peoples: the same signal, now a song. Shared: that day, give thanks, call upon his name, deeds among the peoples, name exalted.

Isaiah 12:5

5“Sing praises to the Lord, for he has done gloriously; let this be made known in all the earth.

Arminian

Sing praises to the Lord, for he has done gloriously; let this be made known in all the earth. The excellent things of 11–12 are not a secret.

Arminian all the earth: historic then gospel. He has done it; they still sing it. Not a millennial timetable.

Reformed

Calvin: the song is not limited to a short period but extends to the whole of Christ’s reign. The work is truly glorious: God sent His Son to reconcile us and to destroy the dominion of death and the devil. This hath been made known through all the earth: the calling of the Gentiles. The work ought not to be concealed in a corner.

Reformed all the earth: Christ’s reign is the excellent thing. Shared: sing, done gloriously, known in all the earth.

Isaiah 12:6

6Shout, and sing for joy, O inhabitant of Zion, for great in your midst is the Holy One of Israel.”

Arminian

Shout, and sing for joy, O inhabitant of Zion, for great in your midst is the Holy One of Israel. Wesley has no separate note; his 12:1 still holds: this is the day of Israel’s reduction and the Gentiles’ conversion, Zion shouting because the Holy One is among them.

Arminian midst: the Holy One of 6:3 and 10:17, now in the city that shook. Historic Zion, then the church. They still shout. Great is not a slogan: He is there.

Reformed

Calvin: we have no other happiness than to have God dwelling in the midst of us. Without this, life is wretched though we have every other riches. He is the Holy One from the effect: gathering us to Himself and saving us by His grace, He sanctifies us as His own. When He is absent we continue in grief. Inhabitant of Zion: not all are capable of so great a blessing; he exhorts them to unity of faith, that by being united to the Church we may partake of this joy.

Reformed midst: the Holy One gathers His own. Shared: shout, sing, inhabitant of Zion, great, in your midst, Holy One of Israel.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on anger turned away, wells of salvation, and the Holy One in the midst of Zion—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Isaiah 12:1–6.

Arminian Notable voices

“In that day—when this great work of the reduction of Israel, and conversion of the Gentiles is fulfilled. God—my salvation hath not been brought to pass by man, but by the almighty power of God. With joy—your thirsty souls shall be filled with Divine graces and comforts, which you may draw from God, in the use of gospel-ordinances.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 12:1–3)

Reformed Notable voices

“Thou shalt say in that day: he exhorts to thanksgiving, and seals the promise by putting a song in their mouth. Though thou wast angry with me: believers first acknowledge their guilt, then ascribe it to the mercy of God that they have been freed. He was satisfied with inflicting a moderate chastisement. Though we feel the anger of the Lord, we may know that it is of short duration, and that we shall be comforted as soon as he has chastened us. Our punishment is ended, not because we have paid to the justice of God what we had deserved, but because through his fatherly love he spares our weakness. This confession belongs properly to the godly and elect. The wrath of the Lord against the ungodly is perpetual, and the chastisements inflicted on them are forerunners of everlasting destruction: no consolation is promised to them. The godly know that God will be gracious, since he punishes their sins for no other reason than to train them to repentance, that they may not perish along with the world. Behold, God is my salvation: confidence proceeds from faith. He is not a part of our strength, but our complete strength. You shall draw waters with joy: that fountain is Christ; out of his fullness we all draw (John 1:16). Cry out and shout, thou inhabitant of Zion: we have no other happiness than to have God dwelling in the midst of us.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 12:1–6)
