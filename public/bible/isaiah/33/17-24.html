Passage

Isaiah 33

Verses 17–24 · ESV · Voices

Your eyes will behold the king in his beauty; they will see a land that stretches afar; your heart will muse on the terror; you will see no more the insolent people of obscure speech; behold Zion, the city of our appointed feasts, Jerusalem an untroubled habitation; the LORD in majesty a place of broad rivers; the LORD is our judge, our lawgiver, our king; he will save us; even the lame will take the prey; no inhabitant will say I am sick; the people who dwell there will be forgiven their iniquity. The unit stays with historic Judah after Sennacherib’s retreat—Hezekiah then Christ, Zion, the LORD our judge and king, as Wesley and Calvin actually read—not a newspaper of later kings.

Isaiah 33:17

17Your eyes will behold the king in his beauty; they will see a land that stretches afar.

Arminian

Wesley: the king—first Hezekiah, and then Christ, triumphing over all enemies, and ruling his own people with righteousness. Very far—thou shalt not be shut up in Jerusalem, but shalt have free liberty to go abroad with honour and safety. The eyes are still Judah’s after Sennacherib’s retreat.

Arminian reading: they might yet have seen that king in beauty. Historic Hezekiah is already named, then Christ. Wesley and Calvin share that order; this is not an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. The church is not to hunt another court to make “the king in his beauty” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: the king in his beauty. He addresses the sincere worshippers of God, to whom he promises this additional blessing. When Jerusalem was besieged, the king shut up within the city and surrounded by treacherous counsellors, the people unsteady and seditious, and everything hastening to ruin, there appeared to be no hope left. Though they behold their king covered with filthy garments, yet he shall be restored to his former rank and splendor. I am, therefore, disposed to view Hezekiah as a figure of Christ, that we may learn how great will be his beauty. The land very far off: the restoration of the Church consists of two parts; first, that the king shall be seen in his beauty; and secondly, that the boundaries of the kingdom shall be extended.

Reformed note: Calvin and Wesley both keep Hezekiah then Christ after that siege. The beauty stays with that restored king, then with Christ. This is already enough without a later newspaper.

Key contrast Both keep the king as Hezekiah then Christ, historic Judah after Sennacherib’s retreat, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads the far land as liberty to go abroad from shut-up Jerusalem with honour and safety. Calvin reads that land as the Church’s second part: the boundaries of the kingdom extended, Christ’s majesty at length openly displayed. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 33:18

18Your heart will muse on the terror: “Where is he who counted, where is he who weighed the tribute? Where is he who counted the towers?”

Arminian

Wesley: thine heart—this is a thankful acknowledgment of deliverance from their former terrors and miseries. Where—these words they spoke in the time of their distress. The scribe, whom we call muster-master, was to make and keep a list of the soldiers, and to call them together as occasion required: the receiver, received and laid out the money for the charges of the war; and he that counted the towers, surveyed all the parts of the city, and considered what towers or fortifications were to be made or repaired. And unto these several officers the people resorted, with great distraction and confusion.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have mused on that terror as past. Historic Jerusalem’s own officers in the siege are already named. The church is not to hunt another tribute-counter to make “where is he who counted” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: thy heart shall meditate terror. He warns the people of approaching distresses, that believers, when delivered, may set a higher value on the condition to which they have attained. Where is the scribe? Where is the weigher? The scribe took account of persons, families, lands, and houses, and kept the registers of the taxes. The weigher received the taxes, for he weighed the money which was paid. The describers of the towers visit and examine each person’s house, hunting-hounds employed by tyrants to scent the track of money; he thinks it probable the Prophet speaks of the receivers of taxes, whom conquerors appoint over vanquished nations.

Reformed accent: the terror is still that tyranny over Judah in the Assyrian hour. The counting stays with those officers of tribute, not a newspaper pasted onto “towers.”

Key contrast Both keep the muse as Judah’s heart after that terror, not a newspaper. Wesley reads the scribe, receiver, and tower-counter as Jerusalem’s own wartime officers—muster-master, war treasurer, surveyor of fortifications—words they spoke in the distress, now a thankful acknowledgment of deliverance. Calvin reads them as the conquerors’ tax men over a vanquished people: the scribe of the registers, the weigher of tribute, the describers of towers as tyrants’ hunting-hounds. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 33:19

19You will see no more the insolent people, the people of an obscure speech that you cannot comprehend, stammering in a tongue that you cannot understand.

Arminian

Wesley: a fierce—that fierce and warlike people, whom thou hast seen with terror, near the walls of Jerusalem, thou shalt see no more. A people—a foreign nation, whose language is unknown to thee. The insolent speech is still Assyria’s after the terror of v. 18.

Arminian reading: they might yet have seen that host no more. Historic Sennacherib’s mixed army is already named. The church is not to stretch “obscure speech” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: thou shalt not see a fierce people. He intends to express the fierceness of the Assyrians, which he afterwards affirms by saying that they would have no intercourse with them, because they spoke a different language. Nothing is more fitted to excite men to compassion than the intercourse of speech; when this is wanting, each party is a barbarian to the other. He dwells on the wretched condition of the people, in order to shew how great was the kindness of God in delivering them from so great terror.

Reformed note: the stammering tongue is still that invader’s. The seeing-no-more stays with Assyria off Judah’s walls, not a newspaper of later speech.

Isaiah 33:20

20Behold Zion, the city of our appointed feasts! Your eyes will see Jerusalem, an untroubled habitation, an immovable tent, whose stakes will never be plucked up, nor will any of its cords be broken.

Arminian

Wesley: look upon—contemplate Zion’s glorious and peculiar privileges. Solemnities—this was the chief part of Zion’s glory, that God was solemnly worshipped, and the solemn assemblies and feasts kept in her. Quiet—this was but imperfectly fulfilled in the literal Zion; but clearly and fully in the mystical Zion, the church of God, in the times of the gospel. The tent is still that Zion’s after the insolent people are gone.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have looked on Zion at peace. Historic Jerusalem’s feasts are already named. Wesley’s own note keeps the literal city first, then the church; this is not a millennial chart and not a newspaper of later tents.

Reformed

Calvin: behold Zion, the city of our solemnities. He brings forward a promise of the restoration of the Church. They assembled there to hear the Law, to confirm the covenant of the Lord, to call upon his name, and to offer sacrifices. Jerusalem a peaceful habitation: although God gave to his people some taste of that peace under the reign of Hezekiah, yet it was only in Christ that the fulfillment of it was manifested. A tent which shall not be carried away, the stakes of which shall never be removed: he describes the condition of the Church; it will remain in spite of storms, in opposition to what appears to be its nature.

Reformed accent: Zion is still that city of appointed feasts. Calvin and Wesley both keep Hezekiah’s quiet, then Christ’s. The immovable tent stays with the Lord’s Zion, not a newspaper pasted onto “stakes.”

Key contrast Both keep Zion as that historic city first, then the Church in Christ, not a newspaper and not a millennial camp fight. Wesley reads the quiet habitation as but imperfectly fulfilled in the literal Zion, clearly and fully in the mystical Zion, the church of God in the times of the gospel. Calvin reads a taste of that peace under Hezekiah, fulfillment in Christ, and the paradox of a tabernacle that cannot be moved because the Lord is in the midst of her. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 33:21

21But there the LORD in majesty will be for us a place of broad rivers and streams, where no galley with oars can go, nor majestic ship can pass.

Arminian

Wesley: there—in and about Zion. Rivers—tho’ we have nothing but a small and contemptible brook to defend us; yet God will be as sure a defence to us, as if we were surrounded with great rivers. No galley—no ships of the enemies shall be able to come into this river to annoy them. The majesty is still the LORD’s over that Zion of v. 20.

Arminian reading: they might yet have had the LORD as their river. Historic Jerusalem’s small brook is already enough. The church is not to hunt another galley to make “broad rivers” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: because there the mighty Jehovah will be to us a place of rivers. He assigns the reason why the Church, which appears to resemble a movable tent, exceeds in stability the best founded cities: the Lord is in the midst of her. He alludes to the situation of Jerusalem, which had only a small rivulet, and not large and rapid rivers like those of Babylon. There shall not pass a ship with oars: large rivers may give access to enemies; but enemies will not be allowed to approach. The hope of safety should not be sought from any other than from God alone.

Reformed note: the streams are still the LORD himself around that Zion. The barred galley stays with Assyria kept off, not a newspaper of later fleets.

Isaiah 33:22

22For the LORD is our judge; the LORD is our lawgiver; the LORD is our king; he will save us.

Arminian

Wesley: is judge—to plead our cause against our enemies. Lawgiver—our chief governor, to whom it belongs, to give laws, and to defend his people. The threefold name is still the LORD’s over historic Judah after the rivers of v. 21.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have been saved by that King. The LORD our judge, lawgiver, and king is already named. Wesley and Calvin share this confession; this is not an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. The church is not to stretch “he will save us” into a later headline.

Reformed

Calvin: for Jehovah is our judge. He explains the manner in which God dwells in the Church: he is there worshipped and acknowledged as Judge, Lawgiver, and King. They who obey God and yield subjection to him as their King shall know by experience that he is the guardian of their salvation. God alone has the right to rule the conscience, because he alone is Lawgiver and Judge, and he alone ought to rule and guide us by his word.

Reformed accent: the saving is still the LORD’s over that Zion. Judge, lawgiver, and king stay with his word in Isaiah’s Judah, not a newspaper pasted onto “he will save us.”

Isaiah 33:23

23Your cords hang loose; they cannot hold the mast firm in its place or keep the sail spread out. Then prey and spoil in abundance will be divided; even the lame will take the prey.

Arminian

Wesley: tacklings—he directs his speech to the Assyrians; and having designed their army under the notion of a gallant ship, verse 21, he here represents their undone condition, by the metaphor of a ship, tossed in a tempestuous sea, having her cables broke, and all her tacklings loose, so that she could have no benefit of her masts and sails; and therefore is quickly swallowed up. The lame—they shall leave so many spoils behind them, that there shall be enough left for the lame, who come last to the spoil.

Arminian reading: they might yet have taken that prey, even the lame. Historic Assyria’s wreck after the retreat is already named. The church is not to hunt another mast to make “the lame will take the prey” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: thy cords were loosed. He directs his discourse to the Assyrians, in whose person he likewise addresses all the enemies of the Church. He compares the condition of wicked men to ships; they have no solid foundation, though they appear to be formidable. The Lord will sink their ships, will take away their ropes and masts, and will involve them in a universal shipwreck. Then is the prey of a great spoil divided; the lame take the prey.

Reformed note: the loose cords are still that army’s. The spoil stays with Sennacherib’s abandoned camp, not a newspaper of later wrecks.

Isaiah 33:24

24And no inhabitant will say, “I am sick”; the people who dwell there will be forgiven their iniquity.

Arminian

Wesley: the inhabitant—of Jerusalem. Sick—shall have no cause to complain of any sickness or calamity. Forgiven—they shall not only receive from me a glorious temporal deliverance; but, which is infinitely better, the pardon of all their sins, and all those spiritual and everlasting blessings, which attend upon that mercy. The health is still Zion’s after the lame take the prey in v. 23.

Arminian close of the unit: they might yet have been forgiven and not said I am sick. Historic Jerusalem after Sennacherib’s retreat is already named. The church is not to stretch “I am sick” into a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: and the inhabitant shall not say, I am sick. He returns to the Church. The citizens of the Church shall be freed from every inconvenience, because through the favor of God they shall enjoy prosperity. The people that dwell in it have been freed from iniquity: all the miseries which press upon us spring from no other source than from our sins; when guilt has been removed, nothing remains but that God will regard us with the affection of a father. True happiness consists in this, that we have obtained pardon from God. None but the citizens of the Church enjoy this privilege.

Reformed close: the forgiven people are still that Zion’s. Calvin and Wesley agree the inhabitant is Jerusalem’s after the deliverance; Calvin will not let pardon become a Papist satisfaction. This health is already enough without a later newspaper.

Key contrast Both keep the inhabitant as Jerusalem’s, forgiven after that historic deliverance, not a newspaper. Wesley reads no cause to complain of sickness or calamity, and then, infinitely better, the pardon of all their sins and the everlasting blessings that attend that mercy. Calvin reads all miseries as springing from sin, so that when iniquity is forgiven the inhabitant no longer says I am sick; he will not let this become a Papist satisfaction, and he will not extend the privilege past the Church. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.
