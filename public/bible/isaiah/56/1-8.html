Passage

Isaiah 56

Verses 1–8 · ESV · Voices

After the Servant’s work and the free invitation of chapter 55, the Lord commands justice and righteousness because his salvation is near. Foreigners and eunuchs who hold the covenant are promised a name in his house; the mountain becomes a house of prayer for all peoples, and the God who gathers Israel’s outcasts will gather still others.

Isaiah 56:1

1Thus says the Lord: “Keep justice, and do righteousness, for soon my salvation will come, and my righteousness be revealed.

Arminian

Wesley: my salvation is that eminent salvation by the Messiah, and without this you shall have no share. “Is near” is Scripture’s way of speaking of things still at a distance as if they were at hand. Righteousness here is the same thing he called salvation.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere nearness: keep justice because the Messiah’s rescue is truly held out. The command is not a ladder into grace; it is the fitting walk of those to whom salvation is coming. Justice and righteousness remain real duties while the door stays open.

Reformed

Calvin: as soon as God holds out tokens of favor, he demands a conversion that changes mind and heart, and then the fruits of repentance. Judgment and righteousness name the second table—not only refusing wrong but helping the neighbor. He calls it “his salvation” and “his righteousness” because he pours them out on men, not because he keeps them shut up in himself.

Reformed accent: nearness excites godliness; it does not wait on our merits. Paul will say the same: put on the armor of light, for salvation is nearer than we thought (Rom 13:11–12). The approaching gift is God’s, and it summons a new life.

Isaiah 56:2

2Blessed is the man who does this, and the son of man who holds it fast, who keeps the Sabbath, not profaning it, and keeps his hand from doing any evil.”

Arminian

Wesley: the man is every man—not only Jews but Gentiles, as the next verses explain. The Sabbath here, like sacrifice elsewhere, stands for the whole worship of God.

Arminian reading: blessing is pronounced on the one who holds fast. Worship and a clean hand are the life of those who receive the coming salvation. The beatitude is sincere toward Jew and Gentile alike; holding fast is a real, grace-enabled perseverance, not a closed caste.

Reformed

Calvin: he calls them happy who, having embraced this doctrine, walk uprightly—lest the wickedness of many who will be deaf retard the elect. Sabbath belongs to the first table; with the hand kept from evil it sketches both tables. God does not rest in ceremony or indolence but demands earnest self-denial.

Reformed note: there is no other way of serving God aright but sincere piety and a blameless life. Salvation is offered by free grace; we are not to be deprived of it by delay. The Sabbath, as synecdoche, includes the whole of religion.

Isaiah 56:3

3Let not the foreigner who has joined himself to the Lord say, “The Lord will surely separate me from his people”; and let not the eunuch say, “Behold, I am a dry tree.”

Arminian

Wesley: the stranger is the Gentile who has turned from dumb idols to the living God. The eunuch is joined with him because Deuteronomy 23:1 forbade him the congregation. Under these two instances he understands all excluded by birth or ceremonial pollution, and so throws open the door to all true believers. A dry tree is a fruitless tree, accursed with barrenness.

Classical Arminians linger here: no joined foreigner and no covenant-keeping eunuch is to preach despair over himself. The Lord forbids that speech. Grace sincerely gathers those the old marks would have shut out.

Reformed

Calvin: even they who formerly were estranged, against whom the door might be said to have been shut, may obtain a new condition. The consolation belongs to those who have followed God when he called; many eunuchs and foreigners never join themselves. He includes under these names all who appear unworthy of being reckoned in his people.

Reformed comfort: look not at yourselves; fix the mind on God’s calling, as Abraham hoped against hope. Peter’s word stands: in every nation the one who fears him and works righteousness is accepted (Acts 10:34–35). Adoption once aimed at Israel is not locked against the joined Gentile or the dry tree God will name.

Isaiah 56:4

4For thus says the Lord: “To the eunuchs who keep my Sabbaths, who choose the things that please me and hold fast my covenant,

Arminian

Wesley: take hold—that steadfastly keep the conditions of my covenant. The eunuch is not blessed as a eunuch, but as one who keeps Sabbaths, chooses what pleases the Lord, and holds the covenant.

Arminian pastoral: choosing and holding are real acts under grace. The old disability does not veto a living grip on the covenant. What God asks is not pedigree but a kept Sabbath and a held promise.

Reformed

Calvin: sincere worshippers who keep the Sabbaths and follow the righteousness of the Law, though they be eunuchs, shall have a place in the Church. With Sabbath-keeping he connects obedience and adherence to the covenant—hence the Sabbath was not an idle ceremony but a figure of perfect holiness.

Reformed accent: they are permitted to “choose,” not whatever they think fit, but what God declares pleasing. Hypocrisy and inconsiderate zeal are condemned. The high rank of the Church is not external. Believers may be despised in the world and still rank high in the sight of God.

Isaiah 56:5

5I will give in my house and within my walls a monument and a name better than sons and daughters; I will give them an everlasting name that shall not be cut off.

Arminian

Wesley: in mine house—in my temple. Better—a far greater blessing and honor than posterity: my favor, my Spirit, and eternal felicity. The dry tree is given a name that sons cannot supply and death cannot crop.

Arminian hope: God himself writes the name. Barrenness in the flesh is not the last word. An everlasting name is gift, held out to those who hold the covenant—comfort for every believer who feared being cut off from the people.

Reformed

Calvin: a place and a name—yd for place, or even dignity as children of God. Better than sons and daughters: the dignity of believers is higher under Christ than it was under the Law. The name is perpetual because it is written in heaven.

Reformed confession: wicked men want a name on earth that fades. This name makes us heirs of the heavenly kingdom. The temple once shut to such persons now holds a memorial God gives, not one they father by succession.

Isaiah 56:6

6“And the foreigners who join themselves to the Lord, to minister to him, to love the name of the Lord, and to be his servants, everyone who keeps the Sabbath and does not profane it, and holds fast my covenant—

Arminian

The foreigner is not a tourist in the covenant. Join, minister, love the Name, be servants, keep Sabbath, hold the covenant—seven marks of a real attachment.

Wesley’s earlier line still governs: the door is open to all true believers. Arminian reading stresses the verbs as sincere, enabled response. Love of the Name is not a Jewish franchise; it is the life of whoever comes and holds fast.

Reformed

Calvin repeats: God will open the temple to all without distinction. Those joined to God by the word—the bond of adoption—are the betrothed of Hosea 2. Not a temple only, but an honorable rank: even those formerly heathens acknowledged as those who minister.

Reformed note: the end of the calling is that they love his name. Hypocrites are excluded; service must be willing. Sabbath again stands for the whole worship, and they must embrace the covenant constantly, not withdraw.

Isaiah 56:7

7these I will bring to my holy mountain, and make them joyful in my house of prayer; their burnt offerings and their sacrifices will be accepted on my altar; for my house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples.”

Arminian

Wesley: mountain—my house on Zion. Joyful—by accepting their services and comforting their hearts with the sense of my love. Accepted—as free access as the Jews themselves. Evangelical worship is described under the expressions then in use. The house is for all peoples; Jesus will cite this word in the temple (Matt 21:13).

Classical Arminians hear the wideness as sincere: God brings, makes joyful, accepts. All peoples are named as welcome to pray, not as a footnote to Israel’s remnant only.

Reformed

Calvin: foreigners formerly excluded are called; the distinction of circumcision is abolished. This cannot mean mere proselytes—that would be nothing new. Grace is diffused through the world, Gentiles united to Jews as one body. He paints the spiritual kingdom of Christ, in which we may everywhere lift up pure hands.

Reformed comfort: sacrifices are acceptable not from their own excellence but from undeserved kindness, and only as sanctified by Christ our altar. The temple is enlarged through the whole world. Calling upon God is the highest sacrifice; those who neglect prayer have no boast in the Church. The vail rent, all peoples call him Abba.

Key contrast Both preach a house of prayer for all peoples and accepted worship in Christ. Arminians typically stress the sincere, worldwide welcome of all who believe and hold the covenant; Reformed readers typically stress God actually bringing and joining those he calls, taking the old wall out of the way by Christ.

Isaiah 56:8

8The Lord God, who gathers the outcasts of Israel, declares, “I will gather yet others to him besides those already gathered.”

Arminian

Wesley: the Lord will gather to himself, and bring into their own land, those cast out of their land. Yet—I will make a far more comprehensive gathering of the Gentiles.

Arminian close: Israel’s outcasts are not forgotten, and “yet others” keeps the door from slamming. The same God restores the scattered and gathers the nations. The invitation of 55 and the justice of 56 meet in a gathering still unfinished until the peoples come.

Reformed

Calvin: repetitions strengthen feeble minds when restoration looks incredible. It belongs to the Lord to gather the dispersed of Israel (Ps 147:2). He will assemble them, and not them only: various peoples added, the Church numerously increased. “Still more will I gather upon him his gathered.”

Reformed close: whenever calamities draw us to doubt the gathering, interpose this shield—it belongs to the Lord. He has never ceased to gather. The outcasts of Israel and the others besides are one work of the God who restores.

Key contrast Both hear Israel gathered and Gentiles added. Arminians typically describe a comprehensive, sincere gathering of all who come; Reformed readers typically describe the Lord’s ongoing, effectual assembling of a numerous Church from Israel’s outcasts and the peoples.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on justice, Sabbath, foreigners and eunuchs, and a house of prayer for all peoples—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Isaiah 56:1–8.

Arminian Notable voices

“My salvation—That eminent salvation by the Messiah… The man—Every man not only Jews but Gentiles… Under these two instances he understands all those, who either by birth, or by any ceremonial pollution, were excluded from church privileges, and so he throws open the door to all true believers… Yet—I will make a far more comprehensive gathering of the Gentiles.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 56:1–3, 8)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Lord now admits, without distinction, those whom he previously forbade… Ye were aliens from the commonwealth of Israel… But now by Christ Jesus, ye who formerly were far off have been made nigh by the blood of Christ. Who gathereth the outcasts of Israel… Still more will I gather upon him his gathered… he will add to them various peoples, that the Church may be very numerously increased.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 56:3, 8)
