Passage

Isaiah 56

Verses 9–12 · ESV · Voices

The welcome of foreigners and eunuchs turns to a summons of wild beasts against a people whose watchmen are blind. Priests and shepherds sleep, crave, and toast tomorrow, while the flock is left to be devoured.

Isaiah 56:9

9All you beasts of the field, come to devour— all you beasts in the forest.

Arminian

Wesley: a prediction of Israel’s destruction by cruel enemies. Having encouraged the Gentiles with gracious promises, the prophet now terrifies unbelieving Jews: as the Gentiles would believe and be saved, so they would reject their Messiah and be destroyed.

Classical Arminians keep this historic: beasts are invading powers, not a modern headline. The fork is faith or refusal. The same Lord who opened the house of prayer (vv. 1–8) now summons judgment on a people who will not have their own salvation.

Reformed

Calvin: the threat seems to clash with the consolation, yet it is mercy to the faithful and a stripping of hypocrites. Believers must not lose courage when ruin draws near; the self-secure must not clutch promises that do not belong to them. Beasts of the field are enemies of every kind—Babylon chiefly, and others after—called in by God, not by chance.

Reformed accent: the gathering of outcasts (v. 8) and the devouring of a neglected flock stand in one book. Providence can restore and still judge shepherds who toast while the wall is down.

Isaiah 56:10

10His watchmen are blind; they are all without knowledge; they are all silent dogs; they cannot bark, dreaming, lying down, loving to slumber.

Arminian

Wesley: his—Israel’s. Watchmen are priests and teachers; ignorance is most shameful in them, and hereby he supposes the people’s ignorance too. Silent dogs: slothful, negligent in instructing, unwilling to reprove sin.

Arminian pastoral: office does not equal sight. Teachers who will not bark leave the flock exposed. The failure is culpable—knowledge was their charge; slumber is chosen. Grace that could have made them faithful watchmen is resisted in the pulpit before it is resisted in the pew.

Reformed

Calvin: the reason for destruction is wicked princes and pastors—not that the people are innocent, but that this is the beginning of the evil. Watchmen include prophets and also judges, kings, the two eyes of the body. If they are unfaithful, no plague is more destructive. Blindness is want of knowledge of duty; dumb dogs are sloth and indifference.

Reformed note: God takes good guides from a people he intends to punish for ingratitude. Shepherds out-slept by ordinary dogs ought to be ashamed. When lazy or savage beasts replace good pastors, acknowledge wrath; destruction is not far off.

Isaiah 56:11

11The dogs have a mighty appetite; they never have enough. But they are shepherds who have no understanding; they have all turned to their own way, each to his own gain, one and all.

Arminian

Wesley: they regard neither God’s glory nor the people’s good, but only the satisfaction of their own base desires. Quarter—in their several stations. Appetite is the theology of the post.

Classical Arminians: gain as a way is a free, guilty turn. Shepherds without understanding still know enough to feed themselves. The contrast with vv. 1–8 is sharp—foreigners holding the covenant while Israel’s teachers hold a purse.

Reformed

Calvin’s third vice: insatiable avarice. Lazy toward government, ravenous toward food. They look to their ways—each consults his own advantage, as if born for himself. From his end: every one in his place, bent on gain, drawing everything to himself without attending to the office.

Reformed warning: no one given up to wicked desires can serve God; the man laboring to amass wealth will not build the Church. Avarice is a blindness pastors must flee if they would be faithful. Isaiah’s complaint is not a curiosity of his century; bad pastors are a recurring judgment.

Isaiah 56:12

12“Come,” they say, “let me get wine; let us fill ourselves with strong drink; and tomorrow will be like this day, great beyond measure.”

Arminian

Wesley: they say it to fellow-priests or jolly companions. Fill ourselves—drink not only to delight but to drunkenness, dreadful security and contempt of God, abandoning care of their own or the people’s souls.

Arminian close: tomorrow as a larger today is unbelief in a toast. The watchmen who would not bark now cannot see the beasts already invited (v. 9). Grace still calls shepherds to wake; this verse shows how a whole order can refuse.

Reformed

Calvin: after avarice and carelessness, desperate obstinacy. They fetch wine against the word, not merely against temperance. Mental drunkenness tramples warnings. “As today, so tomorrow”—if it is well today, it shall be well tomorrow; let us not be miserable before the time. They mock God’s gentleness while he suspends judgment.

Reformed close: when this last medicine is rejected, ruin is near. Let every one examine himself and perceive wrath at a distance, that it may not attack unprepared. The house of prayer for all peoples and the drunken watchman belong to one prophecy: God gathers, and God also hands a neglected flock to the beasts.

Key contrast Both treat the watchmen as truly guilty, not fated. Arminians typically stress sloth and appetite as resistible refusals of a charge they could have kept; Reformed readers typically stress God removing faithful guides and sending devourers as judgment, while the shepherds remain inexcusable.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Israel’s blind watchmen and drunken shepherds—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Isaiah 56:9–12.

Arminian Notable voices

“Come—This is a prediction of Israel’s destruction by their cruel enemies… as the Gentiles would believe, and be saved, so they would reject their Messiah, and be destroyed. Watchmen—Priests and teachers… they are also slothful and negligent in instructing the people, and do not faithfully reprove them for their sins. Fill ourselves—We will drink not only to delight, but even to drunkenness… which shews their dreadful security and contempt of God.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 56:9–12)

Reformed Notable voices

“This prediction appears to be at variance with what goes before… he intended to take from them the ground of false boasting. By the word Watchmen he means not only the prophets… but likewise judges, princes, and kings… If they are wicked or unfaithful, there cannot arise a more destructive plague to a commonwealth. Come ye, I will fetch wine… he points out their desperate wickedness and obstinacy… As today, so tomorrow.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 56:9–12)
