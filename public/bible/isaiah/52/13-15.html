Passage

Isaiah 52

Verses 13–15 · ESV · Voices

After Zion awakes and goes out of Babylon not in haste, a new prophecy opens: Behold, my servant shall act wisely; he shall be high and lifted up. Many were astonished at a visage marred beyond human semblance; so shall he sprinkle many nations, and kings shut their mouths because of him. Historic Servant first, then the gospel of that same Christ.

Isaiah 52:13

13Behold, my servant shall act wisely; he shall be high and lifted up, and shall be exalted.

Arminian

Behold, my servant shall act wisely; he shall be high and lifted up, and shall be exalted. Wesley: Behold—This is the beginning of a new prophecy, which is continued from hence to the end of the next chapter. My servant—That it is Christ who is here spoken of, is so evident, that the Chaldee paraphrast, and other ancient, and some later Hebrew doctors, understand it directly of him, and that divers Jews have been convinced and converted to the Christian faith, by the evidence of this prophecy. Prosper—This is fitly put in the first place to prevent those scandals which otherwise might arise from the succeeding passages, which describe his state of humiliation. Very high—Here are three words signifying the same thing to express the height and glory of his exaltation.

Arminian hearing: a new prophecy, not a continuation of 52:1–12's departure from Babylon; the Servant is Christ, and him only. Shared christology: both camps hear this Behold as Christ. Shared: my servant; act wisely / prosper; high; lifted up; exalted. Historic then gospel: the humiliation is coming in 52:14 and chapter 53, but the first word is his success, so the scandals of the marred visage do not cancel the exaltation. Carry 42:1 and 50:4: the same Servant. Do not merge this unit into 52:1–12; Wesley marks the seam here. No millennial chart of later servants; no newspaper about a living politician.

Reformed

Behold, my servant shall have prosperous success. Calvin: after having spoken of the restoration of the Church, Isaiah passes on to Christ, in whom all things are gathered together. Some explain yashkil to mean shall deal prudently; but, as it is immediately added that he shall be exalted, the context appears to demand that we shall rather understand it to denote prosperous success. He speaks of the prosperity of the Church; and as this was not visible, he draws their attention to the supreme King, by whom all things shall be restored. He calls Christ his Servant on account of the office committed to him; Christ ought not to be regarded as a private individual, but as holding the office to which the Father has appointed him; by giving him the title of Servant, he means that he will be exalted for our sake.

Reformed hearing: after Zion's restoration in 52:1–12, Isaiah passes to Christ. Shared christology: the Servant is Christ; Grotius's Jeremiah-as-type is the exception Calvin names, not the rule. Shared: Servant; prosperity; exaltation. Carry 42:1: Servant by eminence. Do not split 52:13–15 from the atonement of 53, and do not force Babylon's departure back onto this Behold. No dispensational chart; the King restores the Church. No open theism: the success is already spoken.

Key contrast Both hear Behold my servant as Christ, high and lifted up after the restoration of Zion. Wesley takes this as the beginning of a new prophecy continued to the end of chapter 53, prosper fitly put first to prevent scandals from the humiliation that follows, three words stacked to express the height of his exaltation. Calvin takes yashkil as prosperous success rather than merely deal prudently, the Church's unseen prosperity drawn to the supreme King, and Servant as office: exalted for our sake, not as a private man.

Isaiah 52:14

14As many were astonished at you—his appearance was so marred, beyond human semblance, and his form beyond that of the children of mankind—

Arminian

As many were astonished at you—his appearance was so marred, beyond human semblance, and his form beyond that of the children of mankind. Wesley: Astonished—At his humiliation. Thee—At thee, O my servant. His form—Christ, in respect of his birth, breeding, and manner of life, was most obscure and contemptible. His countenance also was so marred with frequent watchings, and fastings, and troubles, that he was thought to be near fifty years old when he was but about thirty, John 8:57, and was farther spoiled with buffetings, and crowning with thorns, and other cruel and despiteful usages.

Arminian streets: the historic Christ, not a later-age chart—mean birth, watchings, thorns, buffetings. Shared: many astonished; visage marred; form beyond the sons of men. Shared christology: Wesley names Christ here without remainder. Carry v. 13: the prospering is spoken first so this marring does not cancel it. The atonement proper waits in 53:4–6, kept whole there; do not split this astonishment off from that song, and do not invent a camp fight on whether the Servant is Christ. No culture war about later faces.

Reformed

As many were amazed. Calvin: he informs them that Christ must first be rejected and humbled, and anticipates that doubt which might have arisen from his singularly debased and unseemly condition. There is no reason why men should be shocked at that unseemliness and disgrace which will be speedily followed by eternal happiness. His glory lay hid under the humble form of the flesh; for though a majesty worthy of the only-begotten Son of God shone forth in him, yet the greater part of men did not see it. The cause of their astonishment was this, that he dwelt among men without any outward show; and the Jews did not think that the Redeemer would come in that condition. When he came to be crucified, their horror was greatly increased. It was necessary that Christ should first be humbled and covered with shame; the shame of the cross was followed by a glorious resurrection attended by the highest honor (Philippians 2:6–11).

Reformed hearing: humiliation first, then the name above every name. Shared: astonished; marred; form beyond men. Shared christology: Calvin names the only-begotten and the cross. Carry v. 13: the King still prospers. Do not force a millennial timetable of later visages; the point is the cross before the exaltation.

Isaiah 52:15

15so shall he sprinkle many nations. Kings shall shut their mouths because of him, for that which has not been told them they see, and that which they have not heard they understand.

Arminian

so shall he sprinkle many nations. Kings shall shut their mouths because of him, for that which has not been told them they see, and that which they have not heard they understand. Wesley: So—His exaltation shall be answerable to his humiliation. Sprinkle—With his word or doctrine; which being often compared to rain or water, may be said to be sprinkled, as it is said to be dropped, Deuteronomy 32:2; Ezekiel 20:46. Kings—Shall be silent before him out of profound humility, reverence, and admiration of his wisdom. For—They shall hear from his mouth many excellent doctrines, which will be new and strange to them. And particularly that comfortable doctrine of the salvation of the Gentiles, which was not only new to them, but strange and incredible to the Jews themselves.

Arminian gospel: the sprinkling is the word dropped like rain; the new thing is Gentile salvation, incredible even to the Jews. Shared: sprinkle many nations; kings shut their mouths; they see what was not told; they understand what they had not heard. Shared christology: the same Christ of v. 13 now teaches the nations. Carry 42:6: a light for the nations. Historic Servant first, then this gospel. Those who do not hear it do not miss it because no word was sprinkled. No dispensational chart of later nations; Wesley's Gentiles are the nations who hear the doctrine. The atonement of 53:1–12 is the next unit, kept whole; do not split it here.

Reformed

So shall he sprinkle many nations. Calvin: some explain it, shall cause to drop, a metaphorical expression for to speak; but since naza signifies to sprinkle, and is commonly found to have this sense in Scripture, I choose rather to adopt this interpretation. He means that the Lord will pour out his Word over many nations. Kings shall shut their mouth in token of astonishment, when the vast magnitude of the subject is such that it cannot be expressed. What they have not heard: this astonishment will not arise merely from Christ's outward appearance, but from the preaching of the Gospel; for, though he had risen from the dead, yet all would have thought that he was still a dead man, if the glory of his resurrection had not been proclaimed. Paul quotes this passage (Romans 15:21). The Jews had heard something of Christ from the Law and the Prophets, but to the Gentiles he was altogether unknown; hence it follows that these words relate strictly to the Gentiles. They shall understand: faith consists in certainty and clear understanding; wherever knowledge of this kind is wanting, faith is unquestionably wanting.

Reformed gospel: the Word poured over nations; resurrection preached, or he still looks dead. Shared: sprinkle; kings silent; what was not told they see. Carry Romans 15:21 as Paul carries it. Historic then gospel: the Servant of v. 13, not a newspaper king. Calvin's certainty of understanding is how faith hears, not a second gospel. No millennial camp fight about later sprinklings.

Key contrast Both hear the marred Servant sprinkling many nations, kings shutting their mouths at what they had not been told. Wesley takes Sprinkle as with his word or doctrine, compared to rain or water dropped, and the new thing as that comfortable doctrine of the salvation of the Gentiles, strange even to the Jews. Calvin also takes sprinkle as the Word poured over many nations, then stresses that the astonishment arises from the preached gospel of the resurrection, not from outward appearance, that the words relate strictly to the Gentiles (Romans 15:21), and that faith is certainty and clear understanding, not implicit ignorance.
