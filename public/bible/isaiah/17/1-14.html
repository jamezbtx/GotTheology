Passage

Isaiah 17

Verses 1–14 · ESV · Voices

An oracle concerning Damascus. Behold, Damascus will cease to be a city and will become a heap of ruins. The cities of Aroer deserted for flocks; the fortress gone from Ephraim and the kingdom from Damascus; the glory of Jacob brought low, gleanings like an olive tree beaten. In that day man will look to his Maker, not to altars his fingers made. Pleasant plants and a stranger’s vine-branch, then a harvest that flees in grief. Ah, the thunder of many peoples; he rebukes them, and at evening terror, before morning they are no more. The unit stays with historic Damascus and Ephraim in the Assyria age—Syria’s league with the northern kingdom against Judah, Tiglath-pileser’s stroke—as Wesley and Calvin actually read—not a newspaper of later states, not a millennial camp fight.

Isaiah 17:1

1An oracle concerning Damascus. Behold, Damascus will cease to be a city and will become a heap of ruins.

Arminian

Wesley: Damascus—both of that city and kingdom. A heap—this was fulfilled by Tiglath-pilneser, 2 Kings xvi, 9, although afterwards it was re-edified. Shared: Damascus falls as a city.

Arminian pastoral: the oracle is a real word to historic Syria. Damascus in the Assyria age, not a twenty-first-century map. Do not turn the heap into a newspaper. Pride’s capital is undone while the league against Judah still stands.

Reformed

Calvin: the burden of Damascus. Here he prophesies against the kingdom of Syria, and mentions the chief city in which the seat of the kingdom lay. The king of Syria had formed an alliance with Israel against Judah, as we saw formerly in the seventh chapter. To free Judah from doubts about God’s assistance, he threatens the destruction of that kingdom. Behold seals the certainty; the metropolis stands for the nation, because a polluted flood of crimes overflows from the chief city into the whole country.

Reformed pastoral: Damascus falls by appointment, for Judah’s comfort. Shared: a heap of ruins. Historic Syria, not a modern state.

Key contrast Both hear historic Damascus undone—Syria’s capital in the Assyria age, not a newspaper. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s fulfillment: Tiglath-pileser, 2 Kings 16:9, though the city was afterwards rebuilt. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: the oracle comforts Judah against the Syria–Israel league of chapter 7; Behold seals it, and the chief city is named because crime floods from the throne into the land.

Isaiah 17:2

2The cities of Aroer are deserted; they will be for flocks, which will lie down, and none will make them afraid.

Arminian

Wesley: Aroer—of that part of Syria, called Aroer, from a great city of that name. These cities were possessed by the Reubenites and Gadites, whom Tiglath-pilneser carried into captivity, 1 Chron. v, 26. These he mentions here, as he doth Ephraim in the next verse, because they were confederate with Syria against Judah. Afraid—because the land shall be desolate, and destitute of men who might disturb them.

Arminian pastoral: flocks lie down where confederates lived. Historic Aroer east of the Jordan, not a modern pasture story. Do not preach deserted cities as a headline.

Reformed

Calvin: the cities of Aroer are forsaken. It is not probable that Aroer here denotes the city which is mentioned elsewhere; it is rather the name of a country. He draws the picture of a country which has been ruined; those places in which cities had been built will be devoted to pasture, and no habitation will be left there but huts and shepherds’ tents.

Reformed pastoral: pasture replaces towns because the Lord has emptied them. Shared: none will make the flocks afraid. Historic Aroer, not a newspaper.

Key contrast Both hear Aroer’s cities given to flocks—historic emptying, not a newspaper. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s Aroer as that Syrian region from a great city of that name, Reuben and Gad carried by Tiglath-pileser because they were confederate with Syria against Judah. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: Aroer is rather the name of a country, not the city of Numbers and Deuteronomy; ruined towns become shepherds’ tents.

Isaiah 17:3

3The fortress will disappear from Ephraim, and the kingdom from Damascus; and the remnant of Syria will be like the glory of the children of Israel, declares the LORD of hosts.

Arminian

Wesley: The fortress—all their fortresses; the singular number being put for the plural. Remnant—the remainders of Damascus and Syria shall be an headless body, a people without a king. Of Israel—Syria shall have as much glory as Israel; that is, neither of them shall have any at all. Shared: fortress and kingdom cease together.

Arminian pastoral: the league’s two heads fall as one. Historic Ephraim and Damascus, not a culture-war speech. Do not hunt another fortress to make this verse a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: the fortress shall cease. He points out the reason why the Lord determines to cut off the kingdom of Syria: they had drawn the kingdom of Israel to their side for the purpose of making war against the Jews. All Israel is here denoted by the name Ephraim. The remnant of Syria: both of these nations shall be brought to nothing. When the Lord punished so severely those two kingdoms, he promoted the benefit of his Church, delivering it by the destruction of its enemies, employing the Assyrians as his agents.

Reformed pastoral: the remnant’s glory is mockery: neither Syria nor Israel keeps what they boasted. Shared: fortress gone from Ephraim, kingdom from Damascus. Historic league, not a millennial decoder.

Isaiah 17:4

4And in that day the glory of Jacob will be brought low, and the fat of his flesh will grow lean.

Arminian

Wesley has no separate lemma on this verse; his gathereth at v. 5 still names the same thinning: so shall the whole body of the ten tribes be carried away captive, some few gleanings only being left. The glory brought low is still Jacob’s after the fortress of v. 3.

Arminian pastoral: fatness grows lean where the ten tribes trusted the league. Historic northern Israel, not a twenty-first-century census. Do not invent a newspaper from “the glory of Jacob.”

Reformed

Calvin: the glory of Jacob shall be diminished. Although he had undertaken to speak of Syria and Damascus, he takes occasion to join Israel with the Syrians, because they were bound by a mutual league. It is probable that he refers only to the ten tribes, who laid claim to the name of the nation; he describes them as glorious in mockery, because, being puffed up with their power and multitude and allies, they despised the Jews their brethren. The fatness of his flesh shall wax lean: on account of their prosperity they became proud, as horses that are fat grow restive; God threatens that he will take away their fatness.

Reformed note: the lean glory is still the ten tribes’. Shared: Jacob brought low in that day. Historic Ephraim, not a later headline.

Isaiah 17:5

5And it shall be as when the reaper gathers standing grain and his arm harvests the ears, and as when one gleans the ears of grain in the Valley of Rephaim.

Arminian

Wesley: Gathereth—taking care, as far as may be, that all may be gathered in, and nothing left. So shall the whole body of the ten tribes be carried away captive, some few gleanings only being left. Rephaim—a very fruitful place near Jerusalem. Shared: a harvest that leaves almost nothing.

Arminian pastoral: Rephaim’s field is a real valley by historic Jerusalem. Do not turn the reaper into a modern coalition. The ten tribes are gathered as grain, not a millennial chart.

Reformed

Calvin: it shall be as when the harvest-man gathereth the corn. He shews by a comparison how great will be the desolation. As the reapers gather the corn in armfuls, so this multitude, though large and extended, will be mowed down by the enemies. That he may not leave a remainder, he adds that at the conclusion of the harvest the ears will be gleaned; even the shaken and scattered ears will not be left. He employs the metaphor of a harvest because the people, trusting to their great numbers, thought themselves beyond the reach of danger.

Reformed pastoral: the Valley of Rephaim names how clean the field is stripped. Shared: standing grain harvested, then gleaned. Historic northern Israel, not a newspaper.

Isaiah 17:6

6Gleanings will be left in it, as when an olive tree is beaten—two or three berries in the top of the highest bough, four or five on the branches of a fruit tree, declares the LORD God of Israel.

Arminian

Wesley: Yet—some few Israelites were left after their captivity, who joined themselves to Judah, and were carried captive to Babylon with them, from whence also they returned with them. Shared: a few berries on the highest bough.

Arminian pastoral: the remnant is real and may yet look to the Maker in v. 7. Historic leftover of the ten tribes, not a modern diaspora decoder. Do not stretch two or three berries into a headline.

Reformed

Calvin: yet gleaning grapes shall be left in it. This metaphor has a different meaning from the former. He comforts the remnant, that they may not be discouraged; though the people be almost wholly destroyed, God will reserve some remnant. Two or three berries in the top of the uppermost bough: the remnant appeared to be almost none at all; this promise belongs also to a small church concealed in a few corners. The Lord will hold out a plank to rescue them from shipwreck.

Reformed pastoral: gleanings are the Lord’s reservation, not chance. Shared: two or three berries left. Historic remnant of Israel, not a millennial camp fight.

Isaiah 17:7

7In that day man will look to his Maker, and his eyes will look on the Holy One of Israel.

Arminian

Wesley: A man—those few men that are left. Look—they shall sincerely respect, and trust, and worship God, and God only. Shared: eyes on the Holy One, not on the altars of v. 8.

Arminian pastoral: the leftover may yet look and live. Historic remnant after the Assyrian stroke, not a newspaper revival. Do not invent open theism from a look; the Maker is already named.

Reformed

Calvin: at that day shall a man look to his Maker. He now shews the fruit of this chastisement, for the godly are thereby led to look to God. The looking is the turning of the heart to the true God, which is the end of afflictions. His eyes shall have respect to the Holy One of Israel: they shall no longer wander after creatures.

Reformed pastoral: the look is the remnant’s repentance under the rod, not process-theism. Shared: man looks to his Maker. Historic Israel, not a later headline.

Isaiah 17:8

8He will not look to the altars, the work of his hands, and he will not look on what his own fingers have made, either the Asherim or the altars of incense.

Arminian

Wesley: Not look—not trust to them, or to worship offered to idols upon them. The work—their own inventions. Groves—which were devised by men, as fit places for the worship of their gods. Images—worshipped in their groves. Shared: fingers’ work is refused.

Arminian pastoral: groves and incense-altars of historic Israel, not a culture-war speech. Do not copy the look that trusted what the hands made.

Reformed

Calvin: and he shall not look to the altars. This contrast shews more clearly that the looking mentioned in the former verse is the true worship of God. The work of his hands: he will not place confidence in idols, the workmanship of his own hands. Groves or images: all the instruments of superstition shall be abhorred.

Reformed note: the refused altars are still those of the ten tribes. Shared: not the work of his hands. Historic Asherim, not a newspaper of later shrines.

Isaiah 17:9

9In that day their strong cities will be like the deserted places of the wooded heights and the hilltops, which they deserted because of the children of Israel, and there will be desolation.

Arminian

Wesley: In—the day of Jacob’s trouble, of which he spake ver. 4. Uppermost branch—which he that prunes the tree neglects, because he esteems it useless and inconsiderable. Left—which they (the Canaanites) left or forsook because of (or for fear of) the children of Israel. And this was a fit example, to awaken the Israelites to a serious belief of this threatening, because God had inflicted the same judgment upon the Canaanites, for the same sins of which they were guilty.

Arminian pastoral: the same emptying that fell on Canaan now waits on Israel. Historic strong cities of the north, not a twenty-first-century ruin. Do not hunt another hilltop to make desolation a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: in that day shall his strong cities be as a forsaken bough. He follows out what he had formerly said about the destruction. As a forsaken bough and an uppermost branch which they left because of the children of Israel: the comparison is taken from the Canaanites, whose cities the Israelites occupied when they were driven out. The Israelites shall be driven out of the same cities, and those places shall be as desolate as they were when the Canaanites fled.

Reformed pastoral: the pattern is the Lord’s: what he did to Canaan he will do to a faithless Israel. Shared: strong cities like deserted heights. Historic land, not a millennial map.

Isaiah 17:10

10For you have forgotten the God of your salvation and have not remembered the Rock of your refuge; therefore, though you plant pleasant plants and sow the vine-branch of a stranger,

Arminian

Wesley: Thou—O Israel. The rock—that God who was thy only sure defense. Plants—excellent flowers and fruit-trees. Strange—fetched from far countries, and therefore highly esteemed. Shared: forgotten Rock, then a stranger’s vine.

Arminian pastoral: pleasant plants do not replace the Rock. Historic Israel’s imported slips, not a modern garden. Do not baptize foreign vines as strength.

Reformed

Calvin: because thou hast forgotten the God of thy salvation. He shews the reason why the Lord determines to inflict so severe a punishment: they have forgotten God, who was the author of their salvation, and have not been mindful of the Rock of their strength. Pleasant plants and strange slips: they were not satisfied with the production of their own country, but in the exercise of unbridled luxury sought for foreign plants; so they forsook God and ran after strange gods.

Reformed note: the stranger’s vine is still that forgetfulness. Shared: forgotten God of salvation. Historic Israel, not a newspaper.

Isaiah 17:11

11though you make them grow on the day that you plant them, and make them blossom in the morning that you sow, yet the harvest will flee away in a day of grief and incurable pain.

Arminian

Wesley: In the day—thou shalt from day to day, beginning early in the morning, use all diligence that what thou hast planted may thrive. But—when this grievous calamity shall come, all your harvest shall be but one heap. Shared: early care, then a harvest that flees.

Arminian pastoral: diligence at dawn does not buy off the stroke. Historic Israel’s plantings, not a twenty-first-century crop report. Incurable pain is the Lord’s day, not a slogan.

Reformed

Calvin: in the day. This denotes the incessant labor which is bestowed on plants and seeds. In the morning shalt thou make thy seed to flourish: they will spare no labor, and will be so eager that they will not wait till the proper season, but will try to hasten the produce. But the harvest shall be a heap in the day of grief and of desperate sorrow: all their labor shall be fruitless, and they shall receive no return.

Reformed pastoral: forced blossom cannot outrun the day of grief. Shared: the harvest flees. Historic planting of v. 10, not a newspaper decoder.

Isaiah 17:12

12Ah, the thunder of many peoples; they thunder like the thundering of the sea! Ah, the roar of nations; they roar like the roaring of mighty waters!

Arminian

Wesley: Woe—this is a new prophecy, added for the comfort of God’s people. Many—combined together against Judah. Seas—who invade my land and people with great force, as the sea does when it enters into the land by a breach. Shared: many peoples roar like waters.

Arminian pastoral: the roar is still the nations around Judah in that age. Do not stretch “many peoples” into a later headline. Comfort is already named: the woe is for Judah’s enemies, not a millennial camp fight.

Reformed

Calvin: Alas for the multitude of many people. Some render Woe, making it to denote execration. Sometimes, as we have said, it is an exclamation of pity. He describes the vast forces of the enemies, that the Jews may not be terrified by their number and power. They make a noise like the noise of the seas: a comparison by which he expresses their rage and violence.

Reformed pastoral: the sea-roar is still those combined against Judah, whom God will rebuke in v. 13. Shared: thunder of many peoples. Historic invaders, not a newspaper of later coalitions.

Isaiah 17:13

13The nations roar like the roaring of many waters, but he will rebuke them, and they will flee far away, chased like chaff on the mountains before the wind and whirling dust before the storm.

Arminian

Wesley has no separate lemma here; his woe of v. 12 and behold of v. 14 still hold the unit: a new prophecy for the comfort of God’s people; combined against Judah like a sea-breach; at even terror, before morning the enemies are cut off. The rebuke is still the Lord’s.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have feared that rebuke. Historic nations roaring against Judah, then fleeing. Do not hunt another chaff to make “he will rebuke them” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: the nations shall rush. Although he appears to follow out that threatening, he now comforts the Church. God shall rebuke them, and they shall flee far off: a single rebuke of God will be sufficient to put to flight all those forces. Chased as the chaff of the mountains before the wind: they shall be scattered with the same ease with which chaff is driven by the wind.

Reformed note: one rebuke, then flight. Shared: roaring waters become chaff. Historic enemies of Judah, not a millennial decoder.

Isaiah 17:14

14At evening time, behold, terror! Before morning, they are no more! This is the portion of those who loot us, and the lot of those who plunder us.

Arminian

Wesley: Behold—at even there is great terror among God’s people, for fear of their enemies; and before the morning comes, their enemies are cut off. Shared: evening terror, morning emptiness.

Arminian close of the unit: the night’s fear is real, and the cut-off is the Lord’s. Historic looters of Judah in the Assyria age, not a twenty-first-century raid. The church is not to stretch “before morning they are no more” into a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: and, behold, at evening tide trouble. The meaning is, As when a storm has been raised in the evening, and as soon as day breaks it is calm, so the terror will be of short duration. Before the morning he is not. This is the portion of them that spoil us: he addresses the believers who were concealed in the kingdom of Israel, and joins them with the Church. We see here what will be the end of the wicked who have persecuted us; they will be like storms which are subdued by their own violence and quickly disappear.

Reformed close: the lot of the plunderer is still that night’s. Calvin and Wesley agree the enemies of Judah vanish before morning; Calvin will not let the storm become a later newspaper. This emptying is already enough.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Damascus a heap, Aroer’s deserted cities, Ephraim’s fortress gone, Jacob’s glory made lean, gleanings on the olive, a look to the Maker, forgotten Rock, and nations roaring like waters then gone by morning—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Isaiah 17:1–14.

Arminian Notable voices

“Damascus—Both of that city and kingdom. A heap—This was fulfilled by Tiglath-pilneser, 2 Kings xvi, 9, although afterwards it was re-edified. Aroer—Of that part of Syria, called Aroer, from a great city of that name. These cities were possessed by the Reubenites and Gadites, whom Tiglath-pilneser carried into captivity, because they were confederate with Syria against Judah. The fortress—All their fortresses. Remnant—The remainders of Damascus and Syria shall be an headless body, a people without a king. Of Israel—Syria shall have as much glory as Israel; that is, neither of them shall have any at all. Gathereth—So shall the whole body of the ten tribes be carried away captive, some few gleanings only being left. Rephaim—A very fruitful place near Jerusalem. Yet—Some few Israelites were left after their captivity, who joined themselves to Judah. A man—Those few men that are left. Look—They shall sincerely respect, and trust, and worship God, and God only. Not look—Not trust to them, or to worship offered to idols upon them. Thou—O Israel. The rock—That God who was thy only sure defense. Plants—Excellent flowers and fruit-trees. Strange—Fetched from far countries, and therefore highly esteemed. Woe—This is a new prophecy, added for the comfort of God’s people. Many—Combined together against Judah. Behold—At even there is great terror among God’s people, for fear of their enemies; and before the morning comes, their enemies are cut off.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 17:1–14)

Reformed Notable voices

“The burden of Damascus. Here he prophesies against the kingdom of Syria, and mentions the chief city in which the seat of the kingdom lay. The king of Syria had formed an alliance with Israel against Judah, as we saw formerly in the seventh chapter. By joining together the Israelites and the Syrians, he summons them to a mutual judgment, in order to show that the only advantage which they had derived from the wicked and disgraceful conspiracy was, to be involved in the same destruction. Isaiah intended to comfort godly persons who were of the tribe of Judah. Behold, Damascus is taken away: the demonstrative particle seals the certainty of the prophecy; a polluted flood of crimes overflows from the chief city into the whole country. The cities of Aroer are forsaken: it is rather the name of a country; those places in which cities had been built will be devoted to pasture. The fortress shall cease: they had drawn the kingdom of Israel to their side for the purpose of making war against the Jews. The glory of Jacob shall be diminished: he takes occasion to join Israel with the Syrians, because they were bound by a mutual league. Yet gleaning grapes shall be left in it: a remnant. At that day shall a man look to his Maker: the fruit of this chastisement. Because thou hast forgotten the God of thy salvation. Alas for the multitude of many people, which make a noise like the noise of the seas: but God shall rebuke them. At evening tide trouble; and before the morning he is not. This is the portion of them that spoil us.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 17:1–14)
