Passage

Isaiah 3

Verses 16–26 · WEB · Voices

After elders who grind the poor, the Lord names the daughters of Zion: haughty walk, wanton glance, tinkling feet. He will strike their heads and take the finery—anklets, crescents, robes, mirrors. Perfume becomes rottenness; a belt, a rope; beauty, branding. Men fall by the sword. Empty, her gates lament; she sits on the ground.

Isaiah 3:16

16Moreover the LORD said, “Because the daughters of Zion are arrogant, and walk with outstretched necks and flirting eyes, walking daintily as they go, jingling ornaments on their feet;

Arminian

The LORD said, Because the daughters of Zion are arrogant, outstretched necks, wanton eyes, mincing, tinkling feet. Wesley: the women; hitherto he reproved the men; tinkling by ornaments on the shoes.

The “daughters” are the women of Jerusalem. Haughty is pride in a walk. The men of 3:14 ground the poor; these daughters share the city’s lifted look (2:11).

Reformed

Calvin: pride is the source; gait shows it. Stretched neck: as a downcast look is modesty, an unnaturally high look is insolence. Wandering eyes: unchaste eyes herald an unchaste heart. Tinkling: wanton gesture. He begins at the fountain so they cannot plead that dress is elegant while the mind is pure.

“Daughters”—the inward disease first. Zion’s daughters, haughty, necks, eyes, feet.

Isaiah 3:17

17therefore the Lord brings sores on the crown of the head of the women of Zion, and the LORD will make their scalps bald.”

Arminian

Therefore the Lord brings sores on the crown of the head of the women of Zion, and the LORD will make their scalps bald. Wesley: by giving her into the power of those enemies that shall strip her of all her raiment.

The humiliation of Zion’s proud women comes through war—enemies who strip the city—under the Lord’s “therefore.” The judgment answers the vanity described in v. 16.

Reformed

Calvin: since words will not reform them, He advances in array. From head to foot, as their obstinacy ran from head to foot. He makes bald the crown where ornament was chiefly bestowed. The Lord himself exhibits the marks of vengeance in every part of the body.

The hand is His before it is the invader’s. Scab, heads, laid bare.

Key contrast Both confess the Lord judges Zion’s haughty daughters, and that the stripping is shame in war. Arminians hear enemies given power to strip (Wesley)—second causes under that rule. Calvinists typically hear the Lord himself making bald and exhibiting vengeance in the body, because words would not reform them (Calvin).

Isaiah 3:18

18In that day the Lord will take away the beauty of their anklets, the headbands, the crescent necklaces,

Arminian

In that day the Lord will take away the beauty of their anklets, the headbands, the crescents. Wesley: ornaments of those times; no need to parse each piece; some wore moon-shaped gear.

The day of 2:12 still historic. Finery is theirs until He takes it. Not a modern fashion fight.

Reformed

Calvin: in that day—the appointed time. He searches the chests and brings the trifles to view so luxury cannot hide. Superfluity, not the body’s need, is on trial.

He removes what they stored. In that day God removes the anklets and crescents.

Isaiah 3:19

19the earrings, the bracelets, the veils,

Arminian

The earrings, the bracelets, the veils, the list continues. The taking continues down the list.

More of Judah’s actual jewelry. The Lord named them; we need not invent a campaign against bracelets.

Reformed

Calvin: he enumerates because their curiosity in gathering ornaments was a world. The heap itself is the evidence.

The catalogue is the charge. The pendants, bracelets, and scarves are named one by one.

Isaiah 3:20

20the headdresses, the ankle chains, the sashes, the perfume containers, the charms,

Arminian

Headdresses, armlets, sashes, perfume boxes, and amulets. Wesley: tablets—boxes of perfumes.

Scent and charm together in Zion. Amulets sit beside the fortune-tellers of 2:6.

Reformed

Calvin: from the head he comes to other parts. Perfume and amulet are not demanded by necessity. The list leaves them without excuse.

Luxury joined to superstition. Both sides agree: headdresses, perfume, amulets.

Isaiah 3:21

21the signet rings, the nose rings,

Arminian

Wesley: nose-jewels fastened to the head, hanging to the beginning of the nose.

The rings worn in the city are taken too. A signet is a name; a nose ring is ornament. Both go.

Reformed

Calvin: rings as part of the same world of ornament. What is the use of earrings and the rest beyond protecting the body? Superfluity has no plea.

God is still the one who takes them away. Both traditions see: signet, nose rings.

Isaiah 3:22

22the fine robes, the capes, the cloaks, the purses,

Arminian

Wesley: pins of silver or gold for hair belong in this drawer of finery; here the verse names the fine robes and purses.

Feast-day dress of Zion’s daughters, not a slogan about closets.

Reformed

Calvin: changeable suits and mantles—more than one garment for show. The palace of pride is a wardrobe.

He takes the feast clothes with the rest. The robes, mantles, cloaks, and purses go as well.

Isaiah 3:23

23the hand mirrors, the fine linen garments, the tiaras, and the shawls.

Arminian

Mirrors, linen garments, turbans, and veils. Wesley: looking-glasses of burnished brass, not glass.

They looked; the Lord takes the looking-piece.

Reformed

Calvin: glasses and fine linen and hoods complete the heap. No end to the contrivances; hence he speaks sharply.

“Mirrors”—the last of the finery. Both note: mirrors, linen, turbans, veils.

Isaiah 3:24

24It shall happen that instead of sweet spices, there shall be rottenness; instead of a belt, a rope; instead of well set hair, baldness; instead of a robe, a wearing of sackcloth; and branding instead of beauty.

Arminian

Wesley: girdles fine and costly; a rent—tattered garments; burning—sun they once guarded against with utmost care.

Reversals in a sacked city. Sackcloth and sun are war’s leftovers, not a lecture on cosmetics.

Reformed

Calvin: things exceedingly opposite. Ungirded, torn, naked. Baldness against curls. Burning against beauty: fashionable ladies hardly expose themselves to the sun; he says this will happen. Men and women are to make a sober use of gifts; the Lord cannot endure extravagance.

He writes the opposite of what they stored.

Isaiah 3:25

25Your men shall fall by the sword, and your mighty in the war.

Arminian

Your men shall fall by the sword, and your mighty in the. Wesley’s city-wide stroke: the daughters’ men die in war.

“Men”—battle over Judah, not a gender war. Sword is still the mouth of the Lord (1:20). Husbands and mighty fall; the women of v. 16 are widowed by that fall.

Reformed

Calvin: he directs the discourse to Jerusalem and the whole kingdom. After the women, he returns to the general doctrine. The whole strength brought down by wars. The body was infected; no part spared.

The same wrath that stripped finery takes the army.

Isaiah 3:26

26Her gates shall lament and mourn. She shall be desolate and sit on the ground.

Arminian

Her gates shall lament and mourn. She shall be desolate and sit on the ground. Wesley: gates of Zion lament because no people go out or in; she sits like a woman bewailing husband and children.

The command to sit pictures an empty city. The daughter of Zion of 1:8 (booth, lodge, besieged) now sits on the dirt. The proud walk of v. 16 has nowhere left to mince.

Reformed

Calvin: where crowds met, a dismal solitude. Public meetings were at the gates; they rejoice at a multitude and mourn at desolation. Jerusalem as a widow sitting on the ground, as mourners of that nation used to do. The city has lost her inhabitants.

The solitude is the last exhibit of the day. On this both agree: gates lament, empty, sit on the ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on haughty daughters of Zion, a city’s finery taken, and gates that sit empty, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“The daughters—the women; hitherto he reproved the men. A tinkling—by some ornaments which they wore upon their shoes. Secret parts—by giving her into the power of those enemies that shall strip her of all her raiment. Cauls—ornaments used in those times; it is of no concern exactly to understand the nature and differences of them. Burning—by the heat of the sun, to which they are now commonly exposed, from which they used formerly to guard themselves with the utmost care. Gates—of Zion or Jerusalem, said to lament to imply the great desolation; no people to go out and come in. Shall sit—like a mournful woman bewailing the loss of her husband and children.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 3:16–26)

Reformed Notable voices

“Because the daughters of Zion are haughty. He justly declares pride to be the source of the evil, and points it out by the sign—the stretched-forth neck. Therefore will the Lord make bald the crown of the head. Since neither gentle advices nor any words can reform them, the Lord will deal with them in a very different manner, and will exhibit the marks of his vengeance in every part of their body. Thy men shall fall by the sword. He directs his discourse to Jerusalem and to the whole kingdom of Judah; after leaving not even spared the women, he returns to the general doctrine. Her gates shall mourn: where there were great crowds, nothing but a dismal solitude will be found. Jerusalem is compared to a woman who bewails her widowhood; it was customary with mourners to sit on the ground.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 3:16–26)
