Passage

Isaiah 3

Verses 16–26 · ESV · Voices

After elders who grind the poor, the Lord names the daughters of Zion: haughty walk, wanton glance, tinkling feet. He will strike their heads and take the finery—anklets, crescents, robes, mirrors. Perfume becomes rottenness; a belt, a rope; beauty, branding. Men fall by the sword. Empty, her gates lament; she sits on the ground.

Isaiah 3:16

16The Lord said: Because the daughters of Zion are haughty and walk with outstretched necks, glancing wantonly with their eyes, mincing along as they go, tinkling with their feet,

Arminian

The Lord said: because the daughters of Zion are haughty, outstretched necks, wanton eyes, mincing, tinkling feet. Wesley: the women; hitherto he reproved the men; tinkling by ornaments on the shoes.

Arminian daughters: historic women of Jerusalem, not a culture-war on fashion or gender. Haughty is real pride in a real walk. The men of 3:14 ground the poor; these daughters share the city’s lifted look (2:11).

Reformed

Calvin: pride is the source; gait shows it. Stretched neck: as a downcast look is modesty, an unnaturally high look is insolence. Wandering eyes: unchaste eyes herald an unchaste heart. Tinkling: wanton gesture. He begins at the fountain so they cannot plead that dress is elegant while the mind is pure.

Reformed daughters: the inward disease first. Shared: Zion’s daughters, haughty, necks, eyes, feet. Therefore is v. 17.

Isaiah 3:17

17therefore the Lord will strike with a scab the heads of the daughters of Zion, and the Lord will lay bare their secret parts.

Arminian

Therefore the Lord will strike with a scab the heads of the daughters of Zion, and the Lord will lay bare their secret parts. Wesley: by giving her into the power of those enemies that shall strip her of all her raiment.

Arminian strike: a real scab on real heads. The laying bare is historic stripping in war—enemies with hands—under the Lord who speaks therefore. Not a leering verse and not a slogan. Match the hub: a King who judges, leaving this enemy-stripping as a real second cause.

Reformed

Calvin: since words will not reform them, He advances in array. From head to foot, as their obstinacy ran from head to foot. He makes bald the crown where ornament was chiefly bestowed. The Lord himself exhibits the marks of vengeance in every part of the body.

Reformed strike: the hand is His before it is the invader’s. Shared: scab, heads, laid bare.

Key contrast Both confess the Lord judges Zion’s haughty daughters, and that the stripping is shame in historic war, not a culture-war on dress. Arminians typically hear enemies given power to strip (Wesley)—real second causes under that rule. Reformed readers typically hear the Lord himself making bald and exhibiting vengeance in the body, because words would not reform them (Calvin).

Isaiah 3:18

18In that day the Lord will take away the finery of the anklets, the headbands, and the crescents;

Arminian

In that day the Lord will take away the finery of the anklets, the headbands, and the crescents. Wesley: ornaments of those times; no need to parse each piece; some wore moon-shaped gear.

Arminian take: the day of 2:12 still historic. Finery is theirs until He takes it. Not a modern fashion fight.

Reformed

Calvin: in that day—the appointed time. He searches the chests and brings the trifles to view so luxury cannot hide. Superfluity, not the body’s need, is on trial.

Reformed take: He removes what they stored. Shared: that day, anklets, crescents.

Isaiah 3:19

19the pendants, the bracelets, and the scarves;

Arminian

The pendants, the bracelets, and the scarves. The list continues. Same taking.

Arminian pendants: more of Judah’s actual jewelry. The Lord named them; we need not invent a campaign against bracelets.

Reformed

Calvin: he enumerates because their curiosity in gathering ornaments was a world. The heap itself is the evidence.

Reformed pendants: the catalogue is the charge. Shared: pendants, bracelets, scarves.

Isaiah 3:20

20the headdresses, the armlets, the sashes, the perfume boxes, and the amulets;

Arminian

Headdresses, armlets, sashes, perfume boxes, and amulets. Wesley: tablets—boxes of perfumes.

Arminian boxes: scent and charm together in historic Zion. Amulets sit beside the fortune-tellers of 2:6.

Reformed

Calvin: from the head he comes to other parts. Perfume and amulet are not demanded by necessity. The list leaves them without excuse.

Reformed boxes: luxury joined to superstition. Shared: headdresses, perfume, amulets.

Isaiah 3:21

21the signet rings and nose rings;

Arminian

Signet rings and nose rings. Wesley: nose-jewels fastened to the head, hanging to the beginning of the nose.

Arminian rings: real wear in that city. A signet is a name; a nose ring is ornament. Both go.

Reformed

Calvin: rings as part of the same world of ornament. What is the use of earrings and the rest beyond protecting the body? Superfluity has no plea.

Reformed rings: still His taking. Shared: signet, nose rings.

Isaiah 3:22

22the festal robes, the mantles, the cloaks, and the handbags;

Arminian

Festal robes, mantles, cloaks, and handbags. Wesley: pins of silver or gold for hair belong in this drawer of finery; here the ESV names the festival clothes and bags.

Arminian robes: feast-day dress of Zion’s daughters, not a slogan about closets.

Reformed

Calvin: changeable suits and mantles—more than one garment for show. The palace of pride is a wardrobe.

Reformed robes: He takes the feast clothes with the rest. Shared: robes, mantles, cloaks, handbags.

Isaiah 3:23

23the mirrors, the linen garments, the turbans, and the veils.

Arminian

Mirrors, linen garments, turbans, and veils. Wesley: looking-glasses of burnished brass, not glass.

Arminian mirrors: they looked; the Lord takes the looking-piece. Historic brass, historic linen.

Reformed

Calvin: glasses and fine linen and hoods complete the heap. No end to the contrivances; hence he speaks sharply.

Reformed mirrors: the last of the finery. Shared: mirrors, linen, turbans, veils. Instead is v. 24.

Isaiah 3:24

24Instead of perfume there will be rottenness; and instead of a belt, a rope; and instead of well-set hair, baldness; and instead of a rich robe, a skirt of sackcloth; and branding instead of beauty.

Arminian

Instead of perfume, rottenness; instead of a belt, a rope; instead of well-set hair, baldness; instead of a rich robe, sackcloth; branding instead of beauty. Wesley: girdles fine and costly; a rent—tattered garments; burning—sun they once guarded against with utmost care.

Arminian instead: real reversals in a sacked city. Sackcloth and sun are war’s leftovers, not a lecture on cosmetics.

Reformed

Calvin: things exceedingly opposite. Ungirded, torn, naked. Baldness against curls. Burning against beauty: fashionable ladies hardly expose themselves to the sun; he says this will happen. Men and women are to make a sober use of gifts; the Lord cannot endure extravagance.

Reformed instead: He writes the opposite of what they stored. Shared: rottenness, rope, baldness, sackcloth, branding.

Isaiah 3:25

25Your men shall fall by the sword and your mighty men in battle.

Arminian

Your men shall fall by the sword and your mighty men in battle. Wesley’s city-wide stroke: the daughters’ men die in war.

Arminian men: historic battle over Judah, not a gender war. Sword is still the mouth of the Lord (1:20). Husbands and mighty fall; the women of v. 16 are widowed by that fall.

Reformed

Calvin: he directs the discourse to Jerusalem and the whole kingdom. After the women, he returns to the general doctrine. The whole strength brought down by wars. The body was infected; no part spared.

Reformed men: the same wrath that stripped finery takes the army. Shared: men, sword, mighty, battle.

Isaiah 3:26

26And her gates shall lament and mourn; empty, she shall sit on the ground.

Arminian

Her gates shall lament and mourn; empty, she shall sit on the ground. Wesley: gates of Zion lament because no people go out or in; she sits like a woman bewailing husband and children.

Arminian sit: a real empty city. The daughter of Zion of 1:8 (booth, lodge, besieged) now sits on the dirt. Historic gates, not a newspaper. The proud walk of v. 16 has nowhere left to mince.

Reformed

Calvin: where crowds met, a dismal solitude. Public meetings were at the gates; they rejoice at a multitude and mourn at desolation. Jerusalem as a widow sitting on the ground, as mourners of that nation used to do. The city has lost her inhabitants.

Reformed sit: the solitude is the last exhibit of the day. Shared: gates lament, empty, sit on the ground.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on haughty daughters of Zion, a city’s finery taken, and gates that sit empty—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Isaiah 3:16–26.

Arminian Notable voices

“The daughters—the women; hitherto he reproved the men. A tinkling—by some ornaments which they wore upon their shoes. Secret parts—by giving her into the power of those enemies that shall strip her of all her raiment. Cauls—ornaments used in those times; it is of no concern exactly to understand the nature and differences of them. Burning—by the heat of the sun, to which they are now commonly exposed, from which they used formerly to guard themselves with the utmost care. Gates—of Zion or Jerusalem, said to lament to imply the great desolation; no people to go out and come in. Shall sit—like a mournful woman bewailing the loss of her husband and children.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 3:16–26)

Reformed Notable voices

“Because the daughters of Zion are haughty. He justly declares pride to be the source of the evil, and points it out by the sign—the stretched-forth neck. Therefore will the Lord make bald the crown of the head. Since neither gentle advices nor any words can reform them, the Lord will deal with them in a very different manner, and will exhibit the marks of his vengeance in every part of their body. Thy men shall fall by the sword. He directs his discourse to Jerusalem and to the whole kingdom of Judah; after leaving not even spared the women, he returns to the general doctrine. Her gates shall mourn: where there were great crowds, nothing but a dismal solitude will be found. Jerusalem is compared to a woman who bewails her widowhood; it was customary with mourners to sit on the ground.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 3:16–26)
