Passage

Isaiah 32

Verses 9–20 · ESV · Voices

Rise up, you women who are at ease; complacent daughters, give ear: in little more than a year the vintage fails; tremble, strip, sackcloth; beat the breast for pleasant fields; thorns on the soil of my people; palace forsaken, hill and watchtower dens. Until the Spirit is poured upon us from on high, and the wilderness becomes a fruitful field; then justice, peace, quietness and trust. The unit stays with historic Judah after the king of 32:1–8—Assyria’s shadow, then the Spirit in Messiah’s days as Wesley and Calvin actually read—not a newspaper of later ease.

Isaiah 32:9

9Rise up, you women who are at ease, hear my voice; you complacent daughters, give ear to my speech.

Arminian

Wesley: ye—that indulge yourselves in idleness and luxury. Careless—who are insensible of your sin and danger. The call is still to Judah’s daughters at ease, after the noble of v. 8, not a later salon.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have risen and heard. Ease and luxury in that court are already enough. The church is not to hunt another set of daughters to make “at ease” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: ye women at ease, arise. He addresses women rather than men, in order to shew the extent of that calamity; in ordinary circumstances women and children are spared. He expressly addresses them as women at ease, who are usually more delicate, and, enjoying the advantages of wealth, have some means of providing for their safety. After promising grace to believers, the prophets next warn hypocrites that the mercy promised to believers will be of no avail to them.

Reformed accent: the voice is still Isaiah’s over Judah’s careless daughters. The church hears that historic ease, not a newspaper pasted onto “rise up.”

Key contrast Both keep the women as Judah’s own daughters at ease, not cities and not a newspaper. Wesley reads idleness and luxury, insensible of sin and danger. Calvin reads why women are named—to show the extent of the blow, since women are commonly spared—and treats the speech as a turn from promised grace to hypocrites, that the mercy will not avail them. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 32:10

10In little more than a year you will shudder, you complacent women; for the grape harvest fails, the fruit harvest will not come.

Arminian

Wesley: the vintage shall fail—during the time of the Assyrian invasion. The gathering—of the other fruits of the earth. The year is still that invasion’s year over complacent Judah.

Arminian reading: they might yet have shuddered before the vintage failed. Historic Assyria is already named. The church is not to stretch “little more than a year” into a later harvest.

Reformed

Calvin: days above a year. By these words he declares that the calamity will be of long duration; he threatens not only that they shall endure them for one year, but that afterwards they must look for new afflictions. Because they were sunk in their pleasures, the Prophet addresses their bellies rather than their understandings, threatening a scarcity of wine and wheat.

Reformed note: the vintage fails to shake off sloth in that Judah. The shudder stays with Isaiah’s year, not a newspaper of later vintages.

Isaiah 32:11

11Tremble, you women who are at ease, shudder, you complacent ones; strip, and make yourselves bare, and tie sackcloth around your waist.

Arminian

Wesley: strip—put off your ornaments. Trembling and sackcloth are still the dress of those women at ease after the vintage of v. 10.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have put off the ornaments. Judah’s own sackcloth is already enough. The church is not to hunt another waist to make “tremble” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: this repetition is not unnecessary; when men are asleep, they are not easily aroused. He describes the manner and dress of mourners: whenever they were visited by deep adversity, they put on sackcloth, made bare the other parts of their body. He desires women to put on sackcloth instead of the luxuries in which they eagerly indulged. This exhortation ought to be explained in the future tense: you shall at length tremble.

Reformed accent: prosperity had blinded them; the Lord will arouse them from ease. The sackcloth stays with those daughters, not a later headline.

Isaiah 32:12

12Beat your breasts for the pleasant fields, for the fruitful vine,

Arminian

Wesley: the teats—for the pleasant and fruitful fields, which like teats yielded you plentiful and excellent nourishment. The beating of the breast is still for Judah’s fields and vine after the strip of v. 11.

Arminian reading: they might yet have lamented the fields that nursed them. Historic vintage-country is already named. The church is not to rename “pleasant fields” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: some understand that there will be so great a scarcity that women will lose their milk; the more appropriate interpretation is to view “breasts” as figuratively denoting fields and vineyards, because, by deriving nourishment from them, we suck the milk or blood of the earth. The Lord will curse the earth, so that it shall yield no fruits.

Reformed note: the fields are still the land of his people. The lament stays with that curse, not a newspaper pasted onto “fruitful vine.”

Isaiah 32:13

13for the soil of my people growing up in thorns and briers, yes, for all the joyous houses in the exultant city.

Arminian

Wesley: yea—upon that ground, where now your houses stand, in which you take your fill of mirth and pleasure. Thorns and briers are still on the soil of my people, Judah’s joyous houses.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have left the houses of mirth. The exultant city is already Jerusalem’s. The church is not to stretch “thorns” into a later map.

Reformed

Calvin: he confirms the former verse: fields which used to be fat and fertile will be uncultivated, desolate, and barren. On the land of my people: by giving it this name he meets the objection that the land God had chosen would not fail; they made void the blessing by their wickedness. Even on all the houses of joy: this desolation will be even in the splendid houses which formerly were the abodes of the most refined luxury.

Reformed accent: a land flowing with milk and honey is covered with briers because they drew down wrath. The houses stay in that Judah, not a newspaper of later cities.

Isaiah 32:14

14For the palace is forsaken, the populous city deserted; the hill and the watchtower will become dens forever, a joy of wild donkeys, a pasture of flocks;

Arminian

Wesley: forsaken—of God, and given up into their enemies hands. A joy—desolate places, in which wild asses delight to be. Palace and watchtower are still that city’s, after the thorns of v. 13.

Arminian close of the waste: they might yet have seen the palace emptied. Historic enemies’ hands are already named. The church is not to hunt another hill to make “dens forever” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: he describes more fully the desolation: palaces and cities, all that was splendid and lofty in Judea, will be brought to nothing. When he says for ever, he gives warning that this calamity will not last only for a single day; they had been long hardened in their vices. Men are dazzled by their own splendor till they lift up their eyes to heaven.

Reformed note: nothing exempted—palace, city, fortress. The forsaking stays with Judea, not a later skyline pasted onto “watchtower.”

Isaiah 32:15

15until the Spirit is poured upon us from on high, and the wilderness becomes a fruitful field, and the fruitful field is deemed a forest.

Arminian

Wesley: until—until the time come, in which God will pour, or, as the Hebrew word properly signifies, reveal, evidently and plentifully pour out his spirit from heaven upon his people, which was fully accomplished in the days of the Messiah. The fruitful field—God’s people who were desolate, shall be revived and flourish, and their flourishing enemies shall be brought to destruction. The pouring is still after that historic waste, then the Messiah’s days.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have waited for that Spirit. Wesley’s own gospel step is already the days of the Messiah, not a millennial chart and not a newspaper of later outpourings. The church is not to hunt another high place to make “poured upon us” later news.

Reformed

Calvin: till the Spirit be poured out upon you. The restoration of the Church proceeds solely from the grace of God; he raises their eyes to the very Author of life. While Isaiah thus prophesies concerning the reign of Hezekiah, all this is declared to relate to the kingdom of Christ as its end and accomplishment. We who were wildernesses may become cultivated and fertile fields; they whose natural dispositions had some appearance of goodness, being renewed by the same Spirit, will afterwards be so fruitful that they will appear as if they had formerly been a wilderness.

Reformed accent: barrenness removed the moment he imparts strength from heaven. The orchard stays with that restoration under Hezekiah and then Christ, not a newspaper pasted onto “from on high.”

Key contrast Both keep the Spirit poured from on high after Judah’s waste, then in Christ, not a newspaper. Wesley reads the pour as fully accomplished in the days of the Messiah: desolate people revived, flourishing enemies brought to destruction. Calvin reads both wilderness and Carmel as the same people’s fertility under grace—Carmel so rich it looks like a former wilderness—and will not map the forest as destroyed enemies; the restoration proceeds solely from the Spirit, Hezekiah’s reign as type, Christ’s kingdom as end. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 32:16

16Then justice will dwell in the wilderness, and righteousness abide in the fruitful field.

Arminian

Wesley: judgment—just judgment. Righteousness—justice shall be executed in all the parts of the land. The dwelling of justice is still that land’s after the Spirit of v. 15.

Arminian reading: they might yet have had just judgment in every part. Historic Judah’s field and wilderness are already enough. The church is not to stretch “justice” into a later court.

Reformed

Calvin: the Prophet shews what is the actual condition of the Church, that is, when justice and judgment prevail; men ought not to be like cattle, which seek nothing but plenty of food. The Jews were not confined to transitory enjoyments. Under justice and judgment he includes all that belongs to uprightness, including piety and the worship of God. This is the end of all the blessings which the Lord bestows, that we should serve him in holiness and righteousness.

Reformed note: the wilderness and the field both get judgment when the Spirit has been poured. The church hears that condition of the restored Church, not a newspaper of later justice.

Isaiah 32:17

17And the effect of righteousness will be peace, and the result of righteousness, quietness and trust forever.

Arminian

Wesley: the work—the effect of this shall be prosperity. Quietness—tranquility, both of mind and outward estate. Assurance—of God’s mercy, and the fulfilling of his promises. Peace is still the fruit of that land’s righteousness after v. 16.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have had that quietness. Wesley’s assurance is the promise kept, not a later chart. The church is not to hunt another peace to make “forever” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: he censured that peace which made the Jews drowsy; he now promises a different kind of repose, a striking proof of the love of God. He contrasts that brutal repose which the reprobate think they obtain by presumption with the repose the children of God obtain by a religious and holy life. Wars proceed from the wrath of God, which we provoke by our wickedness; peace springs from his blessing. These things do not relate exclusively to Hezekiah, but must be referred to Christ.

Reformed accent: the effect of righteousness is not the old ease of vv. 9–11. The quietness stays with the restored Church under Christ, not a newspaper of later calm.

Key contrast Both keep peace as the fruit of righteousness after the Spirit, in Judah then in Christ, not a newspaper. Wesley reads the work as prosperity, quietness as tranquility of mind and outward estate, assurance of God’s mercy and the fulfilling of his promises. Calvin refuses the drowsy ease he has just condemned: a different repose of those reconciled to God, referred to Christ, not the reprobate’s brutal rest. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 32:18

18My people will abide in a peaceful habitation, in secure dwellings, and in quiet resting places.

Arminian

Wesley does not gloss this verse. His notes run from the work of righteousness in v. 17 to the hail of v. 19. The peaceful habitation is still my people after that quietness—historic Judah first, then the gospel rest Wesley names from the Spirit of v. 15.

Arminian close of the dwelling: they might yet have abode in those resting places. Wesley and Calvin both keep this people as Judah under the poured-out Spirit, then under Christ; this is not an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. The church is not to stretch “secure dwellings” into a later newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: as spiritual righteousness is that which has its seat in the hearts of men, we must say the same thing about peace, which is the fruit of it. When quiet habitations are mentioned, let us remember Paul: justified by faith, we have peace with God. When Christ says that he leaves this peace to the disciples, he affirms that it cannot be given by the world. A bad conscience is always alarmed. Assured peace is enjoyed by none but believers, who appeal to the heavenly tribunal by their piety and by their reliance on the mercy of God.

Reformed close: Christ does not yet reign where consciences are uneasy. The habitation stays with that people under his peace, not a newspaper pasted onto “quiet resting places.”

Isaiah 32:19

19And it will hail when the forest falls down, and the city will be utterly laid low.

Arminian

Wesley: it shall hail—as my blessings shall be poured down upon my people, who, from a wilderness, are turned into a fruitful field, so my judgments (which are signified by hail, chap. 28:2, 17, and elsewhere) shall fall upon them, who were a fruitful field, but are turned into a forest, upon the unbelieving and rebellious Jews. The city—Jerusalem, which, though now it was the seat of God’s worship and people, yet he foresaw, would be the great enemy of the Messiah. Low—shall be greatly humbled, or brought very low.

Arminian reading: hail on that forest is still judgment in the same history that ends at Christ, not a millennial camp fight and not a newspaper of later capitals. The church is not to hunt another city to make “laid low” later news.

Reformed

Calvin: the prophets describe under figures the reign of Christ. The Lord will remove from his people distresses and annoyances, and will make them fall on others. By forests he means unfrequented and desert places, where there are no crowds of men. The city shall be situated to a low place: cities which shall be situated on level ground will be out of danger; they will not need the ordinary fortifications, because the city may be safely set down in valleys; the hand of the Lord will protect it.

Reformed note: we must not seek safety by relying on our defences. The hail is turned from the people under Christ’s guard, not a newspaper of later storms.

Key contrast Both keep hail and city in the same restoration after the Spirit, not a newspaper. Wesley reads hail as judgment on the unbelieving and rebellious Jews, the forest as those who were a fruitful field turned barren, and the city as Jerusalem, which he foresaw would be the great enemy of the Messiah and brought very low. Calvin reads the hail as distresses removed from the people and made to fall on desert forests, and the city low in a low place as safety without walls because God is protector. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 32:20

20Happy are you who sow beside all waters, who let the feet of the ox and the donkey range free.

Arminian

Wesley: blessed—as the barren forest shall be destroyed, so the fruitful field shall be improved, and bring forth much fruit; which is signified by a declaration of the blessedness of them that sow in it. Waters—in all moist grounds, which are like to yield good fruit. But this also is to be understood of the times of the gospel, and of the great and happy success of the ministers of it. The ox—which they employed in plowing and sowing the ground.

Arminian close of the range: they might yet have sown beside those waters. Wesley’s gospel ministers are already the success of that sowing, not a later chart. The church is not to stretch “ox and donkey” into a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: blessed are ye. He shews how great will be the change, when Christ shall begin to reign; formerly thorns and briers would overspread the holy land; now they shall be blessed, because God will give them abundant produce. Places overrun with waters shall be fit for sowing; the corn will grow so thick that it shall be necessary to send oxen and asses to crop the early blade. They who complain that it is not yet fulfilled ought to consider whether they themselves are purified from every stain of sin; let them be satisfied with enjoying the blessing of God according to the measure of regeneration, the full enjoyment of which we must not expect till, freed from the pollution of the flesh, we shall bear the perfect image of God.

Reformed close: Calvin and Wesley agree the sowing is gospel fertility under Christ after Judah’s waste; this blessedness is already enough without a later newspaper. No millennial dispute is in this comment.

Key contrast Both keep the sowers as the restored people under the gospel after the forest of v. 19, not a newspaper. Wesley reads the blessedness of those who sow in the improved field, and of the ministers of the gospel whose labor is the ox at the plow. Calvin reads fertility so great they sow in marshes and send cattle into the crop, and answers the complaint that this has not yet been seen: measure the blessing by regeneration, full only when the flesh is put off. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.
