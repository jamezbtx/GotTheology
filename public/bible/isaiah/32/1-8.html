Passage

Isaiah 32

Verses 1–8 · ESV · Voices

Behold, a king will reign in righteousness, and princes will rule in justice: hiding-place from the wind, streams in a dry place, shade of a great rock; eyes of those who see not closed; the hasty understand; the fool no more called noble; the scoundrel’s lying words against the poor; the noble plans noble things. The unit stays with historic Judah after Ahaz—Hezekiah first, then the Messiah of whom he is a type, as Wesley and Calvin actually read—not a newspaper of later thrones.

Isaiah 32:1

1Behold, a king will reign in righteousness, and princes will rule in justice.

Arminian

Wesley: this seems to be a distinct prophecy from the former, and delivered before that which is related in the former chapters. The foregoing prophecy was delivered, not in the time of Ahaz—for he sent to the Assyrian, not the Egyptian, for help; it was Hezekiah who rebelled against the king of Assyria and was too prone to trust upon the staff of Egypt. But this seems to have been delivered in the time of Ahaz. A king—Hezekiah, a type of Christ, and Christ typified by him. The throne is still that historic king’s, then the gospel antitype.

Arminian behold: they might yet have that king. Shared christology—Wesley names Hezekiah as type and Christ as the one typified. The church is not to hunt another throne to make “reign in righteousness” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: God will still be gracious to his Church, so as to restore her entirely; the best method of restoring her is when good government is maintained. This prediction undoubtedly relates to Hezekiah and his reign, under which the Church was reformed; formerly it was wretched and ruinous. Ahaz, a wicked and disgraceful hypocrite, had overturned civil government and religion. He therefore promises another king, namely, Hezekiah. As this cannot be attained without Christ, this description undoubtedly refers to Christ, of whom Hezekiah was a type, and whose kingdom he foreshadowed. And princes shall rule: it would not be enough to be a good prince if he were not supported by upright ministers and counselors.

Reformed accent: they share the same king—Hezekiah, then Christ. The princes stay with that court, not a newspaper of later cabinets.

Key contrast Both name the king as Hezekiah, a type of Christ, and Christ as the one typified—shared christology, not a millennial camp fight, and not a newspaper. Wesley dates the word to Ahaz’s time, a distinct prophecy from the Egypt-trust chapters of Hezekiah’s day. Calvin reads it as the Church restored under Hezekiah after Ahaz’s ruin, and adds that a good king still needs upright princes. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 32:2

2Each will be like a hiding place from the wind, a shelter from the storm, like streams of water in a dry place, like the shade of a great rock in a weary land.

Arminian

Wesley: a man—each of his princes. A hiding place—unto the people under their government. The wind—from the rage and violence of evil men. As rivers—no less refreshing. As the shadow—in a dry and scorched country, which is called weary, because it makes travellers weary; as death is called pale in other authors, because it makes mens faces pale. The covert is still those princes for that people, after the king of v. 1.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have that shelter. Wesley’s “man” is each prince, already enough. The church is not to hunt another rock to make “hiding place” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: and that man shall be. How great is the importance of well-regulated government. If this was said with truth concerning Hezekiah, much more may it be said concerning Christ, in whom we have our best, or rather, our only refuge in those storms by which we must be tossed about as long as we dwell in this world. Whenever, therefore, we are scorched by oppressive heat, let us learn to retire under his shadow; whenever we are tossed about by tempests, let us learn to betake ourselves to him as our safest harbour; he will speedily bring every storm to a calm.

Reformed accent: the man is the king, and much more Christ. The hiding-place stays with Hezekiah’s reign and Christ’s, not a newspaper of later storms.

Key contrast Both keep the hiding-place in Hezekiah’s day and then in Christ, not a newspaper. Wesley reads “a man” as each of his princes, a covert for the people under their government from the rage of evil men. Calvin reads “that man” as the king himself—true of Hezekiah, much more of Christ, our only refuge. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 32:3

3Then the eyes of those who see will not be closed, and the ears of those who hear will give attention.

Arminian

Wesley: the eyes—the people, they shall not shut their eyes and ears against the good counsels and examples of their religious king and rulers, as they have done formerly: both princes and people shall be reformed. The seeing is still that court’s, after the covert of v. 2.

Arminian reading: they might yet have hearkened. Historic reformation of princes and people under that religious king, not a later chart. The church is not to stretch “eyes” into a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: while the Prophet describes the reign of Hezekiah, he intends to lead us farther; here he discourses concerning the restoration of the Church, which indeed was shadowed out by Hezekiah, but has been actually fulfilled in Christ. This promise is contrasted with the dreadful threatening of ch. 29, that he would blind the Jews. He does not merely declare what men will do, but what God himself will do in men. These are extraordinary gifts of God. Out of Christ there is no spiritual life in the world.

Reformed note: the opened eyes are God’s work in the restored Church. The seeing stays with that restoration, not a newspaper pasted onto “give attention.”

Key contrast Both keep opened eyes in Hezekiah’s reform and then in Christ, not a newspaper. Wesley reads the people no longer shutting eyes and ears against their religious king’s counsels: both princes and people shall be reformed. Calvin reads God’s own gifts reversing the blindness of ch. 29: not what men will do, but what God will do in men; out of Christ there is no spiritual life. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 32:4

4The heart of the hasty will understand and know, and the tongue of the stammerers will hasten to speak distinctly.

Arminian

Wesley: the rash—who were hasty in judging of things; which is an argument of ignorance and folly. The tongue—that used to speak of the things of God, darkly, and doubtfully; which though it was in part fulfilled in Hezekiah, yet was truly and fully accomplished only by Christ, who wrought this wonderful change in an innumerable company both of Jews and Gentiles. The stammerers are still that history first, then the gospel fullness.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have spoken plainly. Historic Hezekiah in part, Christ in full among Jews and Gentiles—already Wesley’s own gospel step. The church is not to hunt another tongue to make “distinctly” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: as fools are commonly hasty and rash, so the Hebrew writers take haste as denoting folly. He promises that he will restore to these persons eyes, ears, a tongue, and understanding. Nothing is here promised which does not proceed from the grace of God; he does not merely declare what men will do, but what God himself will do in men. A tongue to speak, a mind to understand, and ears to hear, are restored to us through the kindness of Christ; formerly we were dull of apprehension, and were struck with frightful stupidity.

Reformed accent: the distinct speech is grace in men, not their native skill. The stammerers stay with that restoration under Christ, not a newspaper of later speech.

Key contrast Both keep the stammerers first under Hezekiah and then under Christ, not a newspaper. Wesley reads a change in part fulfilled in Hezekiah, truly and fully accomplished only by Christ among an innumerable company of Jews and Gentiles. Calvin reads the same gifts as what God himself will do in men by grace, through the kindness of Christ, not what men will do. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 32:5

5The fool will no more be called noble, nor the scoundrel said to be honorable.

Arminian

Wesley: the vile—base and worthless men. Liberal—shall no longer be reputed honourable, because of their high and honourable places, but wickedness shall be discovered where ever it is, and virtue manifested and rewarded. The churl—the sordid and covetous man; but under this one vice, all vices are understood, as under the opposite virtue of bountifulness all virtues are comprehended. The unmasking is still that kingdom’s, after the opened eyes.

Arminian reading: they might yet have named things truly. High place no longer buys the name “noble.” The church is not to stretch “fool” into a newspaper of later titles.

Reformed

Calvin: everything will be restored to good order, so that vices will not, as formerly, be reckoned virtues. When the public government is wicked, covetous persons are in power, honoured because men judge of virtue by wealth and power. We ought to raise our minds somewhat higher, so as to view all this as relating to Christ, to whom it specially belongs to expose hidden vices by means of the gospel, which drags into light the disgraceful actions which were formerly concealed. This is the reason why the gospel is so much hated: no man can patiently endure to have his hidden thoughts revealed.

Reformed pastoral: Christ the judge exposes the mask. The fool and the noble stay with that gospel light, not a newspaper of later names.

Isaiah 32:6

6For the fool speaks folly, and his heart is busy with iniquity, to practice ungodliness, to utter error concerning the Lord, to leave the craving of the hungry unsatisfied, and to deprive the thirsty of drink.

Arminian

Wesley: villainy—men shall no longer be miscalled; for every one will discover what he is by his words and actions. Will work—he will, from time to time, be advising wickedness, that he may execute it when he hath opportunity. To practise—to do bad things, tho' with a pretence of religion and justice. To utter—to pass unjust sentence, directly contrary to the command of God. Cause the drink—whereby they take away the bread and the drink of the poor. The folly is still that court’s hypocrisy against the hungry.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have fed the hungry. Pretence of religion is already named. The church is not to hunt another thief to make “error concerning the Lord” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: we might also render it, the wicked man will speak wickedly. He does not inquire what wicked men are, but declares that they shall be revealed under the reign of Christ. Others explain it as a proverbial saying in the present tense; but the Prophet means something higher: Christ is the Judge of the world; when he shall ascend the judgment-seat, he will shew what is the disposition of every person. The gospel is the sieve for separating the wheat from the chaff. Then cruelty: to snatch food from the hungry soul and drink from the thirsty; men so brutalized they are worse than the beasts themselves.

Reformed accent: not a proverb of what fools always do, but exposure under Christ’s reign. The hungry stay in that judgment, not a newspaper pasted onto “folly.”

Key contrast Both keep the fool’s speech unmasked in the righteous kingdom, not a newspaper. Wesley reads men no longer miscalled: each discovers what he is by words and actions; hypocrisy with a pretence of religion; unjust sentence that takes the poor’s drink. Calvin refuses the proverbial present: they shall be revealed under Christ’s reign; he is the Judge; the gospel is the sieve. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 32:7

7As for the scoundrel—his devices are evil; he plans wicked schemes to ruin the poor with lying words, even when the plea of the needy is right.

Arminian

Wesley: lying words—with false and unrighteous decrees. Even—when their cause is just and good. The scoundrel’s devices are still those decrees against the needy whose plea is right, after the fool of v. 6.

Arminian close of the schemes: they might yet have heard the needy. False decrees in that court are already enough. The church is not to rename “lying words” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: the instruments of the covetous man are evil. We must always keep by the future tense; he does not inquire what wicked men are, but declares that they shall be revealed under the reign of Christ. Instruments include every kind of means, tricks, and cunning devices. Shameful circumstances: to deceive the simple, who cannot take care of themselves; blandishments under pretense of friendship; to deceive the poor, whose poverty we ought rather to have relieved; to lay snares in the very court of justice, which ought to be a refuge and becomes a den of robbers.

Reformed note: Christ brings those tricks to light. The poor stay in that court, not a newspaper of later schemes.

Isaiah 32:8

8But he who is noble plans noble things, and on noble things he stands.

Arminian

Wesley does not gloss this verse. His notes run from the king of v. 1 through the churl’s lying decrees in v. 7, then jump to the women at ease in v. 9. The noble who plans noble things is still the liberal opposite of that churl, in Hezekiah’s court and in the Christ he typed.

Arminian close of the range: they might yet have stood on noble things. Wesley and Calvin both keep this king as Hezekiah and as Christ; this is not an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. The church is not to stretch “noble” into a later newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: but the liberal shall devise liberal things. These statements have a deeper meaning than is commonly supposed; he does not speak in the ordinary sense of the words, but treats of the reformation of the Church. This relates therefore to the regenerate, over whom Christ reigns; although all are called by the voice of the gospel, yet there are few who suffer themselves to be placed under his yoke. The Lord makes them truly kind and bountiful. In acting liberally he shall make progress: the liberal will never cease to perform acts of generosity; kum signifies to arise and grow. True liberality is not momentary or of short duration; they who possess that virtue persevere steadily.

Reformed close: all called, few under the yoke—Calvin’s own gospel note on the noble, not a millennial chart. Calvin and Wesley agree the king of vv. 1–8 is Hezekiah and Christ; this standing is already enough without a later newspaper. No millennial dispute is in this comment.

Reformed Notable voices

“Behold, a King shall reign. This prediction undoubtedly relates to Hezekiah and his reign, under which the Church was reformed and restored to its former splendor; Ahaz, a wicked and disgraceful hypocrite, had overturned civil government and religion. He therefore promises another king, namely, Hezekiah. As this cannot be attained without Christ, this description undoubtedly refers to Christ, of whom Hezekiah was a type, and whose kingdom he foreshadowed. But the liberal shall devise liberal things: this relates to the regenerate, over whom Christ reigns; although all are called by the voice of the gospel, yet there are few who suffer themselves to be placed under his yoke.”
