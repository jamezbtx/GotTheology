Passage

Isaiah 19

Verses 1–15 · ESV · Voices

An oracle concerning Egypt. Behold, the LORD is riding on a swift cloud and comes to Egypt; the idols tremble, hearts melt. Egyptians against Egyptians, city against city; the spirit emptied, counsel confounded; they inquire of idols and necromancers. A hard master, a fierce king. Waters of the sea dried, the Nile parched, canals foul, reeds rot, sown ground gone; fishermen mourn, flax-workers despair, pillars crushed. Princes of Zoan foolish, princes of Memphis deluded; the LORD mingles a spirit of confusion; nothing for Egypt that head or tail, palm branch or reed, may do. The unit stays with historic Egypt in the Assyria age—Nile, Zoan, Memphis, Judah’s false refuge—as Wesley and Calvin actually read—not a newspaper of later states, not a millennial camp fight.

Isaiah 19:1

1An oracle concerning Egypt. Behold, the LORD is riding on a swift cloud and comes to Egypt; and the idols of Egypt will tremble at his presence, and the heart of the Egyptians will melt within them.

Arminian

Wesley: Rideth—as a general in the head of his army. A swift cloud—this phrase shews that the judgment should come speedily, unexpectedly, and unavoidably. Shall be moved—so far shall they be from helping the Egyptians, that they shall tremble for themselves. Shared: the LORD comes; idols tremble.

Arminian pastoral: the cloud is historic Egypt’s, not a twenty-first-century sky. Do not turn the riding into a newspaper. Judah’s refuge cannot stand when the LORD arrives.

Reformed

Calvin: the burden of Egypt. Egypt was a kind of refuge to the Jews whenever they saw danger; when they had forsaken God they thought they had no help left but in the Egyptians. It was necessary that that kingdom should be overthrown, that its forces might no longer deceive the Jews. Behold, the Lord rideth on a swift cloud: they thought they were so well fortified that God could not approach; he ridicules that confidence. The idols of Egypt shall be moved at his presence.

Reformed pastoral: the melt is still Egypt’s heart. Shared: swift cloud, trembling idols. Historic Nile land, not a modern state.

Isaiah 19:2

2And I will stir up Egyptians against Egyptians, and they will fight, each against another and each against his neighbor, city against city, kingdom against kingdom;

Arminian

Wesley: I will set—Egypt was now one kingdom, but not many years after this time it was divided into twelve kingdoms, between whom there were many and cruel wars. Shared: Egyptian against Egyptian.

Arminian pastoral: the civil split is that age’s Egypt, not a later civil war on a map. Do not preach city against city as a headline.

Reformed

Calvin: I will set the Egyptians against the Egyptians. He describes internal struggles, in which those who ought to be mutual defenders cut down one another. I will set: God, in whose hands are the hearts of men, inflames them to mutual animosities. They shall fight every one against his brother; city against city, and kingdom against kingdom. Nations never rise in a seditious manner unless the Lord set them against each other.

Reformed note: the stirring is the Lord’s, not chance. Shared: neighbor against neighbor. Historic Egypt, not a newspaper.

Key contrast Both hear Egyptians set against Egyptians in that age, not a newspaper. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s twelve kingdoms not many years after, with many and cruel wars. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: the Lord himself sets them as gladiators; brother against brother is his vengeance, and hearts are in his hand.

Isaiah 19:3

3and the spirit of the Egyptians within them will be emptied out, and I will confound their counsel; and they will inquire of the idols and the sorcerers, and the mediums and the necromancers;

Arminian

Wesley: The spirit—their courage. Shared: emptied spirit, then idols and necromancers.

Arminian pastoral: counsel fails, and they run to the dead. Historic Egypt’s magicians, not a culture-war séance. Do not copy the inquiry that cannot restore courage.

Reformed

Calvin: and the spirit of Egypt shall be emptied. As Isaiah had deprived them of courage, he now adds that their counsel shall be destroyed. They shall seek to the idols, and to the charmers, and to them that have familiar spirits. When God takes away understanding, men fly to unlawful arts; he condemns those arts as contrary to reason, for God has revealed what is necessary by means he approves.

Reformed pastoral: emptied spirit is still Egypt’s. Shared: counsel confounded; necromancers sought. Historic sorcerers, not a millennial decoder.

Isaiah 19:4

4and I will give over the Egyptians into the hand of a hard master, and a fierce king will rule over them, declares the LORD God of hosts.

Arminian

Wesley: A fierce king—Psammetichus, who being at first one of those twelve kings, waged war with the rest, and subdued them, and conquered all the land of Egypt and ruled it with rigor. Shared: a hard master over Egypt.

Arminian pastoral: the fierce king is historic Egypt’s, not a living politician. Do not hunt another hard master to make this verse a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: I will deliver the Egyptians into the hand of a cruel master. After losing courage and understanding, nothing will be left but slavery. He threatens what is still more shocking: the inhabitants will undergo hard bondage. Though cruel is singular, he says they shall be subject to lords. A powerful king shall rule over them: the tyrant’s power shall be so great that it will not be easy to restore them to liberty.

Reformed note: the hard master is still that judgment’s. Shared: a fierce king, declares the LORD of hosts. Historic Egypt, not a later throne.

Key contrast Both hear a hard master over historic Egypt, not a newspaper and not a living politician. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s Psammetichus, one of the twelve who subdued the rest and ruled with rigor. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: after emptied spirit, bondage under cruel lords, and a powerful king whose grip will not easily be broken—he will not name the man as Wesley does.

Isaiah 19:5

5And the waters of the sea will be dried up, and the river will be dry and parched,

Arminian

Wesley: The waters—which may be understood either metaphorically of dominion or commerce, or rather properly, as may be gathered from the following words. The river—Nile: upon whose fulness and overflow both the safety and the wealth of the land depended; therefore this was a very terrible judgment. Dried up—not totally, but in a very great measure. Shared: sea and Nile fail.

Arminian pastoral: the parched river is historic Nile, not a twenty-first-century drought story.

Reformed

Calvin: then the waters shall fail from the sea, and the river shall be wasted and dried up. He follows out Egypt’s wealth, which depended on the Nile. When God dries what they trusted, the kingdom’s pride withers with the stream.

Reformed pastoral: the dry river is still Egypt’s. Shared: waters fail. Historic Nile, not a newspaper.

Isaiah 19:6

6and its canals will become foul, and the branches of Egypt's Nile will diminish and dry up, reeds and rushes will rot away.

Arminian

Wesley: Rivers—the rivulets by which the waters of Nile were distributed into several parts of the land shall be turned far away, as they must needs be when the river which fed them was dried up. Brooks—the several branches of the Nile, which were a great defense to Egypt. Reeds—which were useful to them for making their boats. Shared: canals foul, reeds rot.

Arminian pastoral: defense-brooks fail with the flood. Historic irrigation, not a modern canal project.

Reformed

Calvin: and the brooks of defence shall be emptied and dried up. What he adds about fortifications shews that the drying is not a side note: the branches that guarded and fed the land fail together. Reed and rush wither with them.

Reformed note: foul canals are still the Nile’s. Shared: branches diminish. Historic Egypt, not a millennial map.

Isaiah 19:7

7There will be bare places by the Nile, on the brink of the Nile, and all that is sown by the Nile will be parched, will be driven away, and will be no more.

Arminian

Wesley: Paper-reeds—these by a needle were divided into thin and broad leaves, which being dried were used at that time for writing, and consequently was a very good commodity. By brooks—and much more what was sown in more dry and unfruitful places. Shared: sown ground gone from the brink.

Arminian pastoral: paper-reed and seed fail together. Historic Nile bank, not a commodity headline.

Reformed

Calvin: and the reed and the rush shall wither. He mentions reed and rush because they grew by the Nile and were part of the country’s wealth. All that is sown by the brooks shall wither, be driven away, and be no more. The blessing withdrawn, the brink is bare.

Reformed pastoral: parched sowing is still Egypt’s. Shared: no more by the Nile. Historic field, not a newspaper.

Isaiah 19:8

8The fishermen will mourn and lament, all who cast a hook in the Nile; and they will languish who spread nets on the water.

Arminian

Wesley: Mourn—because they could catch no fish; which was a great loss to the people, whose common diet this was. Shared: hook and net fail.

Arminian pastoral: the fishermen’s diet is historic Egypt’s, not a modern market. Do not restage the lament as entertainment.

Reformed

Calvin: and the fishers shall mourn. Isaiah still keeps in his eye the condition of Egypt. We know how abundant the fish of the Nile were, and how large a part of the people’s food. When the river fails, they who cast hook and spread nets languish.

Reformed note: the mourned catch is still the Nile’s. Shared: fishermen lament. Historic waters, not a later drought story.

Isaiah 19:9

9The workers in combed flax will be in despair, and the weavers of white cotton.

Arminian

Wesley: They—that make fine linen, which was one of their best commodities. Shared: flax and white cloth fail.

Arminian pastoral: Egypt’s linen trade is a real commodity of that land. Do not turn combed flax into a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: and they who work in the finest flax. As he spoke of mourning, so he now speaks of shame. Egypt was famous for fine linen; when the Nile’s produce fails, the weavers are confounded with the fishers.

Reformed pastoral: despair at the loom is still that trade’s. Shared: flax-workers in despair. Historic Egypt, not a millennial decoder.

Isaiah 19:10

10Those who are the pillars of the land will be crushed, and all who work for pay will be grieved.

Arminian

Wesley: Thereof—of Egypt, or of the Egyptians. They shall lose their hopes; for the fishes in them shall die for want of water. Shared: pillars crushed, hired workers grieved.

Arminian pastoral: wage and pillar fall with the river. Historic Egypt’s economy, not a twenty-first-century labor story.

Reformed

Calvin: and all that make ponds. As to the word, there is no absolute necessity of straining it; he means those whose work and wages depended on the waters. The pillars of the land—those who upheld its wealth—are broken when the Lord dries the Nile.

Reformed note: crushed pillars are still Egypt’s. Shared: hired workers grieved. Historic land, not a newspaper.

Isaiah 19:11

11The princes of Zoan are utterly foolish; the wisest counselors of Pharaoh give stupid counsel. How can you say to Pharaoh, “I am a son of the wise, a son of ancient kings”?

Arminian

Wesley: Zoan—the chief city, in which the king and court frequently resided. How—why do you put such foolish words into Pharaoh’s mouth? I am the son—wisdom is heredity and natural to me. Shared: Zoan’s princes are fools.

Arminian pastoral: pedigree does not make counsel. Historic Zoan, not a modern cabinet. Do not baptize ancient kings as a headline.

Reformed

Calvin: surely the princes of Zoan are fools. He joins wisdom with folly, and not without reason: they boasted of their wisdom, and Egypt was celebrated for it. How say ye to Pharaoh, I am the son of the wise, the son of ancient kings? Their pedigree cannot keep the counsel God has destroyed.

Reformed pastoral: stupid counsel is still Pharaoh’s court. Shared: princes of Zoan foolish. Historic Egypt, not a culture-war speech.

Isaiah 19:12

12Where then are your wise men? Let them tell you that they might know what the LORD of hosts has purposed against Egypt.

Arminian

Wesley’s how of v. 11 still holds: why put such foolish words into Pharaoh’s mouth? The wise cannot tell what the LORD of hosts has purposed. Shared: let them declare the purpose.

Arminian pastoral: the purpose is already spoken against historic Egypt. Do not invent open theism from hidden counsel; the LORD of hosts has purposed, and the wise cannot find it out.

Reformed

Calvin: where are thy wise men? that they may tell thee. Though they boast of wisdom, they cannot foresee what the Lord of hosts hath purposed against Egypt. The challenge exposes empty learning: God’s decree is not subject to their arts.

Reformed note: the missing wise men are still Zoan’s. Shared: what the LORD has purposed. Historic purpose against Egypt, not a newspaper decoder.

Isaiah 19:13

13The princes of Zoan have become fools, and the princes of Memphis are deluded; those who are the cornerstones of her tribes have made Egypt stagger.

Arminian

Wesley: Noph—another chief city, and one of the kings seats, called also Moph, and by latter authors, Memphis. The stay—their chief counsellors. Tribes—of the provinces, which he calls by a title borrowed from the Hebrew. Shared: Zoan and Memphis both fool the land.

Arminian pastoral: cornerstones that should stay Egypt make it stagger. Historic Noph, not a later capital.

Reformed

Calvin: the princes of Zoan are become infatuated, the princes of Noph are deceived. Zoan and Noph (Memphis) were chief cities. The corner-stone of the tribes: those who ought to have been the stay of the provinces have made Egypt stagger.

Reformed pastoral: deluded Memphis is still that court’s. Shared: cornerstones make Egypt stagger. Historic cities, not a millennial map.

Isaiah 19:14

14The LORD has mingled within her a spirit of confusion, and they will make Egypt stagger in all its deeds, as a drunken man staggers in his vomit.

Arminian

Wesley: Mingled—or, hath poured out or given them to drink. To err—in all their designs and undertakings. Staggereth—when he is so drunk, that he reels to and fro, and vomits up his drink. Shared: a spirit of confusion from the LORD.

Arminian pastoral: the mix is the Lord’s judgment, not a gap in his knowledge. Historic Egypt reeling, not a culture-war speech. Do not invent open theism from confusion.

Reformed

Calvin: the Lord hath mingled a spirit of perverseness. Because it was unexpected that so wise a nation should err, he shews that it was done by the judgment of God, who mixed a spirit that made them stagger in all their work, as a drunken man staggereth in his vomit. The Lord is not confused; he confuses them.

Reformed note: the stagger is still Egypt’s deeds. Shared: mingled spirit of confusion. Historic judgment, not process-theism.

Isaiah 19:15

15And there will be nothing for Egypt that head or tail, palm branch or reed, may do.

Arminian

Wesley: Head, &c.—all people, both high and low, shall be at their wits end. Shared: nothing left for high or low to do.

Arminian close of this half: palm branch and reed fail together. Historic Egypt from court to canal, not a twenty-first-century state. The church is not to stretch “nothing for Egypt” into a newspaper. The next unit (vv. 16–25) will still keep Egypt in that age, then name a gospel mercy Wesley and Calvin share in Christ.

Reformed

Calvin: neither shall Egypt have any work to do. This is the conclusion of the former statement: neither the highest nor the lowest, branch nor rush, shall be able to restore the state. Head and tail are alike helpless when the Lord has emptied spirit and Nile.

Reformed close: nothing to do is still that land’s. Shared: head or tail, palm or reed. Historic Egypt in the Assyria age—already enough without a later headline.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the LORD riding a swift cloud into Egypt, idols moved, Egyptian against Egyptian, a fierce king, the Nile dried, Zoan’s fools, and a spirit of confusion—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Isaiah 19:1–15.

Arminian Notable voices

Shall be moved—So far shall they be from helping the Egyptians, that they shall tremble for themselves. A fierce king—Psammetichus, who being at first one of those twelve kings, waged war with the rest, and subdued them, and conquered all the land of Egypt and ruled it with rigor. The waters—Properly, as may be gathered from the following words. The river—Nile: upon whose fulness and overflow both the safety and the wealth of the land depended. Dried up—Not totally, but in a very great measure. Rivers—Those rivulets by which the waters of Nile were distributed. Paper-reeds—Used at that time for writing, and consequently a very good commodity. Mourn—Because they could catch no fish; which was a great loss to the people, whose common diet this was. They—That make fine linen, which was one of their best commodities. Zoan—The chief city, in which the king and court frequently resided. How—Why do you put such foolish words into Pharaoh’s mouth? Noph—Another chief city, called also Memphis. The stay—Their chief counsellors. Mingled—Or, hath poured out or given them to drink. Head, &c.—All people, both high and low, shall be at their wits end.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Isa 19:1–15)

Reformed Notable voices

“The burden of Egypt. The Prophet here prophesies against Egypt, because it was a kind of refuge to the Jews whenever they saw any danger approaching; when they had forsaken God they thought they had no help left but in the Egyptians. It was therefore necessary that that kingdom should be overthrown, that its wealth or its forces might no longer deceive the Jews. Behold, the Lord rideth on a swift cloud: the Egyptians thought they were so well fortified that there was no way by which God could approach them; he ridicules their foolish confidence. I will set the Egyptians against the Egyptians: internal struggles, in which those who ought to be mutual defenders cut down one another; the Lord set them against each other. The spirit of Egypt shall be emptied, and I will destroy the counsel thereof: they shall seek to the idols and to the charmers. I will deliver the Egyptians into the hand of a cruel master, and a powerful king shall rule over them. Then the waters shall fail from the sea, and the river shall be wasted: he follows out the subject of Egypt’s wealth, which depended on the Nile. The fishers shall mourn; they who work in the finest flax shall be ashamed. Surely the princes of Zoan are fools; the princes of Noph are deceived. The Lord hath mingled a spirit of perverseness. Neither shall Egypt have any work to do, head or tail, branch or rush.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Isaiah (on Isa 19:1–15)
