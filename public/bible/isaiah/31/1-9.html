Passage

Isaiah 31

Verses 1–9 · ESV · Voices

Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help: eighth-century Judah, after the Egypt plan, is told not to rely on horses and chariots, nor on horsemen because they are very strong. The Lord like a lion over prey, like birds hovering over Jerusalem; turn, cast the idols; Assyria shall fall by a sword not of man, his rock in terror. The unit stays with historic Egypt and Assyria against Isaiah’s Judah—not a newspaper of later nations.

Isaiah 31:1

1Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help and rely on horses, who trust in chariots because they are many and in horsemen because they are very strong, but do not look to the Holy One of Israel or consult the Lord!

Arminian

Wesley: horses—for Egypt had many and choice horses. His heading for vv. 1–3 is still the folly and punishment of trusting in Egypt. They go down for help and stay on those horses, and look not unto the Holy One of Israel. The woe is still Judah’s embassy to historic Egypt, not a later alliance.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have looked. Egypt’s choice horses are already enough to name the folly. The church is not to hunt another cavalry to make “go down to Egypt” a newspaper.

Reformed

Calvin: woe to them that go down to Egypt. He still cries loudly against the Jews, whose ordinary custom it was, in seasons of danger, to resort, not to the Lord, but to the Egyptians. Two reasons: it is impossible to place confidence for our salvation in creatures and at the same time in God; and God had expressly forbidden them to enter into alliance with the Egyptians. They have not looked to the Holy One of Israel; everything that shall turn away our minds from God will be to us like Egypt.

Reformed accent: they defraud God of his right when they bestow on mortal men the glory due to him. The horses and chariots stay with eighth-century Egypt against Isaiah’s Judah, not a newspaper of later riders.

Isaiah 31:2

2And yet he is wise and brings disaster; he does not call back his words, but will arise against the house of the evildoers and against the helpers of those who work iniquity.

Arminian

Wesley: he is safe—you think you are safe, in engaging the Egyptians; but God is not inferior to them in wisdom or strength, and therefore you have done foolishly, in preferring them before him, who will execute his judgments upon you, notwithstanding all the Egyptians can do. The help—the helpers, as it is explained in the next verse. He does not call back his words: the disaster is still the Lord’s on that house, not a later scheme.

Arminian reading: they might yet have preferred him. Wisdom here is God matching and exceeding Egypt, so the embassy was folly. The church is not to stretch “brings disaster” into a newspaper of later helpers.

Reformed

Calvin: yet he also is wise. By calling God wise, he does not merely bestow on him an attribute, but censures the craftiness of those too much delighted with their own wisdom. He said a little before that they dug caves for themselves; he now pours witty ridicule on this madness. He taketh the wise in their own craftiness. He will arise against the house of the evil-doers, and against the aid of the workers of vanity.

Reformed note: they who shelter themselves under craftiness gain nothing but to provoke still more the wrath of God. The words he will not make void are still against Judah’s Egypt plot, not a later cipher.

Key contrast Both keep the wise Lord against Judah’s Egypt helpers, not a newspaper. Wesley reads “wise” as God not inferior to the Egyptians in wisdom or strength: they thought themselves safe in that engagement and preferred Egypt before him. Calvin reads “wise” as a wound to their own craftiness—the dug caves of ch. 29—God taking the wise in their craftiness, not a comparison of strength with Egypt’s horses. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 31:3

3The Egyptians are man, and not God, and their horses are flesh, and not spirit. When the Lord stretches out his hand, the helper will stumble, and he who is helped will fall, and they will all perish together.

Arminian

Wesley: flesh—weak and frail. The help of v. 2 is now named: he that helpeth shall fall, and he that is holpen shall fall down, and they all shall fail together. Egyptians are men, and not God; their horses flesh, and not spirit. The stumble is still that helper and that helped in Isaiah’s century.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have seen the flesh. Weak and frail is already Egypt’s horse, not a later breed. The church is not to hunt another army to make “not God” true.

Reformed

Calvin: surely the Egyptian is a man, and not God; his horses are flesh, and not spirit. The Jews ascribed to them the defense and preservation of the Church, which God claims for himself alone. As soon as Jehovah shall stretch out his arm, the helper shall fall, and he who is helped shall fall down, and all shall fail together. He threatens those who yield assistance to false confidence, as well as those who rely on it.

Reformed accent: whatever is attributed to man in such a contrast is taken from God. Helper and helped perish together in that history, not in a newspaper pasted onto “flesh.”

Isaiah 31:4

4For thus the Lord said to me, “As a lion or a young lion growls over his prey, and when a band of shepherds is called out against him he is not terrified by their shouting or daunted at their noise, so the Lord of hosts will come down to fight on Mount Zion and on its hill.

Arminian

Wesley: for—although you have done evil in sending to Egypt for help, yet the Lord himself will, of his own grace, give you that help which you do not deserve. His heading for vv. 4–5 is still God will fight for Jerusalem. The lion over prey is still the Lord of hosts coming down on historic Zion, not a later hill.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have that undeserved help. Grace here is the Lord’s own fight for Mount Zion after the evil embassy. The church is not to rename “lion” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: the Prophet adds this verse, that it may not be thought that the Lord leaves us destitute of necessary means. The Lord assists, and will assist; he forbids you to ask assistance from the Egyptians. By comparing himself to a lion, a very powerful animal and keenly bent on prey, he shews that he is in the highest degree both able and willing to defend us. He takes hold of his people, keeps them near himself, and is not terrified by the shepherds’ shouting.

Reformed pastoral: if we despise the salvation he offers of his own accord, what pretense can be left? The lion stays over Mount Zion in Isaiah’s hearing, not a newspaper of later shepherds.

Key contrast Both keep the lion as the Lord of hosts fighting for historic Zion, not a newspaper. Wesley reads the “for” as grace after guilt: although they did evil in sending to Egypt, the Lord himself will, of his own grace, give the help they do not deserve. Calvin reads the lion as proof God does not leave them destitute of means: he assists and will assist, so they must not ask Egypt; the comparison shows he is able and willing to defend. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 31:5

5Like birds hovering, so the Lord of hosts will protect Jerusalem; he will protect and deliver it; he will spare and rescue it.”

Arminian

Wesley: as birds—which come from above, and so cannot be kept off; which fly swiftly, and engage resolutely, when their young ones are in danger. Passing over—the destroying angel shall pass over Jerusalem. The hovering is still the Lord’s over that city, after the lion of v. 4.

Arminian reading: they might yet have been passed over. Wesley’s destroying angel is the Passover figure on historic Jerusalem, not a millennial chart and not a later sky. The church is not to hunt another brood to make “birds hovering” true.

Reformed

Calvin: as birds that fly. The second comparison shews how great care the Lord takes of us, and how earnestly he is bent on making us happy. It is taken from birds, which almost kill themselves with hunger, and shrink from no danger, that they may defend and preserve their young. He will never forsake those whom he has once received into his favor. Wherever the worship of God is pure, salvation will be certain.

Reformed accent: he mentions not only Mount Zion but its hill, for on that hill was built the temple. The hovering stays with Jerusalem in Isaiah’s century, not a newspaper pasted onto “protect.”

Key contrast Both keep the birds as the Lord of hosts over historic Jerusalem, not a newspaper. Wesley reads “passing over” as the destroying angel shall pass over Jerusalem, the birds coming from above so they cannot be kept off, flying swiftly when their young are in danger. Calvin does not name that angel; he reads the birds as a parent’s eagerness for the young, and adds that wherever the worship of God is pure, salvation will be certain. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 31:6

6Turn to him from whom people have deeply revolted, O children of Israel.

Arminian

Wesley’s heading for vv. 6–7 is still if they will turn unto him. He does not gloss this verse. Turn is still historic Israel’s, from the Egypt revolt of vv. 1–3, between the hovering of v. 5 and the idols of v. 7.

Arminian pastoral: they might yet have turned. The children of Israel named here are already enough. The church is not to stretch “deeply revolted” into a newspaper of later nations.

Reformed

Calvin: return according as you have made a deep revolt. He does not follow the Hebrew commentators who read, return to the Lord, for you have multiplied revolts. The aggravated nature of their wickedness does not shut the door against them from returning to God, if they repent. He uses this spur that they may not aim at a half repentance. O children of Israel: he does not intend to shew them respect, but reproaches them for their ingratitude; yet he means that the Lord had not forgotten the covenant with their fathers.

Reformed note: not in vain does the Lord invite us to repentance; he likewise offers pardon. The turn stays with that generation’s revolt, not a later headline.

Isaiah 31:7

7For in that day everyone shall cast away his idols of silver and his idols of gold, which your hands have sinfully made for you.

Arminian

Wesley’s heading still joins this verse to “if they will turn unto him.” He does not gloss the idols here; the casting away is the fruit of that turn, still Judah’s silver and gold after the Egypt woe. The hands that made them are Israel’s in Isaiah’s hearing.

Arminian close of the idols: they might yet have cast them. The day is still that people’s, not a later forge. The church is not to hunt another image to make “idols of gold” true.

Reformed

Calvin: he points out the fruits of repentance. Shall cast away the idols: the beginning of repentance is the change of the heart; next we come to outward fruits. He calls them the idols of his silver and of his gold, because they who sincerely repent do not spare any expense in order to possess the pure worship of God. Which your own hands have made: he bids them look to their own hands, that they may know they have committed so great a crime. God rejects everything that is of our contrivance.

Reformed pastoral: when we persevere in being wicked, we restrain his grace from assisting us. The silver and gold stay with historic Israel’s sin, not a newspaper of later idols.

Isaiah 31:8

8“And the Assyrian shall fall by a sword, not of man; and a sword, not of man, shall devour him; and he shall flee from the sword, and his young men shall be put to forced labor.

Arminian

Wesley: the sword—not of any man, but of an angel. Discomfited—Heb. shall melt away, a great part of them being destroyed by the angel; and the hearts of the rest melting for fear. His heading for vv. 8–9 is still the fall of Assyria. The sword not of man is still Asshur’s fall at Jerusalem, not a later blade.

Arminian reading: they might yet have seen that angel. Wesley’s melt is the host under Sennacherib, already enough. The church is not to rename “not of man” every generation.

Reformed

Calvin: then the Assyrian shall fall down; that is, when you shall have turned to the Lord, and when your life shall testify a sincere repentance, then the enemy shall fall. By the sword not of a man: the deliverance of the Church is God's own work; God's secret power is sufficient to deliver them. This prediction was fulfilled when the Assyrian army was destroyed, and Sennacherib was put to flight; not by the arm of man was he destroyed. His young men shall melt away: the hearts of young men, who in other circumstances are wont to be fierce, shall be altogether softened and melt like wax.

Reformed accent: let us not inquire how it shall be done, but be satisfied with the bare promise. Asshur falls in that history, not in a newspaper of later swords.

Key contrast Both keep Assyria falling by a sword not of man at historic Jerusalem, not a newspaper. Wesley names the sword as not of any man, but of an angel: a great part destroyed by the angel, the rest melting for fear. Calvin makes the fall wait on their turning: when you shall have turned, then the enemy shall fall; the deliverance is God's own work and secret power, fulfilled when Sennacherib’s army was destroyed, not by the arm of man. Neither comment is an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight.

Isaiah 31:9

9His rock shall pass away in terror, and his officers desert the standard in panic,” declares the Lord, whose fire is in Zion, and whose furnace is in Jerusalem.

Arminian

Wesley: he—Sennacherib shall flee away, from Jerusalem, to his strong city of Nineveh. The ensign—of the Lord's ensign, which he hath lifted up against them. Whose fire—who is, and will appear to be in Zion, like a fire to defend his people, and to consume their enemies. The rock in terror is still that king’s, after the angel’s sword.

Arminian close of the range: they might yet have feared that fire. Wesley’s Nineveh flight and Zion’s fire are already enough; this is not a millennial chart and not a newspaper of later standards. Wesley and Calvin both keep this as the Lord’s against historic Asshur; this is not an Arminian–Reformed millennial fight. The church is not to stretch “furnace” into a later sky.

Reformed

Calvin: he shall pass to his stronghold for fear. He now speaks of Sennacherib himself, who, trembling, shall betake himself in base and shameful flight to his fortress, Nineveh. His princes shall tremble at the banner. Who hath a fire in Zion: God is justly called a fire in reference to the Assyrians, whom he will consume; some take it of sacrifices, but that interpretation is feeble. This fire is kindled in Zion and Jerusalem, that the persecution of the Church by wicked men shall not pass unpunished.

Reformed close: let us be fully convinced that the Lord will be a fire to our adversaries. Calvin and Wesley agree the flight is Sennacherib’s to Nineveh and the fire is the Lord’s in Zion; this furnace is already enough without a later newspaper. No millennial dispute is in this comment.
