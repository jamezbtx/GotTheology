Passage

Isaiah 31

Verses 1–9 · WEB · Voices

Woe to those who trust Egyptian horses and chariots instead of the Holy One of Israel. Egyptians are only human; their horses are flesh, not spirit. The LORD will defend Jerusalem like a lion over its prey and like birds hovering over their nest. Israel is called to return and throw away their idols, because Assyria will fall by a sword not wielded by man.

Isaiah 31:1

1Woe to those who go down to Egypt for help, and rely on horses, and trust in chariots because they are many, and in horsemen because they are very strong, but they don’t look to the Holy One of Israel, and they don’t seek the LORD!

Arminian

Wesley: horses—for Egypt had many and choice horses. Wesley heads vv. 1–3: the folly and punishment of trusting in Egypt. They go down for help and stay on those horses, and look not unto the Holy One of Israel.

The woe falls on those who count horses and chariots but never look to the Holy One. Trust in numbers blinds them to the one who actually rules.

Reformed

Calvin: woe to them that go down to Egypt. He still cries loudly against the Jews, whose ordinary custom it was, in seasons of danger, to resort, not to the Lord, but to the Egyptians. Two reasons: it is impossible to place confidence for our salvation in creatures and at the same time in God; and God had expressly forbidden them to enter into alliance with the Egyptians. They have not looked to the Holy One of Israel; everything that shall turn away our minds from God will be to us like Egypt.

They defraud God of his right when they bestow on mortal men the glory due to him. The horses and chariots stay with eighth-century Egypt against Isaiah’s Judah.

Isaiah 31:2

2Yet he also is wise, and will bring disaster, and will not call back his words, but will arise against the house of the evildoers, and against the help of those who work iniquity.

Arminian

Wesley: he is safe—you think you are safe, in engaging the Egyptians; but God is not inferior to them in wisdom or strength, and therefore you have done foolishly, in preferring them before him, who will execute his judgments upon you, notwithstanding all the Egyptians can do. The help—the helpers, as it is explained in the next verse.

God is wise too, and his word does not fail. Wesley points out the folly of treating Egypt as wiser or stronger than the LORD.

Reformed

Calvin: yet he also is wise. By calling God wise, he does not merely bestow on him an attribute, but censures the craftiness of those too much delighted with their own wisdom. He said a little before that they dug caves for themselves; he now pours witty ridicule on this madness. He taketh the wise in their own craftiness. He will arise against the house of the evil-doers, and against the aid of the workers of vanity.

They who shelter themselves under craftiness gain nothing but to provoke still more the wrath of God.

Key contrast Both keep the wise Lord against Judah’s Egypt helpers. Wesley reads “wise” as God not inferior to the Egyptians in wisdom or strength: they thought themselves safe in that engagement and preferred Egypt before him. Calvin reads “wise” as a wound to their own craftiness—the dug caves of ch. 29—God taking the wise in their craftiness, not a comparison of strength with Egypt’s horses.

Isaiah 31:3

3Now the Egyptians are men, and not God; and their horses flesh, and not spirit. When the LORD stretches out his hand, both he who helps shall stumble, and he who is helped shall fall, and they all shall be consumed together.

Arminian

Wesley: flesh—weak and frail. The help of v. 2 is now named: he that helpeth shall fall, and he that is holpen shall fall down, and they all shall fail together. Egyptians are men, and not God; their horses flesh, and not spirit.

“Egyptians are men, and not God; their horses flesh, and not spirit.” When the LORD stretches out his hand, helper and helped fall together. Human strength is a poor substitute for God’s.

Reformed

Calvin: surely the Egyptian is a man, and not God; his horses flesh, and not spirit. The Jews ascribed to them the defense and preservation of the Church, which God claims for himself alone. As soon as Jehovah shall stretch out his arm, the helper shall fall, and he who is helped shall fall down, and all shall fail together. He threatens those who yield assistance to false confidence, as well as those who rely on it.

Whatever is attributed to man in such a contrast is taken from God. Helper and helped perish together in that history.”

Isaiah 31:4

4For the LORD says to me, “As the lion and the young lion growling over his prey, if a multitude of shepherds is called together against him, will not be dismayed at their voice, nor abase himself for their noise, so the LORD of Armies will come down to fight on Mount Zion and on its heights.

Arminian

Wesley: for—although you have done evil in sending to Egypt for help, yet the Lord himself will, of his own grace, give you that help which you do not deserve.

The lion does not flinch at the shouting shepherds; the Lord will not be driven off Zion. Wesley stresses that this help comes after the Egyptian embassy—grace given to people who had looked elsewhere.

Reformed

Calvin: the Prophet adds this verse, that it may not be thought that the Lord leaves us destitute of necessary means. The Lord assists, and will assist; he forbids you to ask assistance from the Egyptians. By comparing himself to a lion, a very powerful animal and keenly bent on prey, he shews that he is in the highest degree both able and willing to defend us. He takes hold of his people, keeps them near himself, and is not terrified by the shepherds’ shouting.

If we despise the salvation he offers of his own accord, what pretense can be left? The lion stays over Mount Zion in Isaiah’s hearing.

Key contrast Both keep the lion as the Lord of hosts fighting for Zion. Wesley reads the “for” as grace after guilt: although they did evil in sending to Egypt, the Lord himself will, of his own grace, give the help they do not deserve. Calvin reads the lion as proof God does not leave them destitute of means: he assists and will assist, so they must not ask Egypt; the comparison shows he is able and willing to defend.

Isaiah 31:5

5As birds hovering, so the LORD of Armies will protect Jerusalem. He will protect and deliver it. He will pass over and preserve it.”

Arminian

Wesley: as birds—which come from above, and so cannot be kept off; which fly swiftly, and engage resolutely, when their young ones are in danger. Passing over—the destroying angel shall pass over Jerusalem.

God will pass over Jerusalem as he passed over Israel in Egypt. Wesley pictures birds protecting their young, swift and impossible to drive off.

Reformed

Calvin: as birds that fly. The second comparison shews how great care the Lord takes of us, and how earnestly he is bent on making us happy. It is taken from birds, which almost kill themselves with hunger, and shrink from no danger, that they may defend and preserve their young. He will never forsake those whom he has once received into his favor. Wherever the worship of God is pure, salvation will be certain.

He mentions not only Mount Zion but its hill, for on that hill was built the temple. The hovering stays with Jerusalem in Isaiah’s century.”

Key contrast Both keep the birds as the Lord of hosts over Jerusalem. Wesley reads “passing over” as the destroying angel shall pass over Jerusalem, the birds coming from above so they cannot be kept off, flying swiftly when their young are in danger. Calvin does not name that angel; he reads the birds as a parent’s eagerness for the young, and adds that wherever the worship of God is pure, salvation will be certain.

Isaiah 31:6

6Return to him from whom you have deeply revolted, children of Israel.

Arminian

Wesley heads vv. 6–7: if they will turn unto him. He does not gloss this verse.

The call to return comes right after the promise of protection (v. 5). The deep revolt is named honestly, and so is the way back.

Reformed

Calvin: return according as you have made a deep revolt. He does not follow the Hebrew commentators who read, return to the Lord, for you have multiplied revolts. The aggravated nature of their wickedness does not shut the door against them from returning to God, if they repent. He uses this spur that they may not aim at a half repentance. O children of Israel: he does not intend to shew them respect, but reproaches them for their ingratitude; yet he means that the Lord had not forgotten the covenant with their fathers.

Not in vain does the Lord invite us to repentance; he likewise offers pardon. The turn stays with that generation’s revolt.

Isaiah 31:7

7For in that day everyone shall cast away his idols of silver and his idols of gold—sin which your own hands have made for you.

Arminian

Wesley ties this verse to the call of v. 6 and the promise that follows “if they will turn unto him.”

Casting away the idols grows from the turning called for in v. 6. The silver and gold were made by their own hands, so the renunciation is theirs to make.

Reformed

Calvin: he points out the fruits of repentance. Shall cast away the idols: the beginning of repentance is the change of the heart; next we come to outward fruits. He calls them the idols of his silver and of his gold, because they who sincerely repent do not spare any expense in order to possess the pure worship of God. Which your own hands have made: he bids them look to their own hands, that they may know they have committed so great a crime. God rejects everything that is of our contrivance.

When we persevere in being wicked, we restrain his grace from assisting us. The silver and gold stay with Israel’s sin.

Isaiah 31:8

8“The Assyrian will fall by the sword, not of man; and the sword, not of mankind, shall devour him. He will flee from the sword, and his young men will become subject to forced labor.

Arminian

Wesley: the sword—not of any man, but of an angel. Discomfited—Heb. shall melt away, a great part of them being destroyed by the angel; and the hearts of the rest melting for fear.

The prophecy is fulfilled in 37:36, when the Lord’s angel strikes the Assyrian camp. Jerusalem’s deliverance comes by no human sword, which is exactly why Egypt’s help was never needed.

Reformed

Calvin: then the Assyrian shall fall down; that is, when you shall have turned to the Lord, and when your life shall testify a sincere repentance, then the enemy shall fall. By the sword not of a man: the deliverance of the Church is God's own work; God's secret power is sufficient to deliver them. This prediction was fulfilled when the Assyrian army was destroyed, and Sennacherib was put to flight; not by the arm of man was he destroyed. His young men shall melt away: the hearts of young men, who in other circumstances are wont to be fierce, shall be altogether softened and melt like wax.

Let us not inquire how it shall be done, but be satisfied with the bare promise. Asshur falls in that history.

Key contrast Both keep Assyria falling by the sword, not of man at Jerusalem. Wesley names the sword as not of any man, but of an angel: a great part destroyed by the angel, the rest melting for fear. Calvin makes the fall wait on their turning: when you shall have turned, then the enemy shall fall; the deliverance is God's own work and secret power, fulfilled when Sennacherib’s army was destroyed, not by the arm of man.

Isaiah 31:9

9His rock will pass away by reason of terror, and his princes will be afraid of the banner,” says the LORD, whose fire is in Zion, and his furnace in Jerusalem.

Arminian

Wesley: he—Sennacherib shall flee away, from Jerusalem, to his strong city of Nineveh. The ensign—of the Lord's ensign, which he hath lifted up against them. Whose fire—who is, and will appear to be in Zion, like a fire to defend his people, and to consume their enemies.

Sennacherib’s courage will fail and his officers will flee at the LORD’s banner. The LORD’s fire in Zion defends his people and consumes their enemies.

Reformed

Calvin: he shall pass to his stronghold for fear. He now speaks of Sennacherib himself, who, trembling, shall betake himself in base and shameful flight to his fortress, Nineveh. His princes shall tremble at the banner. Who hath a fire in Zion: God is justly called a fire in reference to the Assyrians, whom he will consume; some take it of sacrifices, but that interpretation is feeble. This fire is kindled in Zion and Jerusalem, that the persecution of the Church by wicked men shall not pass unpunished.

Let us be fully convinced that the Lord will be a fire to our adversaries.
