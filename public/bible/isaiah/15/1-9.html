Isaiah 15

Verses 1–9 · ESV · Voices

An oracle concerning Moab: Ar of Moab is laid waste in a night, and Moab is undone; Kir of Moab is laid waste in a night, and Moab is undone. He has gone up to the temple and to Dibon, to the high places to weep; over Nebo and over Medeba Moab wails. On every head is baldness; every beard is shorn. In the streets they wear sackcloth; on the housetops and in the squares everyone wails and melts in tears. Heshbon and Elealeh cry out as far as Jahaz; the armed men of Moab cry aloud; his soul trembles. A heart cries out for Moab; fugitives flee to Zoar, to Eglath-shelishiyah, weeping on the ascent of Luhith and raising a cry of destruction on the road to Horonaim. The waters of Nimrim are a desolation; they carry what they have laid up over the Brook of the Willows. A cry goes around the land of Moab; the waters of Dibon are full of blood, and a lion waits for those who escape, for the remnant of the land.

Isaiah 15:1

1An oracle concerning Moab. Because Ar of Moab is laid waste in a night, Moab is undone; because Kir of Moab is laid waste in a night, Moab is undone.

Arminian

Wesley: The burden—a prophecy of the destruction of the Moabites, the inveterate enemies of the Jews, begun by the Assyrian, and finished by the Babylonian emperors. In a night—suddenly and unexpectedly. Ar—the chief city of Moab. Kir—another eminent city of Moab. Shared: Moab falls in a night.

Arminian pastoral: the warning is a real call. Historic Moab east of the Dead Sea, Lot’s line, not a twenty-first-century map. Do not turn Ar and Kir into a newspaper. Pride’s cities are undone while they sleep.

Reformed

Calvin: here the Prophet prophesies against the Moabites, neighbors related by blood, descended from Lot; no relationship prevented them from cherishing hostility toward the Jews. On account of their cruelty toward the people of God he threatens destruction. He looked to believers, that they might not think this world is governed by the blind violence of fortune, but might acknowledge the providence of God. In the night he means a sudden and unexpected occurrence.

Reformed pastoral: Moab falls by appointment, not chance. Shared: Ar and Kir laid waste in a night. Historic Moab, not a modern state.

Key contrast Both hear historic Moab undone in a night—Ar and Kir, not a newspaper. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s sequence: begun by the Assyrian, finished by the Babylonian emperors, sudden and unexpected. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: the oracle is for believers, that they may see providence rather than fortune; Moab’s kin-cruelty is judged, and night means the blow they did not dread.

Isaiah 15:2

2He has gone up to the temple, and to Dibon, to the high places to weep; over Nebo and over Medeba Moab wails. On every head is baldness; every beard is shorn.

Arminian

Wesley: Bajith—which signifies an house. It is supposed to be some eminent house or temple of their idols. Dibon—another city of Moab. To weep—to offer their supplications with tears to their idols for help. Medeba—two considerable cities, anciently belonging to the Moabites. Beard—the hair of their heads and beards was shaved, as was usual in great mournings. Shared: pride’s high places become places of weeping.

Arminian pastoral: they go up to weep where they once boasted. Historic Dibon, Nebo, and Medeba, not a modern shrine. Do not copy the tears offered to idols. The warning still calls the proud off the high place.

Reformed

Calvin: he shall go up into the house. By representing the Moabites as bowing down before their idols, he condemns their superstition in worshipping Chemosh. They shall betake themselves to their god when matters are desperate, but to no purpose. They increased their misery by those very means which they considered fitted for appeasing his wrath. The ungodly have no refuge in adversity. Baldness on every head is the eastern token of mourning; the whole kingdom is so mournful.

Reformed pastoral: Chemosh cannot help. Shared: high places become places of weeping. Historic Moab’s temples, not a culture-war speech.

Key contrast Both hear Moab climbing to weep at the high places, bald and shorn—not a newspaper. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s Bajith as an eminent house or temple of their idols, tears offered there for help. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: they run to Chemosh, and those remedies inflame wrath; the ungodly have no refuge in adversity.

Isaiah 15:3

3in the streets they wear sackcloth; on the housetops and in the squares everyone wails and melts in tears.

Arminian

Wesley: On the tops—which were made flat, to which men used to go up, to cry to God in heaven, or to men for help. In the streets they wear sackcloth; on the housetops and in the squares everyone wails. Shared: the wailing is public, from street to roof.

Arminian pastoral: sackcloth is honest grief, not a spectacle. Historic Moab’s housetops, not a twenty-first-century square. Do not melt in their tears as entertainment, and do not aim the oracle at a modern people.

Reformed

Calvin: he proceeds with the tokens of mourning. We are never moved by predictions unless the Lord place them, as it were, before our eyes. The Moabites were at that time in a state of profound peace; believers had the more need of being confirmed. He also points out the despair to which unbelievers are liable in adversity, for the support on which they rely is insecure.

Reformed pastoral: the howling is set before Judah’s eyes so the word will not be taken lightly. Shared: everyone wails and melts in tears. Historic streets of Moab, not a modern map.

Isaiah 15:4

4Heshbon and Elealeh cry out; their voice is heard as far as Jahaz; therefore the armed men of Moab cry aloud; his soul trembles.

Arminian

Wesley: Heshbon—two other Moabitish cities. Jahaz—another city in the utmost borders of Moab. Soldiers—who use to be the most courageous. Heshbon and Elealeh cry out; their voice is heard as far as Jahaz; therefore the armed men of Moab cry aloud. Shared: even the armed men cannot hold the line.

Arminian pastoral: courage fails where the cities wail. Historic Heshbon, Elealeh, and Jahaz, not a newspaper decoder. His soul trembles: a real fear under a real word, not a slogan.

Reformed

Calvin: he names other cities; his design is to bind up, as it were, in a bundle all the cities of that country, that they may be involved in the general destruction. None at all shall be exempted. The bold and courageous shall mourn. Every one shall be so engrossed with his own grief that he will not think of his neighbors.

Reformed pastoral: the cry reaches Jahaz because the Lord has appointed the ruin of the whole land. Shared: the armed men of Moab cry aloud; his soul trembles. Historic cities, not a millennial camp fight.

Isaiah 15:5

5My heart cries out for Moab; her fugitives flee to Zoar, to Eglath-shelishiyah. For at the ascent of Luhith they go up weeping; on the road to Horonaim they raise a cry of destruction.

Arminian

Wesley: Moab—Tho’ they are a most vile nation. Zoar—Zoar was a town bordering upon Moab. Of destruction—such a cry as men send forth when they are just falling into the pit of destruction. My heart cries out for Moab: the prophet’s compassion, not a claim that God does not know what he has decreed.

Arminian pastoral: pity can be honest without denying the judgment. Fugitives on the road to Zoar are historic Moabites, not a modern column of refugees on a map. Do not invent a limited God from a crying heart.

Reformed

Calvin: at length he assumes the character of a mourner. It is customary with the prophets to assume the character of those whose calamities they foretell, and thus to exhibit their condition, as it were, on a stage. Yet there can be no doubt that the prophets shuddered at the judgments of God, even against the wicked. Zoar he compares to a heifer of three years old, still in vigor and not yet yoked. Here all with whom the Lord deals tenderly are taught not to exalt themselves.

Reformed pastoral: the crying heart is the prophet’s office and a real shudder at judgment—not open theism, not a limit on the decree. Shared: fugitives flee weeping. Historic Zoar, Luhith, and Horonaim.

Key contrast Both hear a heart that cries and fugitives on the road—prophet’s compassion and/or the LORD’s pity through that office, not process-theism. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s prophet: my heart cries out though they are a most vile nation. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: Isaiah assumes the mourner’s character to set Moab on a stage, and the prophets also shuddered at the judgments of God even against the wicked.

Isaiah 15:6

6the waters of Nimrim are a desolation; the grass is withered, the vegetation fails, the greenery is no more.

Arminian

Wesley: Waters—watery grounds being very fruitful, are commonly most inhabited; but now they also, much more the dry and barren grounds, shall be desolate and without inhabitant. The waters of Nimrim are a desolation; the grass is withered, the vegetation fails, the greenery is no more.

Arminian pastoral: even the watered places fail. Historic Nimrim, not a twenty-first-century drought story. The land’s green is gone because the word has reached the ground; do not turn Moab into a modern state.

Reformed

Calvin: by an enlarged view of this desolation he says that the grass is withered. The waters will be taken away, which were highly necessary for that dry and parched country; soils of that kind produce nothing without irrigation. A land which is deprived of the blessing of God will be like a desert without any beauty.

Reformed pastoral: Nimrim dries because the blessing is withdrawn. Shared: the greenery is no more. Historic waters of Moab, not a newspaper.

Isaiah 15:7

7Therefore the abundance they have gained and what they have laid up they carry away over the Brook of the Willows.

Arminian

Wesley: They—their enemies. Brook—possibly he means some such river which ran into Euphrates, and so gave them opportunity of carrying their spoils by water unto Babylon. Therefore the abundance they have gained and what they have laid up they carry away over the Brook of the Willows.

Arminian pastoral: stored wealth walks out of the land. Historic spoils on a Moabite watercourse, not a modern market. Do not preach Moab’s barns as a culture-war speech.

Reformed

Calvin: he means the riches that are laid up. All the inhabitants are wont to convey their riches elsewhere, that they may afterwards bring them back when peace has been restored. To the brook of the willows: they will have no storehouse, no fortress in which they can lay them up with safety; so that they will be compelled to hide them among the willows. This certainly is the lowest wretchedness.

Reformed pastoral: what they stored cannot keep them. Shared: abundance carried over the Brook of the Willows. Historic flight, not a twenty-first-century map.

Key contrast Both hear abundance carried away over the Brook of the Willows. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s they—their enemies, bearing the spoils toward Babylon on a river that ran into the Euphrates. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: Moab’s own people hide what they have laid up among the willows, because they have no fortress left.

Isaiah 15:8

8For a cry has gone around the land of Moab; her wailing reaches to Eglaim; her wailing reaches to Beer-elim.

Arminian

Wesley: The cry—their cry fills all the parts of the country. For a cry has gone around the land of Moab; her wailing reaches to Eglaim; her wailing reaches to Beer-elim. Shared: the howl is not a corner of the map; it goes round the land.

Arminian pastoral: Eglaim and Beer-elim are named towns of historic Moab, not headlines. The warning is a real word over a real country. Do not stretch the wailing onto a modern border.

Reformed

Calvin: every part of that country all around shall be full of crying and howling; because that destruction reaches from one extremity to another. Besides the crying he twice mentions the howling, to denote the excess of grief, as men who are in despair surrender themselves entirely to lamentation.

Reformed pastoral: the cry circles because the judgment is appointed for the whole land. Shared: her wailing reaches to Eglaim and to Beer-elim. Historic Moab, not a millennial decoder.

Isaiah 15:9

9For the waters of Dibon are full of blood; for I will bring upon Dibon even more, a lion for those of Moab who escape, for the remnant of the land.

Arminian

Wesley: More—more than hath been already mentioned. Lions—God shall send lions to find out those that escape the fury of men. For the waters of Dibon are full of blood; a lion for those of Moab who escape. Shared: the remnant is not safe from the lion.

Arminian pastoral: blood and the lion are told honestly, not as a spectacle. Historic Dibon, not a culture-war speech. Flight does not finish the word; the remnant still meets what God sends. Do not invent a safe leftover by human cunning.

Reformed

Calvin: he describes not only grief and howling, but the slaughter of men. How great must this have been, when rivers are filled with blood! By additions he means that the Lord will multiply the murders. Though the Assyrians were cruel in this slaughter, yet the Lord was not cruel; he justly punished the barbarity of the Moabites. If any attempted to escape, they had to encounter lions. The hand of the Lord pursues the wicked in such a manner that they cannot in any way escape. These things are spoken for the consolation of the godly.

Reformed pastoral: the lion is the Lord’s further stroke, just and not cruel. Shared: the remnant of the land is not safe. Historic Moab’s blood, named without sensation.

Key contrast Both hear blood in Dibon’s waters and a lion for those who escape—the remnant is not safe. Arminians typically hear Wesley’s more: God shall send lions to find out those that escape the fury of men. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin’s further word: the Lord was not cruel; he justly punished Moab’s barbarity, and even the remnant meets lions so that the wicked cannot escape his hand.
