Passage

Revelation 14

Verses 6–13 · WEB

An angel proclaims an eternal gospel to every nation, calling all to fear God and worship the Creator. A second angel announces Babylon’s fall. A third warns that anyone who worships the beast will drink the wine of God’s wrath. The saints are called to endurance, and a voice blesses those who die in the Lord.

Revelation 14:6

6I saw an angel flying in mid heaven, having an eternal Good News to proclaim to those who dwell on the earth—to every nation, tribe, language, and people.

Arminian

An angel has an eternal gospel for every nation, tribe, language, and people. Arminian readers see God’s offer extended to the whole world even late in history.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gospel proclaimed to all nations, through which God calls his people.

Revelation 14:7

7He said with a loud voice, “Fear the Lord, and give him glory, for the hour of his judgment has come. Worship him who made the heaven, the earth, the sea, and the springs of waters!”

Arminian

“Fear the Lord, and give him glory … Worship him who made the heaven, the earth.” Arminian readers see a universal call to repent and worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Creator’s claim on all people and a call that leaves them without excuse.

Key contrast Both see a universal call. Arminian readers see grace that enables all hearers to respond. Reformed readers see a sincere call to all that is effective in those whom God draws.

Revelation 14:8

8Another, a second angel, followed, saying, “Babylon the great has fallen, which has made all the nations to drink of the wine of the wrath of her sexual immorality.”

Arminian

Babylon the great has fallen. Babylon is widely taken as Rome in John’s day and as the world system opposed to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the certainty of judgment on the world’s idolatry.

Revelation 14:9

9Another angel, a third, followed them, saying with a great voice, “If anyone worships the beast and his image, and receives a mark on his forehead or on his hand,

Arminian

Anyone who worships the beast and receives his mark faces judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the seriousness of false worship.

Revelation 14:10

10he also will drink of the wine of the wrath of God, which is prepared unmixed in the cup of his anger. He will be tormented with fire and sulfur in the presence of the holy angels and in the presence of the Lamb.

Arminian

They will drink the wine of God’s wrath and be tormented with fire and sulfur in the presence of the Lamb. Arminian readers see judgment as a solemn reality, chosen by those who refuse God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s holy justice against those who give their allegiance to the beast.

Revelation 14:11

11The smoke of their torment goes up forever and ever. They have no rest day and night, those who worship the beast and his image, and whoever receives the mark of his name.

Arminian

The smoke of their torment rises forever. Most Arminian and Reformed interpreters read this as eternal conscious judgment, though some in both traditions hold conditional immortality.

Reformed

Reformed readers have historically held eternal punishment, with some recent dissent.

Revelation 14:12

12Here is the perseverance of the saints, those who keep the commandments of God and the faith of Jesus.”

Arminian

“Here is the perseverance of the saints, those who keep the commandments of God and the faith of Jesus.” Arminian readers see a call to persevere, which assumes believers must continue faithful.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the phrase that gave the doctrine its name. The saints persevere, and this endurance marks them as God’s people.

Key contrast Both see endurance as the mark of the saints. Arminian readers read the verse as a call that believers must answer. Reformed readers see a description of what God’s people will certainly do.

Revelation 14:13

13I heard a voice from heaven saying, “Write, ‘Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord from now on.’” “Yes,” says the Spirit, “that they may rest from their labors, for their works follow with them.”

Arminian

Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord; they rest from their labors, and their works follow them. Arminian readers see comfort for those who remain faithful to death.

Reformed

Reformed readers see rest for all who die in Christ, whose works follow them as evidence of grace.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
