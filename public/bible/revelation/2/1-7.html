Passage

Revelation 2

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Christ commends the church in Ephesus for its hard work, endurance, and refusal to tolerate false apostles. Yet it has left its first love. He calls it to remember, repent, and do the first works, or he will remove its lampstand. The overcomer will eat from the tree of life.

Revelation 2:1

1“To the angel of the assembly in Ephesus write: “He who holds the seven stars in his right hand, he who walks among the seven golden lamp stands says these things:

Arminian

Christ holds the seven stars and walks among the lampstands, so he knows this church closely. Arminian readers see his presence as both comfort and accountability.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s sovereign oversight of his churches. He walks among them as their Lord, and what follows is his verdict.

Revelation 2:2

2“I know your works, and your toil and perseverance, and that you can’t tolerate evil men, and have tested those who call themselves apostles, and they are not, and found them false.

Arminian

Ephesus has worked hard, endured, and tested false apostles. Arminian readers note that doctrinal vigilance is commended and that Christ sees every effort.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a church that guarded sound doctrine and exercised discernment. Testing those who claim apostolic authority is a duty Christ praises.

Revelation 2:3

3You have perseverance and have endured for my name’s sake, and have not grown weary.

Arminian

Their endurance for Christ’s name is real faithfulness under pressure, and they have not grown weary. Arminian readers see perseverance practiced by believers who keep going.

Reformed

Reformed readers see endurance as the fruit of grace in a church that belongs to Christ.

Revelation 2:4

4But I have this against you, that you left your first love.

Arminian

Leaving their first love may mean love for Christ, for one another, or both. Arminian readers take the charge as evidence that a church sound in doctrine can still decline in devotion, and that this decline is serious.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that orthodoxy without love is a serious failure. Many see the lost love as directed both to Christ and to fellow believers, since the two cannot be separated (1 John 4:20).

Revelation 2:5

5Remember therefore from where you have fallen, and repent and do the first works; or else I am coming to you swiftly, and will move your lamp stand out of its place, unless you repent.

Arminian

“Remember therefore from where you have fallen” uses the language of falling, and the threat to remove the lampstand is serious. Arminian readers see a warning that a church can lose its standing before Christ if it does not repent.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the threat as directed at the congregation’s existence as a church. A church can cease to be a lampstand, and many have. The warning calls believers back to the love they had at first.

Key contrast Both read the threat seriously. Arminian readers see a warning that a church, and those in it, can fall from grace. Reformed readers read the lampstand as the congregation’s standing as a church, which can be removed, without implying that true believers lose their salvation.

Revelation 2:6

6But this you have, that you hate the works of the Nicolaitans, which I also hate.

Arminian

The Nicolaitans are otherwise little known; many link them with compromise over idolatry and immorality (compare 2:14–15). Christ hates their works, and the church is commended for doing the same.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Christ hates the works, not the people as such. Hatred of false teaching that leads people into sin is shared with Christ himself.

Revelation 2:7

7He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the assemblies. To him who overcomes I will give to eat from the tree of life, which is in the Paradise of my God.

Arminian

The overcomer will eat from the tree of life, which was closed after the fall. Arminian readers see a promise to those who persevere in faith and love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the overcomer as the true believer whose faith endures, and the promise as a restoration of what was lost in Eden.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
