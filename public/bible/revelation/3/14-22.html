Passage

Revelation 3

Verses 14–22 · WEB · Voices

The last letter goes to Laodicea, a wealthy city whose church is neither hot nor cold. Christ, the Amen and faithful witness, exposes its self-satisfaction: it thinks it is rich, yet it is wretched, poor, blind, and naked. He counsels it to buy from him what it lacks, reminds it that he rebukes those he loves, and stands at the door knocking. Whoever opens will share a meal with him, and the overcomer will share his throne.

Revelation 3:14

14“To the angel of the assembly in Laodicea write: “The Amen, the Faithful and True Witness, the Beginning of God’s creation, says these things:

Arminian

Christ is the Amen, the one in whom God’s promises are confirmed, and the faithful and true witness. “The Beginning of God’s creation” is read by most Christians, Arminian and Reformed alike, as the source and ruler of creation, not its first creature (compare Colossians 1:15–18). His titles prepare the church to hear an honest verdict.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take “the Beginning” as the origin and sovereign of creation, in line with John 1:3 and Colossians 1. The Amen guarantees that his word to Laodicea is true, however unwelcome. Christ’s sovereignty over creation stands behind his authority over the church.

Revelation 3:15

15“I know your works, that you are neither cold nor hot. I wish you were cold or hot.

Arminian

Neither cold nor hot describes a church that is useless to Christ. Many interpreters connect the image to Laodicea’s water supply, which arrived lukewarm, unlike the hot springs of Hierapolis or the cold water of Colossae. On that reading, “cold” is not a picture of hostility but of refreshment, and Christ wishes the church were useful in one way or the other.

Reformed

Reformed readers also commonly follow the water-supply background, so the rebuke concerns a church that is ineffective rather than one that is merely moderate in feeling. Christ knows its works, and his wish expresses his concern that it be of use in his service.

Revelation 3:16

16So, because you are lukewarm, and neither hot nor cold, I will vomit you out of my mouth.

Arminian

The image of vomiting out is strong. Arminian readers take it as a serious threat of rejection for a church that remains complacent, though it is spoken to move them to repentance, as verse 19 makes clear.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the threat as a sobering warning to the visible church. A congregation can be rejected by Christ, and those who were never his will be exposed. The warning also serves to rouse true believers within it.

Revelation 3:17

17Because you say, ‘I am rich, and have gotten riches, and have need of nothing,’ and don’t know that you are the wretched one, miserable, poor, blind, and naked;

Arminian

Laodicea was a banking center, a producer of black wool, and known for a medical school associated with eye remedies. The church has absorbed the city’s confidence in its wealth and fails to see its spiritual poverty. Arminian readers see self-deception that keeps a church from responding to grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear in this verse a picture of the human condition apart from grace: wretched, poor, blind, and naked, yet unaware of it. Self-sufficiency is the opposite of the poverty of spirit Jesus blesses. Only Christ can open such eyes.

Revelation 3:18

18I counsel you to buy from me gold refined by fire, that you may become rich; and white garments, that you may clothe yourself, and that the shame of your nakedness may not be revealed; and eye salve to anoint your eyes, that you may see.

Arminian

Christ counsels the church to buy from him gold, white garments, and eye salve, which are the very things the city prided itself on providing. Buying is a figure for receiving, since Isaiah 55:1 invites people to buy without money. Arminian readers stress that the counsel is offered to the church to accept or ignore.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that what Christ offers can only come from him: refined faith, the righteousness that covers shame, and sight given by the Spirit. Buying “without money” means receiving freely. The counsel is a gracious summons through which Christ supplies what he commands.

Revelation 3:19

19As many as I love, I reprove and chasten. Be zealous therefore, and repent.

Arminian

“As many as I love, I reprove and chasten” shows that the harsh words come from love. The call to be zealous and repent assumes that the church can respond. Arminian readers see Christ’s loving discipline as grace that invites cooperation.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect the verse with Proverbs 3:12 and Hebrews 12:6, where the Lord disciplines those he loves as sons. Discipline is a mark of belonging to him. Repentance is commanded, and in God’s children it is also his gift.

Revelation 3:20

20Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, then I will come in to him and will dine with him, and he with me.

Arminian

This verse is central to Arminian readings of Christ’s call. Christ stands and knocks, and “if anyone hears my voice and opens the door,” he will come in and share a meal. Wesley read it as an invitation spoken at that very moment and glossed opening as willingly receiving Christ. Arminian interpreters see grace that comes to the sinner first and enables a response, yet does not force the door.

The immediate audience is a church, so the verse is first a call to lukewarm believers to renew fellowship with Christ. Arminian readers also apply “anyone” broadly, as a sign that Christ’s offer is extended to each person who hears.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the verse is addressed to a church, so it concerns Christ’s call to a complacent congregation to restore fellowship with him. It was not written as an account of how regeneration first occurs. The invitation is sincere, and those who hear are responsible to open.

Reformed theology also holds that a person opens because God has first given ears to hear. The Westminster Confession speaks of sinners coming most freely, being made willing by grace. On this reading the knock and the opening both belong to Christ’s gracious work, while the human response is still free and willing.

Key contrast Both traditions take Christ’s knock as a sincere call and agree that the immediate audience is a church. Arminian readers see grace that comes first, enables a response, and can be resisted, with “anyone” showing the breadth of the offer. Reformed readers see a call that is received when God renews the heart, so that the one who opens does so freely because grace has made him willing.

Revelation 3:21

21He who overcomes, I will give to him to sit down with me on my throne, as I also overcame and sat down with my Father on his throne.

Arminian

The overcomer will sit with Christ on his throne, just as Christ overcame and sat with his Father. Arminian readers see a promise to those who persevere in faith, with Christ’s own victory as their pattern and their strength.

Reformed

Reformed readers see union with Christ: his people share his reign because they share his victory. Christ’s overcoming is the ground of theirs, and the promise assures them of the end God has prepared for his own.

Revelation 3:22

22He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the assemblies.”

Arminian

The final refrain closes the seven letters. Every church is called to hear, and Arminian readers stress the responsibility of each hearer to respond.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the seven letters are addressed to the whole church in every age. The Spirit speaks, and those whom he gives ears to hear will listen.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Voices from both camps

On the Lord who stands at the door and knocks, a classic voice from each tradition on how Christ’s call meets the human will.

Arminian Notable voices

“Behold, I stand at the door, and knock—even at this instant, while he is speaking this word. If any man open—willingly receive me. I will sup with him—refreshing him with my graces and gifts, and delighting myself in what I have given. And he with me—in life everlasting.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Revelation 3:20)

Reformed Notable voices

“All those whom God hath predestinated unto life, and those only, he is pleased, in his appointed and accepted time, effectually to call, by his Word and Spirit, out of that state of sin and death in which they are by nature, to grace and salvation by Jesus Christ; enlightening their minds spiritually and savingly to understand the things of God, taking away their heart of stone, and giving unto them an heart of flesh; renewing their wills, and by his almighty power determining them to that which is good, and effectually drawing them to Jesus Christ: yet so as they come most freely, being made willing by his grace.”
Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) · Chapter 10.1, Of Effectual Calling
