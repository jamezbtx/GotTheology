Passage

Revelation 3

Verses 1–6 · WEB

The risen Christ addresses the church in Sardis, which has a name for being alive but is dead. He calls it to wake up, remember what it received, keep it, and repent, or he will come like a thief. A few in Sardis have not soiled their garments, and the one who overcomes is promised white clothing, a name kept in the book of life, and Christ’s confession before the Father.

Revelation 3:1

1“And to the angel of the assembly in Sardis write: “He who has the seven Spirits of God and the seven stars says these things: “I know your works, that you have a reputation of being alive, but you are dead.

Arminian

Christ holds the seven Spirits of God and the seven stars, so he has both the Spirit’s fullness and authority over the churches’ messengers. His verdict cuts through appearances: Sardis has a reputation for life, yet he calls it dead. Arminian readers hear in this a warning that a church, and the people in it, can drift from a living faith while keeping its outward form.

The word to Sardis is not a final sentence. The next verses call the church to wake up, which assumes the condition can still be changed by repentance and renewed dependence on grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that Christ knows his churches fully and judges by the heart, not by reputation. A congregation can be orthodox in name and lifeless in practice. Many Reformed interpreters see in Sardis a visible church that included many who professed faith without being regenerate, alongside the faithful few named in verse 4.

The seven Spirits, commonly read as the Holy Spirit in his fullness, point to the only source of life for a dead church. Revival is the Spirit’s work, and Christ’s rebuke is one of the means he uses to bring it.

Revelation 3:2

2Wake up and strengthen the things that remain, which you were about to throw away, for I have found no works of yours perfected before my God.

Arminian

“Wake up and strengthen the things that remain” assumes that something remains and that the church can act on Christ’s call. Arminian readers take the command as a summons to respond to the grace still at work among them, before what is left is lost. Works not “perfected” before God are works begun and left unfinished.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the command is addressed to responsible people and must be obeyed. They see the call itself as the means by which Christ rouses those who belong to him. Works that are incomplete before God are measured by his standard, not by the church’s reputation in the city.

Revelation 3:3

3Remember therefore how you have received and heard. Keep it and repent. If therefore you won’t watch, I will come as a thief, and you won’t know what hour I will come upon you.

Arminian

Remembering, keeping, and repenting are three acts Christ asks of the church. The threat to come as a thief is conditional: “if therefore you won’t watch.” Arminian readers take the condition seriously, since Christ’s coming in judgment on this church depends on whether it responds.

Interpreters differ on whether this coming is a visit in judgment within history or the final return. Many take it as a near visitation that anticipates the last day, and the call to watchfulness applies either way.

Reformed

Reformed readers also read the warning as conditional in its form, and they see it as one of the ways Christ keeps his own watchful. The coming “as a thief” echoes Jesus’ teaching in the Gospels. Whether it refers to a judgment on Sardis in history or to the final coming, its purpose is to press the church back to the gospel it first received.

Revelation 3:4

4Nevertheless you have a few names in Sardis that didn’t defile their garments. They will walk with me in white, for they are worthy.

Arminian

Even in a dead church there are a few who have not defiled their garments. Their worthiness is the worthiness of faithful disciples who have kept themselves from compromise, not a claim to have earned salvation. Arminian readers see here personal faithfulness that Christ notices and honors.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the “few names,” a remnant within the visible church. Their worthiness is the fitting result of grace that has kept them, since the white garments elsewhere in Revelation are washed in the blood of the Lamb (7:14). Christ knows his own by name even when the congregation around them has declined.

Revelation 3:5

5He who overcomes will be arrayed in white garments, and I will in no way blot his name out of the book of life, and I will confess his name before my Father, and before his angels.

Arminian

The promise not to blot out the overcomer’s name is often read by Arminian interpreters as implying that a name can be blotted out. If the promise holds for those who overcome, it suggests a danger for those who do not. The verse then stands alongside other warnings in Revelation (22:19) as encouragement to persevere.

The promise is still meant to comfort. Christ confesses before his Father those who have confessed him, and the overcomer can rest in that.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters read “I will in no way blot his name out” as a strong assurance, with an emphatic negative in the Greek. The book of life is written from the foundation of the world (13:8; 17:8), and Christ promises that those written there will not be removed. The overcomer is the one whose faith endures, and endurance is the evidence of a name already written.

On this reading the verse does not imply that names are ever erased. It uses a familiar image of a civic register to promise that Christ will never disown his own.

Key contrast The verse is a fork on perseverance. Arminian readers take the promise to the overcomer as implying that a name could be blotted out from those who do not overcome. Reformed readers take the emphatic negative as a guarantee that names written from the foundation of the world remain, with overcoming as the mark of those written there.

Revelation 3:6

6He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the assemblies.

Arminian

The refrain applies the word to Sardis to every church. Arminian readers stress that hearing is a responsibility laid on all who read, and the Spirit’s call can be heeded or refused.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the Spirit speaks through Christ’s words to all the churches. Those to whom he gives ears to hear will listen, and the refrain also leaves the hearer without excuse.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
