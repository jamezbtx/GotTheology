Passage

Revelation 8

Verses 1–5 · WEB

The Lamb opens the seventh seal, and heaven is silent for about half an hour. Seven angels receive seven trumpets. Another angel offers incense with the prayers of the saints, then hurls fire from the altar onto the earth.

Revelation 8:1

1When he opened the seventh seal, there was silence in heaven for about half an hour.

Arminian

The silence may be a pause of awe before judgment or a hush so that the prayers of the saints can be heard.

Reformed

Reformed readers see reverent silence before God acts.

Revelation 8:2

2I saw the seven angels who stand before God, and seven trumpets were given to them.

Arminian

Seven trumpets recall Jericho and Sinai.

Reformed

Reformed readers see trumpets as warnings and announcements of judgment.

Revelation 8:3

3Another angel came and stood over the altar, having a golden censer. Much incense was given to him, that he should add it to the prayers of all the saints on the golden altar which was before the throne.

Arminian

Incense is added to the prayers of all the saints. Arminian readers see that prayer matters to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see prayer as one of the means God uses to accomplish his purposes.

Revelation 8:4

4The smoke of the incense, with the prayers of the saints, went up before God out of the angel’s hand.

Arminian

The prayers rise before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that no prayer of the saints is lost.

Revelation 8:5

5The angel took the censer, and he filled it with the fire of the altar, then threw it on the earth. Thunders, sounds, lightnings, and an earthquake followed.

Arminian

Fire from the altar is thrown on the earth. The prayers of chapter 6 are being answered in judgment. Arminian readers see God responding to his people’s cries.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign answer to prayer in the judgments that follow.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
