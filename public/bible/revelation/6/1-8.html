Passage

Revelation 6

Verses 1–8 · WEB

The Lamb opens the first four seals, and four horsemen ride out: a conqueror on a white horse, a rider on a red horse who takes peace from the earth, a rider on a black horse with scales signaling scarcity, and Death on a pale horse with Hades following. Their power is given and limited.

Revelation 6:1

1I saw that the Lamb opened one of the seven seals, and I heard one of the four living creatures saying, as with a voice of thunder, “Come and see!”

Arminian

The Lamb opens the seals, so the judgments that follow come under his authority. Preterists link the seals to first-century events, historicists to stages of Roman and church history, futurists to a coming tribulation, and idealists to patterns that recur throughout the age.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that Christ controls history: nothing happens until the Lamb opens the seal. Many Reformed interpreters read the seals as describing conditions that mark the whole period between Christ’s first and second comings.

Revelation 6:2

2Then a white horse appeared, and he who sat on it had a bow. A crown was given to him, and he came out conquering, and to conquer.

Arminian

The rider on the white horse is debated. Some see Christ or the gospel going out conquering (compare 19:11), while many others see a conqueror who imitates Christ, the first of four destructive forces.

Reformed

Reformed readers are divided in the same way. Some older Reformed writers saw the victorious gospel, while many recent ones see military conquest. Either way, the crown “was given,” so his power is permitted by God.

Revelation 6:3

3When he opened the second seal, I heard the second living creature saying, “Come!”

Arminian

The second seal brings the red horse.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the parallel pattern of each seal opened by the Lamb.

Revelation 6:4

4Another came out, a red horse. To him who sat on it was given power to take peace from the earth, and that they should kill one another. There was given to him a great sword.

Arminian

The red horse takes peace from the earth so that people kill one another. Arminian readers see violence as human sin that God permits and uses in judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see war as a judgment under God’s permission. The sword “was given,” and even violence is not outside his rule.

Revelation 6:5

5When he opened the third seal, I heard the third living creature saying, “Come and see!” And behold, a black horse, and he who sat on it had a balance in his hand.

Arminian

The black horse carries scales, a sign of rationing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the third seal as famine or economic hardship.

Revelation 6:6

6I heard a voice in the middle of the four living creatures saying, “A choenix of wheat for a denarius, and three choenix of barley for a denarius! Don’t damage the oil and the wine!”

Arminian

Prices for grain soar while oil and wine are spared, suggesting scarcity that hits the poor hardest. The limitation shows that judgment is restrained.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God restraining judgment even as he sends it, so that hardship serves as a warning.

Revelation 6:7

7When he opened the fourth seal, I heard the fourth living creature saying, “Come and see!”

Arminian

The fourth seal opens.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the final rider of the four.

Revelation 6:8

8And behold, a pale horse, and the name of he who sat on it was Death. Hades followed with him. Authority over one fourth of the earth, to kill with the sword, with famine, with death, and by the wild animals of the earth was given to him.

Arminian

Death and Hades receive authority over a fourth of the earth. The limit shows these are partial judgments that call people to repent before the end.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the judgments as limited by God and as warnings of the final judgment to come.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
