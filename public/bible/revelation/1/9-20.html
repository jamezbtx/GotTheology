Passage

Revelation 1

Verses 9–20 · WEB

On Patmos, John hears a loud voice on the Lord’s day and turns to see one like a son of man walking among seven golden lampstands. His appearance is glorious and terrifying, and John falls at his feet. Christ lays his hand on him, tells him not to fear, and declares himself the living one who holds the keys of Death and Hades. He commissions John to write and explains the stars and lampstands.

Revelation 1:9

9I John, your brother and partner with you in the oppression, Kingdom, and perseverance in Christ Jesus, was on the isle that is called Patmos because of God’s Word and the testimony of Jesus Christ.

Arminian

John calls himself a brother and partner in the oppression, kingdom, and perseverance that belong to those in Christ. Arminian readers note that suffering, kingdom, and endurance belong together in the Christian life, and that perseverance is something believers practice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the kingdom is present in the midst of tribulation, and that perseverance is the mark of those who belong to Christ. John suffers because of God’s word and his testimony, which is the setting for the whole book.

Revelation 1:10

10I was in the Spirit on the Lord’s day, and I heard behind me a loud voice, like a trumpet

Arminian

The Lord’s day is most often taken as Sunday, the day of Christ’s resurrection. Being “in the Spirit” describes a prophetic experience. Arminian readers see God meeting his servant in the setting of worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers also take the Lord’s day as the first day of the week, set apart for worship. The trumpet-like voice signals divine revelation, as at Sinai.

Revelation 1:11

11saying, “What you see, write in a book and send to the seven assemblies: to Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia, and to Laodicea.”

Arminian

The seven churches named were real congregations in the Roman province of Asia. Many interpreters also see them as representative of the whole church, since seven suggests completeness. A historicist minority reads them as successive ages of church history.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly see the seven churches as historical congregations whose messages apply to the church in every age. The book is addressed to them and through them to all believers.

Revelation 1:12

12I turned to see the voice that spoke with me. Having turned, I saw seven golden lamp stands.

Arminian

The seven golden lampstands are the seven churches (v. 20), recalling the lampstand in the tabernacle and in Zechariah 4. Arminian readers see churches called to shine as lights in the world.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church as God’s light-bearing people, whose light depends on the Spirit. The lampstands are the setting in which Christ appears.

Revelation 1:13

13And among the lamp stands was one like a son of man, clothed with a robe reaching down to his feet, and with a golden sash around his chest.

Arminian

Christ walks among the lampstands, present with his churches. The title “son of man” comes from Daniel 7, and the long robe and golden sash suggest priestly and royal dignity. Arminian readers see Christ’s attentive care for each church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as priest and king among his people. He is present in his church, ruling and caring for it, which is the foundation for the letters that follow.

Revelation 1:14

14His head and his hair were white as white wool, like snow. His eyes were like a flame of fire.

Arminian

White hair recalls the Ancient of Days in Daniel 7:9, so Christ shares the attributes of God. Eyes like fire signify searching knowledge. Arminian readers see a Lord who sees the truth about every church.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the description of the Ancient of Days is applied to Christ, a strong testimony to his deity. His eyes search the hearts, as 2:23 says.

Revelation 1:15

15His feet were like burnished brass, as if it had been refined in a furnace. His voice was like the voice of many waters.

Arminian

Feet like refined bronze suggest strength and purity, and a voice like many waters suggests overwhelming power. Both traditions see the majesty of the risen Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the echo of Ezekiel 43:2, where the voice of God is like many waters. The glory of God is the glory of Christ.

Revelation 1:16

16He had seven stars in his right hand. Out of his mouth proceeded a sharp two-edged sword. His face was like the sun shining at its brightest.

Arminian

Christ holds the seven stars, his sword is his word of judgment, and his face shines like the sun. Arminian readers see both comfort and warning: the messengers are in his hand, and his word judges.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s sovereign control over his churches, symbolized by the stars in his right hand. The sword from his mouth is the word by which he rules and judges (Hebrews 4:12).

Revelation 1:17

17When I saw him, I fell at his feet like a dead man. He laid his right hand on me, saying, “Don’t be afraid. I am the first and the last,

Arminian

John falls as though dead, but Christ touches him and says, “Don’t be afraid.” Arminian readers see grace that meets fear with assurance. “The first and the last” is a title of God in Isaiah 44:6.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same title as a claim to deity. The one who terrifies is also the one who comforts, and his people need not fear because he holds them.

Revelation 1:18

18and the Living one. I was dead, and behold, I am alive forever and ever. Amen. I have the keys of Death and of Hades.

Arminian

Christ was dead and is alive forever, and he holds the keys of Death and Hades. Arminian readers see the victory that gives every believer hope.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s sovereign authority over death itself. Those who belong to him cannot be held by death, because he holds the keys.

Revelation 1:19

19Write therefore the things which you have seen, and the things which are, and the things which will happen hereafter.

Arminian

John is told to write what he has seen, what is, and what will happen hereafter. Some futurists use this verse as an outline of the book, while others read it more generally as covering present and future realities throughout the visions.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly read the verse as describing the mixture of present and future in the whole book rather than a strict outline. Idealist and amillennial readers see the visions as applying throughout history.

Revelation 1:20

20The mystery of the seven stars which you saw in my right hand, and the seven golden lamp stands is this: The seven stars are the angels of the seven assemblies. The seven lamp stands are seven assemblies.

Arminian

The angels of the churches may be heavenly beings, human messengers, or the spirit of each congregation. Interpreters differ, but all agree the churches are under Christ’s care.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same range of views. Many take the angels as representing the churches, with the point being that Christ holds each congregation in his hand.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
