Passage

Revelation 19

Verses 1–10 · WEB

After Babylon’s fall, a great multitude in heaven cries “Hallelujah,” praising God for judging the prostitute who corrupted the earth. The elders and living creatures join the praise, and a voice announces that the marriage of the Lamb has come and his bride has made herself ready in fine linen. John is told to write a blessing on those invited to the marriage supper, and when he falls at the angel’s feet he is told to worship God alone.

Revelation 19:1

1After these things I heard something like a loud voice of a great multitude in heaven, saying, “Hallelujah! Salvation, power, and glory belong to our God;

Arminian

The first “Hallelujah” of the New Testament rises after Babylon’s ruin. Arminian interpreters hear in it the joy of people who once suffered under her and now see God keep his word to them.

Reformed

Salvation, glory, and power are ascribed to God and not shared with any creature. Reformed interpreters hear the whole multitude confessing that rescue from Babylon was entirely God’s work.

Revelation 19:2

2for his judgments are true and righteous. For he has judged the great prostitute who corrupted the earth with her sexual immorality, and he has avenged the blood of his servants at her hand.”

Arminian

God’s judgments are called true and righteous because he has avenged the blood of his servants. Arminian writers stress that this vengeance belongs to God, which is why the saints never had to take it into their own hands.

Reformed

The verdict on Babylon is praised as just, not merely as powerful. Reformed writers see God’s holiness on display, since he will not leave the corruption of the earth unanswered.

Revelation 19:3

3A second said, “Hallelujah! Her smoke goes up forever and ever.”

Arminian

The smoke rising forever marks a judgment that will not be reversed. For Arminian readers it is a sober reminder that the time for repentance offered throughout the book was not endless.

Reformed

A second “Hallelujah” follows, and the lasting smoke echoes Isaiah 34:10. Reformed readers take the permanence of the ruin as evidence that God’s decree against evil stands firm.

Revelation 19:4

4The twenty-four elders and the four living creatures fell down and worshiped God who sits on the throne, saying, “Amen! Hallelujah!”

Arminian

The twenty-four elders and four living creatures fall down and add their “Amen.” Arminian commentators note that heaven’s worship is a response, as creatures freely agree with what God has done.

Reformed

The throne-room scene of chapters 4 and 5 returns. Reformed commentators see the whole heavenly order bowing to God as sovereign over history and judgment alike.

Revelation 19:5

5A voice came from the throne, saying, “Give praise to our God, all you his servants, you who fear him, the small and the great!”

Arminian

A voice from the throne calls all God’s servants, small and great, to praise him. The invitation is broad, and Arminian readers hear in it the same welcome extended to anyone who fears God.

Reformed

Small and great stand on the same level before God. Reformed readers find here the leveling effect of grace, since no rank earns a place among those who praise him.

Revelation 19:6

6I heard something like the voice of a great multitude, and like the voice of many waters, and like the voice of mighty thunders, saying, “Hallelujah! For the Lord our God, the Almighty, reigns!

Arminian

The sound of the multitude is like many waters and mighty thunder, because the Lord God Almighty reigns. Arminian expositors see the reign of God celebrated as the moment when the opposition to his rule finally gives way.

Reformed

“The Lord our God, the Almighty, reigns” is the heart of the song. Reformed expositors stress that God has always reigned, and the song celebrates his rule becoming visible to all.

Revelation 19:7

7Let’s rejoice and be exceedingly glad, and let’s give the glory to him. For the wedding of the Lamb has come, and his wife has made herself ready.”

Arminian

The marriage of the Lamb has come, and his wife has made herself ready. Arminian readers give weight to the bride’s own preparation, since the church responds to grace with faithful living.

Reformed

The bride’s readiness follows the Lamb’s initiative; he is the one who chose and redeemed her. Reformed readers see the wedding as the fulfillment of the covenant God made with his people.

Revelation 19:8

8It was given to her that she would array herself in bright, pure, fine linen, for the fine linen is the righteous acts of the saints.

Arminian

Fine linen, bright and pure, is given to her, and it is the righteous acts of the saints. Arminian interpreters notice both halves of the verse: the clothing is a gift, yet it consists of deeds the saints actually did.

Reformed

The linen is “given” before it is described as righteous acts. Reformed interpreters read the saints’ works as fruit of grace already granted, so even their good deeds are a gift.

Revelation 19:9

9He said to me, “Write, ‘Blessed are those who are invited to the wedding supper of the Lamb.’” He said to me, “These are true words of God.”

Arminian

Those invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb are called blessed. Arminian readers connect this with Jesus’ parables of the wedding feast, where the invitation goes out widely and must be accepted.

Reformed

The angel insists that these are true words of God. Reformed readers link the invitation with God’s effectual calling, by which those he summons are brought to the feast.

Revelation 19:10

10I fell down before his feet to worship him. He said to me, “Look! Don’t do it! I am a fellow bondservant with you and with your brothers who hold the testimony of Jesus. Worship God, for the testimony of Jesus is the Spirit of Prophecy.”

Arminian

John falls at the angel’s feet and is stopped at once: the angel is a fellow servant, and God alone is to be worshiped. Arminian commentators find a warning that even a faithful apostle can misdirect devotion.

Reformed

The testimony of Jesus is the spirit of prophecy, so all true prophecy points to him. Reformed commentators see here a firm line between Creator and creature that guards the worship due to God alone.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
