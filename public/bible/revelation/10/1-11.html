Passage

Revelation 10

Verses 1–11 · WEB

A mighty angel stands on sea and land holding a little open scroll. Seven thunders speak, but John is told to seal up what they said. The angel swears that there will be no more delay, and that the mystery of God will be finished at the seventh trumpet. John eats the scroll, sweet in his mouth and bitter in his stomach, and is told he must prophesy again about many peoples and kings.

Revelation 10:1

1I saw a mighty angel coming down out of the sky, clothed with a cloud. A rainbow was on his head. His face was like the sun, and his feet like pillars of fire.

Arminian

The mighty angel is wrapped in a cloud with a rainbow and a face like the sun. Some interpreters identify him with Christ, while most see a powerful angel reflecting divine glory.

Reformed

Reformed readers have held both views. Several older Reformed commentators saw Christ, while many later ones see an angel bearing God’s message.

Revelation 10:2

2He had in his hand a little open book. He set his right foot on the sea, and his left on the land.

Arminian

The little scroll is open, and the angel stands on sea and land, claiming the whole world for God’s message.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the universal scope of God’s word and rule.

Revelation 10:3

3He cried with a loud voice, as a lion roars. When he cried, the seven thunders uttered their voices.

Arminian

The angel roars like a lion, and seven thunders answer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s voice speaking in judgment, as in Psalm 29.

Revelation 10:4

4When the seven thunders sounded, I was about to write; but I heard a voice from the sky saying, “Seal up the things which the seven thunders said, and don’t write them.”

Arminian

John is told to seal up what the thunders said. Arminian readers see a reminder that not everything about the future has been revealed, which should make readers humble about predictions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a limit on revelation: God has secret things that belong to him (Deuteronomy 29:29). Speculation about what was sealed is out of place.

Revelation 10:5

5The angel whom I saw standing on the sea and on the land lifted up his right hand to the sky

Arminian

The angel raises his hand to heaven to swear an oath.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the solemnity of an oath before the Creator.

Revelation 10:6

6and swore by him who lives forever and ever, who created heaven and the things that are in it, the earth and the things that are in it, and the sea and the things that are in it, that there will no longer be delay,

Arminian

He swears by the Creator that there will be no more delay.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Creator’s right to bring history to its end.

Revelation 10:7

7but in the days of the voice of the seventh angel, when he is about to sound, then the mystery of God is finished, as he declared to his servants the prophets.

Arminian

At the seventh trumpet the mystery of God will be finished, as announced to the prophets. Arminian readers see God bringing his saving purpose to completion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s eternal plan, revealed in part to the prophets, reaching its fulfillment.

Revelation 10:8

8The voice which I heard from heaven, again speaking with me, said, “Go, take the book which is open in the hand of the angel who stands on the sea and on the land.”

Arminian

John is told to take the open scroll.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the prophet receiving God’s word.

Revelation 10:9

9I went to the angel, telling him to give me the little book. He said to me, “Take it and eat it. It will make your stomach bitter, but in your mouth it will be as sweet as honey.”

Arminian

The scroll will be sweet in his mouth and bitter in his stomach, echoing Ezekiel 3.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the joy of God’s word and the pain of its message of judgment.

Revelation 10:10

10I took the little book out of the angel’s hand, and ate it. It was as sweet as honey in my mouth. When I had eaten it, my stomach was made bitter.

Arminian

John eats the scroll and experiences both sweetness and bitterness. Arminian readers see the cost of carrying a message of grace and judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the prophet’s calling: God’s word is good, yet proclaiming it brings sorrow.

Revelation 10:11

11They told me, “You must prophesy again over many peoples, nations, languages, and kings.”

Arminian

John must prophesy again about many peoples, nations, languages, and kings. Arminian readers see the universal reach of the message.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the word going to all nations, through whom God gathers his people.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
