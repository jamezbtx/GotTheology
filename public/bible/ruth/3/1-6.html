Passage

Ruth 3

Verses 1–6 · WEB

Naomi, restored from bitterness, seeks rest and security for Ruth. Boaz, a kinsman who has already shown kindness, will be winnowing barley on the threshing floor. Naomi directs Ruth to prepare herself, go there discreetly, and wait for Boaz to tell her what to do. Ruth promises to obey and carries out the plan.

Ruth 3:1

1Naomi her mother-in-law said to her, “My daughter, shall I not seek rest for you, that it may be well with you?

Arminian

Naomi, once the one who sent Ruth away to find rest in a husband’s house (1:9), now takes initiative to seek that rest for her. “Rest” here means a settled home and security. Arminian readers note that the bitterness of chapter 1 has turned to active care: grace received through Boaz’s kindness moves Naomi to act responsibly for another. Providence works through her planning, not around it.

Reformed

Naomi’s question picks up her own wish in 1:9 and shows a heart restored from bitterness to hope. Reformed readers see God’s providence ordering the harvest, the field, and now Naomi’s counsel; the Lord ordinarily accomplishes his purposes through human planning. Her care is a real duty she performs, and it serves a larger plan she cannot yet see.

Ruth 3:2

2Now isn’t Boaz our kinsman, with whose maidens you were? Behold, he will be winnowing barley tonight on the threshing floor.

Arminian

Boaz is “our kinsman,” a relative already shown to be generous (2:1, 2:20). Winnowing took place on the threshing floor, often in the evening breeze, and owners might stay overnight to guard the grain. Naomi reads the ordinary calendar of harvest as an opening. Arminian readers stress human discernment answering the opportunities God gives.

Reformed

Naomi has already named Boaz as one of their near kinsmen (2:20). The details of harvest timing, place, and relationship are the ordinary means Reformed readers associate with providence: God does not need a miracle here, only the rhythms of work and family. Both traditions affirm that the Lord governs such ordinary circumstances.

Ruth 3:3

3Therefore wash yourself, anoint yourself, get dressed, and go down to the threshing floor; but don’t make yourself known to the man until he has finished eating and drinking.

Arminian

Washing, anointing, and dressing prepare Ruth to appear in her best; many readers suggest it may also mark a move from widowhood toward remarriage. The instruction to wait until Boaz has eaten keeps the approach private and orderly. The plan is bold but aimed at a lawful request, not at deception.

Reformed

The preparations signal that Ruth comes with a serious and honorable purpose, often read as a step out of mourning toward a proposal of marriage. Reformed readers note that Scripture records the plan plainly without commending every risk in it, while the outcome shows God guarding the integrity of those involved.

Ruth 3:4

4It shall be, when he lies down, that you shall note the place where he is lying. Then you shall go in, uncover his feet, and lie down. Then he will tell you what to do.”

Arminian

Uncovering Boaz’s feet and lying down is a symbolic, discreet gesture of petition in a culture that understood such signs; the text gives no hint of impropriety. Naomi expects Boaz, a man of known character, to direct the next step. Arminian readers see trust placed in a righteous man’s free and honorable response.

Reformed

The gesture is a quiet, symbolic appeal that leaves the decision with Boaz. The narrative’s own portrait of Boaz as upright (2:1–16) frames how the reader should understand the scene. Reformed readers see dependence on God working through a godly man’s judgment rather than through manipulation.

Ruth 3:5

5She said to her, “All that you say, I will do.”

Arminian

Ruth’s answer echoes the loyalty of 1:16–17. Her ready obedience to her mother-in-law expresses the steadfast kindness (chesed) that marks her throughout the book. Arminian readers see a freely chosen faithfulness, the fruit of a grace she has embraced.

Reformed

Ruth’s submission to Naomi’s counsel continues the covenant loyalty she vowed in 1:16–17. Reformed readers see in her a Gentile brought under the wings of Israel’s God (2:12), whose obedience flows from that new allegiance. Both traditions honor her faithfulness as genuine and commendable.

Ruth 3:6

6She went down to the threshing floor, and did everything that her mother-in-law told her.

Arminian

The narrator summarizes before narrating: Ruth went and did what Naomi asked. The emphasis falls on her reliability and on the ordinary means through which God brings rest. No miracle intervenes; faithful human action carries the plot.

Reformed

The summary underscores Ruth’s obedience and prepares for the scene that follows. Reformed readers see providence operating through faithful action rather than suspending it. Both traditions share the conviction that God’s hidden hand works through ordinary obedience.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
