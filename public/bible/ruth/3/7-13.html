Passage

Ruth 3

Verses 7–13 · WEB

At midnight Boaz wakes to find Ruth at his feet. She asks him to spread the corner of his garment over her as a near kinsman. Boaz blesses her, calls her a worthy woman known to all the town, and pledges with an oath to act, while honestly naming a nearer kinsman with a prior claim.

Ruth 3:7

7When Boaz had eaten and drunk, and his heart was merry, he went to lie down at the end of the heap of grain. She came softly, uncovered his feet, and lay down.

Arminian

Boaz has eaten and drunk and is content after the harvest; the narrator presents this as satisfaction at the end of a good day, not drunkenness, and his clear speech and conduct later in the night confirm it. He sleeps by the grain he guards. Ruth follows Naomi’s plan quietly and exactly.

Reformed

“His heart was merry” describes contentment after the harvest meal, as the verse itself frames it; Boaz’s sober, careful response that follows rules out any reading of excess. Ruth’s quiet approach carries out the plan. Reformed readers note how the narrative keeps attention on the request she will make, not on speculation.

Ruth 3:8

8At midnight, the man was startled and turned himself; and behold, a woman lay at his feet.

Arminian

Boaz wakes startled at midnight to find someone at his feet. The surprise shows he had no part in arranging the encounter. The scene moves quickly to words, where the real meaning lies.

Reformed

The midnight surprise heightens the tension and makes clear that Boaz did not plan the meeting. What follows is conversation and commitment, not concealment. Both traditions read the moment as the hinge where a private appeal becomes a public, lawful resolution.

Ruth 3:9

9He said, “Who are you?” She answered, “I am Ruth your servant. Therefore spread the corner of your garment over your servant; for you are a near kinsman.”

Arminian

Ruth names herself and makes her own request, going beyond Naomi’s instruction that Boaz would tell her what to do. “Spread the corner of your garment” is an idiom for taking under protection in marriage (compare Ezek 16:8), and the Hebrew word for “corner” is the word for “wings” in 2:12. Ruth asks Boaz to become the answer to his own blessing. Both columns share the kinsman-redeemer theme: a relative who restores family and inheritance, a picture many Christians read as pointing to the Redeemer who takes his people under his care.

Reformed

Ruth calls herself his servant and appeals to his role as a near kinsman (go’el), the relative responsible for redeeming family land and helping a relative in need (Lev 25:25). Her wording recalls Boaz’s blessing that she had come under the Lord’s wings (2:12): the protection of God comes to her through a human redeemer. Reformed readers, sharing this typology with Arminians, see a picture of Christ the Redeemer who covers and claims his bride.

Ruth 3:10

10He said, “You are blessed by the LORD, my daughter. You have shown more kindness in the latter end than at the beginning, because you didn’t follow young men, whether poor or rich.

Arminian

Boaz blesses Ruth in the LORD’s name and interprets her act as kindness (chesed), not seduction. Her “latter” kindness exceeds her first (her loyalty to Naomi) because she seeks a husband who will preserve Naomi’s family line rather than pursuing a younger man for herself. Arminian readers see virtue freely chosen and honored.

Reformed

Boaz’s first words are a blessing, setting the moral tone of the scene. He reads her request as loyal kindness to the family, greater even than her care for Naomi in chapter 2, since she chose the family’s redeemer over personal preference. Reformed readers see grace producing self-denying love in a convert.

Ruth 3:11

11Now, my daughter, don’t be afraid. I will do to you all that you say; for all the city of my people knows that you are a worthy woman.

Arminian

Boaz reassures Ruth and pledges to act. The town knows her as a “worthy woman” (’eshet chayil), the same phrase used in Proverbs 31:10; Boaz himself was introduced as a man of chayil (2:1). The narrator matches two people of proven character. Their integrity is public knowledge, not a private claim.

Reformed

“Worthy woman” links Ruth to the ideal wife of Proverbs 31 and pairs her with Boaz, the worthy man of 2:1. Her reputation is the community’s verdict, which frames the scene as honorable. Reformed readers see God’s grace making a Moabite foreigner a model of covenant virtue among his people.

Ruth 3:12

12Now it is true that I am a near kinsman. However, there is a kinsman nearer than I.

Arminian

Boaz is honest about a complication: a nearer kinsman has the prior right. He will not bypass the proper order to get what he wants. His integrity shows in restraint as much as in generosity.

Reformed

Boaz respects the claim of the nearer relative instead of seizing the opportunity. Reformed readers note how righteousness here means doing things in the right order before God and the community. Both traditions see his scruple as part of the book’s portrait of covenant faithfulness.

Ruth 3:13

13Stay this night, and in the morning, if he will perform for you the part of a kinsman, good. Let him do the kinsman’s duty. But if he will not do the duty of a kinsman for you, then I will do the duty of a kinsman for you, as the LORD lives. Lie down until the morning.”

Arminian

Boaz binds himself with an oath, “as the LORD lives,” to redeem if the nearer kinsman declines, and asks Ruth to stay until morning, when travel would be safer. The matter will be settled openly at the gate (ch. 4). Arminian readers see sincere promise-keeping and a free decision that will shape the family’s future.

Reformed

The oath seals Boaz’s commitment before God, and the legal process will be completed publicly. Ruth remains until morning for her protection, not for any hidden purpose; the text states the arrangement plainly. Reformed readers see the Lord’s providence moving through lawful channels toward the line of David (4:17).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
