Passage

Ruth 1

Verses 6–18 · WEB

Hearing that the LORD has given his people bread, Naomi sets out for Judah and urges her widowed daughters-in-law to go home, praying for the LORD’s kindness to them. Orpah returns, while Ruth binds herself by oath to Naomi, her people, and her God. Hesed, steadfast loyal love, is shared ground for both traditions: Ruth’s choice is real and freely made, and both see God’s kindness reaching a Moabite woman who takes the LORD as her God.

Ruth 1:6

6Then she arose with her daughters-in-law, that she might return from the country of Moab; for she had heard in the country of Moab how the LORD had visited his people in giving them bread.

Arminian

Naomi decides to return after hearing that the LORD has visited his people with bread. Arminian readers see good news prompting a real decision to go home. God’s provision for Israel and Naomi’s response belong together, with grace opening the way and Naomi choosing to walk in it, showing the pattern of initiative and response the book repeatedly displays.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the first explicit mention of God’s action: he has visited his people by giving bread. The return is set in motion by divine provision, and Naomi’s decision responds to what God has done. Both traditions see God’s care for his people and human response working together here, not competing as explanations for her journey.

Ruth 1:7

7She went out of the place where she was, and her two daughters-in-law with her. They went on the way to return to the land of Judah.

Arminian

Naomi sets out with both daughters-in-law on the road to Judah. Arminian readers see the younger women initially accompanying her out of loyalty and affection. Their departure from Moab is a real step, though the coming conversation will show that each must still make her own decision about where her future lies.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the journey beginning together before it divides at the decisive conversation that follows. The verse carefully notes the movement from the place where they lived toward Judah. Return language runs through the chapter and will take on deeper meaning as Ruth commits herself to Naomi’s people and Naomi’s God.

Ruth 1:8

8Naomi said to her two daughters-in-law, “Go, return each of you to her mother’s house. May the LORD deal kindly with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me.

Arminian

Naomi urges her daughters-in-law to return home and prays for the LORD to show them kindness, as they have shown kindness to the dead and to her. Arminian readers see hesed, steadfast covenant loyalty, praised as a real virtue the women have practiced. Naomi also hopes God will answer their kindness with his own, a theme shared by both traditions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the first appearance of hesed, the loyal love that runs through the whole book. Naomi asks the LORD to deal kindly with Moabite women, assuming his kindness can reach beyond Israel. Both traditions see human loyalty mirroring God’s faithfulness here, not earning it; Naomi’s prayer looks to God as the true source of the kindness she hopes for.

Ruth 1:9

9May the LORD grant you that you may find rest, each of you in the house of her husband.” Then she kissed them, and they lifted up their voices, and wept.

Arminian

Naomi prays that each woman will find rest in a husband’s house, and they weep together. Arminian readers see sincere love wishing security for others even at personal cost. Her blessing seeks their good rather than her own comfort, and the tears show the depth of the relationships formed during the years in Moab.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Naomi looking to God to grant rest and security to her daughters-in-law. Rest here means a settled home and provision, an important hope for widows in this setting. The emotional farewell shows genuine love, and both traditions recognize that the book treats these family bonds as a real good, not a sentimental aside.

Ruth 1:10

10They said to her, “No, but we will return with you to your people.”

Arminian

The daughters-in-law insist they will return with Naomi to her people. Arminian readers see loyalty expressed in a willing choice made under emotional pressure. Both women speak the same words here, which prepares for the contrast that follows when Naomi presses them further and their choices separate.

Reformed

Reformed readers see both women first declaring their intention to stay with Naomi. The scene builds tension, as Naomi will test their resolve with blunt arguments. Their initial unity highlights how significant Ruth’s later persistence becomes, while the narrative remains fair to Orpah’s affection as well.

Ruth 1:11

11Naomi said, “Go back, my daughters. Why do you want to go with me? Do I still have sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands?

Arminian

Naomi argues that she has no more sons to offer as husbands. Arminian readers see a mother-in-law reasoning honestly about their prospects and seeking their welfare. She does not manipulate them toward staying with her; she lays out the realities of their situation so that their choice can be a clear one.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Naomi’s argument rooted in the practical expectations of family and marriage in her world. She sees no human way forward for the younger women if they come with her. Her hopelessness about future provision sets up the surprise of the later chapters, where provision comes through a relative and divine kindness.

Ruth 1:12

12Go back, my daughters, go your way; for I am too old to have a husband. If I should say, ‘I have hope,’ if I should even have a husband tonight, and should also bear sons,

Arminian

Naomi presses the point that even if she remarried and bore sons, the women could not wait for them. Arminian readers see her insisting on realistic hope rather than wishful thinking. Her honesty about her age and limits is part of her love, freeing the younger women to choose a future without false promises.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Naomi’s sense that her own hope is exhausted. The hypothetical scenario underlines the impossibility of the situation on human terms. Both traditions note that the story will show God’s provision arriving through means Naomi cannot foresee, without suggesting her realism was itself sinful.

Ruth 1:13

13would you then wait until they were grown? Would you then refrain from having husbands? No, my daughters, for it grieves me seriously for your sakes, for the LORD’s hand has gone out against me.”

Arminian

Naomi says her grief is great for their sakes because the LORD’s hand has gone out against her. Arminian readers hear honest lament that interprets suffering in relation to God, while not presenting her words as the narrator’s final verdict. The book will later show God’s kindness toward her, so this lament is a real but partial perspective.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Naomi acknowledging God’s sovereign hand in her suffering, even though she experiences it as bitter. Scripture allows such laments to be voiced honestly before God. Both traditions treat her words as faithful complaint rather than unbelief, recognizing that she still speaks of the LORD as the one who governs her life.

Ruth 1:14

14They lifted up their voices and wept again; then Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth stayed with her.

Arminian

They weep again, Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye, and Ruth clings to her. Arminian readers see two women making different free decisions in the same circumstances. Orpah is not condemned by the narrator; her return is reasonable and follows Naomi’s urging. Ruth’s clinging marks a costly loyalty that goes beyond what was expected of her.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the decisive parting of the ways. The narrator records Orpah’s return without blame and highlights Ruth’s clinging, a word that suggests deep commitment. Both traditions agree the text contrasts the two choices without turning Orpah into a villain; the focus is on the extraordinary loyalty Ruth shows.

Ruth 1:15

15She said, “Behold, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her god. Follow your sister-in-law.”

Arminian

Naomi urges Ruth to follow Orpah back to her people and her god. Arminian readers see Naomi giving Ruth a final, clear opportunity to turn back. The mention of Orpah’s god shows that returning to Moab also meant returning to its worship, so Ruth’s coming decision involves religious allegiance as well as family loyalty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the issue of worship made explicit: returning to Moab means returning to its god. Naomi’s urging tests Ruth’s resolve one last time. Both traditions read Ruth’s answer in the next verses as a commitment to the God of Israel, not merely a personal attachment to Naomi.

Ruth 1:16

16Ruth said, “Don’t urge me to leave you, and to return from following you, for where you go, I will go; and where you stay, I will stay. Your people will be my people, and your God my God.

Arminian

Ruth refuses to leave and binds herself to Naomi’s path, people, and God. Arminian readers see a free and wholehearted commitment, a choice of faith and loyalty made against the advice offered to her. Her words show hesed in action and a turning toward the God of Israel that the rest of the book honors.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Ruth embracing Naomi’s God as her own, which they understand as the fruit of God’s grace at work in her. Her confession of loyalty to Israel’s God is the turning point of the chapter. Both traditions affirm the reality of Ruth’s choice and God’s kindness in bringing a Moabite woman into covenant faith.

Ruth 1:17

17Where you die, I will die, and there I will be buried. May the LORD do so to me, and more also, if anything but death parts you and me.”

Arminian

Ruth extends her commitment to death and burial and seals it with an oath invoking the LORD. Arminian readers see a solemn, freely spoken vow showing that her faith is personal and lasting. The oath in the LORD’s name confirms that her loyalty to Naomi is also loyalty to Israel’s God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Ruth calling on the LORD by his covenant name, showing that she now counts herself under his authority. Her vow binds her to Naomi for life. Both traditions see her words as a model of loyal love, grounded in a new allegiance to the God of Israel rather than mere sentiment.

Ruth 1:18

18When Naomi saw that she was determined to go with her, she stopped urging her.

Arminian

Naomi sees Ruth’s determination and stops urging her. Arminian readers see Naomi respecting a choice Ruth has made firmly and freely. The silence that follows is notable: Naomi does not yet rejoice in Ruth’s loyalty, which fits her grief and prepares for her bitter words on arrival in Bethlehem.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Ruth’s resolve settled beyond further argument. Naomi’s silence may reflect exhaustion or grief rather than rejection, since the text does not explain it. Both traditions recognize that the story now moves forward with these two widows together, and that Ruth’s steadfastness will become the means of Naomi’s restoration.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
