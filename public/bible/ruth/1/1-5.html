Passage

Ruth 1

Verses 1–5 · WEB

Ruth opens in the days of the judges with famine in Bethlehem. Elimelech’s family moves to Moab, where the sons marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth. Within about ten years Elimelech and both sons die, leaving Naomi bereaved and without heirs. Arminian and Reformed readers share the narrator’s restraint here: hardship is recorded without an explanation, and the story prepares for a reversal that will come through covenant kindness.

Ruth 1:1

1In the days when the judges judged, there was a famine in the land. A certain man of Bethlehem Judah went to live in the country of Moab with his wife and his two sons.

Arminian

The story opens in the era of the judges, a period remembered in Israel’s narrative for instability, with famine in the land. Arminian readers see a family making a hard, real decision under pressure, and they notice that the narrator reports it without immediate praise or blame. The move to Moab sets the stage for loss, return, and an unexpected story of covenant kindness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the famine and the journey set within the larger story of God’s dealings with his people, even before God is named as acting. The narrator records the choice plainly and does not stop to judge it. Readers in both traditions find here the opening of a story in which ordinary hardship becomes the setting for God’s faithful care.

Ruth 1:2

2The name of the man was Elimelech, and the name of his wife Naomi. The names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion, Ephrathites of Bethlehem Judah. They came into the country of Moab and lived there.

Arminian

The family is named and placed in Bethlehem of Judah, while the move to Moab is described as a settled residence. Arminian readers note that the names make this a story about particular people rather than types. Leaving the promised land in a time of famine is real human agency with real consequences, which the following verses will make painfully clear.

Reformed

Reformed readers see careful attention to family and place, since lineage and land matter greatly by the end of the book. Bethlehem and the Ephrathite identity quietly connect this household to Israel’s story. The narrator does not yet explain what God is doing, but the details prepare for the redemption and genealogy that close the book.

Ruth 1:3

3Elimelech, Naomi’s husband, died; and she was left with her two sons.

Arminian

Elimelech dies, and Naomi is left with her sons in a foreign land. Arminian readers see grief entering the story without any stated explanation, and they resist assigning a hidden sin as its cause when the text does not. Naomi’s vulnerability as a widow becomes the human center of the narrative from this point onward.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the first of several losses that will bring Naomi to the end of her own resources. The text gives no reason for the death, and Reformed readers also avoid supplying one. Later Naomi will speak of God’s hand in her suffering, but here the narrator simply records the sorrow and its effect on her household.

Ruth 1:4

4They took for themselves wives of the women of Moab. The name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other was Ruth. They lived there about ten years.

Arminian

The sons marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth, and the family remains about ten years. Arminian readers see the household putting down roots in Moab and forming real bonds of kinship. Ruth is introduced quietly, with no hint yet of her coming importance, showing how the narrative lets character emerge through later choices and actions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the introduction of Ruth, a Moabite woman who will become part of Israel’s story and of David’s line. Her outsider status is stated plainly and will be noted repeatedly. Both traditions recognize that the book presents her inclusion as an act of loyalty and divine kindness, without needing to explain away her origins.

Ruth 1:5

5Mahlon and Chilion both died, and the woman was bereaved of her two children and of her husband.

Arminian

Both sons die, and Naomi is left without husband or children. Arminian readers see the full weight of her bereavement and the social danger it brings to a widow without heirs. The narrative refuses to rush past grief, and readers in both traditions can take her loss seriously before the story moves toward restoration.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Naomi brought to an emptiness that she will later describe in her own words. The verse marks the low point from which the story of provision and redemption begins. God has not yet been named as acting, yet the narrative is preparing for a reversal that comes through human faithfulness and divine kindness together.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
