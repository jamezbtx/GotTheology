Passage

Ruth 1

Verses 19–22 · WEB

Naomi and Ruth arrive in Bethlehem, and the town is stirred. Naomi asks to be called Mara, bitter, saying she went out full and the LORD has brought her back empty. Both traditions read her words as honest lament that still acknowledges God’s rule, while the narrator quietly notes Ruth beside her and the start of barley harvest, hints that emptiness is not the end of the story.

Ruth 1:19

19So they both went until they came to Bethlehem. When they had come to Bethlehem, all the city was excited about them, and they asked, “Is this Naomi?”

Arminian

Naomi and Ruth reach Bethlehem, and the whole town is stirred, asking whether this is Naomi. Arminian readers see a homecoming marked by surprise and perhaps shock at how she has changed. The return is real, but it does not yet feel like restoration, and the community’s question opens the way for Naomi’s lament.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the return to Bethlehem, in the land where the LORD has again given bread, after years of loss. The town’s reaction highlights the change in Naomi’s condition. Both traditions see this as the end of the outward journey but not yet the end of Naomi’s inward one, which the story will continue through provision and kindness.

Ruth 1:20

20She said to them, “Don’t call me Naomi. Call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me.

Arminian

Naomi asks to be called Mara, bitter, because the Almighty has dealt bitterly with her. Arminian readers hear raw lament spoken honestly before God and the community. The WEB notes that Naomi means pleasant and Mara means bitter. Her words express how she experiences her losses, which the narrative will later answer with unexpected kindness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Naomi attributing her suffering to the Almighty, acknowledging God’s rule even when it is painful. The name change captures her sense of loss. Both traditions see this as honest lament rather than rebellion, and the story allows her bitterness to stand before showing God’s faithful provision through Ruth and Boaz.

Ruth 1:21

21I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty. Why do you call me Naomi, since the LORD has testified against me, and the Almighty has afflicted me?”

Arminian

Naomi says she went out full and the LORD brought her back empty. Arminian readers see grief shaping her perspective so strongly that she overlooks the loyal daughter-in-law beside her. The narrative gently invites readers to notice that Ruth’s presence already contradicts the claim of complete emptiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Naomi confessing that the LORD is sovereign over both fullness and emptiness. Her interpretation of events is honest but incomplete, as Ruth stands beside her as a sign of God’s continuing care. Both traditions read the lament as real faith under strain, awaiting a fuller view of God’s kindness.

Ruth 1:22

22So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess, her daughter-in-law, with her, who returned out of the country of Moab. They came to Bethlehem in the beginning of barley harvest.

Arminian

The chapter closes with Naomi and Ruth the Moabitess arriving in Bethlehem at the start of barley harvest. Arminian readers see hope quietly introduced through the harvest timing. Ruth’s foreign origin is named again, emphasizing her loyalty as an outsider who has chosen to come with Naomi to a new people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the barley harvest as a hopeful note after a chapter of loss, suggesting provision is near. The narrator again highlights Ruth’s Moabite identity. Both traditions see the timing as part of the story’s design, preparing for Ruth’s gleaning and her encounter with Boaz in the next chapter.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
