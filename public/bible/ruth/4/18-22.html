Passage

Ruth 4

Verses 18–22 · WEB

The book closes with a ten-name genealogy from Perez, son of Judah, to David. It ties the story of Ruth and Boaz into Israel’s larger history and matches the line in 1 Chronicles 2:5–15. Many interpreters think the list is selective rather than complete, since it covers a long span with few generations. It ends with David, pointing toward the kingship, and Matthew picks up the same names on the way to Christ (Matt 1:3–6).

Ruth 4:18

18Now this is the history of the generations of Perez: Perez became the father of Hezron,

Arminian

“The generations of” is a heading used throughout Genesis, and the list begins with Perez, the son named in the blessing of 4:12. The story of one family is placed within the history of God’s promises to Judah (Gen 49:10). Arminian readers see God’s faithfulness carried through many generations of human faith and failure.

Reformed

“The generations of” is a heading used throughout Genesis, and the list begins with Perez, the son named in the blessing of 4:12. The story of one family is placed within the history of God’s promises to Judah (Gen 49:10). Reformed readers see the covenant line kept by God’s steady faithfulness through many generations.

Ruth 4:19

19and Hezron became the father of Ram, and Ram became the father of Amminadab,

Arminian

Hezron went down to Egypt with Jacob’s family (Gen 46:12), and Ram and Amminadab follow. Little is told of these men; the list values faithfulness that passes the promise on even when it goes unrecorded.

Reformed

Hezron went down to Egypt with Jacob’s family (Gen 46:12), and Ram and Amminadab follow. Reformed readers note that God keeps his promise through generations that Scripture barely records; the covenant line does not depend on fame.

Ruth 4:20

20and Amminadab became the father of Nahshon, and Nahshon became the father of Salmon,

Arminian

Nahshon was the leader of Judah in the wilderness (Num 1:7; 2:3), and Amminadab’s daughter married Aaron (Exod 6:23). Salmon is the father of Boaz. The names connect Ruth’s story to the exodus and the wilderness generation.

Reformed

Nahshon was the leader of Judah in the wilderness (Num 1:7; 2:3), and Amminadab’s daughter married Aaron (Exod 6:23). Salmon is the father of Boaz. Reformed readers see the line linking exodus redemption to the coming king.

Ruth 4:21

21and Salmon became the father of Boaz, and Boaz became the father of Obed,

Arminian

Boaz and Obed appear in their place in the line. The faithful love shown in this book is now part of Israel’s official history. Matthew adds that Salmon’s wife was Rahab (Matt 1:5), so this short stretch includes two outsiders received by faith.

Reformed

Boaz and Obed appear in their place in the line. The faithful love shown in this book is now part of Israel’s official history. Matthew adds that Salmon’s wife was Rahab (Matt 1:5), and Reformed readers, with Arminians, see God’s grace bringing outsiders into the line of the Messiah.

Ruth 4:22

22and Obed became the father of Jesse, and Jesse became the father of David.

Arminian

The list ends at David, Israel’s king after God’s heart. The book that began with famine and death in the time of the judges (1:1) ends with the coming king. Both traditions read this as a step toward the Son of David, Christ the greater Redeemer.

Reformed

The list ends at David, Israel’s king after God’s heart. The book that began with famine and death in the time of the judges (1:1) ends with the coming king. Reformed and Arminian readers alike see the line moving toward Christ, the Son of David and the greater Redeemer.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
