Passage

Ruth 4

Verses 13–17 · WEB

The story resolves quickly. Boaz marries Ruth, the LORD enables her to conceive, and a son is born. The women of Bethlehem, who heard Naomi’s bitter lament in chapter 1, now bless the LORD for giving her a redeemer and a restorer of life. They praise Ruth as better than seven sons. Naomi nurses the child, the neighbors name him Obed, and the narrator links him to Jesse and David.

Ruth 4:13

13So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and he went in to her, and the LORD enabled her to conceive, and she bore a son.

Arminian

Marriage, union, conception, and birth come in a single sentence, and the narrator credits the conception to the LORD. Ruth had borne no child during her marriage in Moab (1:4–5). Arminian readers affirm God’s direct gift of life and his providence over the whole story. They hold that he worked through the free and faithful choices of Ruth and Boaz, which he enabled but did not determine.

Reformed

Marriage, union, conception, and birth come in a single sentence, and the narrator credits the conception to the LORD. Ruth had borne no child during her marriage in Moab (1:4–5). Reformed readers see the LORD’s sovereign hand over life and birth (compare Ps 127:3), and through it his ordering of every turn in the book. Ruth and Boaz still act freely and responsibly within that ordering.

Key contrast Both traditions confess that the LORD gave this conception, and that the whole story moved under his providence through real human choices. They differ on how God’s governance relates to those choices. Arminian readers typically hold that God rules and guides the free decisions of Ruth, Boaz, and the nearer kinsman without determining each one, working with human responses that he foreknows. Reformed readers typically hold that God’s providence ordains and governs all events, including those decisions, while the people act willingly and responsibly as true second causes.

Ruth 4:14

14The women said to Naomi, “Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you today without a near kinsman. Let his name be famous in Israel.

Arminian

The women bless the LORD, who has not left Naomi without a redeemer. Most interpreters take the redeemer here to be the child, who will secure the family’s future, though some think of Boaz. Naomi’s complaint that the LORD had dealt bitterly with her (1:20–21) is answered with praise, with no rebuke of her honest lament.

Reformed

The women bless the LORD, who has not left Naomi without a redeemer. Most interpreters take the redeemer here to be the child, who will secure the family’s future, though some think of Boaz. Reformed readers see God’s purpose ripening: the hand that seemed to be against Naomi (1:13) was working for her good all along (compare Rom 8:28).

Ruth 4:15

15He shall be to you a restorer of life and sustain you in your old age; for your daughter-in-law, who loves you, who is better to you than seven sons, has given birth to him.”

Arminian

The child will restore Naomi’s life and support her in old age. Ruth’s steady love is the human center of the story, and the women name it openly: she is better than seven sons, a number that suggests completeness. Arminian readers emphasize the free and costly love that grace produced in this Moabite convert.

Reformed

The child will restore Naomi’s life and support her in old age. Ruth’s steady love is the human center of the story, and the women name it openly: she is better than seven sons, a number that suggests completeness. Reformed readers see Ruth’s loyalty as the fruit of God’s grace in a convert who chose the LORD as her God (1:16).

Ruth 4:16

16Naomi took the child, laid him in her bosom, and became nurse to him.

Arminian

Naomi takes the child to her bosom and cares for him. The woman who came back empty (1:21) now holds the family’s future. This is likely a gesture of nurture and family belonging, and possibly of formal adoption, though the text does not say so explicitly.

Reformed

Naomi takes the child to her bosom and cares for him. The woman who came back empty (1:21) now holds the family’s future. Reformed readers note that God turns mourning into joy, and that the reversal comes through an ordinary household and the care of a grandmother.

Ruth 4:17

17The women, her neighbors, gave him a name, saying, “A son is born to Naomi”. They named him Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David.

Arminian

The neighbors name the child Obed, meaning “servant” or “worker.” Their words, “A son is born to Naomi,” show the family line restored. The narrator then names Jesse and David, revealing that this small family story belongs to Israel’s royal history. Both traditions see in a Moabite woman joining the messianic line a sign of God’s purpose for all nations, which Matthew’s genealogy highlights (Matt 1:5).

Reformed

The neighbors name the child Obed, meaning “servant” or “worker.” Their words, “A son is born to Naomi,” show the family line restored. The closing link to Jesse and David reveals that God was preparing Israel’s king through the hidden faithfulness of ordinary people. Reformed and Arminian readers share the christological reading: a Moabite woman stands in the line of David and of Christ (Matt 1:5).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
