Passage

Ruth 4

Verses 1–12 · WEB

The morning after the threshing floor, Boaz settles the matter in public. At Bethlehem’s gate, where elders heard legal business, he calls the nearer kinsman and ten elders as witnesses. He offers Elimelech’s land for redemption, then adds that the redeemer must also take Ruth the Moabitess to raise up the name of the dead. The kinsman declines, and a sandal is handed over to confirm the transfer. Boaz acquires the land and Ruth before witnesses, and the people bless the marriage by recalling Rachel, Leah, Tamar, and Perez.

Ruth 4:1

1Now Boaz went up to the gate and sat down there. Behold, the near kinsman of whom Boaz spoke came by. Boaz said to him, “Come over here, friend, and sit down!” He came over, and sat down.

Arminian

The gate was the town’s court and marketplace, so Boaz takes Ruth’s request where it can be settled openly and lawfully. The text says the kinsman simply “came by,” in plain narrative, much as Ruth “happened” on Boaz’s field in 2:3. Arminian readers see God’s care at work through ordinary timing and the honest steps Boaz chooses to take, without the narrator needing to point to a miracle.

Reformed

The gate was the town’s court and marketplace, so Boaz takes Ruth’s request where it can be settled openly and lawfully. The kinsman “came by,” and Reformed readers note the same quiet providence as Ruth’s arrival in 2:3: God orders the ordinary timing of events. Boaz still acts with prudence and diligence, since providence works through means, not around them.

Ruth 4:2

2Boaz took ten men of the elders of the city, and said, “Sit down here,” and they sat down.

Arminian

Ten elders make a formal quorum of witnesses. Boaz does not use private influence or a secret deal; he binds himself to public law and accountability. Faithful love (hesed) here takes the shape of justice done openly before the community.

Reformed

Ten elders make a formal quorum of witnesses. Boaz honors the lawful order God gave Israel and sets the matter where it can be tested and confirmed. Reformed readers see in this a picture of godliness that keeps God’s ordinances in civil life as well as in worship.

Ruth 4:3

3He said to the near kinsman, “Naomi, who has come back out of the country of Moab, is selling the parcel of land, which was our brother Elimelech’s.

Arminian

Boaz states the case: Elimelech’s land is to be sold, with Naomi acting for the family. The law provided that a near relative could redeem a poor kinsman’s land so it stayed in the clan (Lev 25:25). Naomi, who came home “empty” (1:21), still holds a claim that the law protects.

Reformed

Boaz states the case: Elimelech’s land is to be sold, with Naomi acting for the family. The law of redemption kept a family’s inheritance in Israel (Lev 25:25), reflecting that the land belonged to the LORD and was held as his gift. Naomi’s claim is weak in human terms but guarded by God’s law.

Ruth 4:4

4I thought I should tell you, saying, ‘Buy it before those who sit here, and before the elders of my people.’ If you will redeem it, redeem it; but if you will not redeem it, then tell me, that I may know. For there is no one to redeem it besides you; and I am after you.” He said, “I will redeem it.”

Arminian

Boaz offers the nearer kinsman his rightful first option and keeps the order of precedence, even though he wants the outcome for himself. The man agrees at once, since land is an asset. Integrity means Boaz will not grasp what is not yet his, and he trusts the right outcome to an honest process.

Reformed

Boaz offers the nearer kinsman his rightful first option and keeps the order of precedence, even though he wants the outcome for himself. The man agrees at once, since land is an asset. Reformed readers note that Boaz pursues a lawful end only by lawful means and leaves the result with God, without manipulating it.

Ruth 4:5

5Then Boaz said, “On the day you buy the field from the hand of Naomi, you must buy it also from Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance.”

Arminian

Boaz adds the second part of the obligation: whoever acquires the field also takes Ruth, so that a son might carry on the dead man’s name and inheritance. This joins land redemption to a levirate-like duty (compare Deut 25:5–6). Arminian and Reformed interpreters alike see the redeemer’s role as personal, not merely financial: he takes on the cost of restoring a family.

Reformed

Boaz adds the second part of the obligation: whoever acquires the field also takes Ruth, so that a son might carry on the dead man’s name and inheritance. This joins land redemption to a levirate-like duty (compare Deut 25:5–6). Reformed and Arminian interpreters alike note that the redeemer is a kinsman who takes on the cost of restoring a family, a pattern commonly read as pointing to Christ.

Ruth 4:6

6The near kinsman said, “I can’t redeem it for myself, lest I endanger my own inheritance. Take my right of redemption for yourself; for I can’t redeem it.”

Arminian

The kinsman withdraws, since a son born to Ruth would inherit the field and his own estate would bear the cost. He is not condemned for breaking the law; he simply will not pay the price. The contrast highlights Boaz’s costly loyalty, and many Christian readers draw the shared typology that true redemption costs the redeemer.

Reformed

The kinsman withdraws, since a son born to Ruth would inherit the field and his own estate would bear the cost. He is not condemned for breaking the law; he simply will not pay the price. Reformed readers share the common typology that the one who redeems must be both near of kin and willing to bear the cost, which Christ fulfills.

Ruth 4:7

7Now this was the custom in former time in Israel concerning redeeming and concerning exchanging, to confirm all things: a man took off his sandal, and gave it to his neighbor; and this was the way of formalizing transactions in Israel.

Arminian

The narrator explains an older custom for people who no longer know it: handing over a sandal sealed a transfer of rights, perhaps symbolizing the right to walk on the land. It differs from the shaming ritual of Deut 25:9, since here the man removes his own sandal and no disgrace is mentioned. The note implies the story was written down some time after these events.

Reformed

The narrator explains an older custom for people who no longer know it: handing over a sandal sealed a transfer of rights, perhaps symbolizing the right to walk on the land. It differs from the shaming ritual of Deut 25:9, since here the man removes his own sandal and no disgrace is mentioned. The aside shows the book’s care to record real history in its own setting.

Ruth 4:8

8So the near kinsman said to Boaz, “Buy it for yourself,” then he took off his sandal.

Arminian

The transfer is completed by word and sign before witnesses. The nearer kinsman stays unnamed, which leaves him in obscurity while the man who acted in costly faithfulness is remembered. The story values faithful choices that build up others.

Reformed

The transfer is completed by word and sign before witnesses. The nearer kinsman stays unnamed, which leaves him in obscurity, while Boaz, who keeps faith with the dead and the living, is remembered. Reformed readers see God honoring those who honor him (1 Sam 2:30).

Ruth 4:9

9Boaz said to the elders and to all the people, “You are witnesses today, that I have bought all that was Elimelech’s, and all that was Chilion’s and Mahlon’s, from the hand of Naomi.

Arminian

Boaz formally names what he acquires: everything that belonged to Elimelech and his sons. The public declaration fixes the legal fact. The family’s loss in chapter 1 (land, husband, sons) is now being reversed through lawful redemption.

Reformed

Boaz formally names what he acquires: everything that belonged to Elimelech and his sons. The public declaration fixes the legal fact. Reformed readers note that the God who emptied Naomi (1:21) now restores her house by lawful means.

Ruth 4:10

10Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of Mahlon, I have purchased to be my wife, to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance, that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brothers and from the gate of his place. You are witnesses today.”

Arminian

Boaz states his purpose: to take Ruth as his wife to preserve Mahlon’s name in Israel and at the town gate. A Moabite widow is received into Israel by covenant marriage, and she is valued as a person, not only a legal means. Both traditions see here God’s welcome of outsiders who take refuge under his wings (2:12).

Reformed

Boaz states his purpose: to take Ruth as his wife to preserve Mahlon’s name in Israel and at the town gate. Ruth, a Moabite, is fully joined to Israel. Reformed readers, like Arminians, see a foretaste of the gospel’s ingathering of the nations into God’s people (compare Eph 2:12–13).

Ruth 4:11

11All the people who were in the gate, and the elders, said, “We are witnesses. May the LORD make the woman who has come into your house like Rachel and like Leah, which both built the house of Israel; and treat you worthily in Ephrathah, and be famous in Bethlehem.

Arminian

The people and elders accept the role of witnesses and pray that Ruth will be like Rachel and Leah, the matriarchs who built Israel’s house (Gen 29–30). Ephrathah is the clan name tied to Bethlehem (compare Mic 5:2). The community’s blessing is a prayer for God’s action, and the people speak it in faith.

Reformed

The people and elders accept the role of witnesses and pray that Ruth will be like Rachel and Leah, the matriarchs who built Israel’s house (Gen 29–30). Ephrathah is the clan name tied to Bethlehem (compare Mic 5:2). Reformed readers note that the blessing treats building a house as God’s work, and that God answers it beyond what the speakers knew.

Ruth 4:12

12Let your house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah, of the offspring which the LORD will give you by this young woman.”

Arminian

The blessing recalls Perez, son of Judah and Tamar (Gen 38), another irregular family line that God continued through a woman who sought the rights of the dead. The offspring is expected as something “the LORD will give.” Both traditions treat this line as the path to David and to Christ.

Reformed

The blessing recalls Perez, son of Judah and Tamar (Gen 38), another irregular family line that God continued through a woman who sought the rights of the dead. The offspring is expected as something “the LORD will give.” Reformed and Arminian readers alike follow this line toward David and the promised Son (Matt 1:3–5).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
