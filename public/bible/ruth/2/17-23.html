Passage

Ruth 2

Verses 17–23 · WEB

Ruth returns with an abundant harvest and shares it with Naomi. Learning that Boaz is a close relative, Naomi blesses the LORD, who has not abandoned his kindness to the living and the dead. Ruth continues gleaning through the barley and wheat harvests. Both traditions see God’s faithful hesed at work through human loyalty, diligence, and generosity, as Naomi’s bitterness begins to give way to hope.

Ruth 2:17

17So she gleaned in the field until evening; and she beat out that which she had gleaned, and it was about an ephah of barley.

Arminian

Ruth gleans until evening and beats out about an ephah of barley. Arminian readers see diligent work rewarded with a generous result. The amount shows that Boaz’s kindness and Ruth’s labor together produced abundant provision. The WEB notes that an ephah is about 22 liters, roughly two-thirds of a bushel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the remarkable quantity as evidence of Boaz’s generosity and God’s provision through ordinary means. Ruth’s labor is real, and the outcome exceeds what gleaning normally might yield. Both traditions see human diligence and generous kindness working together here as the means of provision.

Ruth 2:18

18She took it up, and went into the city. Then her mother-in-law saw what she had gleaned; and she brought out and gave to her that which she had left after she had enough.

Arminian

Ruth brings the grain home and shares her leftover food with Naomi. Arminian readers see loyal love expressed in sharing her provision. The scene shows the relationship of mutual care between the two widows, and Ruth’s generosity continues the pattern of hesed she has shown since the first chapter.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the provision received in the field now shared at home, extending the blessing to Naomi. The leftover food from the meal also shows abundance. Both traditions see Ruth’s care for Naomi as genuine loyalty, quietly beginning the reversal of Naomi’s earlier claim of emptiness.

Ruth 2:19

19Her mother-in-law said to her, “Where have you gleaned today? Where have you worked? Blessed be he who noticed you.” She told her mother-in-law with whom she had worked, “The man’s name with whom I worked today is Boaz.”

Arminian

Naomi asks where Ruth gleaned and blesses the one who noticed her, and Ruth names Boaz. Arminian readers see Naomi’s interest and hope reawakening as she sees the abundance. Her blessing on the unknown benefactor shows a turn from bitterness toward gratitude, which grows when she learns his name.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the moment of revelation, as Naomi learns that Ruth worked in Boaz’s field. Her blessing anticipates her recognition of God’s kindness. Both traditions see this as a turning point in Naomi’s outlook, prepared by the narrator’s earlier introduction of Boaz.

Ruth 2:20

20Naomi said to her daughter-in-law, “May he be blessed by the LORD, who has not abandoned his kindness to the living and to the dead.” Naomi said to her, “The man is a close relative to us, one of our near kinsmen.”

Arminian

Naomi blesses Boaz by the LORD, who has not abandoned his kindness to the living and the dead, and identifies Boaz as a close relative. Arminian readers see Naomi recognizing God’s faithfulness at work in these events. Her outlook moves from bitterness to hope as she sees kindness extending to her family.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Naomi acknowledging God’s unfailing hesed toward her family, living and dead. The identification of Boaz as a near kinsman introduces the possibility of redemption. Both traditions see this verse as central to the book’s theme of God’s faithful kindness working through human loyalty and family obligation.

Ruth 2:21

21Ruth the Moabitess said, “Yes, he said to me, ‘You shall stay close to my young men until they have finished all my harvest.’ ”

Arminian

Ruth reports that Boaz told her to stay close to his young men until the harvest is finished. Arminian readers see continuing provision and protection promised for the whole season. Ruth’s report shows her trust in Boaz’s word and the security it offers her and Naomi.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Boaz’s commitment extending through the entire harvest, providing ongoing security. The narrator again calls Ruth the Moabitess, keeping her outsider status in view. Both traditions see this as a picture of sustained kindness and provision rather than a single act of generosity.

Ruth 2:22

22Naomi said to Ruth her daughter-in-law, “It is good, my daughter, that you go out with his maidens, and that they not harm you in any other field.”

Arminian

Naomi encourages Ruth to stay with Boaz’s young women, where she will be safe from harm. Arminian readers see wise counsel attentive to Ruth’s safety and wellbeing. Naomi’s concern shows renewed engagement and care, reflecting how her hope has begun to return.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Naomi recognizing the protection available in Boaz’s field. Her advice reflects awareness of real dangers facing a foreign widow. Both traditions see Naomi’s counsel as an expression of love and practical wisdom, as she now takes an active role in seeking Ruth’s good.

Ruth 2:23

23So she stayed close to the maidens of Boaz, to glean to the end of barley harvest and of wheat harvest; and she lived with her mother-in-law.

Arminian

Ruth gleans with Boaz’s young women through the barley and wheat harvests while living with Naomi. Arminian readers see faithful persistence and continuing loyalty to her mother-in-law. The chapter closes with provision secured for the season, though the deeper question of their future remains open.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a season of steady provision under Boaz’s protection. The mention of both harvests marks the passage of time and prepares for the next stage of the story. Both traditions see the faithful daily work and God’s quiet provision as the setting for the redemption still to come.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
