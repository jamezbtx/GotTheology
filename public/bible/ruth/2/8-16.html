Passage

Ruth 2

Verses 8–16 · WEB

Boaz invites Ruth to stay in his field, protects her, and shares water and food with her. When she asks why a foreigner should receive favor, he points to her loyalty to Naomi and prays that the LORD, under whose wings she has taken refuge, will reward her. Both traditions see covenant kindness, generous righteousness toward the vulnerable, and Ruth’s trust in Israel’s God as shared ground in this scene.

Ruth 2:8

8Then Boaz said to Ruth, “Listen, my daughter. Don’t go to glean in another field, and don’t go from here, but stay here close to my maidens.

Arminian

Boaz tells Ruth to stay in his field close to his young women. Arminian readers see kindness and protection offered freely to a vulnerable foreign widow. He goes beyond the minimum requirement of allowing gleaning, inviting her to remain under his care, which reflects the hesed theme running through the book.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Boaz extending generous protection that anticipates his later role as redeemer. His instruction keeps Ruth safe and provided for in a place where she might otherwise be vulnerable. Both traditions see his kindness as a real expression of covenant loyalty and generosity toward the poor and the stranger.

Ruth 2:9

9Let your eyes be on the field that they reap, and go after them. Haven’t I commanded the young men not to touch you? When you are thirsty, go to the vessels, and drink from that which the young men have drawn.”

Arminian

Boaz assures Ruth of protection from harassment and access to water drawn by his workers. Arminian readers see practical care that addresses real dangers and needs. His concern for her safety shows that kindness must take concrete form, especially toward someone with little social protection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Boaz using his authority to protect and provide for Ruth. The command to his young men addresses a real risk, and the offer of water shows hospitality. Both traditions see in Boaz a picture of righteous use of power on behalf of the vulnerable, consistent with Israel’s law.

Ruth 2:10

10Then she fell on her face and bowed herself to the ground, and said to him, “Why have I found favor in your sight, that you should take knowledge of me, since I am a foreigner?”

Arminian

Ruth bows and asks why she has found favor, since she is a foreigner. Arminian readers see humble astonishment at kindness she did not expect. Her question shows awareness of her position and gratitude for grace freely shown, without any sense that she has a claim upon Boaz’s generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Ruth recognizing that she has received favor she could not demand. Her outsider status makes the kindness all the more striking. Both traditions see in this moment a picture of gratitude for undeserved favor, while recognizing the story’s primary focus on human kindness between Boaz and Ruth.

Ruth 2:11

11Boaz answered her, “I have been told all about what you have done for your mother-in-law since the death of your husband, and how you have left your father, your mother, and the land of your birth, and have come to a people that you didn’t know before.

Arminian

Boaz explains that he has heard of Ruth’s loyalty to Naomi and her decision to leave her homeland. Arminian readers see his favor responding to her character and choices, which have become known. Her costly commitment is honored, showing the book’s high regard for loyal love freely given.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Boaz recognizing the faithfulness Ruth has shown, which they understand as fruit of God’s work in her. Her departure from family and land echoes a pattern of leaving the familiar to follow God. Both traditions see her loyalty praised as genuine virtue, and the narrative presents it as a model of hesed.

Ruth 2:12

12May the LORD repay your work, and a full reward be given to you from the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge.”

Arminian

Boaz prays that the LORD will reward Ruth fully, as one who has come to take refuge under his wings. Arminian readers see God honoring faithful trust and loyalty, with Boaz’s blessing pointing beyond himself to God as the one who rewards. Taking refuge under God’s wings expresses Ruth’s trust in Israel’s God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the beautiful image of refuge under God’s wings, describing Ruth’s faith in the LORD. The reward Boaz prays for comes from God’s generosity, not from strict merit. Both traditions affirm that Ruth has sought refuge in the God of Israel and that God delights to bless those who trust him.

Ruth 2:13

13Then she said, “Let me find favor in your sight, my lord, because you have comforted me, and because you have spoken kindly to your servant, though I am not as one of your servants.”

Arminian

Ruth thanks Boaz for comforting her and speaking kindly, though she is not even one of his servants. Arminian readers see humility and gratitude in response to kindness. She acknowledges his generosity without presuming, showing how grace received freely leads to thankful acknowledgment of the giver.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Ruth’s gratitude for kindness extended beyond what her position would suggest. Her words emphasize the undeserved nature of the comfort she has received. Both traditions see her response as a fitting example of humility before generosity, maintaining the human focus of the scene.

Ruth 2:14

14At meal time Boaz said to her, “Come here, and eat some bread, and dip your morsel in the vinegar.” She sat beside the reapers, and they passed her parched grain. She ate, was satisfied, and left some of it.

Arminian

At mealtime Boaz invites Ruth to eat with the reapers, and she eats until satisfied with food left over. Arminian readers see hospitality that includes her in the community’s table. Her leftover portion, which she later shares with Naomi, shows that kindness received becomes kindness given.

Reformed

Reformed readers see abundant provision as Ruth eats until satisfied and has some left. Boaz’s generosity moves from permission to glean toward fellowship at the table. Both traditions see the scene as a picture of provision and inclusion, foreshadowing the fuller provision that will come later in the story.

Ruth 2:15

15When she had risen up to glean, Boaz commanded his young men, saying, “Let her glean even among the sheaves, and don’t reproach her.

Arminian

Boaz instructs his workers to let Ruth glean among the sheaves and not to shame her. Arminian readers see generosity that protects dignity as well as providing food. He allows her more than the usual gleaning, showing that kindness can exceed the minimum the law requires.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Boaz going beyond ordinary gleaning practice, granting Ruth access to the sheaves. His instruction also protects her from humiliation. Both traditions see this as generous righteousness, reflecting God’s heart for the poor and the stranger.

Ruth 2:16

16Also pull out some for her from the bundles, and leave it. Let her glean, and don’t rebuke her.”

Arminian

Boaz tells his workers to pull out grain from the bundles and leave it for Ruth. Arminian readers see quiet, intentional generosity that allows her to gather more without feeling like a recipient of charity. The care for her dignity shows a thoughtful kindness attentive to how help is given.

Reformed

Reformed readers see deliberate and discreet generosity, providing more for Ruth than she could gather alone. His instructions show careful planning for her good. Both traditions see Boaz as a model of covenant kindness that combines practical help with respect for the recipient.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
