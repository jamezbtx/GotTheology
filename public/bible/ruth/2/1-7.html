Passage

Ruth 2

Verses 1–7 · WEB

The narrator introduces Boaz, a wealthy relative of Elimelech. Ruth sets out to glean and happens to come to his field, where he greets his reapers in the LORD’s name and asks about her. Both traditions hold together what the text holds together: Ruth’s real initiative and God’s providence quietly guiding events. The understated wording of verse 3 invites wonder at God’s care rather than a dispute over chance and choice.

Ruth 2:1

1Naomi had a relative of her husband’s, a mighty man of wealth, of the family of Elimelech, and his name was Boaz.

Arminian

The narrator introduces Boaz, a relative of Elimelech and a man of standing, before Ruth meets him. Arminian readers see the story giving readers information the characters do not yet have. Boaz’s family connection and resources will make him a key figure, and his character will soon be shown through how he uses his position.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the narrator quietly placing the right person in view before the encounter takes place. Boaz’s kinship with Elimelech will matter for the redemption theme later in the book. Both traditions recognize that the introduction builds anticipation, inviting readers to watch how God’s care and human faithfulness meet in what follows.

Ruth 2:2

2Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, “Let me now go to the field, and glean among the ears of grain after him in whose sight I find favor.” She said to her, “Go, my daughter.”

Arminian

Ruth asks permission to glean in the hope of finding favor with someone. Arminian readers see her taking responsible initiative to provide for Naomi and herself. Her plan is practical and humble, and Naomi’s consent shows mutual respect in their relationship. Human effort here is presented as good and fitting, not as a lack of trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Ruth acting diligently within the provision Israel’s law made for the poor to glean. Her hope to find favor already anticipates the kindness she will receive. Both traditions affirm that her initiative and God’s provision are not rivals; she works faithfully, and the story shows God caring for her through ordinary means.

Ruth 2:3

3She went, and came and gleaned in the field after the reapers; and she happened to come to the portion of the field belonging to Boaz, who was of the family of Elimelech.

Arminian

Ruth gleans after the reapers and happens to come to the portion of the field belonging to Boaz. Arminian readers see the narrator’s understatement inviting readers to sense God’s guidance within what feels like chance. Ruth chooses freely where to work, and God’s providence works with and through that choice without overriding it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the phrase she happened to come as a deliberate understatement that points to God’s providence over seemingly chance events. What appears accidental to Ruth is part of God’s caring plan. Both traditions agree that the narrative holds Ruth’s real choice and God’s guiding hand together, and the verse invites wonder rather than a dispute.

Ruth 2:4

4Behold, Boaz came from Bethlehem, and said to the reapers, “May the LORD be with you.” They answered him, “May the LORD bless you.”

Arminian

Boaz arrives from Bethlehem and greets the reapers with a blessing in the LORD’s name, and they respond in kind. Arminian readers see godliness shaping everyday work relationships. The exchange of blessings shows a field where faith is part of ordinary speech, and it hints at the character Boaz will display toward Ruth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see covenant faith expressed in the greetings of a workplace. Boaz and his workers invoke the LORD as the source of blessing in their daily labor. Both traditions see this as a picture of faith lived out in ordinary life, preparing readers to trust Boaz’s coming kindness as genuine.

Ruth 2:5

5Then Boaz said to his servant who was set over the reapers, “Whose young lady is this?”

Arminian

Boaz asks his foreman about the young woman. Arminian readers see attentiveness: he notices someone new among the gleaners and wants to know who she is. The question is natural in a community where people knew one another, and it opens the door to his later kindness without suggesting any improper motive.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Boaz taking notice of Ruth, a small moment that becomes significant in the story’s unfolding. His question concerns her family connection, which matters in this setting. Both traditions see the question as the beginning of an encounter that will display kindness and lead toward Ruth and Naomi’s provision.

Ruth 2:6

6The servant who was set over the reapers answered, “It is the Moabite lady who came back with Naomi out of the country of Moab.

Arminian

The foreman identifies her as the Moabite woman who returned with Naomi. Arminian readers see that Ruth’s reputation and origin are already known in Bethlehem. Her foreign identity is stated plainly, yet the foreman’s report will quickly move to her diligent work, showing how her conduct shapes how she is seen.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the narrative again highlighting Ruth’s Moabite origin alongside her connection to Naomi. Her loyalty to Naomi is already part of her identity in the community. Both traditions recognize the book’s steady emphasis on her outsider status as the backdrop for the kindness she receives.

Ruth 2:7

7She said, ‘Please let me glean and gather after the reapers among the sheaves.’ So she came, and has continued even from the morning until now, except that she rested a little in the house.”

Arminian

The foreman reports that Ruth asked permission to glean and has worked steadily since morning with little rest. Arminian readers see diligence and humility in her conduct. She seeks permission rather than presuming, and her steady labor reflects responsibility toward Naomi, presented in the narrative as an expression of her loyal love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Ruth’s industriousness and respectful request as marks of her character. She works hard within the provisions open to her, not presuming upon others’ generosity. Both traditions honor her diligence, seeing it as fitting faithfulness rather than an attempt to earn favor from God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
