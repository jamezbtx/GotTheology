Passage

2 Corinthians 12

Verses 19–21 · WEB

Paul clarifies that his defense is not self-justification but for the church’s upbuilding, spoken before God in Christ. He fears that on his next visit he will find quarreling, jealousy, and disorder, and that some who sinned earlier have not repented of sexual immorality. Both traditions read this as a sober pastoral warning; they differ on how to describe the spiritual state of professing believers who persist unrepentant.

2 Corinthians 12:19

19Again, do you think that we are excusing ourselves to you? In the sight of God we speak in Christ. But all things, beloved, are for your edifying.

Arminian

Paul is not defending himself to them; he speaks before God in Christ for their upbuilding. Arminian and Reformed readers agree his motive is the church’s good.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that ministry is accountable to God first, and aims at edification.

2 Corinthians 12:20

20For I am afraid that perhaps when I come, I might find you not the way I want to, and that I might be found by you as you don’t desire, that perhaps there would be strife, jealousy, outbursts of anger, factions, slander, whisperings, proud thoughts, or riots,

Arminian

He fears finding quarreling, jealousy, anger, rivalry, slander, gossip, arrogance, and disorder. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that community sins are serious threats to the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the need for church discipline and repentance before his arrival.

2 Corinthians 12:21

21that again when I come my God would humble me before you, and I would mourn for many of those who have sinned before now, and not repented of the uncleanness, sexual immorality, and lustfulness which they committed.

Arminian

He fears God will humble him and he will mourn over many who sinned and have not repented of impurity and sexual immorality. Arminian readers take this as a sobering warning that professing believers who persist in sin without repentance are in real spiritual danger and may forfeit salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see persistent unrepentance as a serious warning sign that may indicate a profession never truly rooted in Christ, while also recognizing that true believers can fall into serious sin and need restoration through repentance.

Key contrast Both treat unrepentant sin as grave and call for repentance. Arminians typically see a real danger of believers falling from grace; Reformed readers typically see persistent unrepentance as calling a profession into question while true believers are restored.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
