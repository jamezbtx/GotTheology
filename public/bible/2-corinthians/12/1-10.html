Passage

2 Corinthians 12

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Paul turns, still reluctantly, to visions and revelations. Speaking of himself in the third person, he describes being caught up to the third heaven and Paradise fourteen years earlier, hearing words not to be spoken. To keep him from conceit he was given a thorn in the flesh, a messenger of Satan; three times he asked for its removal. Christ answered that his grace is sufficient and his power is made perfect in weakness. Both traditions treasure this text; they differ mainly on how God’s purpose relates to the thorn’s satanic agency.

2 Corinthians 12:1

1It is doubtless not profitable for me to boast, but I will come to visions and revelations of the Lord.

Arminian

Boasting is unprofitable, yet Paul turns to visions and revelations of the Lord. Arminian readers note his reluctance: experiences are not the measure of authority.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul refusing to ground his apostleship in mystical experience. The point he is moving toward is weakness.

2 Corinthians 12:2

2I know a man in Christ who was caught up into the third heaven fourteen years ago—whether in the body, I don’t know, or whether out of the body, I don’t know; God knows.

Arminian

Paul speaks of a man in Christ caught up to the third heaven fourteen years earlier. Arminian and Reformed readers agree he is describing himself, using the third person to distance himself from boasting.

Reformed

Reformed readers note his honest uncertainty about whether it was in the body. He refuses to claim more than he knows.

2 Corinthians 12:3

3I know such a man (whether in the body, or outside of the body, I don’t know; God knows),

Arminian

He repeats that he does not know whether it was in or out of the body; God knows. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the repetition shows humility and restraint.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that God alone knows the details. Paul does not build doctrine on his experience.

2 Corinthians 12:4

4how he was caught up into Paradise and heard unspeakable words, which it is not lawful for a man to utter.

Arminian

He was caught up into Paradise and heard inexpressible words not lawful to speak. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this experience gives no new revelation for the church to follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the content was not given for proclamation, which reinforces Scripture as the church’s rule.

2 Corinthians 12:5

5On behalf of such a one I will boast, but on my own behalf I will not boast, except in my weaknesses.

Arminian

He will boast about such a man but not about himself, except in weaknesses. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that Paul redirects boasting from experience to weakness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the theology of the cross at work: God’s glory shows in human weakness.

2 Corinthians 12:6

6For if I would desire to boast, I will not be foolish; for I will speak the truth. But I refrain, so that no man may think more of me than that which he sees in me or hears from me.

Arminian

He could boast truthfully but refrains so that no one thinks more of him than what they see and hear. Arminian readers note Paul’s concern for honest assessment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that credibility rests on observable life and teaching, not secret experiences.

2 Corinthians 12:7

7By reason of the exceeding greatness of the revelations, that I should not be exalted excessively, a thorn in the flesh was given to me: a messenger of Satan to torment me, that I should not be exalted excessively.

Arminian

A thorn in the flesh, a messenger of Satan, was given to keep him from conceit. Many Arminian readers see God permitting Satan’s harm and turning it to a good purpose, so that God uses what he does not directly will.

Reformed

Many Reformed readers see God as the one who gave the thorn for his purpose, with Satan as a secondary agent acting with evil intent, much like Job. The nature of the thorn is unknown; interpreters across traditions suggest illness, persecution, or opponents.

Key contrast Both see God turning the thorn to Paul’s good. Arminians typically speak of God permitting Satan’s harm for a purpose; Reformed readers typically see God’s purposeful giving of the thorn, with Satan acting within God’s decree.

2 Corinthians 12:8

8Concerning this thing, I begged the Lord three times that it might depart from me.

Arminian

Paul asked the Lord three times to remove it. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that earnest, repeated prayer is fitting, and that God may answer differently than requested.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that prayer is addressed to the Lord, likely Christ, and is answered by a word rather than removal.

2 Corinthians 12:9

9He has said to me, “My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness.” Most gladly therefore I will rather glory in my weaknesses, that the power of Christ may rest on me.

Arminian

Christ answered: my grace is sufficient, for power is made perfect in weakness. Arminian readers stress grace as an enabling power that sustains believers who rely on Christ, and Paul’s willing embrace of weakness.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress grace as God’s sovereign sufficiency that works through weakness. Both traditions find here one of Scripture’s richest comforts: Christ’s power rests on the weak.

2 Corinthians 12:10

10Therefore I take pleasure in weaknesses, in injuries, in necessities, in persecutions, and in distresses, for Christ’s sake. For when I am weak, then am I strong.

Arminian

For Christ’s sake he delights in weaknesses, insults, hardships, persecutions, and difficulties, since when weak he is strong. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this is not love of suffering but trust in Christ’s power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the paradox of the cross shaping Christian life. Strength is Christ’s, received through dependence.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
