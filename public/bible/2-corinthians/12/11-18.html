Passage

2 Corinthians 12

Verses 11–18 · WEB

Paul ends his foolish boasting by blaming the Corinthians, who should have commended him. Apostolic signs were done among them with patience. His only failure was not burdening them financially, which he ironically asks them to forgive. Preparing for a third visit, he promises again to seek them and not their money, spending himself like a parent for children, and he defends Titus and himself against charges of crafty exploitation. Arminian and Reformed readers share this reading of pastoral integrity.

2 Corinthians 12:11

11I have become foolish in boasting. You compelled me, for I ought to have been commended by you, for I am in no way inferior to the very best apostles, though I am nothing.

Arminian

Paul has become foolish, compelled by them; they should have commended him, since he is not inferior though he is nothing. Arminian and Reformed readers agree on the irony and humility.

Reformed

Reformed readers note though I am nothing: apostolic authority rests on God’s call, not personal worth.

2 Corinthians 12:12

12Truly the signs of an apostle were worked among you in all perseverance, in signs and wonders and mighty works.

Arminian

The signs of an apostle were done among them with patience, in signs, wonders, and mighty works. Arminian and Reformed readers agree Paul appeals to evidence they witnessed.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly see these signs as attesting the apostolic office; views differ, within both traditions, on whether such signs continue today.

2 Corinthians 12:13

13For what is there in which you were made inferior to the rest of the assemblies, unless it is that I myself was not a burden to you? Forgive me this wrong!

Arminian

How were they worse off than other churches, except that he did not burden them? Forgive me this wrong! Arminian and Reformed readers agree this is pointed irony.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul’s refusal of support was a gift, not a slight.

2 Corinthians 12:14

14Behold, this is the third time I am ready to come to you, and I will not be a burden to you; for I seek not your possessions, but you. For the children ought not to save up for the parents, but the parents for the children.

Arminian

Ready to come a third time, he will not burden them; he seeks not their possessions but them, as parents save for children. Arminian readers note the parental love behind his ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a model of pastoral ministry seeking people, not profit.

2 Corinthians 12:15

15I will most gladly spend and be spent for your souls. If I love you more abundantly, am I loved the less?

Arminian

He will gladly spend and be spent for their souls; if he loves more, is he loved less? Arminian and Reformed readers agree this shows Christlike self-giving.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the pain of unreturned love, which Paul accepts for their sake.

2 Corinthians 12:16

16Even so, I myself didn’t burden you. But you might say that being crafty, I caught you with deception.

Arminian

Some may say he was crafty and caught them by trickery. Arminian and Reformed readers agree he is quoting an accusation, not admitting it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the slander that his collection for Jerusalem was a covert scheme.

2 Corinthians 12:17

17Did I take advantage of you by anyone of those whom I have sent to you?

Arminian

Did he exploit them through anyone he sent? Arminian and Reformed readers agree the expected answer is no.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul inviting scrutiny of his associates’ conduct as well as his own.

2 Corinthians 12:18

18I exhorted Titus, and I sent the brother with him. Did Titus take any advantage of you? Didn’t we walk in the same spirit? Didn’t we walk in the same steps?

Arminian

He sent Titus and the brother; did Titus exploit them? Did they not walk in the same spirit and steps? Arminian and Reformed readers agree this appeals to consistent integrity.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the same spirit may mean the Holy Spirit or a shared attitude; interpreters differ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
