Passage

2 Corinthians 1

Verses 12–14 · WEB

Paul begins defending the integrity of his conduct and communication toward the Corinthians. He appeals to sincerity enabled by God’s grace and hopes that their partial understanding will deepen into mutual confidence. The day of the Lord Jesus gives their relationship its final horizon. Both traditions see transparent ministry and shared joy as fruits of grace rather than grounds for self-exaltation.

2 Corinthians 1:12

12For our boasting is this: the testimony of our conscience that in holiness and sincerity of God, not in fleshly wisdom but in the grace of God, we behaved ourselves in the world, and more abundantly toward you.

Arminian

Paul appeals to a conscience formed by sincerity and God’s grace rather than worldly calculation. Arminian readers see integrity as a real, grace-enabled manner of life. His defense concerns how he has treated the Corinthians, not a claim to sinless perfection or independence from God’s help.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a legitimate testimony to grace’s fruit rather than boasting in self-generated virtue. Paul distinguishes sincerity before God from manipulative wisdom. A clear conscience supports his defense, while the explicit appeal to grace locates the source of his conduct outside personal merit.

2 Corinthians 1:13

13For we write no other things to you than what you read or even acknowledge, and I hope you will acknowledge to the end—

Arminian

Paul insists that his writing has no hidden message behind what the Corinthians read. Arminian readers see straightforward communication serving reconciliation and inviting fuller understanding. His hope concerns their recognition of his sincerity, so the reference to the end should not automatically become a separate debate about final perseverance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see apostolic honesty expressed in transparent writing rather than coded motives. Paul hopes that partial acknowledgment will grow into full understanding. The immediate concern is the credibility of his communication, not a new promise that every reader will certainly recognize the truth.

2 Corinthians 1:14

14as also you acknowledged us in part—that we are your boasting, even as you also are ours, in the day of our Lord Jesus.

Arminian

Paul anticipates mutual rejoicing when the Lord Jesus appears. Arminian readers see pastoral service and the church’s faithful response as grounds for shared gratitude, not competing claims to merit. Present misunderstanding is set within the hope that Christ will reveal the value of their relationship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the day of Christ giving ministry its final perspective. The church can rejoice in Paul, and Paul in the church, because both display God’s grace. Mutual boasting here honors what the Lord has done rather than turning either party into the ultimate source of confidence.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
