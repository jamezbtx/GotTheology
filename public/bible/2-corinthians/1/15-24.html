Passage

2 Corinthians 1

Verses 15–24 · WEB

Paul explains his earlier travel plans and denies that changing them makes his message unreliable. Christ confirms God’s promises, and God establishes his people in Christ, sealing them with the Spirit as a down payment. Paul stayed away to spare the Corinthians, not to control their faith. Both traditions affirm God’s faithfulness while differing over how the Spirit’s pledge relates to final perseverance.

2 Corinthians 1:15

15In this confidence, I was determined to come first to you, that you might have a second benefit,

Arminian

Paul explains that his original plan intended an additional benefit for the Corinthians. Arminian readers see a pastoral motive behind the travel arrangements rather than casual promises. A sincere intention can be revised for good reasons without implying that the underlying desire to serve was false.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the planned visit as an expression of confidence in a restored relationship. Paul’s purpose was to bring benefit, not to manipulate expectations. The verse introduces his defense of changed plans, distinguishing the stability of his ministry from an unchangeable travel schedule.

2 Corinthians 1:16

16and by you to pass into Macedonia, and again from Macedonia to come to you, and to be sent forward by you on my journey to Judea.

Arminian

The proposed route would have brought Paul through Corinth twice and involved their help toward Judea. Arminian readers see practical partnership within the gospel mission. Churches share in ministry through hospitality and assistance as well as hearing teaching; the details also explain what expectations the changed plans affected.

Reformed

Reformed readers see concrete arrangements through which churches support gospel service. Paul’s route depended on cooperation and circumstances, not simply his private wishes. Explaining the plan helps distinguish an honest intention from the accusation that his promises were careless or deliberately contradictory.

2 Corinthians 1:17

17When I therefore planned this, did I show fickleness? Or the things that I plan, do I plan according to the flesh, that with me there should be the “Yes, yes” and the “No, no?”

Arminian

Paul challenges the claim that his revised itinerary reveals fickleness or self-serving planning. Arminian readers see responsibility for truthful commitments without assuming that faithful people never change plans. The moral issue is whether his words conceal contrary intentions, not whether he possesses complete control of future circumstances.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a contrast between fleshly calculation and sincere ministry under God. Paul rejects the picture of a man affirming and denying the same commitment for personal advantage. Dependence on providence allows revised plans while still requiring truthfulness about one’s intentions and reasons.

2 Corinthians 1:18

18But as God is faithful, our word toward you was not “Yes and no.”

Arminian

Paul invokes God’s faithfulness as he defends the reliability of his message. Arminian readers see a firm distinction between changed travel plans and a contradictory gospel. Trustworthy ministry reflects the God it proclaims, while acknowledging that human arrangements remain subject to pastoral judgment and changing circumstances.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s steadfast character grounding confidence in the apostolic proclamation. Paul does not claim that his schedule shares God’s immutability. He denies that a revised visit makes the message spoken to the Corinthians an unstable mixture of affirmation and denial.

2 Corinthians 1:19

19For the Son of God, Jesus Christ, who was preached among you by us—by me, Silvanus, and Timothy—was not “Yes and no,” but in him is “Yes.”

Arminian

Christ himself is the reliable center of the message preached by Paul and his coworkers. Arminian readers see the Corinthians directed beyond disputes about a messenger to the Son of God. The gospel’s truth does not fluctuate with Paul’s itinerary, and their confidence should rest in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the stability of the gospel grounded in the person of God’s Son. The common proclamation of Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy does not offer competing answers about God’s saving purpose. Christ embodies divine faithfulness even when the servants who announce him must explain changed plans.

2 Corinthians 1:20

20For however many are the promises of God, in him is the “Yes.” Therefore also through him is the “Amen”, to the glory of God through us.

Arminian

God’s promises find their confirmation in Christ, drawing a response of faith and praise. Arminian readers see assurance centered on the trustworthiness of God’s saving provision rather than human achievement. The church’s response receives and celebrates what Christ establishes; it does not make divine promises true.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as the fulfillment and guarantee of God’s promises. The church’s affirmation follows the reality God has established in his Son. Its response brings glory to God through Christ, placing the apostolic defense within a larger confession of divine faithfulness.

2 Corinthians 1:21

21Now he who establishes us with you in Christ and anointed us is God,

Arminian

God establishes Paul and the Corinthians together in Christ and anoints them. Arminian readers see divine initiative and continuing support as the basis of their shared life. Paul does not reserve grace for leaders; the same God binds ministers and congregation to Christ and equips them for service.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the acting subject who establishes his people in Christ. Their stability rests in his gracious work rather than their fluctuating relationship with Paul. The shared anointing prepares for the reference to the Spirit, grounding Christian assurance in divine action rather than status.

2 Corinthians 1:22

22who also sealed us and gave us the down payment of the Spirit in our hearts.

Arminian

The seal and the Spirit’s down payment give real assurance of belonging to God and of his promised future. Arminian readers understand this assurance within continuing faith in Christ, not as making apostasy impossible. The Spirit is God’s present gift and pledge, never a reward for self-sufficient perseverance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the seal marking God’s ownership and the Spirit’s down payment assuring the completion of his saving work. God’s pledge grounds the final perseverance of those truly united to Christ. Assurance rests on the giver’s faithfulness, while the Spirit also sustains the faith and holiness of his people.

Key contrast Both find real assurance in God’s seal and the gift of the Spirit. Arminian readers understand the pledge within an ongoing faith that can be abandoned. Reformed readers understand it as guaranteeing that those truly united to Christ will finally persevere through God’s preserving grace.

2 Corinthians 1:23

23But I call God for a witness to my soul, that to spare you, I didn’t come to Corinth.

Arminian

Paul solemnly explains that postponing his visit was intended to spare the Corinthians. Arminian readers see pastoral restraint rather than evasion: correction must serve the community’s good. Calling God as witness makes his explanation accountable to God rather than merely an attempt to manage appearances.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the change of plans as a merciful exercise of pastoral judgment. Paul’s authority does not require him to press every confrontation immediately. The appeal to God’s witness underlines the seriousness of his motive and connects his defense of integrity with care for the church.

2 Corinthians 1:24

24We don’t control your faith, but are fellow workers with you for your joy. For you stand firm in faith.

Arminian

Paul describes himself as a coworker for their joy, not a master over their faith. Arminian readers see responsible faith directed toward Christ rather than controlled by a minister. Pastoral authority should support the church’s steadfast trust, not replace it with dependence on the leader’s will.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ministerial authority as service under Christ, never ownership of another person’s faith. The Corinthians stand by faith, not by submission to Paul’s personal preferences. Genuine oversight aims at their joy and stability while acknowledging that God alone is Lord of the conscience.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
