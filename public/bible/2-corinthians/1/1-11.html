Passage

2 Corinthians 1

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Paul and Timothy greet the church at Corinth and the saints throughout Achaia. Paul then praises the God of all comfort, describing how divine consolation sustains suffering servants and equips them to help others. His experience of near death in Asia leads to trust in the God who raises the dead and to gratitude for the church’s prayers. Both traditions share this account of grace, dependence, endurance, and mutual care.

2 Corinthians 1:1

1Paul, an apostle of Christ Jesus through the will of God, and Timothy our brother, to the assembly of God which is at Corinth, with all the saints who are in the whole of Achaia:

Arminian

Paul identifies his apostleship as God’s calling and includes Timothy in the greeting. Arminian readers see divine initiative grounding his ministry rather than personal ambition. Addressing the whole church and the saints throughout Achaia places his strained relationship with Corinth within the wider fellowship of God’s people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s authority rooted in God’s will rather than the Corinthians’ approval. The assembly belongs to God, and its members are set apart for him. Naming Timothy as a brother also frames apostolic ministry within the family of faith rather than as isolated personal power.

2 Corinthians 1:2

2Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Grace and peace come from the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. Arminian readers see God’s generosity preceding the church’s response, including the repentance and reconciliation this letter seeks. Even a strained pastoral relationship begins with a blessing grounded in God’s provision rather than human performance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the greeting locating the church’s life in divine favor and reconciliation. Father and Son together supply what the Corinthians need. Paul does not begin by making peace depend on their approval of him; he directs them to the God whose grace sustains their fellowship.

2 Corinthians 1:3

3Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies and God of all comfort,

Arminian

Paul praises God as the source of mercy and comfort before describing his distress. Arminian readers see divine compassion as the foundation for endurance, not a reward for keeping pain hidden. God’s fatherly care invites trust even when believers cannot immediately understand or escape their suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers see suffering interpreted through the character of the Father revealed in Jesus Christ. God is not absent because his servants are afflicted. His abundant comfort gives grounds for praise amid weakness and prepares the church to understand Paul’s ministry without measuring it by outward ease.

2 Corinthians 1:4

4who comforts us in all our affliction, that we may be able to comfort those who are in any affliction, through the comfort with which we ourselves are comforted by God.

Arminian

Comfort received from God equips believers to care for others in affliction. Arminian readers see grace calling forth active compassion rather than remaining a private benefit. The verse does not promise immediate removal of every hardship; it shows how God’s help can be shared within a suffering community.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sustaining grace extending through one believer’s experience to another’s need. Affliction is not itself a saving merit, and comfort is not merely self-generated resilience. God supplies consolation that enables his people to become real instruments of care for one another.

2 Corinthians 1:5

5For as the sufferings of Christ abound to us, even so our comfort also abounds through Christ.

Arminian

Suffering in Christ’s service is accompanied by comfort through Christ. Arminian readers see a living relationship that sustains costly discipleship. Sharing his sufferings does not supplement the sufficiency of his saving death; it describes the hardships of belonging to him and carrying his message.

Reformed

Reformed readers see fellowship with Christ embracing both suffering and consolation. Paul’s distress does not discredit his ministry, since the pattern of service follows the crucified Lord. The abundance of comfort comes through Christ, not from treating the pain itself as spiritually valuable apart from him.

2 Corinthians 1:6

6But if we are afflicted, it is for your comfort and salvation. If we are comforted, it is for your comfort, which produces in you the patient enduring of the same sufferings which we also suffer.

Arminian

Paul’s affliction and comfort both serve the Corinthians’ good, encouraging patient endurance. Arminian readers see ministry that helps believers continue in the salvation God supplies. Their endurance is a genuine response sustained by grace, not a way to purchase salvation through hardship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God using apostolic suffering and comfort as means of strengthening the church. Paul does not claim to save by his own pain; his ministry serves the saving gospel and its fruit. The same grace produces patience as believers share similar trials.

2 Corinthians 1:7

7Our hope for you is steadfast, knowing that, since you are partakers of the sufferings, so you are also of the comfort.

Arminian

Paul expects the Corinthians to share comfort as they share suffering. Arminian readers see confident pastoral encouragement rather than an assumption that hardship makes faithfulness automatic. God’s consolation is available in the common life of faith, giving them strong reason to endure together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see steadfast hope grounded in the connection between fellowship in Christ’s sufferings and his sustaining comfort. Their troubles do not mean God has rejected them. Paul encourages confidence in grace while recognizing the concrete suffering they must continue to face.

2 Corinthians 1:8

8For we don’t desire to have you uninformed, brothers, concerning our affliction which happened to us in Asia: that we were weighed down exceedingly, beyond our power, so much that we despaired even of life.

Arminian

Paul openly describes distress beyond his own strength and fear that he would not survive. Arminian readers see that sincere faith does not exclude exhaustion or anguish. The passage does not identify the particular incident in Asia, so its details should not be supplied from conjecture.

Reformed

Reformed readers see apostolic weakness stated without embarrassment. Dependence on providence does not require claiming that every burden feels manageable. Paul’s candor prepares for the contrast between self-reliance and the God who raises the dead, rather than explaining the exact cause of this crisis.

2 Corinthians 1:9

9Yes, we ourselves have had the sentence of death within ourselves, that we should not trust in ourselves, but in God who raises the dead,

Arminian

The experience of facing death redirected trust from self to the God of resurrection. Arminian readers see grace teaching dependence at the limits of human ability. God’s sustaining action calls for trust; the verse does not commend seeking danger in order to become spiritually mature.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God bringing a redemptive purpose through Paul’s helplessness: confidence rests in divine power rather than personal resources. The God who raises the dead remains sufficient when survival appears impossible. This does not make the suffering unreal or require minimizing its severity.

2 Corinthians 1:10

10who delivered us out of so great a death, and does deliver, on whom we have set our hope that he will also still deliver us,

Arminian

Past deliverance grounds Paul’s continuing hope in God. Arminian readers see a reason for active trust and prayer, not an unconditional promise that every believer will escape physical death. The surrounding appeal to resurrection places hope in God beyond the limits of any one rescue.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providential care linking remembered rescue with future confidence. Paul hopes in God rather than in his capacity to control events. The verse expresses trust amid continuing danger; it should not be turned into a universal guarantee of earthly safety for faithful servants.

2 Corinthians 1:11

11you also helping together on our behalf by your supplication; that, for the gift given to us by means of many, thanks may be given by many persons on your behalf.

Arminian

The Corinthians help through prayer, and answered prayer widens thanksgiving. Arminian readers see intercession as real participation in God’s gracious help, not a ceremonial addition to an inevitable outcome. The gift remains God’s, while many people share in asking for it and responding with gratitude.

Reformed

Reformed readers see prayer as a means God uses in providing deliverance and gathering praise. Divine sovereignty does not make the church’s supplication unnecessary. Paul connects received mercy with corporate thanksgiving, drawing the Corinthians into partnership rather than leaving them spectators of his ministry.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
