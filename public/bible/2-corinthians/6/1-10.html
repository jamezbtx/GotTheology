Passage

2 Corinthians 6

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Working together with God, Paul urges the Corinthians not to receive God’s grace in vain, because now is the day of salvation. He describes his ministry under hardship and in the Spirit’s power, with paradoxes of weakness and joy. Both traditions agree on the urgency of the call; they differ on what receiving grace in vain implies.

2 Corinthians 6:1

1Working together, we entreat also that you do not receive the grace of God in vain.

Arminian

Do not receive the grace of God in vain. Arminian readers see a real possibility of failing to benefit from grace already received. The warning presupposes that believers must continue in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a genuine warning used by God to keep His people. Some read it as aimed at those whose profession may not be real, or at the danger of a fruitless life.

Key contrast Both treat the warning as genuine. Arminian readers usually allow that grace truly received can be lost through unbelief. Reformed readers usually see the warning as a means of perseverance for true believers or a test of false profession.

2 Corinthians 6:2

2For he says, “At an acceptable time I listened to you. In a day of salvation I helped you.” Behold, now is the acceptable time. Behold, now is the day of salvation.

Arminian

Now is the acceptable time, now is the day of salvation. Arminian readers see Isaiah 49:8 applied to the present gospel age. The offer is urgent and real for all who hear.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same urgent offer. The day of salvation has dawned in Christ, and God calls people now through the word.

2 Corinthians 6:3

3We give no occasion of stumbling in anything, that our service may not be blamed,

Arminian

We give no cause for stumbling, so the ministry may not be blamed. Arminian readers see Paul guarding the gospel’s credibility by his conduct.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that a minister’s life commends or discredits the message.

2 Corinthians 6:4

4but in everything commending ourselves as servants of God: in great endurance, in afflictions, in hardships, in distresses,

Arminian

We commend ourselves as God’s servants through great endurance in afflictions, hardships, and distresses. Arminian readers see faithfulness proved under pressure.

Reformed

Reformed readers see servanthood marked by suffering, not worldly success. Endurance displays God’s sustaining grace.

2 Corinthians 6:5

5in beatings, in imprisonments, in riots, in labors, in watchings, in fastings,

Arminian

In beatings, imprisonments, riots, labors, watchings, and fastings. Arminian readers see the real cost of apostolic ministry, borne willingly.

Reformed

Reformed readers compare the fuller list in 11:23–27. Suffering accompanies faithful service and shows that the power belongs to God.

2 Corinthians 6:6

6in pureness, in knowledge, in perseverance, in kindness, in the Holy Spirit, in sincere love,

Arminian

In pureness, knowledge, perseverance, kindness, the Holy Spirit, and sincere love. Arminian readers see inner virtues and the Spirit’s presence marking real ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit’s fruit in the minister’s character, the true credentials of service.

2 Corinthians 6:7

7in the word of truth, in the power of God, by the armor of righteousness on the right hand and on the left,

Arminian

In the word of truth, the power of God, and the armor of righteousness. Arminian readers see the ministry’s strength in God’s truth and power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. The armor on both hands likely means equipment for every situation.

2 Corinthians 6:8

8by glory and dishonor, by evil report and good report, as deceivers and yet true,

Arminian

By glory and dishonor, by evil report and good report, as deceivers and yet true. Arminian readers see faithfulness that does not depend on reputation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s paradoxes as the shape of Christ’s own ministry, misjudged by the world yet approved by God.

2 Corinthians 6:9

9as unknown and yet well known, as dying and behold—we live, as punished and not killed,

Arminian

Unknown yet well known, dying and yet we live. Arminian readers see God sustaining His servants through extreme danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers see resurrection power at work in weakness, echoing 4:10–11.

2 Corinthians 6:10

10as sorrowful yet always rejoicing, as poor yet making many rich, as having nothing and yet possessing all things.

Arminian

Sorrowful yet rejoicing, poor yet making many rich, having nothing yet possessing all. Arminian readers see the gospel’s reversal of values.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same reversal: those who have Christ possess all things.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
