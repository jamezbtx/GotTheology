Passage

2 Corinthians 9

Verses 1–5 · WEB

Paul continues his appeal for the collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem. He has boasted of Corinth’s readiness to the Macedonians and sends brothers ahead so that the promised gift will be ready as a willing blessing, not a forced payment. Arminian and Reformed readers share nearly all of this ground: generosity must be free, and good intentions need follow-through.

2 Corinthians 9:1

1It is indeed unnecessary for me to write to you concerning the service to the saints,

Arminian

Paul says it is almost unnecessary to write about the collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem, which he calls “the service to the saints.” Arminian readers note his pastoral tact: he treats the Corinthians as willing partners whose generosity he expects, not as people to be forced.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same tact and the theological weight of the word “service,” which presents giving as ministry to God’s people. Both traditions read chapters 8–9 together as Paul’s fullest teaching on Christian giving.

2 Corinthians 9:2

2for I know your readiness, of which I boast on your behalf to those of Macedonia, that Achaia has been prepared for the past year. Your zeal has stirred up very many of them.

Arminian

Paul has boasted to the Macedonians that Achaia was ready a year ago, and the Corinthians’ zeal stirred many others. Arminian readers see how one church’s willing response can encourage others to act.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the mutual encouragement of churches, and in light of 8:1 they trace such zeal ultimately to God’s grace at work among the churches. Shared ground: generosity is contagious within the body of Christ.

2 Corinthians 9:3

3But I have sent the brothers so that our boasting on your behalf may not be in vain in this respect, that, just as I said, you may be prepared,

Arminian

Paul sends the brothers so his boasting will not prove empty and the Corinthians will be ready. Arminian readers see practical accountability: good intentions need follow-through, and Paul helps them complete what they began.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same wise oversight. Paul trusts their readiness yet still arranges means to bring it about, a pattern Reformed theology sees often in Scripture, where God’s purposes are carried out through ordinary human planning.

2 Corinthians 9:4

4lest by any means, if anyone from Macedonia comes there with me and finds you unprepared, we (to say nothing of you) would be disappointed in this confident boasting.

Arminian

Paul wants to spare them and himself embarrassment if Macedonians arrive and find them unprepared. Arminian readers note the real possibility of failure Paul has in view; readiness was not automatic.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Paul speaks frankly about a real risk and uses honest motivation, including reputation, to spur faithfulness. Both read this as ordinary pastoral wisdom rather than a doctrinal dispute.

2 Corinthians 9:5

5I thought it necessary therefore to entreat the brothers that they would go before to you and arrange ahead of time the generous gift that you promised before, that the same might be ready as a matter of generosity, and not of greediness.

Arminian

The brothers will arrange the promised gift beforehand so it comes as generosity, not as something extracted. Arminian readers stress that the gift must remain voluntary to be truly generous.

Reformed

Reformed readers also stress the contrast between a gift freely given and one grudgingly squeezed out. The word translated “generous gift” can also mean “blessing,” which shows that giving is meant to bless others. Both traditions agree on the free and willing character of Christian giving.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
