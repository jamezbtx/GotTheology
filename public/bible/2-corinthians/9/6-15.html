Passage

2 Corinthians 9

Verses 6–15 · WEB

Paul grounds Christian giving in God’s generosity. Those who sow bountifully reap bountifully; God loves a cheerful giver and supplies everything needed for good works. The collection meets real needs, produces thanksgiving, and unites the churches, and Paul ends by thanking God for his indescribable gift. Both traditions agree that God’s grace is the source and goal of giving, while describing how grace and the human will relate in different ways.

2 Corinthians 9:6

6Remember this: he who sows sparingly will also reap sparingly. He who sows bountifully will also reap bountifully.

Arminian

Whoever sows sparingly reaps sparingly; whoever sows bountifully reaps bountifully. Arminian readers take the harvest principle as a real link between faithful generosity and God’s rewarding response, without making it a transaction.

Reformed

Reformed readers also affirm the principle and usually stress that the harvest is God’s gracious gift, not a debt owed to the giver. Both traditions reject using this verse to promise guaranteed financial return; the harvest includes spiritual fruit and thanksgiving, as verses 10–12 show.

2 Corinthians 9:7

7Let each man give according as he has determined in his heart, not grudgingly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver.

Arminian

Each should give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under pressure, for God loves a cheerful giver. Arminian readers emphasize the genuine freedom of the giver’s decision; God delights in a willing heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers equally affirm that giving must be willing. They add that a cheerful heart is itself a fruit of grace, as 8:1 and 9:14 suggest. Both agree that coerced giving misses the point.

2 Corinthians 9:8

8And God is able to make all grace abound to you, that you, always having all sufficiency in everything, may abound to every good work.

Arminian

God is able to make all grace abound so that believers have enough for every good work. Arminian readers see God’s enabling grace equipping believers, who then use what they receive for good works.

Reformed

Reformed readers highlight “all grace” and “every good work,” seeing God as the source both of the resources and of the good works themselves. Both agree that God’s sufficiency, not the giver’s wealth, is the foundation of generosity.

2 Corinthians 9:9

9As it is written, “He has scattered abroad. He has given to the poor. His righteousness remains forever.”

Arminian

Paul quotes Psalm 112:9 about the righteous person who gives freely to the poor and whose righteousness endures. Arminian readers see the generous believer as reflecting the character of the godly person described in the psalm.

Reformed

Reformed readers read “righteousness” here mainly as practical righteousness, a life of justice and mercy, rather than the righteousness credited in justification. Many in both traditions read it this way. The psalm shows that generosity is part of lasting godliness.

2 Corinthians 9:10

10Now may he who supplies seed to the sower and bread for food, supply and multiply your seed for sowing, and increase the fruits of your righteousness,

Arminian

God, who supplies seed and bread, will multiply their seed and increase the harvest of their righteousness. Arminian readers see God faithfully resourcing those who choose to sow generously.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that every part of the cycle, seed, bread, and harvest, comes from God, echoing Isaiah 55:10. The giver is a steward of what God supplies. Both see God as the provider behind every gift.

2 Corinthians 9:11

11you being enriched in everything for all generosity, which produces thanksgiving to God through us.

Arminian

Believers are enriched in every way so they can be generous, and their generosity produces thanksgiving to God. Arminian readers note the purpose of enrichment: not accumulation but generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same purpose and stress the final goal, thanksgiving to God. God’s glory is the end of the whole process. This is broad shared ground.

2 Corinthians 9:12

12For this service of giving that you perform not only makes up for lack among the saints, but abounds also through much giving of thanks to God,

Arminian

The collection meets the needs of the saints and also overflows in thanksgiving to God. Arminian readers see giving as ministry with both practical and spiritual effects.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the double outcome too: human need is met and God is glorified. The word for “service” has a sense of public service or worship, which underlines that giving is an act of worship.

2 Corinthians 9:13

13seeing that through the proof given by this service, they glorify God for the obedience of your confession to the Good News of Christ and for the generosity of your contribution to them and to all,

Arminian

The Jerusalem believers will glorify God for the Corinthians’ obedience to their confession of the gospel and for their generosity. Arminian readers see giving as evidence that faith is genuine and active.

Reformed

Reformed readers similarly see generosity as proof of genuine faith, though not its ground. Obedience flows from confession of the gospel. Both traditions agree that the gift also helped unite Jewish and Gentile believers.

2 Corinthians 9:14

14while they themselves also, with supplication on your behalf, yearn for you by reason of the exceeding grace of God in you.

Arminian

The recipients will pray for the Corinthians and long for them because of the surpassing grace of God in them. Arminian readers see God’s grace at work in the Corinthians’ willing response.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that Paul credits the Corinthians’ generosity to “the exceeding grace of God in you.” Their giving is God’s work in them. Both agree that the collection deepens love and prayer between churches.

Key contrast Both traditions agree the Corinthians’ generosity is a work of God’s grace. Arminian readers typically understand that grace as enabling a free response that the Corinthians could have resisted; Reformed readers typically understand it as effectively producing the willing response it calls for.

2 Corinthians 9:15

15Now thanks be to God for his unspeakable gift!

Arminian

Paul ends with thanks to God for his indescribable gift. Arminian readers, with most interpreters, take the gift as Christ himself, or God’s saving grace in Christ, the source of all Christian generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise usually take the gift as Christ, recalling 8:9, where Christ became poor for our sake. Some read it more broadly as the grace that produces generosity and unity. In either case, God’s giving comes first and grounds every human gift.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
