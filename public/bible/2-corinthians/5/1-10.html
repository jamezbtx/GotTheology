Passage

2 Corinthians 5

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Paul has just contrasted the outer self that is wasting away with the inner self renewed day by day (4:16–18). Now he explains the hope behind that endurance: if this earthly tent is torn down, believers have an eternal building from God. They groan, longing not to be unclothed but further clothed, so that mortality is swallowed up by life, and God has given the Spirit as a down payment. So they walk by faith, prefer to be at home with the Lord, and aim to please him, knowing all must appear before Christ’s judgment seat. Both traditions share this hope; they differ mainly on how the coming judgment relates to final salvation and assurance.

2 Corinthians 5:1

1For we know that if the earthly house of our tent is dissolved, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal, in the heavens.

Arminian

If the earthly tent is dissolved, we have a building from God, eternal in the heavens. Arminian readers find here a firm hope for believers facing death. The confidence rests on God’s promise and power, not on the strength of the body. Interpreters differ on whether the building is the resurrection body or a heavenly dwelling received at death.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same sure hope grounded in God’s work: the new dwelling is from God and not made with hands. Many read it as the resurrection body, held secure in God’s purpose even before it is given. Paul’s we know expresses confidence, not wishful thinking.

2 Corinthians 5:2

2For most certainly in this we groan, longing to be clothed with our habitation which is from heaven,

Arminian

In this tent we groan, longing to be clothed with our dwelling from heaven. Arminian readers see honest Christian longing: faith does not erase the weight of mortal life but directs it toward God’s promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the same groaning that Romans 8:23 links to awaiting the redemption of the body. The longing is itself a sign of the Spirit’s work in those who belong to the age to come.

2 Corinthians 5:3

3if indeed being clothed, we will not be found naked.

Arminian

If indeed being clothed, we will not be found naked. Arminian readers take nakedness as an image of the soul without a body, which Paul does not desire. The hope is resurrection life, not mere escape from the body. Some manuscripts and editions read unclothed rather than clothed here; the sense of the passage is similar.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the verse as Paul’s preference for resurrection over a bodiless state. Some take found naked as a hint of shame before God, but most see the body–soul image. The textual variant between clothed and unclothed does not change the passage’s hope.

2 Corinthians 5:4

4For indeed we who are in this tent do groan, being burdened, not that we desire to be unclothed, but that we desire to be clothed, that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life.

Arminian

We groan, not wanting to be unclothed but further clothed, so that what is mortal is swallowed up by life. Arminian readers see God’s purpose to renew, not discard, human life. The goal is life overwhelming death.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the full redemption of the person, body included. Life swallows up mortality as God completes what He has begun, echoing 1 Corinthians 15:54.

2 Corinthians 5:5

5Now he who made us for this very thing is God, who also gave to us the down payment of the Spirit.

Arminian

God prepared us for this and gave the Spirit as a down payment. Arminian readers see the Spirit as God’s real pledge to believers. Many Arminians add that believers are called to continue in faith, through which they keep enjoying that pledge.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit as God’s guarantee that the full inheritance will surely come. God who made us for this very thing will finish His work.

Key contrast Both see the Spirit as God’s pledge of future glory. Arminian readers usually hold that the pledge is enjoyed through continuing faith and can be forfeited by persistent unbelief. Reformed readers usually see the pledge as God’s guarantee that every true believer will reach glory.

2 Corinthians 5:6

6Therefore we are always confident and know that while we are at home in the body, we are absent from the Lord;

Arminian

We are always confident, knowing that at home in the body we are away from the Lord. Arminian readers see courage that comes from trusting God’s promise even while still on the journey.

Reformed

Reformed readers see confidence rooted in God’s pledge in verse 5. Present life in the body is real fellowship with Christ by faith, yet not yet the face-to-face presence to come.

2 Corinthians 5:7

7for we walk by faith, not by sight.

Arminian

We walk by faith, not by sight. Arminian readers see daily trust in what God has promised but not yet shown. Faith is a life lived, not just a moment of believing.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the Christian life is lived by trust in the unseen. Faith rests on God’s word until sight replaces it at Christ’s coming.

2 Corinthians 5:8

8We are courageous, I say, and are willing rather to be absent from the body and to be at home with the Lord.

Arminian

We would rather be away from the body and at home with the Lord. Arminian readers find here comfort that believers who die are with the Lord, awaiting resurrection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see support for the intermediate state, conscious fellowship with Christ after death and before the resurrection. Both traditions widely share this reading.

2 Corinthians 5:9

9Therefore also we make it our aim, whether at home or absent, to be well pleasing to him.

Arminian

Whether at home or away, we aim to please him. Arminian readers see hope shaping present obedience. The goal is not anxious performance but a life lived for the Lord’s approval.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same ambition as the fruit of grace. Pleasing Christ is the believer’s aim in life and death, flowing from confidence rather than fear of rejection.

2 Corinthians 5:10

10For we must all be revealed before the judgment seat of Christ that each one may receive the things in the body according to what he has done, whether good or bad.

Arminian

We must all appear before Christ’s judgment seat to receive according to what we have done. Arminian readers take this as a serious call to faithfulness. Many see the judgment as including believers’ works in a way that relates to final salvation as well as reward.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm that believers will be judged according to their works. Many read this as an assessment of reward or as the public display of a faith already justified in Christ, not a reopening of justification.

Key contrast Both affirm that believers will be judged according to their deeds. Arminian readers more often connect this judgment to final salvation and continued faithfulness. Reformed readers more often treat it as the evaluation and reward of those already secure in Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
