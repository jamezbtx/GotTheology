Passage

2 Corinthians 5

Verses 16–21 · WEB

Since Christ has died and risen, Paul no longer judges anyone by worldly standards. Anyone in Christ is a new creation. God reconciled us to Himself through Christ and entrusted the ministry of reconciliation to His messengers, who plead with people to be reconciled to God. God made the sinless Christ to be sin for us so that in Him we become the righteousness of God. Both traditions treasure this as a summit of the gospel; they nuance how reconciliation reaches the world and how righteousness is given.

2 Corinthians 5:16

16Therefore we know no one according to the flesh from now on. Even though we have known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we know him so no more.

Arminian

We regard no one according to the flesh. Arminian readers see a new way of seeing people that flows from the cross. Once Paul judged Christ by worldly standards; now he sees through the lens of new creation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same reorientation. According to the flesh refers to a worldly assessment, not to the humanity of Jesus. The cross overturns worldly standards of worth.

2 Corinthians 5:17

17Therefore if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old things have passed away. Behold, all things have become new.

Arminian

Anyone in Christ is a new creation. Arminian readers rejoice that this new life is open to anyone who comes to Christ by faith. The change is real and transforming.

Reformed

Reformed readers see new creation as God’s decisive act in uniting believers to Christ. The old has passed away, and the new creation has broken into the present age.

2 Corinthians 5:18

18But all things are of God, who reconciled us to himself through Jesus Christ, and gave to us the ministry of reconciliation;

Arminian

All things are from God, who reconciled us to Himself through Christ. Arminian readers stress God’s initiative: reconciliation begins with Him, not with human effort.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same divine initiative. All things are of God, including the new creation and the ministry that announces it.

2 Corinthians 5:19

19namely, that God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not reckoning to them their trespasses, and having committed to us the word of reconciliation.

Arminian

God was in Christ reconciling the world to Himself. Arminian readers see world as including all people, for whom reconciliation is provided and offered. People enter it as they respond to the word of reconciliation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that world stresses scope beyond Israel and the grandeur of God’s work. Not counting trespasses describes God’s gracious justification of those reconciled, proclaimed through the gospel.

2 Corinthians 5:20

20We are therefore ambassadors on behalf of Christ, as though God were entreating by us: we beg you on behalf of Christ, be reconciled to God.

Arminian

We are ambassadors; God entreats through us: be reconciled to God. Arminian readers see a genuine plea addressed to people who can respond. God makes His appeal through human messengers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s call through the preached word. The appeal is sincere and urgent, and God uses it to bring His people to faith.

Key contrast Both affirm a sincere appeal from God through the gospel. Arminian readers often see the plea as showing that grace may be accepted or resisted. Reformed readers often see the plea as the means through which God effectively draws the elect.

2 Corinthians 5:21

21For him who knew no sin he made to be sin on our behalf, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God.

Arminian

God made the sinless one to be sin for us, that we might become God’s righteousness in Him. Arminian readers affirm Christ’s substitutionary death and the gift of righteousness in Him. Arminian views vary on how to describe imputation, but they agree that righteousness comes through union with Christ by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here a classic text for double imputation: our sin counted to Christ and His righteousness counted to us. Others in the tradition stress union with Christ, but all agree that righteousness is a gift, not an achievement.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
