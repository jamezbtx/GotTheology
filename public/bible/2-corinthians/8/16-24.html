Passage

2 Corinthians 8

Verses 16–24 · WEB

Paul commends Titus and two accompanying brothers entrusted with the collection. Congregational appointment, tested character, and shared oversight protect the gift’s integrity. Both traditions see honor before God and public accountability as belonging together in Christian service.

2 Corinthians 8:16

16But thanks be to God, who puts the same earnest care for you into the heart of Titus.

Arminian

Paul thanks God for putting earnest care for the Corinthians in Titus’s heart. Arminian readers see divine initiative enabling sincere personal concern. Both traditions credit God without treating Titus’s affection as unreal or his service as mechanical.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God working inwardly to produce pastoral care. Thanksgiving belongs to God even as Titus genuinely loves and serves the congregation. The gift is entrusted to people whose concern extends beyond the money itself.

2 Corinthians 8:17

17For he indeed accepted our exhortation, but being himself very earnest, he went out to you of his own accord.

Arminian

Titus accepts Paul’s encouragement and also acts from his own eagerness. Arminian readers see willing service rather than compulsion. Both traditions hold together God’s work in his heart and his own earnest decision to go.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the previous verse’s divine initiative expressed in Titus’s voluntary action. God’s work does not cancel personal purpose. Titus’s readiness reassures the Corinthians that he comes with genuine concern rather than reluctant compliance.

2 Corinthians 8:18

18We have sent together with him the brother whose praise in the Good News is known throughout all the assemblies.

Arminian

A brother respected for service in the Good News accompanies Titus. Arminian readers see shared responsibility helping the churches trust the mission. Paul does not name this brother, so the passage should not be used to assert a particular identity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see recognized character supporting the delegation’s credibility. The brother’s reputation concerns gospel service across the assemblies, not financial influence. Both traditions avoid identifying an unnamed messenger without evidence.

2 Corinthians 8:19

19Not only so, but he was also appointed by the assemblies to travel with us in this grace, which is served by us to the glory of the Lord himself, and to show our readiness.

Arminian

The assemblies appoint this brother to travel with the collection, joining communal authorization to service. Arminian readers see accountability protecting the gift and its purpose. Both traditions affirm that administration should honor the Lord and make willingness to help visible.

Reformed

Reformed readers see church participation in selecting a trusted representative. God’s glory is served through responsible arrangements, not through bypassing them. Shared oversight helps ensure that generous intentions lead to trustworthy delivery.

2 Corinthians 8:20

20We are avoiding this, that any man should blame us concerning this abundance which is administered by us.

Arminian

Paul takes precautions against blame in handling a substantial gift. Arminian readers see financial integrity requiring safeguards as well as good intentions. Both traditions recognize that accountable administration protects donors, recipients, and those entrusted with the funds.

Reformed

Reformed readers see deliberate oversight as wise stewardship, not distrust of God. Even apostolic ministry does not dispense with measures that prevent suspicion. Gifts for relief must be handled in ways that can withstand scrutiny.

2 Corinthians 8:21

21Having regard for honorable things, not only in the sight of the Lord, but also in the sight of men.

Arminian

Honorable conduct must be evident before people as well as before the Lord. Arminian readers see transparency as part of loving responsibility. Both traditions reject secrecy justified by claiming that God alone knows a leader’s heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers see inward integrity joined to public accountability. Human approval is not the ultimate standard, but avoidable suspicion should not be ignored. Faithful handling of money should be both honest and demonstrably trustworthy.

2 Corinthians 8:22

22We have sent with them our brother whom we have many times proved earnest in many things, but now much more earnest, by reason of the great confidence which he has in you.

Arminian

Another tested brother joins the delegation with renewed confidence in the Corinthians. Arminian readers see proven earnestness, not mere enthusiasm, fitting people for responsibility. Both traditions value character and experience when entrusting resources to others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see careful selection of a coworker whose diligence has been demonstrated repeatedly. Confidence in the congregation strengthens his eagerness. Paul commends tested service without supplying a name that readers should attempt to invent.

2 Corinthians 8:23

23As for Titus, he is my partner and fellow worker for you. As for our brothers, they are the apostles of the assemblies, the glory of Christ.

Arminian

Paul commends Titus as his partner and the other brothers as representatives of the assemblies. Arminian readers see cooperative service reflecting Christ’s honor. Here apostles describes the churches’ commissioned messengers, not a claim that these men share Paul’s apostolic office.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a delegation serving with both personal trust and congregational authorization. These messengers bring honor to Christ through faithful service. Both traditions distinguish their representative role here from the foundational authority of Christ’s apostles.

2 Corinthians 8:24

24Therefore show the proof of your love to them before the assemblies, and of our boasting on your behalf.

Arminian

The Corinthians are to make their love visible in receiving and supporting the delegates. Arminian readers see generosity confirming rather than merely professing affection. Both traditions recognize that Paul’s confidence invites a willing response before the wider church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see practical love bringing the appeal to its conclusion. The delegation gives the congregation an opportunity to demonstrate the sincerity Paul has commended. Public witness should encourage faithful service, not reduce giving to a performance for reputation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
