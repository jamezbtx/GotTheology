Passage

2 Corinthians 8

Verses 10–15 · WEB

Paul urges the Corinthians to complete the collection they began a year earlier, according to their available means. The aim is mutual provision rather than relief for others at their expense. His citation of Exodus 16:18 connects this sharing with God’s provision of manna so that none lacked.

2 Corinthians 8:10

10I give advice in this: it is expedient for you who were the first to start a year ago, not only to do, but also to be willing.

Arminian

Paul recalls the Corinthians’ earlier willingness and beginning of the collection a year before. Arminian readers see his advice encouraging them to fulfill a freely undertaken purpose. Both traditions value honoring commitments without turning past enthusiasm into grounds for shame.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wise pastoral counsel connecting intention and action. Paul does not dismiss their earlier willingness but urges it toward completion. Faithful stewardship includes sustaining a good purpose beyond its first excitement.

2 Corinthians 8:11

11But now complete the doing also, that as there was the readiness to be willing, so there may be the completion also out of your ability.

Arminian

Readiness should now become completed action within their ability. Arminian readers see responsible cooperation with grace in carrying out a generous purpose. Both traditions hear a call to follow-through rather than a demand to give resources they do not possess.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience expressed in finishing the work, not merely approving it. Paul links completion to available means, combining earnest encouragement with care for the givers. God-honoring service is concrete and proportionate.

2 Corinthians 8:12

12For if the readiness is there, it is acceptable according to what you have, not according to what you don’t have.

Arminian

A willing gift is accepted according to what a person has. Arminian readers see grace honoring genuine readiness rather than comparing amounts. Both traditions reject treating those with little as less faithful because they cannot match wealthier donors.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the giver’s real capacity shaping the contribution. Willingness matters, but it is not detached from practical action where means exist. Paul neither demands imaginary resources nor makes a large sum the measure of love.

2 Corinthians 8:13

13For this is not that others may be eased and you distressed,

Arminian

Paul does not seek relief for others by reducing the Corinthians to distress. Arminian readers see care for recipients and givers held together. Both traditions reject using the Macedonians’ extraordinary example to erase the safeguards in Paul’s appeal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the collection aimed at mutual care rather than merely transferring suffering from one congregation to another. Generosity should not become exploitation. The unfinished thought leads into the equality described in the next verse.

2 Corinthians 8:14

14but for equality. Your abundance at this present time supplies their lack, that their abundance also may become a supply for your lack, that there may be equality.

Arminian

Present abundance should meet present need, with openness to help flowing the other way if circumstances change. Arminian readers see reciprocal care across congregations. Equality here concerns meeting need through sharing, not a promise that every believer will have identical possessions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see material resources entrusted for service within the wider church. Giving creates mutual solidarity, not a permanent hierarchy of benefactors and dependents. Both traditions recognize that Paul’s appeal addresses real lack rather than merely praising generosity in principle.

2 Corinthians 8:15

15As it is written, “He who gathered much had nothing left over, and he who gathered little had no lack.”

Arminian

Paul cites Exodus 16:18, drawing on the manna account to illuminate provision without lack. Arminian readers see sharing as a way to honor God’s care for all his people. The citation supports the collection’s aim of meeting need.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the manna passage grounding the appeal in God’s provision for his people. Abundance is not a warrant for indifference while others lack necessities. Both traditions read the citation as support for generous mutual care, not a promise of limitless wealth.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
