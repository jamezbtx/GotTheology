Passage

2 Corinthians 8

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Paul turns to the collection for the needy saints in Jerusalem, presenting the Macedonian assemblies’ generosity amid poverty as God’s grace at work. He encourages Corinth to complete its participation willingly, grounding the appeal in Christ’s self-giving. Both traditions see giving as a response to grace rather than a way to purchase it.

2 Corinthians 8:1

1Moreover, brothers, we make known to you the grace of God which has been given in the assemblies of Macedonia,

Arminian

Paul introduces the Macedonian generosity as God’s grace at work. Arminian readers see grace enabling willing participation in care for the saints. Both traditions locate the source of generosity in God’s gift rather than in a congregation’s wealth or superiority.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s grace producing generous service in the Macedonian assemblies. The emphasis falls on what God has given before what people contribute. Their example encourages Corinth without turning giving into a way to earn divine favor.

2 Corinthians 8:2

2how in a severe ordeal of affliction, the abundance of their joy and their deep poverty abounded to the riches of their generosity.

Arminian

Affliction and deep poverty have not extinguished the Macedonians’ joy or generosity. Arminian readers see grace sustaining costly love amid hardship. Both traditions reject measuring spiritual faithfulness by prosperity or assuming that poverty makes a believer unable to serve.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace bearing unexpected fruit where material resources are scarce. Joy and generosity coexist with real suffering, rather than proving that suffering has disappeared. The example is not a promise that generous believers will become wealthy.

2 Corinthians 8:3

3For according to their power, I testify, yes and beyond their power, they gave of their own accord,

Arminian

The Macedonians gave voluntarily, even beyond what Paul expected from their means. Arminian readers emphasize the willing response enabled by grace. Their exceptional generosity is testimony to love, not permission to pressure vulnerable people into unsustainable gifts.

Reformed

Reformed readers see divine grace and willing human action together, not as rivals. The Macedonians freely participate rather than submit to coercion. Both traditions read this alongside Paul’s later concern that giving be according to ability.

2 Corinthians 8:4

4begging us with much entreaty to receive this grace and the fellowship in the service to the saints.

Arminian

The Macedonians ask to share in service to fellow believers. Arminian readers see giving as a privilege of fellowship rather than a fee imposed by leaders. Both traditions recognize the collection as practical solidarity with needy saints, not a contest for prestige.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace moving the Macedonians to seek participation in the relief effort. Fellowship takes material form when believers support one another. Their eagerness contrasts with a view of giving as merely losing possessions.

2 Corinthians 8:5

5This was not as we had expected, but first they gave their own selves to the Lord, and to us through the will of God.

Arminian

Their gift begins with giving themselves to the Lord, then cooperating with Paul’s ministry under God’s will. Arminian readers see personal dedication taking shape in service. Both traditions subordinate loyalty to ministers to the prior claim of the Lord.

Reformed

Reformed readers see generosity flowing from belonging to God. Financial service is an expression of whole-life devotion, not a substitute for it. Their commitment to Paul serves God’s will and does not grant leaders unrestricted authority over them.

2 Corinthians 8:6

6So we urged Titus, that as he had made a beginning before, so he would also complete in you this grace.

Arminian

Titus is urged to help complete the collection he had already begun among them. Arminian readers see gracious intention calling for practical follow-through. Both traditions recognize organized pastoral encouragement as appropriate without confusing it with coercive fundraising.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s grace expressed through patient ministry and responsible planning. Titus’s work helps the congregation carry its commitment into action. Calling the collection grace keeps the focus on thankful service rather than buying God’s approval.

2 Corinthians 8:7

7But as you abound in everything—in faith, utterance, knowledge, all earnestness, and in your love to us—see that you also abound in this grace.

Arminian

Paul encourages the Corinthians to add generosity to their other gifts and commitments. Arminian readers see love needing material expression as well as speech and knowledge. Both traditions reject a spirituality rich in religious ability but indifferent to neighbors’ needs.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the collection belonging within the full life of grace. Faith, knowledge, and earnestness should bear fruit in generous service. Paul’s encouragement dignifies their gifts while calling them to practical maturity.

2 Corinthians 8:8

8I speak not by way of commandment, but as proving through the earnestness of others the sincerity also of your love.

Arminian

Paul tests the sincerity of love through others’ earnestness rather than imposing a command for a particular contribution. Arminian readers stress willing love, not compelled performance. Both traditions recognize that pastoral encouragement may challenge people without manipulating them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see generosity as evidence of love rather than a price paid for acceptance. The Macedonian example encourages a genuine response, not a competition. Paul distinguishes this appeal from enforcing a fixed contribution by apostolic command.

2 Corinthians 8:9

9For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that you through his poverty might become rich.

Arminian

Christ’s gracious self-giving supplies the deepest reason for generosity. Arminian readers see his becoming poor for their sake as an invitation to respond with costly love. The riches received through him are not a guarantee of material wealth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s willing humiliation and saving self-giving as the foundation of Christian generosity. His grace first enriches believers, who then share with others. Both traditions see more than moral example here: service flows from receiving Christ’s saving gift.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
