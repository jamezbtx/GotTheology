Passage

2 Corinthians 13

Verses 5–10 · WEB

Paul turns the Corinthians’ demand for proof of his ministry into an invitation to examine their own faith and recognise Christ’s presence among them. His prayer is for their good conduct and restoration, even if that leaves him appearing weak. The authority he received from the Lord exists to build up the community, so he writes now to avoid a severe confrontation later.

2 Corinthians 13:5

5Examine your own selves, whether you are in the faith. Test your own selves. Or don’t you know about your own selves, that Jesus Christ is in you?—unless indeed you are disqualified.

Arminian

Paul turns their demand to test him back upon them: they must examine whether they are in the faith and recognise Christ among them. Arminian readers stress present trust in Christ and a life responsive to his grace. Many connect this warning with the possibility of abandoning faith, though the verse does not describe a particular person losing salvation. Examination should lead to repentance and renewed assurance, not endless anxiety.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-examination as a test of genuine faith, not a denial that believers may know Christ dwells in them. Final unbelief would expose a false profession, while warnings help preserve true believers. Paul also appeals to the congregation’s own experience of Christ as evidence relevant to his ministry. The verse must not be reduced to a demand for flawless conduct as proof of acceptance.

Key contrast Both call for honest examination grounded in Christ rather than moral perfection or perpetual doubt. Arminian readers commonly connect the warning with the need to continue in faith and the possibility of apostasy; Reformed readers distinguish false profession from saving faith that God preserves. The immediate argument tests the Corinthians’ faith in relation to their challenge to Paul and does not by itself settle the whole perseverance debate.

2 Corinthians 13:6

6But I hope that you will know that we aren’t disqualified.

Arminian

Paul hopes their self-examination will also help them recognise that his ministry has not failed the test. Arminian readers see their faith and his service as related: recognising Christ’s work among them should challenge their rejection of his messenger. The concern is faithful ministry, not a claim that Paul is beyond correction.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect this hope with the demand for proof in verse 3. Christ’s work through Paul is not invalidated by his unimpressive appearance. Both traditions distinguish approval in this ministerial dispute from speculation about a hidden decree concerning particular individuals.

2 Corinthians 13:7

7Now I pray to God that you do no evil; not that we may appear approved, but that you may do that which is honorable, though we may seem to have failed.

Arminian

Paul prays for their good conduct even if it leaves him looking unsuccessful. Arminian readers stress that divine help and responsible action belong together: he asks God to enable the response he desires. Their restoration matters more than proving his power by exercising discipline.

Reformed

Reformed readers see dependence on God expressed in prayer for the congregation’s holiness. Paul would rather seem weak than establish his standing through a painful confrontation. Both traditions find a pastoral priority here: the people’s good takes precedence over the minister’s reputation.

2 Corinthians 13:8

8For we can do nothing against the truth, but for the truth.

Arminian

Paul’s authority serves the truth rather than standing above it. Arminian readers see a limit on every claim to spiritual power: it cannot properly be used to oppose the gospel or protect personal prestige. His hope is that their obedience will make corrective action unnecessary.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand apostolic power as commissioned for the truth, not available for arbitrary ends. The statement describes ministry’s allegiance rather than claiming that ministers are incapable of wrongdoing. Both traditions affirm that church authority must be judged by its faithfulness to Christ’s message.

2 Corinthians 13:9

9For we rejoice when we are weak and you are strong. We also pray for this: your becoming perfect.

Arminian

Paul rejoices if their strength allows him to appear weak and prays for their restoration. Arminian readers understand becoming perfect here as being put right and brought to maturity, not a demand for instant flawlessness. Grace is sought in prayer so that the community can respond faithfully.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the congregation’s maturity as God’s gift and the goal of pastoral labour. Paul’s apparent weakness is welcome if it means no severe intervention is needed. Both traditions read this prayer in light of the community’s need for repair, rather than treating it as a complete account of Christian perfection.

2 Corinthians 13:10

10For this cause I write these things while absent, that I may not deal sharply when present, according to the authority which the Lord gave me for building up and not for tearing down.

Arminian

The letter aims to secure repentance before the visit, so that sharp correction will not be necessary. Arminian readers stress the opportunity to respond to warning and the constructive purpose of authority. Even severe measures must seek restoration rather than the satisfaction of defeating an opponent.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasise authority as a gift from the Lord for building his church. Written warning is itself a pastoral means toward that end. Like Arminian readers, they distinguish necessary correction from destructive leadership that treats people as obstacles to a minister’s success.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
