Passage

2 Corinthians 13

Verses 1–4 · WEB

Preparing for a third visit to Corinth, Paul warns that persistent sin will receive serious attention and invokes the requirement for reliable witnesses. His readers have demanded proof that Christ speaks through him, but he locates apostolic power in the pattern of the crucified and risen Christ rather than outward impressiveness. The warning continues his aim of repentance and restoration, not personal vindication at any cost.

2 Corinthians 13:1

1This is the third time I am coming to you. “At the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word be established.”

Arminian

Paul prepares for a third visit by invoking the requirement for two or three witnesses. Arminian readers see responsible discipline that tests allegations rather than acting on rumour or personal resentment. The coming confrontation must serve truth and repentance, not the apostle’s pride.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see church discipline governed by a scriptural standard of evidence. Paul’s authority does not remove the need for accountable judgment. Both traditions can affirm that serious accusations require careful examination rather than unquestioning acceptance of a leader’s claims.

2 Corinthians 13:2

2I have warned previously, and I warn again, as when I was present the second time, so now, being absent, I write to those who have sinned before now and to all the rest that if I come again, I will not spare,

Arminian

Repeated warning gives those persisting in sin an opportunity to repent before Paul arrives. Arminian readers stress the responsibility to respond to correction through grace. Not sparing concerns the seriousness of apostolic discipline; the verse does not specify every measure Paul might take.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the warning as a means of correction within Christ’s care for his church. Patience does not mean that persistent wrongdoing will be ignored. Both traditions distinguish this threatened discipline from a licence for vindictive or unaccountable treatment of offenders.

2 Corinthians 13:3

3seeing that you seek a proof of Christ who speaks in me who is not weak, but is powerful in you.

Arminian

The Corinthians’ demand for proof of Christ speaking through Paul will be answered by Christ’s power among them. Arminian readers stress that rejecting faithful correction is not merely a dispute over Paul’s personal standing. Spiritual authority is accountable to Christ, not measured by an impressive public image.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasise Christ’s effective authority exercised through his appointed messenger. The congregation’s experience of Christ should challenge its dismissal of Paul’s apparent weakness. Like Arminian readers, they should not turn this apostolic claim into a blanket exemption of later leaders from scrutiny.

2 Corinthians 13:4

4For he was crucified through weakness, yet he lives through the power of God. For we also are weak in him, but we will live with him through the power of God toward you.

Arminian

Christ’s crucifixion and risen life supply the pattern for Paul’s apparently weak ministry. Arminian readers see dependence on divine power rather than self-assertion, with Christ’s resurrection grounding both courage and correction. The weakness of the cross does not mean Christ lacked divine dignity or that suffering itself earns authority.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the crucified and risen Christ shaping apostolic ministry. God’s power is displayed through weakness, yet that weakness does not exclude decisive action for the church’s good. Paul’s confidence concerns Christ working through him, not a personal right to dominate.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
