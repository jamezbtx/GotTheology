Passage

2 Corinthians 13

Verses 11–14 · WEB

Paul closes his difficult letter with calls to restoration, encouragement, shared purpose, and peace. Mutual greetings place the Corinthians within the wider fellowship of God’s people. The final blessing names the grace of Christ, the love of God, and the fellowship of the Spirit as the source of their common life.

2 Corinthians 13:11

11Finally, brothers, rejoice! Be perfected. Be comforted. Be of the same mind. Live in peace, and the God of love and peace will be with you.

Arminian

Paul’s final appeals gather the letter’s pastoral aims: joy, restoration, encouragement, agreement, and peace. Arminian readers stress the grace-enabled practices by which the community answers this appeal. God’s promised presence sustains their response; it is not a wage earned by achieving perfect harmony.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see these commands as the fitting life of a community cared for by the God of love and peace. Restoration and shared purpose grow through his grace, not through suppression of every difference. The promised presence encourages reconciliation without making peace an excuse to ignore wrongdoing.

2 Corinthians 13:12

12Greet one another with a holy kiss.

Arminian

The holy kiss gives visible expression to restored fellowship. Arminian readers stress sincere, holy affection rather than a merely formal greeting. Applications of the physical practice vary, but its purpose excludes coercion, impropriety, or disregard for another person’s boundaries.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the greeting as an embodied sign of belonging to one family in Christ. Some retain the practice, while others express its purpose through culturally fitting forms of welcome. Both traditions should preserve its holiness and mutual affection rather than use it to compel unwanted contact.

2 Corinthians 13:13

13All the saints greet you.

Arminian

Greetings from all the saints place the Corinthians within a fellowship wider than their local disputes. Arminian readers see encouragement to receive one another as people set apart for God. Saint names their shared belonging in Christ, not an elite class of unusually accomplished Christians.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the saints as God’s people, whose common identity extends beyond Corinth. The greeting quietly resists the isolation that factional conflict can produce. Both traditions hear a reminder that reconciliation matters to the wider church as well as to this congregation.

2 Corinthians 13:14

14The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, God’s love, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all. Amen.

Arminian

Paul blesses the whole community with Christ’s grace, God’s love, and the Spirit’s fellowship. Arminian readers affirm the shared Trinitarian pattern and see divine generosity sustaining the life of faith and reconciliation. The benediction is a prayerful blessing for all addressed, not a declaration that every individual is saved regardless of faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise confess the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together as the source of the church’s life. Grace, love, and fellowship belong together rather than being divided into rival divine purposes. Both traditions find here a fitting conclusion to the appeal for restoration: the community depends on the triune God, not on its own resources.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
