Passage

2 Corinthians 10

Verses 12–18 · WEB

Paul refuses to measure himself by self-commending rivals. He boasts only within the sphere God assigned him, which includes Corinth, and hopes to carry the gospel further. Quoting Jeremiah, he insists that anyone who boasts should boast in the Lord, because only the Lord’s commendation counts. Both traditions affirm this rejection of self-promotion in ministry.

2 Corinthians 10:12

12For we are not bold to number or compare ourselves with some of those who commend themselves. But they themselves, measuring themselves by themselves, and comparing themselves with themselves, are without understanding.

Arminian

Paul refuses to compare himself with those who commend themselves; measuring themselves by themselves, they lack understanding. Arminian readers see the folly of using oneself as the standard.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same folly and note that Paul’s rivals are not clearly identified. Scholars across traditions debate who they were, and the text does not settle it. The point is that self-made standards are blind.

2 Corinthians 10:13

13But we will not boast beyond proper limits, but within the boundaries with which God appointed to us, which reach even to you.

Arminian

Paul will boast only within the limits God assigned him, which include Corinth. Arminian readers see his ministry boundaries as God’s calling, which Paul honors by staying within it.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God “appointed” or measured out the sphere of Paul’s ministry. His confidence rests on God’s assignment rather than his own ambition. Both see calling as the basis for legitimate ministry.

2 Corinthians 10:14

14For we don’t stretch ourselves too much, as though we didn’t reach to you. For we came even as far as to you with the Good News of Christ,

Arminian

Paul is not overreaching, since he was the first to come to Corinth with the gospel. Arminian readers see this as a factual answer to rivals who were claiming ground he had already planted.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree and note Paul’s principle of pioneering where Christ has not been named, as in Romans 15:20. Both read this as historical defense of Paul’s founding role at Corinth.

2 Corinthians 10:15

15not boasting beyond proper limits in other men’s labors, but having hope that as your faith grows, we will be abundantly enlarged by you in our sphere of influence,

Arminian

Paul does not boast in other people’s work, but hopes that as their faith grows his work will expand through them. Arminian readers note that Paul’s further mission depends partly on the Corinthians’ growth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same connection and stress that growth in faith is God’s work in them. A healthy church becomes a base for wider mission. This is shared ground.

2 Corinthians 10:16

16so as to preach the Good News even to the parts beyond you, not to boast in what someone else has already done.

Arminian

He hopes to preach in regions beyond them without boasting in work already done by others. Arminian readers see a missionary heart eager to reach the unreached.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a missionary vision, driven by the conviction that God gathers his people from every place through preaching. Both traditions read this verse as a model for mission.

2 Corinthians 10:17

17But “he who boasts, let him boast in the Lord.”

Arminian

“He who boasts, let him boast in the Lord,” drawn from Jeremiah 9:24 and also quoted in 1 Corinthians 1:31. Arminian readers see this as the right posture for all ministry: credit goes to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same principle, and they often connect it to salvation itself as wholly God’s work, leaving no room for human boasting. Both traditions affirm this verse fully; they differ elsewhere on how grace and human response relate.

2 Corinthians 10:18

18For it isn’t he who commends himself who is approved, but whom the Lord commends.

Arminian

It is not the one who commends himself who is approved, but the one the Lord commends. Arminian readers see this as a call to seek God’s approval through faithful ministry rather than self-promotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same truth and stress that final approval belongs to the Lord’s judgment, not human applause. Paul closes the chapter by placing his whole ministry before God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
