Passage

2 Corinthians 10

Verses 1–6 · WEB

Paul begins a forceful defense of his ministry against critics who say he is bold only in letters and lives by worldly standards. He answers that his warfare is spiritual, with weapons empowered by God to demolish proud arguments and bring every thought captive to Christ. Both traditions read this as a call to spiritual, not worldly, means in ministry.

2 Corinthians 10:1

1Now I Paul, myself, entreat you by the humility and gentleness of Christ, I who in your presence am lowly among you, but being absent am bold toward you.

Arminian

Paul appeals to them by the meekness and gentleness of Christ, answering the charge that he is timid in person but bold in letters. Arminian readers see him modeling Christlike gentleness even while defending his authority.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see gentleness as the character of Christ that shapes apostolic ministry. Chapters 10–13 have a sharper tone than earlier chapters, and scholars in both traditions debate whether they were once a separate letter. That question is about composition, not doctrine.

2 Corinthians 10:2

2Yes, I beg you that I may not, when present, show courage with the confidence with which I intend to be bold against some, who consider us to be walking according to the flesh.

Arminian

Paul begs them not to force him to be bold when present against those who say he lives “according to the flesh.” Arminian readers see Paul giving the church a chance to respond before discipline is needed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same pastoral restraint. “According to the flesh” here means by merely human standards and motives. Both read the verse as a warning given in hope of repentance.

2 Corinthians 10:3

3For though we walk in the flesh, we don’t wage war according to the flesh;

Arminian

We live in the flesh, but we do not wage war according to the flesh. Arminian readers note that Paul admits his human weakness while denying that his ministry runs on human methods.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same distinction between living in a human body and living by worldly principles. The church’s battle is spiritual. Both traditions agree here.

2 Corinthians 10:4

4for the weapons of our warfare are not of the flesh, but mighty before God to the throwing down of strongholds,

Arminian

Their weapons are not fleshly but powerful before God to destroy strongholds. Arminian readers see the weapons as the gospel, truth, and prayer, which God empowers to overcome resistance.

Reformed

Reformed readers also identify the weapons with the word and prayer, and stress “before God”: the power belongs to God, not the messenger. The “strongholds” are false arguments and proud resistance to God, not people.

2 Corinthians 10:5

5throwing down imaginations and every high thing that is exalted against the knowledge of God and bringing every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ,

Arminian

They demolish arguments and every lofty thing raised against the knowledge of God, taking every thought captive to obey Christ. Arminian readers see persuasion and truth confronting false ideas, inviting people to submit freely to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see here the lordship of Christ over all thought, including how Christians think about every area of life. Both agree the verse is about bringing human pride and reasoning under Christ, not about physical force.

2 Corinthians 10:6

6and being in readiness to avenge all disobedience when your obedience is made full.

Arminian

Paul is ready to punish disobedience once the church’s obedience is complete. Arminian readers note that discipline is aimed at holdouts after the congregation itself has responded.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the same sequence and see church discipline as a real, God-given tool for the church’s health. Both see discipline as a last resort that serves restoration and order.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
