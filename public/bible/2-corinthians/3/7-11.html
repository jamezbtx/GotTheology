Passage

2 Corinthians 3

Verses 7–11 · WEB

Paul develops the contrast between the letter and the Spirit through Moses’ radiant face after receiving the covenant at Sinai. The former ministry truly possessed glory, yet its condemnation and passing character are surpassed by the righteousness and enduring glory of the new covenant. Arminian and Reformed readers alike affirm the law’s divine origin and the greater fullness of salvation in Christ.

2 Corinthians 3:7

7But if the service of death, written engraved on stones, came with glory, so that the children of Israel could not look steadfastly on the face of Moses for the glory of his face, which was passing away,

Arminian

Paul recalls Moses’ radiant face after receiving the covenant at Sinai. Calling it a service of death describes the law’s condemnation of sinners, not an evil origin for the law. Arminian readers affirm both its divine glory and its inability by itself to impart life.

Reformed

The stone tablets identify the Mosaic covenant, which truly came with God-given glory. Reformed readers distinguish the holiness of God’s law from its condemning effect on sinners; the fading radiance prepares Paul’s comparison with the greater glory of the Spirit’s ministry.

2 Corinthians 3:8

8won’t service of the Spirit be with much more glory?

Arminian

The argument moves from the lesser glory to the greater: if the former ministry had glory, the Spirit’s life-giving ministry has much more. Arminian readers see the promised inward work of God now displayed through the gospel.

Reformed

The Spirit’s ministry surpasses the glory associated with the stone tablets. Reformed readers emphasize the richer manifestation of God’s saving work in the new covenant, not the absence of grace or the Spirit among earlier believers.

2 Corinthians 3:9

9For if the service of condemnation has glory, the service of righteousness exceeds much more in glory.

Arminian

The contrast now names condemnation and righteousness. Arminian readers understand the gospel to bring right standing with God through faith in Christ, which the law’s verdict against sin cannot supply. Both traditions locate this gift in grace rather than earned obedience.

Reformed

The law exposes guilt, while the gospel ministers righteousness through Christ. Reformed readers connect this with justification freely received by faith; the greater glory belongs to God’s saving provision, not to greater human achievement.

2 Corinthians 3:10

10For most certainly that which has been made glorious has not been made glorious in this respect, by reason of the glory that surpasses.

Arminian

The earlier glory is eclipsed by the surpassing glory of the new covenant. Arminian readers take this as a comparison of brightness, not a denial that God truly revealed himself through Moses.

Reformed

Paul’s comparison is so strong that the former glory seems without glory beside the latter. Reformed readers affirm the reality of both revelations while emphasizing the fullness now manifested in Christ.

2 Corinthians 3:11

11For if that which passes away was with glory, much more that which remains is in glory.

Arminian

What passes away is contrasted with what remains, giving the church a lasting ground of hope. Arminian readers understand the new covenant as God’s enduring provision in Christ; its permanence calls for continuing trust in him.

Reformed

The temporary Mosaic administration gives way to the enduring new covenant. Reformed readers see fulfillment rather than a failure of God’s earlier purpose: the glory of Christ’s ministry will not be displaced by another covenantal order.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
