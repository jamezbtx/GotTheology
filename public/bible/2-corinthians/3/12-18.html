Passage

2 Corinthians 3

Verses 12–18 · WEB

The new covenant’s lasting glory gives Paul boldness. Drawing on Moses’ veil, he describes hardened understanding at the reading of Moses and the removal of that veil in turning to the Lord. The Spirit brings liberty, and all believers behold the Lord’s glory and are transformed. Both traditions affirm the necessity of divine illumination; they differ over how grace brings about the turning to faith.

2 Corinthians 3:12

12Having therefore such a hope, we use great boldness of speech,

Arminian

The enduring glory just described gives Paul hope and boldness in proclaiming the gospel. Arminian readers see confidence in God’s promise producing open, courageous ministry rather than concealment or self-reliance.

Reformed

Paul’s bold speech rests on the lasting character of the new covenant. Reformed readers see assurance in God’s saving work generating plain proclamation; the messenger’s confidence belongs to the message, not his personal strength.

2 Corinthians 3:13

13and not as Moses, who put a veil on his face so that the children of Israel wouldn’t look steadfastly on the end of that which was passing away.

Arminian

Paul draws on Moses’ veil in Exodus 34 to contrast concealed, passing glory with the openness of gospel ministry. Arminian readers follow this covenantal comparison without treating Moses as a false messenger or the law as contrary to God’s purpose.

Reformed

The veil prevents Israel from gazing at the end of the passing glory. Reformed readers see Paul using the Exodus scene to depict the limited, temporary character of the Mosaic administration alongside the open revelation now given in Christ.

2 Corinthians 3:14

14But their minds were hardened, for until this very day at the reading of the old covenant the same veil remains, because in Christ it passes away.

Arminian

The veil becomes an image of hardened understanding when the old covenant is read apart from Christ. Arminian readers see unbelief that only grace can overcome, and affirm that Christ, rather than possession of the written text alone, brings understanding.

Reformed

Paul moves from a veil over Moses’ face to a veil over understanding. Reformed readers emphasize the need for divine illumination to recognize the fulfillment of the old covenant in Christ. The veil’s removal is found in him, not in natural interpretive ability.

2 Corinthians 3:15

15But to this day, when Moses is read, a veil lies on their heart.

Arminian

The reading of Moses points to the public reading of the law in Jewish worship. Arminian readers understand the heart’s veil as failure to recognize Christ in that reading; the image concerns unbelief, not an inherent defect in Jewish identity or in Scripture.

Reformed

The veil lies on the heart rather than on the text of Moses. Reformed readers locate the obstacle in spiritual understanding and affirm the need for inward illumination. Paul’s argument concerns receiving Christ, not a claim of ethnic superiority.

2 Corinthians 3:16

16But whenever someone turns to the Lord, the veil is taken away.

Arminian

Paul adapts the image of Moses turning to the Lord: the veil is removed when someone turns to him. Classical Arminian readers understand this turning as repentance and faith made possible by prior, resistible grace; God removes the veil as that grace-enabled response is made.

Reformed

Turning to the Lord brings the removal of the veil, entirely through divine mercy. Reformed readers understand the turn itself as the fruit of the Spirit’s effectual renewal: God overcomes spiritual blindness and thereby brings the person willingly to faith.

Key contrast Both traditions attribute the removal of spiritual blindness to God. Classical Arminian readers see turning as a response enabled but not determined by prior grace; Reformed readers see willing turning as the result of effectual renewal that overcomes blindness. The difference concerns how grace brings about faith, not whether grace is necessary.

2 Corinthians 3:17

17Now the Lord is the Spirit; and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty.

Arminian

The Lord’s liberating presence is experienced through the Spirit in the new covenant. Arminian readers see freedom from condemnation and veiled understanding for a life of willing obedience, not permission to disregard God’s will. This unity of divine action preserves the distinction between Christ and the Spirit.

Reformed

Paul closely joins the Lord’s presence with the Spirit’s work of liberation. Reformed readers see freedom from condemnation and spiritual bondage, enabling service to God. This unity of saving action does not erase the personal distinction between Christ and the Spirit.

2 Corinthians 3:18

18But we all, with unveiled face seeing the glory of the Lord as in a mirror, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory, even as from the Lord, the Spirit.

Arminian

Unlike the restricted access pictured around Moses, all believers behold the Lord’s glory with unveiled faces. Arminian readers see transformation into Christ’s likeness as the Spirit’s gracious work in which believers actively participate through continuing faith and obedience; holiness is not self-produced.

Reformed

All believers share access to the Lord’s glory and are progressively transformed into his image. Reformed readers see sanctification flowing from the Spirit through union with Christ, engaging believers in active faith and obedience. Both traditions attribute the transformation to the Lord rather than human merit.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
