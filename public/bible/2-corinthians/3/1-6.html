Passage

2 Corinthians 3

Verses 1–6 · WEB

Continuing his defense of sincere gospel ministry, Paul asks whether he needs letters of recommendation. The Corinthians themselves are Christ’s living letter, written by the Spirit through apostolic service. The imagery moves from stone tablets to renewed hearts and introduces the new covenant. Both traditions affirm that competence and spiritual life come from God through Christ, not from unaided human ability.

2 Corinthians 3:1

1Are we beginning again to commend ourselves? Or do we need, as do some, letters of commendation to you or from you?

Arminian

Paul answers the implied charge of self-promotion by asking whether he needs letters of recommendation. Arminian readers see the Corinthians’ changed lives, rather than credentials alone, as evidence of faithful gospel ministry.

Reformed

Letters of recommendation provided recognition for traveling ministers. Reformed readers see Paul appealing beyond such documents to God’s work among the Corinthians; his defense concerns the authenticity of his ministry, not personal prestige.

2 Corinthians 3:2

2You are our letter, written in our hearts, known and read by all men,

Arminian

The congregation itself is a public letter, held affectionately in the apostles’ hearts. Arminian readers see a community responding to grace whose visible life commends the gospel to others.

Reformed

The Corinthians are a living testimony rather than a paper credential. Reformed readers emphasize that God’s work in the congregation authenticates the ministry, while Paul’s reference to his heart expresses enduring pastoral affection.

2 Corinthians 3:3

3being revealed that you are a letter of Christ, served by us, written not with ink, but with the Spirit of the living God; not in tablets of stone, but in tablets that are hearts of flesh.

Arminian

Christ authors the letter, the apostles serve it, and the Spirit writes it on human hearts. The contrast recalls the stone tablets and the prophetic promise of inward renewal; Arminian readers affirm that genuine transformation depends on divine grace, not human instruction alone.

Reformed

Christ is the author and the apostles are servants, not the source of spiritual life. Reformed readers connect the hearts of flesh with God’s promise of inward renewal: the Spirit accomplishes what an external inscription cannot. Both traditions affirm the necessity of this inward work.

2 Corinthians 3:4

4Such confidence we have through Christ toward God,

Arminian

Paul’s confidence is directed toward God through Christ, not toward his own standing. Arminian readers ground assurance about ministry in Christ’s mediation and God’s gracious action among those who hear.

Reformed

Christ mediates Paul’s confidence before God. Reformed readers see this as dependence on divine faithfulness rather than confidence in apostolic ability or the congregation’s outward success.

2 Corinthians 3:5

5not that we are sufficient of ourselves to account anything as from ourselves; but our sufficiency is from God,

Arminian

Paul denies that he can claim independent competence for his ministry. Classical Arminian readers affirm that even the ability to respond to God and serve him depends on prior grace; willing service never makes grace unnecessary.

Reformed

Paul traces his competence entirely to God rather than to natural ability. Reformed readers see ministry as dependent on divine calling and enablement, with the apostle’s real labor flowing from what God supplies.

2 Corinthians 3:6

6who also made us sufficient as servants of a new covenant, not of the letter but of the Spirit. For the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life.

Arminian

The immediate contrast is between the written covenant that condemns sinners and the Spirit who gives new-covenant life, not between literal reading and spiritual interpretation. Arminian readers affirm that the Spirit alone gives life, understanding saving renewal as received through faith enabled by prevenient, resistible grace.

Reformed

God equips his servants for the new covenant, whose life comes from the Spirit rather than the condemning letter. Reformed readers likewise affirm the harmony of Scripture and the Spirit; they understand saving renewal as the Spirit’s effectual work producing faith, not merely enabling a response.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm that only the Spirit gives saving life. Classical Arminian readers understand renewal as received through faith enabled by prevenient, resistible grace; Reformed readers understand effectual renewal as producing that faith. The verse names the giver of life without detailing the order of conversion.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
