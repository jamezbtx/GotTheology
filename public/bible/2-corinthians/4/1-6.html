Passage

2 Corinthians 4

Verses 1–6 · WEB

Continuing chapter 3’s account of the new-covenant ministry, Paul defends open proclamation without deceit. The gospel’s veiling reflects unbelief and satanic blindness, not a hidden or manipulated message. Paul proclaims Christ as Lord, and God gives the light of his glory in Christ. Both traditions affirm the necessity of divine illumination; they differ over whether saving illumination can be resisted.

2 Corinthians 4:1

1Therefore, seeing we have this ministry, even as we obtained mercy, we don’t faint.

Arminian

Paul’s confidence follows the Spirit-given ministry described in chapter 3. Arminian readers see mercy, not personal merit, as the source of his calling and the strength for his continuing faithfulness amid discouragement.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise ground Paul’s ministry in undeserved mercy. God’s gracious appointment and sustaining power explain why hardship does not lead him to abandon his task.

2 Corinthians 4:2

2But we have renounced the hidden things of shame, not walking in craftiness nor handling the word of God deceitfully, but by the manifestation of the truth commending ourselves to every man’s conscience in the sight of God.

Arminian

Paul defends an open ministry that refuses manipulation and distortion of God’s word. Arminian readers emphasize truthful proclamation addressed to responsible hearers, whose consciences are engaged before God rather than bypassed by deceit.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that divine power never excuses dishonest preaching. Paul manifests the truth openly, entrusting its results to God while appealing to every hearer’s conscience under God’s scrutiny.

2 Corinthians 4:3

3Even if our Good News is veiled, it is veiled in those who are dying,

Arminian

The veiled gospel recalls the veil imagery of chapter 3. Arminian readers understand the condition of those perishing in connection with unbelief, not a defect in the gospel; saving understanding requires grace rather than natural insight.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the gospel’s clarity from the spiritual blindness of those perishing. Faithful proclamation does not by itself remove the veil; sinners need God to open their understanding.

2 Corinthians 4:4

4in whom the god of this world has blinded the minds of the unbelieving, that the light of the Good News of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God, should not dawn on them.

Arminian

The god of this world is Satan, who blinds unbelieving minds to Christ’s glory as God’s image. Arminian readers connect this darkness with culpable unbelief and resistance to God’s enabling grace; Satan’s activity neither makes him God’s equal nor removes human responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise identify Satan as the blinding enemy and unbelief as culpable. They understand this darkness within God’s sovereign judgment: he passes some by, leaving them in their sin, while Satan blinds them; only effectual grace overcomes that blindness.

Key contrast Both affirm satanic blindness and culpable unbelief. Arminian readers relate continued blindness to resistance to enabling grace; Reformed readers relate it to God’s passing some by in judgment, leaving them in unbelief while Satan blinds them.

2 Corinthians 4:5

5For we don’t preach ourselves, but Christ Jesus as Lord, and ourselves as your servants for Jesus’ sake,

Arminian

Christ Jesus, not the preacher’s importance, is the message; apostolic authority takes the form of service for Jesus’ sake. Arminian readers join Reformed readers in seeing Christ’s lordship as the reason ministers serve rather than dominate the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise place Christ’s lordship at the center of proclamation. Paul’s servant role rules out self-promotion: the messenger belongs to Christ and labors for the congregation’s good.

2 Corinthians 4:6

6seeing it is God who said, “Light will shine out of darkness,” who has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Paul likens God’s illumination of hearts to the creation of light from darkness. Arminian readers affirm that saving knowledge begins with God’s initiative, not unaided human choice; they understand enlightening grace as enabling a faith-response that can be resisted. Christ reveals God’s glory.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the creation analogy as expressing effectual illumination: God’s sovereign word brings saving sight where there was darkness, producing faith rather than merely making it possible. The light is knowledge of God’s glory in Christ, not confidence in the messenger.

Key contrast Both affirm that God must give saving light. Arminian readers understand enlightening grace as enabling faith without making the response inevitable; Reformed readers understand saving illumination as effectually producing faith, like God’s creative command bringing light.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
