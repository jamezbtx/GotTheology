Passage

2 Corinthians 4

Verses 7–15 · WEB

Paul explains how the gospel’s treasure is carried by fragile apostles. Pressure, persecution, and repeated exposure to death display Jesus’ life rather than discredit the ministry. Faith speaks through suffering, confident in the God who raised Jesus. Both traditions affirm that this costly service benefits the church and multiplies thanksgiving to God.

2 Corinthians 4:7

7But we have this treasure in clay vessels, that the exceeding greatness of the power may be of God and not from ourselves.

Arminian

The treasure is the gospel light just described; the clay vessels are its frail human bearers. Arminian readers share the conviction that ministry’s power comes from God, not natural ability, and that grace enables service without removing human weakness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see frail messengers displaying the surpassing power of God. The contrast directs confidence away from apostolic prestige toward the God who makes the gospel fruitful through weak servants.

2 Corinthians 4:8

8We are pressed on every side, yet not crushed; perplexed, yet not to despair;

Arminian

Paul names real pressure and bewilderment without claiming uninterrupted emotional ease. Arminian readers see sustaining grace enabling endurance when ministers cannot see the way forward; dependence on God does not require denying distress.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish severe trouble from ultimate defeat. God sustains Paul through perplexity rather than shielding him from it, so his endurance displays divine strength rather than personal invulnerability.

2 Corinthians 4:9

9pursued, yet not forsaken; struck down, yet not destroyed;

Arminian

Persecution and being struck down do not mean God has abandoned his servants. Arminian readers see reassurance for faithful witness under threat, not a guarantee that believers will escape injury or martyrdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise find God’s preserving presence amid persecution. Paul’s continued ministry bears witness to that care, without making physical safety or freedom from suffering the measure of God’s faithfulness.

2 Corinthians 4:10

10always carrying in the body the putting to death of the Lord Jesus, that the life of Jesus may also be revealed in our body.

Arminian

Paul’s bodily suffering in service to Christ mirrors the pattern of Jesus’ death, while his endurance manifests Jesus’ life. Arminian readers understand this as costly participation in Christ, not an addition to the atoning sufficiency of his death.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see union with Christ expressed through a death-and-life pattern in apostolic service. The risen Lord’s power becomes visible in a suffering body; Paul’s suffering does not supplement Christ’s finished atonement.

2 Corinthians 4:11

11For we who live are always delivered to death for Jesus’ sake, that the life also of Jesus may be revealed in our mortal flesh.

Arminian

The repeated exposure to death explains what carrying Jesus’ dying means in Paul’s ministry. Arminian readers see grace sustaining willing, costly service so that the risen Jesus’ life becomes evident within mortal weakness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God working through the apostle’s repeated exposure to danger for Christ’s sake. The purpose is not the glorification of suffering itself, but the manifestation of Jesus’ life in fragile human flesh.

2 Corinthians 4:12

12So then death works in us, but life in you.

Arminian

Paul contrasts the death-threatening cost to the messengers with the gospel life received by the Corinthians. Arminian readers see self-giving ministry serving others’ faith, without making the minister’s suffering a saving sacrifice.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read this as the fruitful cost of apostolic service. God brings life to the church through a message carried by endangered servants; Christ alone is the source of that life.

2 Corinthians 4:13

13But having the same spirit of faith, according to that which is written, “I believed, and therefore I spoke.” We also believe, and therefore we also speak,

Arminian

The citation of Psalm 116:10 places Paul’s speech alongside faith expressed amid distress. Arminian readers see trusting God issuing in courageous testimony, with the Spirit sustaining faith rather than suffering silencing it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect believing and speaking: faith sustained by God finds expression in testimony despite affliction. The psalm supplies a shared pattern of trust under trial, not a promise that faithful witnesses avoid danger.

2 Corinthians 4:14

14knowing that he who raised the Lord Jesus will raise us also through Jesus, and will present us with you.

Arminian

The resurrection of Jesus grounds Paul’s hope of being raised and presented before God together with the Corinthians. Arminian readers share this bodily, communal hope: present service and suffering are not the end because God raises those who belong to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise ground resurrection hope in God’s raising of Jesus. The promise embraces both apostle and congregation, so Paul’s endurance serves a people whom God will bring with him into Christ’s resurrection life.

2 Corinthians 4:15

15For all things are for your sakes, that the grace, being multiplied through the many, may cause the thanksgiving to abound to the glory of God.

Arminian

Paul’s costly service aims at the Corinthians’ good and ultimately God’s glory. Arminian readers see grace reaching more people and awakening thanksgiving; its multiplication is God’s generous work, not a tribute to the messenger’s strength.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see grace, thanksgiving, and God’s glory as the goal of apostolic labor. As more people share in grace, praise increases, placing the credit for the ministry’s fruit where it belongs.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
