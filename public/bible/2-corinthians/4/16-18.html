Passage

2 Corinthians 4

Verses 16–18 · WEB

Paul returns to his refusal to lose heart, setting bodily decline beside daily inward renewal. Present affliction is weighed against the surpassing glory of resurrection life, anticipating chapter 5’s discussion of the body and future hope. Both traditions see persevering faith looking beyond temporary suffering to God’s eternal promise.

2 Corinthians 4:16

16Therefore we don’t faint, but though our outward person is decaying, yet our inward person is renewed day by day.

Arminian

Paul returns to his refusal to lose heart in verse 1. Arminian readers see daily inward renewal by God’s grace sustaining believers amid bodily decline; spiritual growth is compatible with weakness and does not promise restored physical vigor.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish bodily deterioration from God’s continuing inward renewal. The same grace that sustains ministry strengthens the inner life day by day, giving hope without denying mortality.

2 Corinthians 4:17

17For our light affliction, which is for the moment, works for us more and more exceedingly an eternal weight of glory,

Arminian

Paul’s affliction is light and momentary only in comparison with the surpassing, eternal glory ahead. Arminian readers see suffering endured in faith serving God’s gracious purpose, not earning glory or making present pain insignificant.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the comparison in light of resurrection hope, not as a dismissal of severe suffering. God orders affliction toward future glory; the outcome rests on grace rather than the merit of endurance.

2 Corinthians 4:18

18while we don’t look at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen. For the things which are seen are temporal, but the things which are not seen are eternal.

Arminian

Looking toward unseen realities means trusting God’s promised future rather than judging everything by present conditions. Arminian readers see a deliberate, grace-enabled orientation of faith; the contrast concerns what lasts, not contempt for the created body.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand this gaze as faith resting on God’s enduring promises. Present troubles are temporary, but promised glory is eternal; the coming bodily resurrection keeps this hope from becoming a rejection of material creation.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
