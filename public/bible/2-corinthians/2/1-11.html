Passage

2 Corinthians 2

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Paul explains the painful correspondence and his decision not to make another sorrowful visit. The discipline imposed on an unnamed offender has been sufficient, and the church must now forgive, comfort, and reaffirm its love. Paul joins in that forgiveness so that Satan cannot exploit their divisions. Both traditions see correction and restoration as expressions of the same concern for the church’s good.

2 Corinthians 2:1

1But I determined this for myself, that I would not come to you again in sorrow.

Arminian

Paul decided against another visit marked by sorrow. Arminian readers see a deliberate pastoral response to a damaged relationship, not an abandonment of correction. Restraint can give the community space to respond, while his aim remains renewed fellowship rather than avoidance of difficult truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wise care shaping the timing of Paul’s presence. Authority is not proved by repeatedly producing grief. His decision continues the explanation that he stayed away to spare the church, showing concern for the effects of ministry as well as its rightful demands.

2 Corinthians 2:2

2For if I make you grieve, then who will make me glad but he who is made to grieve by me?

Arminian

Paul’s joy is bound to the same people whom his correction could grieve. Arminian readers see the mutual character of Christian fellowship: the church is not simply a project to manage. Love must consider how correction affects shared life, without making pleasant feelings the only measure of faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pastoral affection rather than detached control. Paul needs the fellowship of those he serves, even when he must cause them pain through correction. His question explains why he seeks restoration instead of treating their grief as a satisfying demonstration of his authority.

2 Corinthians 2:3

3And I wrote this very thing to you, so that when I came, I wouldn’t have sorrow from them of whom I ought to rejoice; having confidence in you all that my joy would be shared by all of you.

Arminian

The letter sought to address trouble before a reunion so that shared joy could return. Arminian readers see confidence inviting the Corinthians’ willing response rather than assuming reconciliation requires no action. The verse does not by itself identify the sorrowful letter with a specific surviving document.

Reformed

Reformed readers see written admonition used as a means toward the church’s recovery and renewed fellowship. Paul’s confidence in them encourages the response he seeks. His purpose is neither emotional distance nor domination but a visit in which minister and congregation can genuinely rejoice together.

2 Corinthians 2:4

4For out of much affliction and anguish of heart I wrote to you with many tears, not that you should be made to grieve, but that you might know the love that I have so abundantly for you.

Arminian

Paul explains that tears and anguish accompanied his writing because he loved them. Arminian readers see correction measured by restorative love rather than the desire to wound. Causing grief can be an unwanted part of confronting wrong, but grief itself is not the final aim of faithful ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see tenderness and serious admonition belonging together. Apostolic authority does not make Paul indifferent to the pain his words cause. His abundant love supplies the context for the difficult letter, guarding against reading its severity as personal hostility or enjoyment of punishment.

2 Corinthians 2:5

5But if any has caused sorrow, he has caused sorrow not to me, but in part (that I not press too heavily) to you all.

Arminian

The wrong has wounded the community, not merely Paul’s feelings. Arminian readers see sin’s relational effects and the need for a response concerned with everyone involved. Paul avoids pressing the charge too heavily; the passage leaves the offender unnamed, so his precise identity should not be treated as certain.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pastoral restraint in Paul’s description of communal sorrow. He refuses to turn church discipline into a private campaign for vindication. The offense matters because it harms shared life, yet his careful wording prepares the congregation to consider restoration rather than indefinite condemnation.

2 Corinthians 2:6

6This punishment which was inflicted by the many is sufficient for such a one;

Arminian

The punishment imposed by the many has been sufficient. Arminian readers see discipline as purposeful and limited, not a permanent label attached to an offender. The church must discern when correction has done enough rather than assuming that continued severity always expresses greater faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the congregation’s corrective action recognized while its continuation is now restricted. Discipline serves the recovery of the person and the health of the body. Paul’s judgment that it is sufficient prevents punishment from becoming an end in itself or a display of collective power.

2 Corinthians 2:7

7so that, on the contrary, you should rather forgive him and comfort him, lest by any means such a one should be swallowed up with his excessive sorrow.

Arminian

Forgiveness and comfort must now replace punishment lest sorrow overwhelm the offender. Arminian readers see a genuine responsibility to help restore someone whom discipline has grieved. Grace calls the church beyond merely ending a penalty toward active care that makes renewed fellowship possible.

Reformed

Reformed readers see forgiveness and consolation as essential parts of restorative discipline. Serious treatment of sin does not justify crushing a person under endless shame. God’s grace works through the church’s welcome as well as its correction, and Paul directs the congregation to act accordingly.

2 Corinthians 2:8

8Therefore I beg you to confirm your love toward him.

Arminian

Paul urges the church to make its love toward the offender clear. Arminian readers see forgiveness requiring a visible communal response rather than remaining an unspoken private sentiment. The appeal asks them to use their freedom in service of restoration and to overcome the divisions that prolonged exclusion could deepen.

Reformed

Reformed readers see love confirmed in the church’s actual treatment of the person. Discipline and restoration belong to the same ministry under Christ. The congregation should not leave the offender uncertain whether forgiveness has truly opened the way back into fellowship and care.

2 Corinthians 2:9

9For to this end I also wrote, that I might know the proof of you, whether you are obedient in all things.

Arminian

The letter tested the Corinthians’ readiness to obey. Arminian readers see obedience as a real response that includes mercy as well as correction. They cannot fulfill Paul’s instruction by punishing the offender while refusing the equally serious summons to forgive and confirm their love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see apostolic instruction directing the church’s whole response rather than one isolated action. Their obedience is shown through restoration as well as the earlier discipline. The test concerns practical submission to gospel-shaped instruction, not the acquisition of saving merit through loyalty to Paul.

2 Corinthians 2:10

10Now I also forgive whomever you forgive anything. For if indeed I have forgiven anything, I have forgiven that one for your sakes in the presence of Christ,

Arminian

Paul aligns his forgiveness with the church’s action for their good before Christ. Arminian readers see reconciliation as communal and accountable to the Lord, not merely a settlement of personal grievances. His support removes any excuse for withholding mercy on the assumption that he still demands punishment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul exercising authority in unity with the congregation and under Christ’s gaze. Forgiveness serves the church rather than protecting the apostle’s pride. The verse does not set human pardon against divine judgment; it locates their restorative action in responsibility to Christ.

2 Corinthians 2:11

11that no advantage may be gained over us by Satan, for we are not ignorant of his schemes.

Arminian

Failure to forgive can give Satan an advantage within the community. Arminian readers see vigilance requiring practical obedience in love, not fascination with hidden schemes. Excessive severity and division can undermine the restoration discipline was intended to serve, so resisting evil includes offering comfort and forgiveness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see spiritual conflict expressed in ordinary church relationships. Confidence in Christ does not excuse an unforgiving spirit that damages fellowship. Paul identifies a concrete danger rather than inviting speculation: the adversary can exploit unresolved grief and division when the church refuses restorative mercy.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
