Passage

2 Corinthians 2

Verses 12–17 · WEB

Paul recalls an opportunity at Troas and his unsettled departure when Titus did not arrive. He turns to thanksgiving that God spreads the knowledge of Christ through his servants despite their weakness. The message is an aroma associated with both life and death, raising the question of a messenger’s sufficiency. Both traditions insist on dependence and sincerity, while relating the differing responses to grace in distinct ways.

2 Corinthians 2:12

12Now when I came to Troas for the Good News of Christ, and when a door was opened to me in the Lord,

Arminian

At Troas, Paul found an opening for the gospel in the Lord. Arminian readers see divine provision creating a real opportunity for service. The following verse shows that discernment also involves pastoral concerns; an open door does not automatically settle every decision about where a minister must remain.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providence opening a way for proclamation, while Paul’s account remains honest about his inward unrest. God’s guidance works through opportunities without turning every favorable circumstance into an inflexible command. The narrative prepares for thanksgiving that God works through a ministry marked by weakness.

2 Corinthians 2:13

13I had no relief for my spirit, because I didn’t find Titus my brother, but taking my leave of them, I went out into Macedonia.

Arminian

Not finding Titus left Paul deeply unsettled, and he continued to Macedonia. Arminian readers see concern for a coworker and the Corinthians affecting practical decisions. The passage does not condemn this departure as faithlessness, so distress should not be treated as proof that Paul had ceased to trust God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see genuine human anxiety within faithful service. Paul’s dependence on God does not remove his need for news and fellowship. Moving to Macedonia fits a ministry conducted through relationships and limited knowledge, not through constant certainty about every providential circumstance.

2 Corinthians 2:14

14Now thanks be to God who always leads us in triumph in Christ, and reveals through us the sweet aroma of his knowledge in every place.

Arminian

Paul turns from unrest to gratitude that God spreads the knowledge of Christ through his servants. Arminian readers see divine victory working through vulnerable witnesses rather than promising uninterrupted ease. The aroma image emphasizes the message carried into different places, not the personal impressiveness of the messenger.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God taking the initiative in the triumph and making Christ known through dependent servants. The movement from anxiety to thanksgiving keeps success from being measured by Paul’s emotional stability. His weakness does not prevent God from using his ministry to spread knowledge of Christ.

2 Corinthians 2:15

15For we are a sweet aroma of Christ to God in those who are being saved and in those who perish:

Arminian

The aroma of Christ is offered to God even where hearers respond differently. Arminian readers see faithful proclamation as pleasing to God without implying that every hearer receives salvation. The distinction between those being saved and those perishing makes response to the message serious rather than spiritually neutral.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God honored by the ministry of Christ’s message among both groups. Faithfulness cannot be measured simply by universal acceptance. The verse distinguishes salvation and perishing while locating the messenger’s primary accountability before God, not in the approval of every audience.

2 Corinthians 2:16

16to the one a stench from death to death, to the other a sweet aroma from life to life. Who is sufficient for these things?

Arminian

The same proclamation is associated with life for some and death for others. Arminian readers understand the difference in relation to receiving or resisting God’s gracious call, never unaided self-salvation. Paul’s question about sufficiency rejects confidence in technique or natural ability to produce spiritual life.

Reformed

Reformed readers relate the differing outcomes to God’s effectual grace bringing faith, while those who reject the gospel remain responsible for their unbelief. The verse itself does not explain every cause of the distinction. Paul’s question directs attention away from a messenger’s inherent competence toward dependence on God.

Key contrast Both affirm the seriousness of the gospel and the messenger’s dependence on God. Arminian readers relate the differing outcomes to a response made possible by grace that can be resisted. Reformed readers relate saving reception to effectual grace, while affirming the responsibility of those who reject the message.

2 Corinthians 2:17

17For we are not as so many, peddling the word of God. But as of sincerity, but as of God, in the sight of God, we speak in Christ.

Arminian

Paul contrasts sincere proclamation before God with treating the word as merchandise. Arminian readers see motives and conduct as accountable to Christ, not excused by public religious activity. Serving the gospel requires dependence on God and honest concern for hearers rather than using spiritual authority for personal advantage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the message as entrusted by God, spoken in Christ and under divine scrutiny. The contrast targets corrupt handling of the word for gain rather than establishing a blanket rule against supporting ministers. Sincerity follows from recognizing whose word is being proclaimed and before whom the servant stands.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
