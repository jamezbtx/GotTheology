Passage

2 Corinthians 7

Verses 5–12 · WEB

Paul resumes the account of his distress in Macedonia and the comfort brought by Titus’s report from Corinth. The severe letter had caused grief, but their repentant response renewed the relationship. Both traditions distinguish godly sorrow that turns toward salvation from worldly sorrow that produces death.

2 Corinthians 7:5

5For even when we had come into Macedonia, our flesh had no relief, but we were afflicted on every side. Fightings were outside. Fear was inside.

Arminian

Returning to his experience in Macedonia, Paul acknowledges both external conflict and inward fear. Arminian readers see an apostle who needs encouragement, not a model of emotional invulnerability. Both traditions reject equating distress with faithlessness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faithful ministry accompanied by real weakness and anxiety. God’s care does not require Paul to conceal his fears. His vulnerability prepares for the account of comfort coming through Titus.

2 Corinthians 7:6

6Nevertheless, he who comforts the lowly, God, comforted us by the coming of Titus,

Arminian

God comforts Paul through Titus’s arrival. Arminian readers see divine help working through human presence and friendship. Both traditions recognize that dependence on God includes receiving the encouragement he gives through fellow believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providential care in the timely arrival of a fellow worker. The comfort is genuinely God’s gift even though delivered through ordinary companionship. Care for the lowly includes those entrusted with demanding ministries.

2 Corinthians 7:7

7and not by his coming only, but also by the comfort with which he was comforted in you while he told us of your longing, your mourning, and your zeal for me, so that I rejoiced still more.

Arminian

Titus brings news of the Corinthians’ longing, grief, and renewed loyalty. Arminian readers see repentance healing a damaged relationship through concrete changes of heart. Both traditions recognize mutual encouragement flowing between the congregation, Titus, and Paul.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s comfort extending through a network of restored relationships. The congregation’s changed response refreshes Titus and then Paul. Their mourning is not the end of the story but part of reconciliation that gives rise to joy.

2 Corinthians 7:8

8For though I grieved you with my letter, I do not regret it, though I did regret it. For I see that my letter made you grieve, though just for a while.

Arminian

Paul admits the pain caused by his severe letter and his own initial regret. Arminian readers see correction judged by its restorative purpose, not by the pleasure of making others hurt. Both traditions distinguish loving rebuke from deliberate humiliation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s pastoral tenderness alongside the necessity of correction. His changing feelings do not make him indifferent to truth. Temporary grief is bearable because of the repentant response described next, not because distress is good in itself.

2 Corinthians 7:9

9I now rejoice, not that you were grieved, but that you were grieved to repentance. For you were grieved in a godly way, that you might suffer loss by us in nothing.

Arminian

Paul rejoices in repentance rather than emotional suffering. Arminian readers stress the Corinthians’ grace-enabled turning from wrong, not mere regret about consequences. Both traditions see correction succeeding when it brings restoration instead of needless harm.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grief bearing healthy fruit when God uses it to turn people from sin. Pain alone is not spiritual progress. Paul’s concern is the congregation’s good, and his joy rests in repentance rather than the success of asserting authority.

2 Corinthians 7:10

10For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, which brings no regret. But the sorrow of the world produces death.

Arminian

Godly sorrow turns people toward repentance and salvation; worldly sorrow ends in death. Arminian readers understand repentance as a necessary, grace-enabled response, not a meritorious payment. In this congregational setting, grief must lead to a real return to God rather than hopeless remorse.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand saving repentance as God’s gracious gift, brought forth through his renewing work and expressed in an actual turning from sin. Godly sorrow is distinguished by its fruit, not simply its intensity. Both traditions deny that tears or self-punishment earn salvation.

Key contrast Both affirm that repentance is necessary and cannot earn salvation. Arminian readers emphasize the grace-enabled response that can be resisted; Reformed readers emphasize God’s effectual renewal producing genuine repentance. The immediate contrast is between sorrow that turns to God and sorrow that ends in death, not between the two traditions.

2 Corinthians 7:11

11For behold, this same thing, that you were grieved in a godly way, what earnest care it worked in you. Yes, what defense, indignation, fear, longing, zeal, and vindication! In everything you demonstrated yourselves to be pure in the matter.

Arminian

Paul points to earnestness, longing, and efforts to set matters right as fruit of repentance. Arminian readers see sorrow becoming responsible action. Being clear in this matter concerns their response to the particular wrong, not a declaration that they have never sinned.

Reformed

Reformed readers see evidence of repentance in the congregation’s changed conduct. Their zeal and concern to address the wrong show more than regret at being confronted. Both traditions distinguish vindication in this affair from a claim of sinless perfection.

2 Corinthians 7:12

12So although I wrote to you, I wrote not for his cause that did the wrong, nor for his cause that suffered the wrong, but that your earnest care for us might be revealed in you in the sight of God.

Arminian

Paul’s concern reaches beyond the wrongdoer and the injured person to the congregation’s renewed care. Arminian readers see correction restoring communal responsibility before God. The verse does not identify the people involved, so their identities should not be assumed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the letter serving reconciliation between Paul and the church in God’s presence. Addressing the particular wrong also reveals the congregation’s deeper commitments. Both traditions avoid reducing discipline to punishment of an isolated offender.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
