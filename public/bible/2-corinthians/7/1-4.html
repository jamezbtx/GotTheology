Passage

2 Corinthians 7

Verses 1–4 · WEB

Following God’s promises of welcome and fatherly care, Paul calls the Corinthians to holiness and renewed openness toward his ministry. His appeal combines a defense of his integrity with affection and joy amid affliction. Both traditions see reconciliation and holy living as responses to grace.

2 Corinthians 7:1

1Having therefore these promises, beloved, let’s cleanse ourselves from all defilement of flesh and spirit, perfecting holiness in the fear of God.

Arminian

God’s promises of belonging in the preceding chapter ground the call to holiness. Arminian readers stress believers’ active, grace-enabled cleansing of outward conduct and inward loyalties. Both traditions see reverence and obedience as responses to God’s welcome, not its purchase.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s covenant promises motivating ongoing sanctification. Believers truly act to put sin away, depending on the Spirit who renews them. Perfecting holiness describes the pursuit of mature obedience, not self-salvation or a claim that every believer is already sinless.

Key contrast Both affirm active holiness dependent on grace, not earned acceptance. Classical Arminian readers emphasize cooperation with enabling grace that may be resisted; Reformed readers emphasize Spirit-wrought sanctification in which believers actively obey. The exhortation does not itself settle every question about sanctification’s extent or progress.

2 Corinthians 7:2

2Open your hearts to us. We wronged no one. We corrupted no one. We took advantage of no one.

Arminian

Paul asks for renewed openness and denies harming or exploiting the Corinthians. Arminian readers see reconciliation requiring truthful conduct as well as affection. Both traditions recognize that spiritual authority must be exercised without manipulation or financial advantage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul defending the integrity of his ministry while inviting restored fellowship. His appeal rests on his conduct toward them, not a right to unquestioning loyalty. Pastoral trust should be accompanied by freedom from corruption and exploitation.

2 Corinthians 7:3

3I say this not to condemn you, for I have said before that you are in our hearts to die together and live together.

Arminian

Paul’s defense is not a rejection of the people he loves. Arminian readers see enduring solidarity making room for honest correction. Both traditions hear a pastoral commitment to share life and suffering rather than abandon a strained relationship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see affection holding together rebuke and reassurance. Paul regards the Corinthians as dear companions even amid conflict. Correction serves their fellowship; it is not an excuse for condemnation or emotional withdrawal.

2 Corinthians 7:4

4Great is my boldness of speech toward you. Great is my boasting on your behalf. I am filled with comfort. I overflow with joy in all our affliction.

Arminian

Paul can speak openly and rejoice over the Corinthians even while suffering. Arminian readers see restored relationships bringing genuine comfort without removing every hardship. Both traditions recognize that Christian joy can coexist with affliction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s confidence in the congregation alongside his dependence on God’s comfort. His joy does not deny suffering but grows amid it. The favorable report that follows explains why he can speak so warmly.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
