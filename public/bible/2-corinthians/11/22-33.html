Passage

2 Corinthians 11

Verses 22–33 · WEB

Paul’s fool’s speech reaches its surprising content. He matches his rivals’ Jewish credentials, then claims to be a better servant of Christ by listing not triumphs but sufferings: beatings, shipwrecks, dangers, sleeplessness, hunger, and daily anxiety for the churches. He chooses to boast of weakness, sealing the list with the humiliating escape from Damascus in a basket. Arminian and Reformed readers share the reading: apostolic authority is marked by the shape of the cross.

2 Corinthians 11:22

22Are they Hebrews? So am I. Are they Israelites? So am I. Are they the offspring of Abraham? So am I.

Arminian

Are they Hebrews, Israelites, offspring of Abraham? So is he. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the rivals stressed Jewish credentials, which Paul shares fully.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul does not despise his heritage, but refuses to make it the ground of authority (compare Philippians 3:4 to 8).

2 Corinthians 11:23

23Are they servants of Christ? (I speak as one beside himself.) I am more so: in labors more abundantly, in prisons more abundantly, in stripes above measure, and in deaths often.

Arminian

Are they servants of Christ? He is more, in labors, prisons, beatings, and near deaths. Arminian and Reformed readers agree he redefines service by suffering, speaking as one beside himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the cross shaping true ministry: the servant is not above the master.

2 Corinthians 11:24

24Five times I received forty stripes minus one from the Jews.

Arminian

Five times he received forty lashes minus one from Jewish authorities. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this reflects synagogue discipline (Deut 25:3) that Paul accepted while still seeking his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note his continued love for Israel despite persecution.

2 Corinthians 11:25

25Three times I was beaten with rods. Once I was stoned. Three times I suffered shipwreck. I have been a night and a day in the deep.

Arminian

Three times beaten with rods, once stoned, three times shipwrecked, a night and day adrift. Arminian and Reformed readers agree Acts records only some of these events, so the list fills out his story.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s preservation of his servant through repeated danger.

2 Corinthians 11:26

26I have been in travels often, perils of rivers, perils of robbers, perils from my countrymen, perils from the Gentiles, perils in the city, perils in the wilderness, perils in the sea, perils among false brothers;

Arminian

Frequent journeys, dangers from rivers, robbers, his own people, Gentiles, city, wilderness, sea, and false brothers. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that the final item is the most painful: betrayal within the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that no realm of life was safe, yet he persevered in his calling.

2 Corinthians 11:27

27in labor and travail, in watchings often, in hunger and thirst, in fastings often, and in cold and nakedness.

Arminian

Labor, hardship, sleepless nights, hunger, thirst, fasting, cold, and nakedness. Arminian and Reformed readers agree these are the costs of mission, not ascetic achievements.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul identifying with Christ who had nowhere to lay his head.

2 Corinthians 11:28

28Besides those things that are outside, there is that which presses on me daily: anxiety for all the assemblies.

Arminian

Beyond these is the daily pressure of anxiety for all the churches. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that pastoral care can be the heaviest burden.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul’s concern is not distrust of God but love for the churches.

2 Corinthians 11:29

29Who is weak, and I am not weak? Who is caused to stumble, and I don’t burn with indignation?

Arminian

Who is weak and he is not weak? Who stumbles and he does not burn? Arminian and Reformed readers agree this shows deep empathy and protective zeal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a shepherd who shares his people’s burdens.

2 Corinthians 11:30

30If I must boast, I will boast of the things that concern my weakness.

Arminian

If he must boast, he will boast of his weakness. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this is the key to the whole speech.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s strength displayed through weakness (12:9).

2 Corinthians 11:31

31The God and Father of the Lord Jesus Christ, he who is blessed forever more, knows that I don’t lie.

Arminian

God, the Father of the Lord Jesus, knows he is not lying. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this solemn oath underscores the truth of a surprising list.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Paul’s appeal to God’s knowledge as final witness.

2 Corinthians 11:32

32In Damascus the governor under King Aretas guarded the Damascenes’ city, desiring to arrest me.

Arminian

In Damascus the governor under King Aretas guarded the city to seize him. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this is a historical event (compare Acts 9:23 to 25), though Acts emphasizes Jewish plotters.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the precise political arrangement in Damascus is debated by historians.

2 Corinthians 11:33

33I was let down in a basket through a window by the wall, and escaped his hands.

Arminian

He was lowered in a basket through a window in the wall and escaped. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the humiliating escape is a deliberate anticlimax: his first story is of flight, not triumph.

Reformed

Reformed readers see it as the opposite of a Roman soldier’s honor for being first over the wall, a reading many interpreters suggest, though it is not certain.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
