Passage

2 Corinthians 11

Verses 1–15 · WEB

Paul reluctantly adopts the role of a fool to answer rivals in Corinth whom he calls false apostles. He fears, like a father who has promised his daughter in marriage, that the church will be seduced from simple devotion to Christ by another Jesus, a different spirit, and a different gospel. He defends his refusal of financial support as love, not inferiority, and exposes the rivals as servants of Satan disguised as servants of righteousness. Arminian and Reformed readers agree on the danger of false teaching; they read Paul’s fear for the church slightly differently in light of their views on perseverance.

2 Corinthians 11:1

1I wish that you would bear with me in a little foolishness, but indeed you do bear with me.

Arminian

Paul asks the Corinthians to bear with a little foolishness, a rhetorical role he adopts reluctantly. Arminian readers note his pastoral humility: he is willing to look foolish for the sake of a church he loves.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul’s foolishness is irony aimed at the rivals’ self-promotion. He boasts only because the gospel is at stake, not from pride.

2 Corinthians 11:2

2For I am jealous over you with a godly jealousy. For I promised you in marriage to one husband, that I might present you as a pure virgin to Christ.

Arminian

Paul feels godly jealousy, having promised the church as a pure virgin to Christ. Arminian readers stress the covenant faithfulness Christ expects of his bride and Paul’s role in guarding it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the betrothal image pointing to Christ’s claim on his church and the final presentation at his coming. Paul’s jealousy reflects God’s own jealousy for his people.

2 Corinthians 11:3

3But I am afraid that somehow, as the serpent deceived Eve in his craftiness, so your minds might be corrupted from the simplicity that is in Christ.

Arminian

Paul fears their minds may be led astray from sincere devotion to Christ, as the serpent deceived Eve. Arminian readers take this as a real danger that genuine believers could be drawn away and fall from Christ if they embrace a false gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers take the fear as a real pastoral warning that God uses to preserve his people. True believers may be seriously misled for a time, but the warning is one of the means by which God keeps them; those who finally depart show they were not truly his.

Key contrast Both take the threat of deception seriously. Arminians typically see a real possibility that true believers could fall away; Reformed readers typically see the warning as a God-given means of preserving the elect from final apostasy.

2 Corinthians 11:4

4For if he who comes preaches another Jesus whom we didn’t preach, or if you receive a different spirit which you didn’t receive, or a different “good news” which you didn’t accept, you put up with that well enough.

Arminian

The rivals preach another Jesus, a different spirit, and a different gospel, and the Corinthians tolerate it. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this is the central issue: the content of the gospel, not merely style or personality.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the three terms cover the person of Christ, the work of the Spirit, and the message itself. Tolerance of error here is not a virtue.

2 Corinthians 11:5

5For I reckon that I am not at all behind the very best apostles.

Arminian

Paul considers himself in no way inferior to the so-called super-apostles. Arminian readers note this refers to the rival teachers in Corinth, not the Jerusalem apostles, as many interpreters hold.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the reference is most likely to the Corinthian rivals, though some interpreters think of the Twelve. Paul’s point is equality of authority, not personal superiority.

2 Corinthians 11:6

6But though I am unskilled in speech, yet I am not unskilled in knowledge. No, in every way we have been revealed to you in all things.

Arminian

He may be untrained in speech but not in knowledge. Arminian readers note that the Corinthians valued polished rhetoric, which Paul subordinates to truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here a theology of preaching: the power lies in the message, not the eloquence of the messenger (compare 1 Cor 2:1 to 5).

2 Corinthians 11:7

7Or did I commit a sin in humbling myself that you might be exalted, because I preached to you God’s Good News free of charge?

Arminian

Paul asks whether he sinned by humbling himself and preaching the gospel free of charge. Arminian readers see self-giving love that refuses to make the gospel a commodity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an imitation of Christ’s humbling for the exaltation of others. The irony exposes the rivals who charged for their teaching.

2 Corinthians 11:8

8I robbed other assemblies, taking wages from them that I might serve you.

Arminian

He robbed other churches by accepting support so he could serve Corinth. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the language is hyperbolic: other churches gave freely, and Paul used it to serve Corinth without charge.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Paul’s freedom to receive support or not, guided by what best serves the gospel in each place.

2 Corinthians 11:9

9When I was present with you and was in need, I wasn’t a burden on anyone, for the brothers, when they came from Macedonia, supplied the measure of my need. In everything I kept myself from being burdensome to you, and I will continue to do so.

Arminian

Macedonian believers met his needs so he would not burden Corinth, and he will continue that policy. Arminian readers see generosity across churches and Paul’s concern to remove obstacles to the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the partnership of churches in mission. Paul’s policy is a deliberate strategy to keep his motives above suspicion.

2 Corinthians 11:10

10As the truth of Christ is in me, no one will stop me from this boasting in the regions of Achaia.

Arminian

By the truth of Christ in him, no one will silence this boast in Achaia. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the boast is his free ministry, not personal achievement.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul swearing by Christ’s truth, underlining the seriousness of his integrity.

2 Corinthians 11:11

11Why? Because I don’t love you? God knows.

Arminian

Is it because he does not love them? God knows he does. Arminian readers note the tender heart beneath the argument.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Paul’s appeal to God as witness. Love, not pride, drives the refusal of payment.

2 Corinthians 11:12

12But what I do, that I will continue to do, that I may cut off opportunity from those who desire an opportunity, that in which they boast, they may be recognized just like us.

Arminian

He will continue his practice to deny rivals the chance to claim equality with him. Arminian and Reformed readers agree Paul refuses to let opponents use money as a measure of apostleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom in removing occasions for false comparison.

2 Corinthians 11:13

13For such men are false apostles, deceitful workers, masquerading as Christ’s apostles.

Arminian

Such men are false apostles and deceitful workers disguised as apostles of Christ. Arminian and Reformed readers agree on the severity: the issue is not a rival style but a counterfeit ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the church must test teachers by the gospel, not by appearances or credentials.

2 Corinthians 11:14

14And no wonder, for even Satan masquerades as an angel of light.

Arminian

Even Satan disguises himself as an angel of light. Arminian and Reformed readers agree on the reality of spiritual deception behind false teaching.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the need for discernment rooted in Scripture, since evil often appears attractive.

2 Corinthians 11:15

15It is no great thing therefore if his servants also masquerade as servants of righteousness, whose end will be according to their works.

Arminian

His servants disguise themselves as servants of righteousness, and their end will match their deeds. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that God will judge false teachers justly according to their works.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that judgment according to works applies to all, and false ministers will be exposed by what they have done.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
