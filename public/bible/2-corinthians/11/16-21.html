Passage

2 Corinthians 11

Verses 16–21 · WEB

Paul asks the Corinthians to tolerate him as a fool so that he may boast a little, as his rivals do. With biting irony he notes that they gladly put up with men who enslave, exploit, and strike them. He admits this kind of boasting is not according to the Lord, yet he will match any claim his opponents make. Both traditions read this as rhetoric in service of the gospel; there is little theological disagreement here.

2 Corinthians 11:16

16I say again, let no one think me foolish. But if so, yet receive me as foolish, that I also may boast a little.

Arminian

Paul asks them to accept him even as a fool so he may boast a little. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that he adopts the fool’s role knowingly and for their sake.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the speech is a deliberate parody of the rivals’ boasting.

2 Corinthians 11:17

17That which I speak, I don’t speak according to the Lord, but as in foolishness, in this confidence of boasting.

Arminian

He admits he speaks not according to the Lord but as a fool in this boastful confidence. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this does not deny inspiration; Paul is labeling the manner of speech, not disowning its truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Paul’s candor: boasting is not the Lord’s way, so he flags it as a concession.

2 Corinthians 11:18

18Seeing that many boast after the flesh, I will also boast.

Arminian

Since many boast according to the flesh, he will boast too. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that fleshly boasting means confidence in human credentials and status.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul meeting the rivals on their own ground only to overturn it.

2 Corinthians 11:19

19For you bear with the foolish gladly, being wise.

Arminian

They gladly bear with fools, being so wise. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this is sharp sarcasm aimed at the church’s self-image.

Reformed

Reformed readers note how pride in wisdom can make a church vulnerable to manipulation.

2 Corinthians 11:20

20For you bear with a man if he brings you into bondage, if he devours you, if he takes you captive, if he exalts himself, or if he strikes you on the face.

Arminian

They tolerate those who enslave, devour, exploit, exalt themselves, and strike them. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the rivals are abusive and the church has accepted it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a warning against leaders who domineer rather than serve (compare 1 Peter 5:3).

2 Corinthians 11:21

21To my shame, I speak as though we had been weak. Yet in whatever way anyone is bold (I speak in foolishness), I am bold also.

Arminian

To his shame he admits he was too weak for that, yet he will be bold in whatever they dare boast. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the weakness is ironic: he refused to abuse them.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Paul’s gentleness is reframed as Christlike strength.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
