Passage

Galatians 5

Verses 7–15 · WEB

Paul reminds the Galatians they were running well until someone hindered them. The persuasion does not come from the one who calls them, and a little leaven spreads through the whole lump. Paul is confident in the Lord about them and warns of judgment on the troublemaker. He then turns to the use of freedom: they were called to freedom, not as an opportunity for the flesh, but to serve one another in love, which fulfils the law. Both traditions read this as guarding freedom from both legalism and license.

Galatians 5:7

7You were running well! Who interfered with you that you should not obey the truth?

Arminian

You were running well; who hindered you from obeying the truth? Arminian readers see a race that could be abandoned. Their responsibility to continue is real. The Galatians are not helpless victims; they are responsible to resume the race.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the race image as describing the Christian life. Hindrances come from false teaching. Obeying the truth means believing the gospel. Reformed readers note that the false teachers broke the Galatians’ stride by adding conditions to the gospel. To obey the truth is to rest in Christ alone.

Galatians 5:8

8This persuasion is not from him who calls you.

Arminian

This persuasion is not from the one who calls you. Arminian readers note God’s call is ongoing. The new teaching does not come from God. Arminian readers stress that God’s call continues to address them; the agitators’ persuasion competes with it. They must choose which voice to follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s effectual call contrasted with human persuasion. False teaching does not originate from God’s calling. The God who calls by grace does not suddenly add the law as a condition. The agitators’ message therefore cannot come from him, however persuasive it seems. Reformed readers see in verses 8 and 10 a contrast between human persuasion and God’s faithful calling.

Galatians 5:9

9A little yeast grows through the whole lump.

Arminian

A little leaven leavens the whole lump. Arminian readers see how small errors spread. False doctrine affects the whole church. Paul uses a proverb also found in 1 Corinthians 5:6. One small requirement added to the gospel can corrupt the whole congregation’s understanding of grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the danger of compromising the gospel even slightly. A small addition corrupts the whole. Making one ritual a condition of justification changes the entire character of the gospel. Reformed readers see here why the Reformers resisted any addition to grace, however minor it seemed.

Galatians 5:10

10I have confidence toward you in the Lord that you will think no other way. But he who troubles you will bear his judgment, whoever he is.

Arminian

Paul has confidence in the Lord that they will take no other view, and the troublemaker will bear his judgment. Arminian readers note Paul’s confidence rests in the Lord and his hope that they will respond. It is not a guarantee irrespective of their response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s confidence in the Lord’s work in the Galatians. Confidence rests on God’s faithfulness to preserve his own. The troublemakers face judgment. Paul’s in the Lord grounds his hope not in the Galatians’ strength but in Christ’s hold on them.

Galatians 5:11

11But I, brothers, if I still preach circumcision, why am I still persecuted? Then the stumbling block of the cross has been removed.

Arminian

If Paul still preached circumcision, why is he persecuted? Then the offense of the cross would be removed. Arminian readers see that the cross offends because it excludes human achievement. Paul’s suffering proves he has not compromised to please Jewish opponents. Removing the cross’s offense would remove the gospel itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the cross as the stumbling block because it leaves no room for merit. Preaching circumcision would remove the offense. Paul’s persecution is evidence that he preaches grace without circumcision. Reformed readers note 1 Corinthians 1:23, where Christ crucified is a stumbling block. A gospel that offends no one’s pride is no longer the gospel.

Galatians 5:12

12I wish that those who disturb you would cut themselves off.

Arminian

Paul wishes the agitators would emasculate themselves. Arminian readers recognize the shocking sarcasm aimed at teachers obsessed with circumcision. Paul’s anger arises from love for the flock. Interpreters note that the image may also allude to pagan priests who castrated themselves, placing the agitators’ ritual on that level.

Reformed

Reformed readers see righteous indignation against those destroying the gospel. The language is deliberately severe. Paul’s harshness is not personal vindictiveness but zeal for the flock and for the gospel. Interpreters across traditions note the jarring language and caution against imitating its tone casually. Its sharpness reflects the stakes.

Galatians 5:13

13For you, brothers, were called for freedom. Only don’t use your freedom for gain to the flesh, but through love be servants to one another.

Arminian

You were called to freedom; do not use it as an opportunity for the flesh, but serve one another in love. Arminian readers see freedom as responsibility. Love governs Christian liberty. Freedom is not the absence of obligation but liberation to love. The flesh looks for any opportunity to turn freedom into selfishness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see freedom from the law’s condemnation as freedom for loving service. Liberty is not license. Christian freedom is freedom from slavery to sin and from the curse of the law, so that believers become willing servants of others. Luther’s well-known treatise The Freedom of a Christian develops this paradox: the Christian is free lord of all and dutiful servant of all.

Galatians 5:14

14For the whole law is fulfilled in one word, in this: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”

Arminian

The whole law is fulfilled in one word: love your neighbour as yourself. Arminian readers see love as the essence of the law’s moral intent. Believers fulfil it by Spirit-enabled love. Paul quotes Leviticus 19:18, as Jesus did, showing continuity between the law and life in the Spirit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the moral law summarized in love. The law remains a guide for the believer, the so-called third use. Love fulfils the law not by abolishing its commands but by embodying their intent. Reformed readers commonly cite this as support for the law’s use as a rule of life for believers. The Spirit writes this love on the heart.

Galatians 5:15

15But if you bite and devour one another, be careful that you don’t consume one another.

Arminian

If you bite and devour one another, watch out lest you be consumed. Arminian readers see the destructive results of conflict. Legalism breeds division. Paul’s imagery of wild animals devouring each other shows how far law-centred rivalry has damaged the Galatian churches.

Reformed

Reformed readers see communal sin as a sign of the flesh. Grace produces unity. Paul uses the image of wild animals to show what happens when people turn from grace to rivalry. Legalism tends to breed comparison and contempt. A church that devours itself destroys its witness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
