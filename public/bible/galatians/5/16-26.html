Passage

Galatians 5

Verses 16–26 · WEB

Paul describes the Christian life as walking by the Spirit in conflict with the flesh. The works of the flesh are evident, and those who practise them will not inherit the kingdom, while the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, and the virtues that follow. Those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh, so they should keep in step with the Spirit. Arminian and Reformed readers share this vision but differ on the nature of the inner conflict in verse 17 and on the warning in verse 21.

Galatians 5:16

16But I say, walk by the Spirit, and you won’t fulfill the lust of the flesh.

Arminian

Walk by the Spirit and you will not gratify the desires of the flesh. Arminian readers see the Spirit enabling real victory over sin when believers yield to him. Many Wesleyan Arminians see here the possibility of a life marked by consistent obedience through the Spirit’s power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see walking by the Spirit as the believer’s ongoing dependence on the Spirit’s power. Victory is real but not perfect in this life. The promise is strong: the Spirit enables resistance to sin.

Key contrast Both see the Spirit enabling believers to resist the flesh. Arminian, especially Wesleyan, readers often see a fuller victory possible. Reformed readers see real but always incomplete victory in this life.

Galatians 5:17

17For the flesh lusts against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh; and these are contrary to one another, that you may not do the things that you desire.

Arminian

The flesh and Spirit are opposed, so that you do not do what you want. Arminian readers see this conflict as real but not inevitable defeat. The Spirit is stronger, and believers can walk in victory. John Wesley taught that believers can be freed from outward sin while still facing inner temptation, and many Arminians read this verse in that light.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an ongoing lifelong struggle between remaining sin and the Spirit. This verse is often linked with Romans 7. Believers never in this life attain perfection. The phrase that you may not do the things that you desire is read as describing a believer whose renewed desires are hindered by remaining sin. Victory comes through dependence on the Spirit.

Key contrast Both see conflict between flesh and Spirit. Arminian readers often stress the Spirit’s triumph enabling believers to overcome. Reformed readers stress a lifelong struggle that prevents perfection in this life.

Galatians 5:18

18But if you are led by the Spirit, you are not under the law.

Arminian

If you are led by the Spirit, you are not under the law. Arminian readers see the Spirit’s guidance replacing the law as a system. Believers live by Spirit-led obedience. This does not mean lawlessness, since the Spirit produces the love that fulfils the law.

Reformed

Reformed readers see freedom from the law as covenant of works. The Spirit leads into obedience. Those led by the Spirit no longer stand under the law’s condemnation or its custodial role. Yet the Spirit leads into obedience to God’s will, not away from it. Romans 8:14 links being led by the Spirit with being sons of God.

Galatians 5:19

19Now the deeds of the flesh are obvious, which are: adultery, sexual immorality, uncleanness, lustfulness,

Arminian

The works of the flesh are evident: sexual immorality, impurity, sensuality. Arminian readers see clear moral boundaries. These are not compatible with the Spirit. Paul’s list begins with sexual sins, reflecting common vices in the Greco-Roman world.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fruit of fallen nature. Sin manifests in observable ways. Paul calls them works, suggesting human activity, in contrast with the fruit of the Spirit. The flesh refers to human nature in rebellion against God, not physical bodies as such. Its products are evident to all.

Galatians 5:20

20idolatry, sorcery, hatred, strife, jealousies, outbursts of anger, rivalries, divisions, heresies,

Arminian

Idolatry, sorcery, enmity, strife, jealousy, anger, rivalries, dissensions, divisions. Arminian readers note many of these are communal sins. The flesh damages relationships. Strife and divisions were evidently happening in Galatia, so the list is pointed.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that religious and relational sins appear together. False worship and broken fellowship stem from the same root. Reformed readers note that heresies here means factions, a sin of community life. Dividing the church by adding conditions to the gospel is itself a work of the flesh.

Galatians 5:21

21envy, murders, drunkenness, orgies, and things like these; of which I forewarn you, even as I also forewarned you, that those who practice such things will not inherit God’s Kingdom.

Arminian

Those who practise such things will not inherit God’s kingdom. Arminian readers see a warning to believers that persistent sin can result in loss of inheritance. The warning is addressed to the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the warning describing those whose lives are characterized by such sins, revealing they are not truly regenerate. True believers may fall into sin but do not remain in settled practice of it.

Key contrast Both see a serious warning. Arminian readers take it as a real danger that believers could forfeit their inheritance by persistent sin. Reformed readers take it as describing those whose practice shows they never belonged to Christ.

Galatians 5:22

22But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faith,

Arminian

The fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness. Arminian readers see this fruit growing as believers cooperate with the Spirit. It is the evidence of genuine faith. Wesleyan Arminians often link this fruit with the Spirit’s sanctifying work in perfecting love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fruit as the Spirit’s work in believers, singular fruit with many aspects. It is the result of union with Christ. Reformed readers note that fruit grows; it is not manufactured by effort. Love heads the list as the source of the other qualities. The Spirit reproduces the character of Christ in his people.

Galatians 5:23

23gentleness, and self-control. Against such things there is no law.

Arminian

Gentleness and self-control; against such things there is no law. Arminian readers see that the Spirit produces what the law aimed at. No law condemns such fruit. The Spirit-produced life accomplishes what the law could only command.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit fulfilling the law’s intent. Sanctification brings conformity to God’s will. No law can condemn a life marked by the fruit of the Spirit. The law’s moral demand is satisfied in this Spirit-produced character. This is why freedom from the law does not mean lawlessness.

Galatians 5:24

24Those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh with its passions and lusts.

Arminian

Those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh. Arminian readers see a decisive act at conversion that must be maintained. Believers continue to put sin to death. Romans 6:6 and Galatians 2:20 express the same union with Christ in his death. Arminian readers stress that believers must continue to live out what was decisively done.

Reformed

Reformed readers see definitive sanctification: believers have crucified the flesh in union with Christ. This is the basis for ongoing mortification. The flesh with its passions has been crucified, so it no longer rules. Romans 8:13 calls believers to keep putting deeds of the body to death by the Spirit.

Galatians 5:25

25If we live by the Spirit, let’s also walk by the Spirit.

Arminian

If we live by the Spirit, let us keep in step with the Spirit. Arminian readers see a call to align conduct with new life. Cooperation with the Spirit is essential. The verb for walk can mean keep in line, like soldiers in formation, suggesting disciplined obedience. Believers must follow the Spirit’s lead day by day.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the indicative and imperative together. Life in the Spirit calls for walking in step. Since believers live by the Spirit, their daily conduct should match the source of their life. The Spirit gives life, and believers follow his lead. The verb suggests walking in line with the Spirit.

Galatians 5:26

26Let’s not become conceited, provoking one another, and envying one another.

Arminian

Let us not become conceited, provoking and envying one another. Arminian readers see communal implications of Spirit-led living. Pride undermines fellowship. Conceit and envy were natural fruits of the law-centred competition in Galatia.

Reformed

Reformed readers see humility as the fruit of grace. Conceit contradicts the gospel. Conceit and envy destroy fellowship, which is why Paul turns to mutual care in chapter 6. Those who know they are justified by grace have no grounds for boasting over others.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
