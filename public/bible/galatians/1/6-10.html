Passage

Galatians 1

Verses 6–10 · WEB

Instead of thanking God for the Galatians, Paul marvels that they are so quickly deserting the one who called them. Teachers have arrived insisting that Gentile believers must accept circumcision and the law, and Paul treats this as a different gospel that is no gospel at all. His double curse is among the sharpest words in his letters. Arminian and Reformed readers both hear a real danger here, but they explain differently what it means for called believers to desert their caller.

Galatians 1:6

6I marvel that you are so quickly deserting him who called you in the grace of Christ to a different “good news”;

Arminian

Paul marvels that the Galatians are quickly deserting the one who called them in the grace of Christ. Arminian readers take the present tense deserting seriously: real believers are in the process of turning from God himself, not merely from a doctrine. This shows that apostasy is a genuine possibility which must be resisted, and the warning is meant to halt them before the turn is complete.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree the danger is real and the language severe, but they note that Paul speaks of a process still under way and later expresses confidence in the Lord about them (5:10). Desertion from the gospel would reveal that a profession was not genuine, while warnings like this are one of the means God uses to keep his elect. The one who called them is God, whose calling Paul elsewhere describes as effectual.

Key contrast Both agree Paul describes a real and urgent danger of turning from God’s gracious call. Arminian readers see this as evidence that true believers can fall away. Reformed readers see such warnings as means God uses to preserve his people, with final desertion revealing a profession that was never genuine.

Galatians 1:7

7and there isn’t another “good news.” Only there are some who trouble you, and want to pervert the Good News of Christ.

Arminian

Paul clarifies that there is not really another gospel, only people troubling them and wanting to pervert Christ’s good news. Arminian readers note that adding circumcision does not create an alternative gospel but distorts the only one. The troublemakers are responsible for their influence, yet the Galatians are responsible for listening.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the gospel is singular and fixed: grace plus works is not a variation but a contradiction. To pervert the gospel is to turn it into its opposite. This sets the stakes of justification as a matter of the gospel’s very existence, not a secondary disagreement.

Galatians 1:8

8But even though we, or an angel from heaven, should preach to you any “good news” other than that which we preached to you, let him be cursed.

Arminian

Even if Paul or an angel from heaven preached a different gospel, let him be cursed. Arminian readers see that the authority of the message outranks the authority of any messenger, including Paul himself. The Galatians are therefore responsible to test what they hear against the gospel they first received.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sufficiency and finality of the apostolic gospel: no later revelation, however impressive, can alter it. The curse, anathema, consigns false teaching to God’s judgment. The Reformers applied this verse in arguing that Scripture’s message stands above church tradition and any claimed new revelation.

Galatians 1:9

9As we have said before, so I now say again: if any man preaches to you any “good news” other than that which you received, let him be cursed.

Arminian

Paul repeats the curse for emphasis, now applying it to anyone preaching a gospel other than what they received. Arminian readers note the shift to what you received: the Galatians had genuinely accepted the gospel, so turning away would be a real departure. The repetition underscores how serious the danger is.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe the doubled warning as deliberate solemnity, the language of covenant curse. The gospel received is a deposit handed on, not a starting point for innovation. Faithful ministry consists in guarding and repeating it rather than improving it.

Galatians 1:10

10For am I now seeking the favor of men, or of God? Or am I striving to please men? For if I were still pleasing men, I wouldn’t be a servant of Christ.

Arminian

Paul asks whether he is seeking human approval, insisting that a people-pleaser could not be Christ’s servant. Arminian readers note this answers a charge that Paul made the gospel easier for Gentiles to win converts. His harsh words prove he is not flattering anyone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in this verse the servant’s single allegiance: a minister of Christ answers to God, not public opinion. The phrase if I were still pleasing men hints at Paul’s former life of zeal for human traditions. Faithfulness to the gospel will sometimes require displeasing people.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
