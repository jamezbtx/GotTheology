Passage

Galatians 2

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Fourteen years later Paul returned to Jerusalem with Barnabas and Titus and privately laid his gospel before the recognized leaders. They did not require Titus, a Greek, to be circumcised, and they extended the right hand of fellowship, recognizing that the same God worked through Peter among Jews and through Paul among Gentiles. Both traditions see here the unity of the apostolic gospel and the freedom of Gentile believers from circumcision. Disagreement is minimal; the emphases fall on human responsibility and divine working respectively.

Galatians 2:1

1Then after a period of fourteen years I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus also with me.

Arminian

Fourteen years later Paul went to Jerusalem with Barnabas, bringing Titus. Arminian readers note that bringing an uncircumcised Gentile convert was a deliberate test case. Paul is showing the Galatians that the very issue they face was already settled.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul presenting a living exhibit of grace: Titus was a full believer without circumcision. The long interval reinforces Paul’s independence, since he preached for years before this meeting.

Galatians 2:2

2I went up by revelation, and I laid before them the Good News which I preach among the Gentiles, but privately before those who were respected, for fear that I might be running, or had run, in vain.

Arminian

Paul went up by revelation and privately laid out his gospel, so that he might not be running in vain. Arminian readers take the concern seriously: division between Paul and Jerusalem could damage the mission and the Gentile churches. Paul was not doubting his gospel but seeking to protect its fruit.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that Paul went by revelation, so even this consultation was God-directed. Running in vain refers to the practical effect of a split church, not to uncertainty about the truth. The gospel’s unity across the apostles matters for the church’s witness.

Galatians 2:3

3But not even Titus, who was with me, being a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised.

Arminian

Titus was not compelled to be circumcised. Arminian readers see a precedent that directly answers the Galatian teachers. If Jerusalem’s leaders did not require it, the agitators cannot claim their authority. The test case reveals the principle: Gentiles are accepted as Gentiles through faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a decisive confirmation that circumcision is not a condition of acceptance with God. The church’s leaders recognized Titus as a brother on the basis of faith alone. Reformed readers note that Paul later circumcised Timothy for missionary reasons (Acts 16:3), showing that the rite itself was not the issue but making it a condition of justification.

Galatians 2:4

4This was because of the false brothers secretly brought in, who stole in to spy out our liberty which we have in Christ Jesus, that they might bring us into bondage;

Arminian

False brothers slipped in to spy on the liberty believers have in Christ, wanting to enslave them. Arminian readers note that liberty in Christ is a real possession that can be surrendered under pressure. Paul calls the agitators false brothers because they opposed the gospel while claiming fellowship.

Reformed

Reformed readers highlight the contrast between liberty in Christ and bondage under law. The freedom is not license but deliverance from a works-based standing before God. False teaching that adds conditions to grace is portrayed as an infiltration aimed at enslavement.

Galatians 2:5

5to whom we gave no place in the way of subjection, not for an hour, that the truth of the Good News might continue with you.

Arminian

Paul did not yield for even an hour so that the truth of the gospel would remain with the Galatians. Arminian readers note Paul’s firmness was for the sake of others, not personal pride. Compromise would have cost future converts their freedom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the phrase truth of the gospel as central to the letter. Some things in the church can be negotiated, but the ground of acceptance before God cannot. Paul’s resolve preserved the gospel for later generations, including the Galatians.

Galatians 2:6

6But from those who were reputed to be important (whatever they were, it makes no difference to me; God doesn’t show partiality to man)—they, I say, who were respected imparted nothing to me,

Arminian

Those reputed to be important added nothing to Paul, and God shows no partiality. Arminian readers see Paul neither despising nor flattering the Jerusalem leaders. God’s impartiality is a principle that also undermines Jewish-Gentile distinctions.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that status does not determine truth because God does not judge by appearances. The leaders’ confirming that they added nothing shows Paul’s gospel was already complete. Human reputation is never the basis of authority in the church.

Galatians 2:7

7but to the contrary, when they saw that I had been entrusted with the Good News for the uncircumcised, even as Peter with the Good News for the circumcised

Arminian

The leaders saw Paul had been entrusted with the gospel for the uncircumcised, just as Peter for the circumcised. Arminian readers see a division of labour, not two gospels. Recognition came from seeing the evidence of God’s work.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the passive entrusted: God gave the commission. The one gospel has different spheres of mission. Unity of message with diversity of ministry is a pattern for the church. Peter and Paul preach the same Christ to different audiences.

Galatians 2:8

8(for he who worked through Peter in the apostleship with the circumcised also worked through me with the Gentiles);

Arminian

The same God worked effectively through Peter and through Paul. Arminian readers note the fruit of ministry was evidence that God was behind it, and both apostles cooperated with that work. Divine empowerment and human labour went together.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the verb worked, divine energizing that makes ministry effective. Success in ministry is God’s doing. This guards against boasting in the messenger and directs attention to the Lord of the mission.

Galatians 2:9

9and when they perceived the grace that was given to me, James and Cephas and John, they who were reputed to be pillars, gave to me and Barnabas the right hand of fellowship, that we should go to the Gentiles, and they to the circumcision.

Arminian

James, Cephas, and John gave Paul and Barnabas the right hand of fellowship when they perceived the grace given to Paul. Arminian readers see a formal recognition of partnership on equal footing. The grace given is the apostolic gift and calling.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the pillars acknowledging God’s grace already at work rather than conferring authority. The right hand of fellowship expresses the oneness of the church built on one gospel. Grace is the decisive category even in recognizing ministry.

Galatians 2:10

10They only asked us to remember the poor—which very thing I was also zealous to do.

Arminian

They asked only that Paul remember the poor, which he was eager to do. Arminian readers note that concern for the poor is integral to gospel fellowship, not an addition to it. Paul’s later collection for Jerusalem fulfilled this.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that the sole request was ethical, not doctrinal, confirming that nothing was added to the gospel. Faith that justifies apart from works still expresses itself in mercy toward the needy. Reformed readers see the Jerusalem collection as a concrete bond of unity between Gentile and Jewish churches.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
