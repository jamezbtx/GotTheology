Passage

Galatians 2

Verses 15–21 · WEB

Paul’s rebuke of Peter flows into one of the densest statements of justification in the New Testament. Even Jews by birth know that a person is not justified by works of the law but through faith in Christ, so rebuilding the law as a boundary condemns oneself. Paul has died to the law and been crucified with Christ, who now lives in him. Arminian and Reformed readers both treasure this passage, though they differ on matters such as the meaning of faith in Jesus Christ and how union with Christ relates to justification and perseverance.

Galatians 2:15

15“We, being Jews by nature, and not Gentile sinners,

Arminian

Paul speaks as a Jew to a Jew: we are Jews by birth, not Gentile sinners. Arminian readers note Paul is using the ordinary Jewish language about Gentiles before overturning its assumptions. Even privileged Jews must come to God through faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul setting up the argument that covenant privilege does not justify. If Jews by nature needed faith in Christ, Gentiles certainly cannot be required to become Jews to be justified. Paul’s phrase Gentile sinners echoes the language of the circumcision party only to show how hollow such distinctions are before God.

Galatians 2:16

16yet knowing that a man is not justified by the works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ, even we believed in Christ Jesus, that we might be justified by faith in Christ, and not by the works of the law, because no flesh will be justified by the works of the law.

Arminian

A person is not justified by works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ. Arminian readers stress that faith is the condition God has appointed for justification, a real human response enabled by grace and not a meritorious work. Many also read works of the law with attention to their role as identity markers, while maintaining that no human effort earns acceptance. Justification is open to all who believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the classic statement of justification by faith alone: God declares sinners righteous not on the basis of their obedience but through faith that receives Christ. Calvin and the Reformation tradition read works of the law broadly as any human obedience to God’s law. Some Reformed scholars also discuss whether faith in Jesus Christ refers to Christ’s own faithfulness, but the verse repeatedly makes clear that we believed in Christ Jesus.

Key contrast Both affirm justification by faith in Christ, not by works of the law. Arminian readers present faith as the God-enabled condition offered to all. Reformed readers present faith as the God-given instrument receiving Christ’s righteousness, and they more often stress imputation.

Galatians 2:17

17But if, while we sought to be justified in Christ, we ourselves also were found sinners, is Christ a servant of sin? Certainly not!

Arminian

If seeking justification in Christ shows we are sinners, is Christ a servant of sin? By no means. Arminian readers see Paul answering the objection that abandoning law boundaries promotes sin. Being found sinners means admitting Jews too need grace, not that Christ encourages sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that justification apart from works does not make Christ a promoter of sin. This anticipates objections Paul also answers in Romans 6. Grace exposes sin but also delivers from it. Paul’s emphatic Certainly not rejects the idea that grace makes Christ an agent of sin.

Galatians 2:18

18For if I build up again those things which I destroyed, I prove myself a law-breaker.

Arminian

If Paul rebuilds what he tore down, he proves himself a transgressor. Arminian readers see the danger of returning to law as a basis of standing after renouncing it. Rebuilding law-boundaries would condemn one’s own earlier faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Peter’s withdrawal as rebuilding the wall of law. To return to law as the ground of fellowship is to admit one was wrong to trust Christ alone. The real transgression lies in reestablishing the law’s role, not in leaving it.

Galatians 2:19

19For I, through the law, died to the law, that I might live to God.

Arminian

Through the law Paul died to the law so that he might live to God. Arminian readers see the law exposing sin and leading the believer to Christ, where a new life of devotion begins. Dying to the law means its condemning hold is broken.

Reformed

Reformed readers read this through union with Christ: the law’s sentence was executed on Christ, and believers died with him. Freed from the law as a covenant of works, they now live to God. The law itself, by condemning, drove Paul to Christ.

Galatians 2:20

20I have been crucified with Christ, and it is no longer I that live, but Christ lives in me. That life which I now live in the flesh, I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself up for me.

Arminian

Paul has been crucified with Christ and now Christ lives in him, so he lives by faith in the Son of God who loved him and gave himself for him. Arminian readers stress the ongoing, personal faith by which this union is maintained; Christ lives in the believer who continues trusting him. The words loved me and gave himself for me show the love of Christ is personal, and Arminians see it as extended to every person.

Reformed

Reformed readers see union with Christ as the fountain of the Christian life: the believer’s old self died with Christ, and the risen Christ indwells him. Many Reformed interpreters read loved me and gave himself for me as expressing Christ’s particular love for his own, though the verse also invites every believer to apply the cross personally. Faith is the means by which believers live out this union.

Key contrast Both treasure this verse as a picture of union with Christ lived by faith. Arminian readers emphasize continuing faith and Christ’s love for all people applied personally. Reformed readers emphasize the definitive union God has created and Christ’s particular love for his people.

Galatians 2:21

21I don’t reject the grace of God. For if righteousness is through the law, then Christ died for nothing!”

Arminian

Paul does not set aside God’s grace; if righteousness came through the law, Christ died for nothing. Arminian readers see this as the logical climax: adding law-keeping as a requirement nullifies grace. The cross would be pointless if righteousness could be achieved another way.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an argument from the necessity of the atonement. If any human obedience could secure righteousness, Christ’s death would be superfluous. Justification by works empties the cross of meaning, which is why Paul defends grace so fiercely.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
