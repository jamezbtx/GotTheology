Passage

Galatians 3

Verses 15–22 · WEB

Using an illustration from human covenants, Paul argues that the law given 430 years after the promise to Abraham cannot annul or add conditions to it. The promise was made to Abraham and his offspring, ultimately Christ. The law was added because of transgressions until the offspring came, and it could not give life. Scripture confined all things under sin so that the promise might be given to those who believe. Both traditions agree on the priority of promise; they differ on the law’s place in covenant history and on God’s purpose in confining all under sin.

Galatians 3:15

15Brothers, speaking of human terms, though it is only a man’s covenant, yet when it has been confirmed, no one makes it void, or adds to it.

Arminian

Paul uses a human analogy: once a covenant is ratified, no one annuls or adds to it. Arminian readers see this as an argument from ordinary fairness. If humans keep their agreements, God will surely keep his promise. Paul’s word for covenant can also mean a will or testament, which strengthens the point that its terms cannot later be altered.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the irrevocability of God’s covenant promise. The covenant with Abraham, ratified by God, cannot be modified by the later law. This undergirds the reliability of God’s saving purpose. A promise God himself ratified is surer than any human agreement.

Galatians 3:16

16Now the promises were spoken to Abraham and to his offspring. He doesn’t say, “To descendants”, as of many, but as of one, “To your offspring”, which is Christ.

Arminian

The promises were spoken to Abraham and his offspring, singular, which is Christ. Arminian readers note that Christ is the true heir through whom others share the promise by faith. Paul’s reading highlights Christ as the focus of the promise.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as the head of the covenant, through whom the promise flows to his people. The singular seed stresses that all blessing is in Christ. Believers become heirs by union with him. Reformed theology sometimes describes Christ as the federal head through whom covenant promises reach those united to him.

Galatians 3:17

17Now I say this: A covenant confirmed beforehand by God in Christ, the law, which came four hundred thirty years after, does not annul, so as to make the promise of no effect.

Arminian

The law, coming 430 years later, cannot annul a covenant previously ratified by God. Arminian readers see the chronological argument as decisive: promise precedes law. God’s promise does not depend on later legal conditions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priority of grace over law in redemptive history. The law serves the covenant of grace, not replacing it. The promise remains effective. Reformed readers therefore see the Mosaic covenant as an administration of the one covenant of grace, though Reformed theologians debate how far it also republished a works principle. Either way, the law cannot make the promise of no effect.

Galatians 3:18

18For if the inheritance is of the law, it is no more of promise; but God has granted it to Abraham by promise.

Arminian

If the inheritance comes by law, it no longer comes by promise, but God gave it to Abraham by promise. Arminian readers note the two principles cannot be mixed. Inheritance is a gift, not a wage. Paul’s word granted stresses that God gave the inheritance freely, as a gracious gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize God granted the inheritance freely. Grace and works are mutually exclusive as grounds of inheritance. This is central to the Reformation’s sola gratia. Romans 4:14-16 makes the same point: if heirs are of the law, faith is made void, so it is by faith that it may be according to grace.

Galatians 3:19

19Then why is there the law? It was added because of transgressions, until the offspring should come to whom the promise has been made. It was ordained through angels by the hand of a mediator.

Arminian

The law was added because of transgressions until the offspring came. Arminian readers see the law as a temporary provision to restrain and expose sin. It was never meant to be the means of inheritance. Arminian readers also note that the law served Israel faithfully as a guide while awaiting the offspring.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see the law’s role in making sin evident as transgression and leading to Christ. Its temporariness in this form does not deny the abiding moral law. The mention of angels and a mediator shows its mediated character in contrast to the direct promise.

Galatians 3:20

20Now a mediator is not between one, but God is one.

Arminian

A mediator implies more than one party, but God is one. Arminian readers admit this verse is notoriously difficult; many see a contrast between the mediated law and the direct promise. God’s unilateral promise rests on himself.

Reformed

Reformed readers similarly see the promise as resting on God alone, unlike the law given through mediation. The oneness of God may also point to his unified purpose for Jews and Gentiles. The verse remains among the hardest in Paul.

Galatians 3:21

21Is the law then against the promises of God? Certainly not! For if there had been a law given which could make alive, most certainly righteousness would have been of the law.

Arminian

Is the law against God’s promises? Certainly not, for no law could give life. Arminian readers see that the law is good but limited. It was never designed to impart life. Law and promise come from the same God and serve one purpose.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law’s inability to give life as the reason righteousness must come through Christ. The law and promise are not enemies but have different functions. Only the Spirit gives life. The law, though holy, cannot change the heart; that requires the Spirit promised in the new covenant.

Galatians 3:22

22But the Scriptures imprisoned all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe.

Arminian

Scripture imprisoned everything under sin so the promise might be given to those who believe. Arminian readers stress that God’s purpose in exposing universal sinfulness is to open salvation to all on the basis of faith. The promise is given to those who believe, with belief a real responsive act.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign purpose in shutting all under sin, so that salvation rests entirely on his promise and grace. The phrase faith in Jesus Christ is again much discussed, but the promise comes to those who believe. Romans 11:32 offers a parallel.

Key contrast Both see Scripture confining all under sin so that the promise comes through faith. Arminian readers emphasize the universal offer this creates. Reformed readers emphasize God’s sovereign purpose in securing salvation by grace alone.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
