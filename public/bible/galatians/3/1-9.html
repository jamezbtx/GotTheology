Passage

Galatians 3

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Paul turns from history to direct appeal. The Galatians received the Spirit by hearing with faith, not by works of the law, and Abraham himself was counted righteous by believing God. Those who are of faith are therefore Abraham’s true children, and Scripture foresaw the justification of Gentiles by faith. Both traditions read this as a foundational text for justification by faith. They differ on how faith arises and on the precise sense in which Abraham’s faith was counted for righteousness.

Galatians 3:1

1Foolish Galatians, who has bewitched you not to obey the truth, before whose eyes Jesus Christ was openly portrayed among you as crucified?

Arminian

Paul calls the Galatians foolish and asks who bewitched them, since Christ crucified was vividly portrayed before them. Arminian readers see their lapse as a real failure of judgment they are responsible for. The vivid preaching of the cross should have been enough to keep them anchored.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the language of bewitching as describing the deceptive power of false teaching. The remedy is renewed focus on Christ crucified, the centre of apostolic preaching. Any addition to the cross diminishes what was so clearly displayed.

Galatians 3:2

2I just want to learn this from you: Did you receive the Spirit by the works of the law, or by hearing of faith?

Arminian

Did they receive the Spirit by works of the law or by hearing with faith? Arminian readers note that receiving the Spirit followed their believing response to the message. The Galatians’ own experience proves that faith, not law-keeping, brought them into the new life.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the phrase hearing of faith can mean hearing accompanied by faith or the message that produces faith. Either way, the Spirit came through the gospel, not through human performance. Reformed readers often emphasize that faith itself is generated by the Spirit through the word.

Galatians 3:3

3Are you so foolish? Having begun in the Spirit, are you now completed in the flesh?

Arminian

Having begun by the Spirit, are they now trying to be perfected by the flesh? Arminian readers see that the Christian life continues the way it began, by Spirit-enabled faith. Turning to fleshly means is a genuine regression.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here that sanctification, like justification, depends on the Spirit’s work and not on human effort apart from grace. Reformed theology distinguishes justification from sanctification but insists both are gifts of God.

Galatians 3:4

4Did you suffer so many things in vain, if it is indeed in vain?

Arminian

Did they suffer so much in vain, if indeed it was in vain? Arminian readers see Paul warning that abandoning grace could render their past faithfulness fruitless. The hopeful qualifier shows he does not yet regard them as lost.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the question as pastoral provocation meant to recall them to their beginnings. The phrase if indeed it was in vain expresses Paul’s hope that God’s work in them is genuine. Suffering for Christ was meant to bear fruit, not be thrown away.

Galatians 3:5

5He therefore who supplies the Spirit to you, and does miracles among you, does he do it by the works of the law, or by hearing of faith?

Arminian

God supplies the Spirit and works miracles among them by hearing with faith, not works of the law. Arminian readers see the present tense as evidence of ongoing divine activity that responds to faith. Miracles confirmed the gospel they had believed.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that God is the one who supplies the Spirit, a continual gift. The Galatians’ experience flows from God’s initiative through the gospel. Works of the law contribute nothing to these blessings.

Galatians 3:6

6Even as Abraham “believed God, and it was counted to him for righteousness.”

Arminian

Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him for righteousness. Arminian readers often stress that God reckoned Abraham’s faith itself as righteousness, graciously accepting faith in place of perfect obedience. Faith is not meritorious but is the condition God honours.

Reformed

Reformed readers typically read this as God crediting righteousness to Abraham through faith, with Christ’s righteousness ultimately as its ground. Faith is the instrument, not the substance, of the righteousness counted. Paul cites Genesis 15:6 to show justification by faith predates the law.

Key contrast Both affirm that Abraham was justified by faith, not by works. Arminian readers often speak of faith reckoned as righteousness by God’s gracious acceptance. Reformed readers speak of faith as the instrument receiving an alien righteousness.

Galatians 3:7

7Know therefore that those who are of faith, the same are children of Abraham.

Arminian

Those of faith are the sons of Abraham. Arminian readers stress that Abraham’s family is open to anyone who believes, Jew or Gentile. Physical descent is not the defining mark. This was a radical claim in a setting where descent from Abraham was a prized identity marker.

Reformed

Reformed readers see spiritual sonship as the true continuation of the Abrahamic covenant. The people of God are defined by faith. Covenant continuity is through promise, not circumcision. Reformed covenant theology sees here the one people of God across both testaments, united by faith in the promise.

Galatians 3:8

8The Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the Good News beforehand to Abraham, saying, “In you all the nations will be blessed.”

Arminian

Scripture foresaw that God would justify the Gentiles by faith and preached the gospel to Abraham in advance. Arminian readers emphasize the universal scope: all nations are included in the promise. God’s plan always intended blessing for the world through faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the unity of the covenant of grace across testaments. The gospel was preached to Abraham, so the way of salvation has always been by faith. Scripture’s foresight reflects God’s own eternal plan.

Galatians 3:9

9So then, those who are of faith are blessed with the faithful Abraham.

Arminian

Those of faith are blessed along with faithful Abraham. Arminian readers note that believers share Abraham’s blessing by sharing his trust. The blessing is offered to everyone on the same terms. Abraham is called faithful, a believer whose trust God honoured, and believers walk in his footsteps.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that believers are blessed with Abraham, not merely like him. They share in the same covenant blessing promised to him. Faith unites believers across history into one people. Whatever Abraham received, believers receive with him, including justification and the promise of the Spirit.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
