Passage

Galatians 4

Verses 12–20 · WEB

Paul shifts to a personal plea, reminding the Galatians of their warm welcome when he first preached to them in weakness. They would have given him their eyes, yet now they treat him as an enemy for telling the truth. The rival teachers court them for selfish ends. Paul, like a mother in labour, longs for Christ to be formed in them. Both traditions read this as a model of pastoral affection and see sanctification as Christ formed in his people.

Galatians 4:12

12I beg you, brothers, become as I am, for I also have become as you are. You did me no wrong,

Arminian

Paul pleads with them to become like him, as he became like them. Arminian readers see Paul’s appeal to his freedom from the law and his identification with Gentiles. He calls them to share his freedom. You did me no wrong suggests the quarrel is not personal but about the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the plea as appealing to his example of resting in grace. He became like a Gentile to reach them. The personal appeal reinforces the theological argument. Paul’s flexibility in non-essentials (1 Corinthians 9:20-21) stands in contrast to his firmness about the gospel itself.

Galatians 4:13

13but you know that because of weakness in the flesh I preached the Good News to you the first time.

Arminian

Paul first preached to them because of a bodily weakness. Arminian readers note God used Paul’s illness to bring the gospel. Weakness was not an obstacle but an occasion. The Galatians first heard the gospel not through impressive strength but through a sick man, which fits Paul’s theology of the cross. Their reception was a work of grace, not human attraction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence in Paul’s illness leading to the Galatians’ hearing the gospel. God’s purposes often work through weakness. Paul’s weakness kept the focus on the message rather than the messenger. Reformed readers see this as an example of 2 Corinthians 12:9, where God’s power is made perfect in weakness.

Galatians 4:14

14That which was a temptation to you in my flesh, you didn’t despise nor reject; but you received me as an angel of God, even as Christ Jesus.

Arminian

They did not despise his condition but received him as an angel of God, even as Christ Jesus. Arminian readers note their warm response to the message and messenger. Their reception showed genuine faith. Their earlier generosity shows how far they have now drifted under the influence of the agitators.

Reformed

Reformed readers note how the Galatians honoured Christ’s messenger as Christ himself. This shows the reality of the work of grace among them. Paul’s illness could have been a reason to dismiss him, since it was a temptation to them, yet they did not despise him. Their early response revealed a work of God’s Spirit, which Paul now calls them to remember.

Galatians 4:15

15What was the blessing you enjoyed? For I testify to you that, if possible, you would have plucked out your eyes and given them to me.

Arminian

Paul asks where their blessedness has gone, for they would have given him their eyes. Arminian readers see a contrast between their former joy and present coldness. The change reflects their drift from grace. Paul reminds them of their joy to call them back to the grace that produced it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the loss of joy as a symptom of turning to law. When grace is obscured, gratitude fades. Some suggest Paul’s illness affected his eyes. The gospel had brought them a blessedness that the law-centred message cannot supply. Whatever Paul’s illness, their willingness to give up their own eyes showed extraordinary love.

Galatians 4:16

16So then, have I become your enemy by telling you the truth?

Arminian

Has Paul become their enemy by telling the truth? Arminian readers see how false teaching distorts relationships. Truth-telling can be misunderstood as hostility. The agitators had apparently portrayed Paul as an opponent of the Galatians’ spiritual maturity. Paul asks them to judge whether truth-telling is hostility or love.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that faithful ministers often face hostility for unwelcome truth. Love speaks the truth even when it costs friendship. Paul refuses to soften the truth in order to regain their affection. Ephesians 4:15 calls believers to speak the truth in love, and this letter is an example of that hard love.

Galatians 4:17

17They zealously seek you in no good way. No, they desire to alienate you, that you may seek them.

Arminian

The rival teachers zealously court them for no good purpose, wanting to shut them out so they will court them. Arminian readers see manipulation aimed at making the Galatians dependent. False teachers seek followers for themselves.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-serving zeal in false teaching. Excluding the Galatians from Paul’s fellowship makes them dependent on the agitators. True ministry seeks Christ’s glory, not personal following. Their aim is to isolate the Galatians so they depend on the agitators’ approval.

Galatians 4:18

18But it is always good to be zealous in a good cause, and not only when I am present with you.

Arminian

It is good to be zealously sought in a good cause, always, not only when Paul is present. Arminian readers note that zeal is good when directed rightly. Paul wants their devotion to continue in his absence. Paul is not jealous of other teachers’ attention, only concerned that it be for a good cause.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that the question is not zeal itself but its object. Zeal for the gospel is commendable. Paul wants their eagerness to be for Christ and the gospel whether he is present or absent. Faithfulness that depends on a particular teacher’s presence is fragile. Zeal is only as good as its direction.

Galatians 4:19

19My little children, of whom I am again in travail until Christ is formed in you—

Arminian

Paul is again in labour until Christ is formed in them. Arminian readers see sanctification as a process requiring ongoing cooperation with grace. Paul’s pain reflects deep care for their spiritual growth. The word again suggests Paul feels he must labour for them as he did when he first preached.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ formed in believers as the goal of sanctification, the work of the Spirit. Paul’s maternal image shows the cost of pastoral labour. Christ’s formation is God’s work through ministry. Reformed readers note that Christ formed in you is the goal toward which God’s predestining purpose moves (Romans 8:29).

Galatians 4:20

20but I could wish to be present with you now, and to change my tone, for I am perplexed about you.

Arminian

Paul wishes he could be present and change his tone, for he is perplexed. Arminian readers see his honesty about uncertainty regarding their state. He longs for personal restoration. A letter cannot carry his voice and tone, and he wishes he could speak more gently in person.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pastoral humility: Paul does not presume to know the outcome. His perplexity shows the seriousness of the situation. Paul would rather speak gently than harshly, but the situation required firmness. His perplexity reflects an honest pastor who cannot read the hidden state of the heart. The letter’s sternness is the voice of love under strain.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
