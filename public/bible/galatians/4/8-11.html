Passage

Galatians 4

Verses 8–11 · WEB

Paul reminds the Gentile Galatians that before they knew God they were enslaved to beings that are not gods. Now that they know God, or rather are known by him, returning to calendar observances and elemental principles is a return to slavery. He fears his labour may have been wasted. Arminian and Reformed readers both hear a real warning, and they read the phrase known by God in light of their wider views on election.

Galatians 4:8

8However at that time, not knowing God, you were in bondage to those who by nature are not gods.

Arminian

Formerly, not knowing God, the Galatians served beings that are not gods. Arminian readers note Paul’s honest description of their pagan past. Ignorance of God led to slavery. Paul’s words by nature are not gods affirm the emptiness of idols while recognizing their enslaving power over those who serve them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human religion apart from God’s revelation as bondage. Idolatry is serving what by nature is not divine. The gospel brought them out of darkness. Reformed readers note that the Galatians did not find their way out by their own insight; they were brought out by the gospel God sent.

Galatians 4:9

9But now that you have come to know God, or rather to be known by God, why do you turn back again to the weak and miserable elemental principles, to which you desire to be in bondage all over again?

Arminian

Now that you know God, or rather are known by God, how can you turn back to weak and miserable elements? Arminian readers stress that the Galatians genuinely knew God and are now genuinely at risk of turning back. Being known by God signifies his gracious relationship with them, which they can reject by returning to bondage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s self-correction, or rather to be known by God, as emphasizing divine initiative: God’s knowing precedes and grounds our knowing him. Many connect this with God’s electing love. Turning back would contradict this relationship, which is why Paul expresses alarm.

Key contrast Both read this as a real warning against turning back. Arminian readers emphasize that the Galatians could forfeit a relationship they genuinely had. Reformed readers emphasize that God’s prior knowing grounds the relationship and makes turning back unthinkable for those truly his.

Galatians 4:10

10You observe days, months, seasons, and years.

Arminian

The Galatians are observing days, months, seasons, and years. Arminian readers see these as Jewish calendar observances adopted as necessary for acceptance. Such practices become bondage when made conditions of standing with God. Romans 14:5-6 shows Paul allowing such observances as matters of conscience, so the problem is their use as a condition of standing with God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the issue as treating ceremonial observances as obligatory. Paul elsewhere permits personal practices, but not as requirements for justification. Returning to these is returning to shadows. Colossians 2:16-17 calls such observances a shadow of the things to come, whose substance is Christ.

Galatians 4:11

11I am afraid for you, that I might have wasted my labor for you.

Arminian

Paul fears his labour may have been wasted. Arminian readers see this as a genuine possibility that his ministry could be fruitless if they turned away. His fear expresses real danger. Arminian readers note that Paul’s anxiety would make little sense if their final outcome were already fixed regardless of their response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pastoral concern rather than doubt about God’s power. Paul cannot see their hearts and fears their profession may prove empty. Warnings are part of God’s means of preservation. Paul speaks as a pastor who sees only the outward signs, not the hidden counsel of God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
