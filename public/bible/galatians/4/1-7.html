Passage

Galatians 4

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Paul extends the image of a minor heir under guardians. Before Christ, humanity was enslaved under the elemental principles of the world, but in the fullness of time God sent his Son, born of a woman and under the law, to redeem those under the law so they might receive adoption. God then sent the Spirit of his Son into their hearts crying Abba, Father. Both traditions celebrate this Trinitarian picture of salvation; they differ mainly on how the sending of the Spirit relates to faith and assurance.

Galatians 4:1

1But I say that so long as the heir is a child, he is no different from a bondservant, though he is lord of all;

Arminian

An heir who is a child is no different from a slave, though he owns everything. Arminian readers see Israel under the law as a minor heir with real status but limited freedom. The image prepares for the arrival of maturity in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the heir illustration as describing the old covenant era. The heir is truly heir but not yet in possession. The analogy shows that the law’s period was preparatory. Reformed writers often speak of the old covenant church as the church in its minority.

Galatians 4:2

2but is under guardians and stewards until the day appointed by the father.

Arminian

The heir is under guardians until the date set by the father. Arminian readers emphasize the temporary nature of the guardianship. God set the time for its end. Guardians and stewards were household managers who controlled an heir’s affairs until he came of age. Israel’s time under the law was real and appointed by God, but never meant to be permanent.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the father appoints the time, reflecting God’s sovereign ordering of redemptive history. The guardianship ends exactly when God intends. Paul’s phrase the day appointed by the father prepares for the fullness of time in verse 4, when God acted at his chosen moment.

Galatians 4:3

3So we also, when we were children, were held in bondage under the elemental principles of the world.

Arminian

Paul says we were enslaved under the elemental principles of the world. Arminian readers often see this as including both Jewish and Gentile religious systems as forms of bondage. Without Christ, humanity is under powers it cannot overcome.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the elemental principles as the rudimentary religious order of the old age, including the law’s ceremonial structures and pagan religion. Both are forms of bondage apart from Christ. The point is humanity’s need for deliverance.

Galatians 4:4

4But when the fullness of the time came, God sent out his Son, born to a woman, born under the law,

Arminian

When the fullness of time came, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law. Arminian readers see God acting at the right historical moment in a way that respects human history. The Son’s real humanity and his subjection to the law qualified him to redeem.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize God’s sovereign timing and the incarnation of the pre-existent Son. Born under the law points to Christ’s active obedience, fulfilling the law on behalf of his people. Reformed theology sees this as essential to justification.

Galatians 4:5

5that he might redeem those who were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of children.

Arminian

God sent his Son to redeem those under the law so we might receive adoption. Arminian readers see adoption as a gift received through faith by all who believe. Redemption makes adoption possible. Redemption from the law and adoption into the family belong together as the purpose of the Son’s mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers see adoption as a distinct benefit of union with Christ, flowing from redemption. Those redeemed are brought into God’s family by grace. The Westminster tradition treats adoption as a separate heading of saving grace.

Galatians 4:6

6And because you are children, God sent out the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying, “Abba, Father!”

Arminian

Because you are sons, God sent the Spirit of his Son into your hearts crying Abba, Father. Arminian readers see the Spirit as the confirmation and experience of sonship given to believers. The cry of Abba reflects personal relationship and assurance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit’s indwelling as the result of adoption, enabling believers to call God Father. The Trinitarian structure is striking: the Father sends the Son and the Spirit of the Son. Assurance flows from the Spirit’s witness.

Galatians 4:7

7So you are no longer a bondservant, but a son; and if a son, then an heir of God through Christ.

Arminian

You are no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then an heir through God. Arminian readers see this as a present status believers enjoy and are called to live out. The inheritance is through God’s action. Through God stresses that heirship is God’s doing, not the result of law-keeping.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the certainty of the inheritance grounded in God. Sonship guarantees heirship. Believers stand secure because their standing rests on God’s work. Paul now switches to the singular you, applying the promise personally to each believer. Slavery to the law and the elements is behind them.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
