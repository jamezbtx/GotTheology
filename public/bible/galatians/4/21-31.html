Passage

Galatians 4

Verses 21–31 · WEB

Paul ends his theological argument with a figurative reading of Hagar and Sarah. Hagar, Sinai, and the present Jerusalem bear children into slavery, while Sarah and the Jerusalem above bear children of promise. Believers, like Isaac, are children of the free woman, and they will face persecution from those born according to the flesh. Both traditions see the contrast between promise and slavery, and they differ mainly in how they frame the relationship between the covenants.

Galatians 4:21

21Tell me, you that desire to be under the law, don’t you listen to the law?

Arminian

Paul asks those wanting to be under the law whether they listen to the law. Arminian readers note Paul uses the law itself to argue his case. The Torah testifies against misuse of the law. Those who want the law must listen to what the law itself, including Genesis, actually says.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Scripture interpreting Scripture. The law, rightly heard, points to promise. Paul turns to Genesis, part of the Torah, to show that the law itself points beyond itself. Those eager to be under the law should hear its witness to promise and freedom. The Reformers often stressed that rightly read, Moses points to Christ.

Galatians 4:22

22For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one by the servant, and one by the free woman.

Arminian

Abraham had two sons, one by a slave and one by a free woman. Arminian readers note Paul sets up a contrast between two lines. Physical descent from Abraham is not enough. The agitators may have used the Abraham story to argue that Gentiles must become his descendants by circumcision.

Reformed

Reformed readers see two kinds of descent, according to flesh and promise. True sonship is by promise. Paul draws attention to the two mothers, slave and free. Both sons were physically Abraham’s, so physical descent alone cannot settle who inherits. The decisive question is how each son was born.

Galatians 4:23

23However, the son by the servant was born according to the flesh, but the son by the free woman was born through promise.

Arminian

The slave’s son was born according to the flesh, the free woman’s through promise. Arminian readers note the difference lies in the manner of birth. Isaac’s birth depended on God’s promise. Ishmael came through human planning, while Isaac came only because God kept his word.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s supernatural intervention in Isaac’s birth as a picture of salvation by grace. Human effort produced Ishmael; God’s promise produced Isaac. Reformed readers see Isaac’s birth from a barren womb as a picture of new birth by the Spirit.

Galatians 4:24

24These things contain an allegory, for these are two covenants. One is from Mount Sinai, bearing children to bondage, which is Hagar.

Arminian

These things are an allegory: two covenants, one from Sinai bearing children for slavery, Hagar. Arminian readers see Paul’s figurative reading pointing to two ways of relating to God. Sinai, used as a way of acceptance, leads to bondage.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the contrast between the covenant of works, as misused, and the covenant of grace. Many Reformed interpreters carefully note that Sinai itself was gracious but became bondage when used as a means of justification. The point is not a disparagement of Moses but of using Sinai as a way to earn standing.

Galatians 4:25

25For this Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia, and answers to the Jerusalem that exists now, for she is in bondage with her children.

Arminian

Hagar corresponds to Sinai and the present Jerusalem, in slavery with her children. Arminian readers see Paul’s critique of the religious establishment of his day. Bondage marks those trusting the law. Paul’s point is not about Jewish people as such but about relying on the law for acceptance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the present Jerusalem representing reliance on the law. The startling identification shows physical lineage does not secure promise. The Jerusalem that exists now stands for the present order of law-reliance that rejected the Messiah. It is a startling reversal, placing the city that prided itself on Sarah’s line with Hagar. Paul is reading Scripture in light of Christ’s coming.

Galatians 4:26

26But the Jerusalem that is above is free, which is the mother of us all.

Arminian

The Jerusalem above is free and is our mother. Arminian readers see the heavenly city as the true home of believers. Freedom marks those in Christ. The Jerusalem above anticipates the new Jerusalem of Hebrews 12:22 and Revelation 21. Gentile believers belong to God’s city without becoming Jews.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church as the heavenly Jerusalem, mother of believers. Freedom is the gift of grace. Reformed readers often cite this verse when speaking of the church as the mother of believers, an image Calvin uses in the Institutes. The church belongs to the heavenly city, not to the present order of slavery. Her children are born free.

Galatians 4:27

27For it is written, “Rejoice, you barren who don’t bear. Break out and shout, you that don’t travail. For more are the children of the desolate than of her who has a husband.”

Arminian

Paul quotes Isaiah 54:1: the barren woman will have more children. Arminian readers see the expansion of God’s people among Gentiles. The promise brings unexpected abundance. In Isaiah the verse comforts exiled Jerusalem; Paul sees its fulfilment in the growth of the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s power to create life from barrenness. The church grows by promise, not natural means. Paul quotes Isaiah 54:1, which promised restoration to desolate Zion. As Sarah’s barrenness gave way to Isaac by promise, so God multiplies children through the gospel. The growth of the Gentile church fulfils this promise.

Galatians 4:28

28Now we, brothers, as Isaac was, are children of promise.

Arminian

Believers, like Isaac, are children of promise. Arminian readers see this as including all who believe. The promise is fulfilled in them. Their status rests on God’s word, not on circumcision or physical descent. Gentile believers can be confident that they belong.

Reformed

Reformed readers see believers’ identity as rooted in God’s promise. Their birth is supernatural. Like Isaac, believers are born by God’s word and power, not by human achievement. This gives the Galatians assurance without circumcision. Their identity is a gift.

Galatians 4:29

29But as then, he who was born according to the flesh persecuted him who was born according to the Spirit, so also it is now.

Arminian

As then, the one born according to the flesh persecuted the one born according to the Spirit, so now. Arminian readers see conflict between the two lines continuing. Believers should expect opposition. The Galatians’ current pressure from the agitators fits this pattern.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the ongoing hostility between flesh and Spirit. Persecution is a mark of the children of promise. Paul reads Ishmael’s mocking of Isaac (Genesis 21:9) as a pattern that recurs. The agitators’ pressure on the Galatians is the same hostility. Reformed readers see the enmity of Genesis 3:15 running through redemptive history.

Galatians 4:30

30However what does the Scripture say? “Throw out the servant and her son, for the son of the servant will not inherit with the son of the free woman.”

Arminian

Scripture says to cast out the slave and her son. Arminian readers see a call to reject reliance on law. The two ways cannot coexist. Paul quotes Sarah’s words in Genesis 21:10 as Scripture’s verdict. The Galatians must refuse the agitators’ demand rather than mix law-reliance with grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a decisive call to separate from those insisting on law for justification. The inheritance belongs to children of promise. Reformed readers note that Scripture itself, not mere personal preference, separates the heirs of promise from the way of slavery. Law-reliance and grace cannot share the inheritance.

Galatians 4:31

31So then, brothers, we are not children of a servant, but of the free woman.

Arminian

We are children not of the slave but of the free woman. Arminian readers see freedom as believers’ identity. They are called to live in it. This freedom is not abstract; Paul will spell out its practical meaning in chapter 5.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the conclusion of the argument: believers stand in grace. This prepares for the call to stand firm in freedom. Their freedom rests on their birth by promise, not their performance. It is the same freedom of verse 26, given by the Jerusalem above.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
