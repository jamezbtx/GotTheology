Passage

John 10

Verses 22–42 · ESV · Voices

At the Feast of Dedication in winter they demand that he tell them plainly if he is the Christ. He answers: you do not believe because you are not among my sheep; my sheep hear my voice; I give them eternal life; they will never perish; no one snatches them from his hand or the Father’s; I and the Father are one. They charge blasphemy, seek to arrest him; he escapes across the Jordan, where John had baptized, and many believed.

John 10:22

22At that time the Feast of Dedication took place at Jerusalem. It was winter,

Arminian

Dedication—Hanukkah—in Jerusalem, and it is winter. Wesley: instituted by Judas Maccabeus when he purged and dedicated altar and temple after they had been polluted. So our Lord observed festivals even of human appointment; is it not, at least, innocent for us to do the same?

Arminian scene-setting: the Shepherd of vv. 1–18 now walks a feast of restored worship. The God who had a temple rescued from Antiochus is the same God whose Son will claim oneness with the Father in that precinct. Time and place are not padding.

Reformed

Calvin: the Greek names renovations; the temple, polluted, was reconsecrated under Judas Maccabaeus, and the day was kept yearly that the people might recall the grace that ended Antiochus’s tyranny. Christ appeared in the temple at that time, according to custom, that his preaching might yield more abundant fruit in a large assembly.

Reformed note: providence stages the plain demand of v. 24 in a feast that already remembers God reclaiming his house. Winter in Solomon’s colonnade will not be a quiet historical footnote; it is the appointed room for 10:26–30.

John 10:23

23and Jesus was walking in the temple, in the colonnade of Solomon.

Arminian

He walks in the temple, in Solomon’s colonnade. Wesley, after Josephus: Solomon filled part of the adjacent valley and built a portico toward the east—a noble structure on a wall four hundred cubits high, still standing years after Christ’s death.

Arminian note: the Word is in the precincts named for Solomon, available to be questioned. He does not hide in a private fold. Walking there is already a kind of public shepherding.

Reformed

Calvin: “temple” here is not the sanctuary but an appendage. Not Solomon’s original porch, destroyed by the Chaldeans, but the later structure built on that pattern and honored with the name; Herod had built a new temple.

Reformed framing: the Son walks the house’s edge, not yet seized, still teaching. Location will matter when they pick up stones in the same precinct (v. 31) and when he later leaves their hands (v. 39).

John 10:24

24So the Jews gathered around him and said to him, “How long will you keep us in suspense? If you are the Christ, tell us plainly.”

Arminian

They surround him: how long will you keep us in suspense? If you are the Christ, tell us plainly. The demand sounds like hunger for clarity. He has been speaking as door and shepherd; they want a title they can use.

Arminian reading: a plain word is not an unfair ask, and he will not refuse to be the Christ. What they lack is not a sentence. They have had works and words; “tell us plainly” can be a sincere plea or a trap. Either way the next verses treat unbelief as the problem, not his obscurity.

Reformed

Calvin: a cunning attack, at least from those who originated the scheme—draw a word that a mob or Rome might kill him for—though common people might desire a deliverer without fraud. They pretend ardent desire for redemption, which would be true piety if they meant it: nowhere but in Christ will the mind find rest.

Reformed diagnosis: they are wrong to accuse him of not confirming their faith; the fault is theirs. Unbelievers choose to remain in doubt rather than be founded on the certainty of God’s word. They charge his doctrine with obscurity which was plain if they had not been deaf.

John 10:25

25Jesus answered them, “I told you, and you do not believe. The works that I do in my Father’s name bear witness about me,

Arminian

I told you, and you do not believe. The works in the Father’s name bear witness. Plainness has already been given in speech and in deeds. Unbelief is not an information gap.

Classical Arminians put the hinge on receiving what has been told. Testimony is sufficient to obligate. He still answers them; grace keeps speaking to people who have already heard. The works remain a sincere witness meant to be believed.

Reformed

Calvin: he does not conceal that he is the Christ, yet he does not teach them as if they were willing to learn, but reproaches obstinate malice. They have been taught by word and works and have not progressed. That they do not know him he imputes to their own fault: the doctrine was understandable; they maliciously resist God.

Reformed weight: he speaks of works to convict them of being doubly obstinate. He twice (here and v. 26) presses “you do not believe”—deaf to doctrine, blind to works, a proof of extreme and desperate malice. The works are done in the Father’s name to testify that he came from God.

John 10:26

26but you do not believe because you are not among my sheep.

Arminian

The clause is causal, and Wesley reads the cause as their will: you do not believe because you are not of my sheep—because you do not, will not follow me; because you are proud, unholy, lovers of praise, world, and pleasure, not of God. Sheep, in this reading, are those who hear and follow. Unbelief is explained by refusal to be that kind of person, not by a secret non-membership that made believing impossible to ask.

Do not straw-man this as Arminians denying Christ’s keeping. They deny that “not among my sheep” is a shrug over people who never could have heard. The same Lord still calls; not-sheep here is the present fact of not hearing/following. They could have been among the sheep by hearing the voice already sent. Remaining outside is culpable pride, not missing paperwork in heaven.

Reformed

Calvin: a higher reason why they believe neither miracles nor doctrine—they are reprobate. The gift of believing is special. Before people know God they must be known by him. Those to whom God does not look must continue to look away. If someone murmurs that the cause of unbelief therefore dwells in God, because he alone can make sheep, Calvin answers: he is free from all blame; only by voluntary malice do men reject his grace. God does all that is necessary to induce them to believe; who shall tame wild beasts? Never, till the Spirit changes them into sheep.

Reformed accent: not-sheep explains unbelief. It is not wonderful if few obey, because all whom the Spirit does not subdue are wild. This does not make the Gospel’s authority rest on human belief; it binds believers the more, because while others remain blind they are drawn by the Spirit’s illumination. Ministers may labor to bring the whole world, and still be consoled that Christ’s sheep listen.

Key contrast Do not flatten “you do not believe because you are not among my sheep.” Shared ground: they do not believe, and they are not his sheep; the voice has been public. Arminians (Wesley) typically take not-sheep as the present refusal to hear and follow—pride and love of the world—so the cause is their will, and the call remains sincere. Reformed readers (Calvin) typically take not-sheep as the deeper reason unbelief persists: they are not the flock he effectually calls; wild until the Spirit tames them—while still insisting their malice, not God’s, is to blame. Neither camp should be heard as making God the author of sin or as treating the Shepherd’s keeping as imaginary.

John 10:27

27My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me.

Arminian

My sheep hear my voice; I know them; they follow me. Wesley still alludes to the earlier discourse: sheep are they who (1) hear his voice by faith; (2) are known—approved—as loving him; (3) follow him, keep his commandments with a believing, loving heart. Hearing and following are the flock’s marks, not extras.

Classical Arminians: the security promised next attaches to this description. Sheep are those who hear and follow. Remaining in the flock is continuing to hear and follow. That is conditionality of remaining, not a claim that Christ is a weak keeper. The knowing is mutual affection, not a mere inventory.

Reformed

Calvin: an argument from contraries—they are not sheep because they do not obey the Gospel. God effectually calls all whom he has elected, so that the sheep of Christ are proved by their faith. Believers are called sheep because they surrender to be governed by the Chief Shepherd and, laying aside fierceness, become mild and teachable.

Reformed consolation: though most of the world does not listen, he has his sheep whom he knows and by whom he is known. Teachers should labor to bring the whole world into the unity of the faith, and be satisfied that those who are sheep will be gathered by their agency. Effectual hearing is the evidence, not the origin, of being his.

Key contrast Both take hearing, being known, and following as the sheep’s real marks. Arminians typically treat those marks as the living conditions of belonging—sheep are those who hear and follow, and remaining in the flock is remaining in that hearing. Reformed readers typically treat hearing and following as the fruit of effectual calling: the elect are proved by their faith. Do not hear Arminians as denying that Christ knows and keeps a real flock, or Reformed readers as making following optional.

John 10:28

28I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand.

Arminian

Three gifts for the sheep just described: I give eternal life; they will never perish; no one will snatch them out of my hand. Wesley: he does not say I will, but I give—for he that believeth hath everlasting life. Those he knows truly to love him shall never perish, provided they abide in my love. Those who follow him, neither men nor devils can pluck out of his hand.

Fair Arminian reading: this is massive security, not a shrug. Christ actually keeps those who hear. External snatching is denied in Christ’s own words—no wolf, man, or devil yanks a following sheep out of his hand. What Wesley will not do is turn that into a promise that one may cease to hear and still be a sheep who cannot perish. Abiding is in the sentence because the sheep of v. 27 are hearers and followers. Do not straw-man Arminians as teaching that Christ cannot keep; they teach that he keeps the flock that hears, and that remaining a hearer is not a threat to his power but the shape of being his.

Reformed

Calvin: an inestimable fruit of faith—Christ bids us be convinced of our security when brought by faith into his fold. The foundation is that he will be a faithful guardian; our salvation is in his hand, and they will be safely guarded by the Father’s power. A remarkable passage: the salvation of all the elect is not less certain than the power of God is invincible. He did not throw the word into the air; the elect are absolutely certain of their salvation.

Reformed perseverance: we are surrounded by adversaries and weak enough to be in danger every moment, but he who keeps what we have committed to him is greater than all. Confidence in free-will, virtue, and merits is mad beside this. Sheep live at ease among wolves because salvation is in God’s hand. “No one will snatch” is the Shepherd’s grip, not the sheep’s fist. None snatched means none lost of those he gives life to.

Key contrast Shared ground: Jesus gives eternal life; he says they will never perish; no one snatches them from his hand. Arminians typically attach those promises to hearing, loving, following sheep, and hear “never perish” with Wesley’s proviso of abiding in his love—while insisting men and devils cannot pluck those who follow. Reformed readers typically hear absolute certainty for the elect: Christ’s and the Father’s invincible guardianship, perseverance of all who are given life. Do not hear Arminians as denying Christ’s keeping, or Reformed readers as making holiness optional because none can snatch.

John 10:29

29My Father, who has given them to me, is greater than all, and no one is able to snatch them out of the Father’s hand.

Arminian

A second hand: the Father’s, greater than all. He has given them to the Son. Wesley: the Father who hath, by an unchangeable decree, given me all that believe, love, and obey, is greater than all in heaven or earth, and none is able to pluck them out of his hand.

Arminian care: the given are described as those who believe, love, and obey—not a bare list irrespective of the hearing of v. 27. The Father’s hand is real protection. Unchangeable decree, in Wesley’s mouth, is God’s settled gift of the believing, loving, obedient to the Son. Snatching from that hand is still denied. The double grip (Son’s hand, Father’s hand) is comfort for the following flock, not a reason to stop following.

Reformed

Calvin: the “and” means therefore—since God’s power is invincible, the salvation of believers is not exposed to enemies’ passions; ere they perish, God must be overcome, who has taken them under his hand’s protection. The Father gave them to the Son (the same giving as 6:37, 39). Two hands, one keeping.

Reformed weight: greater than all is not a comparative boast; it is why none is able. Giving is the Father’s act before the flock’s grip. Perseverance is Trinitarian: what the Father gives, the Son holds, and no created snatcher is able. Weak faith looks here, not in the mirror.

Key contrast Both confess the Father greater than all, a real giving to the Son, and inability of any snatcher. Arminians typically identify the given as those who believe, love, and obey, kept while they abide as hearers. Reformed readers typically identify the given as the elect handed to the Son, whose keeping is as invincible as God—therefore none of them perish. Neither side should be heard as making the Father’s hand a metaphor with no grip.

John 10:30

30I and the Father are one.”

Arminian

I and the Father are one. Wesley: not by consent of will only, but by unity of power, and consequently of nature. “Are” confutes Sabellius (plurality of persons); “one” confutes Arius (unity of nature). Never did any prophet use an expression of himself that could be so interpreted by all who heard; therefore if he was not God he must have been the vilest of men.

Arminian confession: the keeping of vv. 28–29 is the keeping of God. Oneness here is why no one snatches. Classical Arminians do not reduce this to teamwork. The Son who gives eternal life is one with the Father who gave the sheep. Unity of nature grounds unity of saving work—without turning sheep into spectators of a grip they need not hear.

Reformed

Calvin: he meets the jeer that God’s power does not belong to him, so that he could not promise protection. He testifies that his affairs are so closely united to the Father’s that the Father’s assistance will never be withheld from himself and his sheep. Calvin thinks the ancients wrongly pressed this verse alone for homoousios; Christ argues agreement such that whatever is done by Christ will be confirmed by the Father’s power.

Reformed center, even with Calvin’s caution on the word “one”: Father and Son are united in giving and keeping life. The next verses show the hearers took a divine claim (v. 33). Unity of Father and Son in the grip of vv. 28–29 is the pastoral point: the sheep are not held by a junior deity. One saving hand, two persons, no snatching.

Key contrast Shared ground: Jesus claims a oneness with the Father that his enemies hear as making himself God, and both camps historically confess the full deity of the Son. Arminians (Wesley) typically take “one” as unity of power and nature—against both Sabellius and Arius. Reformed readers take the verse as at least the Father’s inseparable assistance in keeping the sheep; many go with the creeds beyond Calvin’s caution here. The contrast to keep is pastoral: Arminians still bind that divine keeping to hearing/abiding sheep; Reformed bind it to the elect who cannot be lost. Do not hear either as Arian, or as denying that Father and Son together give and keep life.

John 10:31

31The Jews picked up stones again to stone him.

Arminian

Stones again—as in 8:59. They heard v. 30 as more than a proverb about teamwork. The Dedication crowd moves from “tell us plainly” to lethal judgment without a legal pause.

Arminian note: unbelief can be violent. The same people who asked for a plain Christ now treat the plainness as blasphemy. The Shepherd does not yet flee; he will argue from Scripture and works.

Reformed

Calvin: unbelief is the mother of rage; the devil hurries the wicked so that they breathe nothing but slaughter. The result shows the intention of their question: the open confession they pretended to want instantly drives them to madness.

Reformed realism: they likely had a plausible warrant in their own minds—false prophets to be stoned (Deut. 13). Zeal for God’s glory, misaimed, will kill the Son. A good conscience must be a wall of brass when calumny comes.

John 10:32

32Jesus answered them, “I have shown you many good works from the Father; for which of them are you going to stone me?”

Arminian

He answers stones with works: many good works from the Father; for which will you stone me? The question is not coy. Healing, feeding, opening eyes—these are the Father’s kindnesses in his hand.

Arminian pastoral force: he still reasons with a stoning crowd. Works remain a sincere path to faith (vv. 37–38). They are not told that evidence is unavailable; they are asked which gift they are punishing.

Reformed

Calvin: he not only says they have no reason for cruelty but accuses them of ingratitude for so unjust a requital of God’s favors—not one or two works, but many. He upbraids them with being ungrateful not only to himself but to God: he is the Father’s minister; God is the Author of the works.

Reformed edge: “Banish me as you please; I have done nothing that does not deserve praise. In persecuting me you show rage against the gifts of God.” The interrogative pierces more than a direct assertion would.

John 10:33

33The Jews answered him, “It is not for a good work that we are going to stone you but for blasphemy, because you, being a man, make yourself God.”

Arminian

Not for a good work, they say, but for blasphemy: you, being a man, make yourself God. They understood “I and the Father are one” as a divine claim. Their error is not hearing too much deity; it is refusing the deity the works have shown.

Arminian alignment with Wesley on v. 30: if he is not God he is vile. They have named the issue correctly as God-making. The tragedy is to call the true Son a self-made God.

Reformed

Calvin: wicked men never wish to sin without a plausible pretense; they constitute themselves defenders of God’s glory. Two kinds of blasphemy: depriving God of honor, or ascribing what is unsuitable to him. Their definition would be just if Christ were only a man.

Reformed diagnosis: they err in not contemplating his Divinity, conspicuous in the miracles. A good conscience is a wall of brass; we still need patience, because the wicked never lack pretenses and hardened impudence.

John 10:34

34Jesus answered them, “Is it not written in your Law, ‘I said, you are gods’?

Arminian

He answers from their Law: I said, you are gods—Psalm 82:6. He does not retract “I and the Father are one.” He exposes their malice by Scripture they claim to honor.

Arminian note: the argument is from the lesser. If Scripture can call even unjust judges “gods,” they are reckless to treat the consecrated Son’s title as automatic blasphemy. The Law is on his side; they are not defending it.

Reformed

Calvin: he clears himself not by denying that he is the Son of God but by maintaining that he said so justly. He adapts the reply to the persons rather than giving a full explanation; it was enough for the present to expose their malice. The argument is from the less to the greater, not from equals.

Reformed reading: Scripture gives the name “gods” to those on whom God conferred honorable office. He whom God has distinguished above all is far more worthy of the title. They are false expounders if they admit the first and take offense at the second.

John 10:35

35If he called them gods to whom the word of God came—and Scripture cannot be broken—

Arminian

If God called them gods to whom the word of God came—and Scripture cannot be broken. Wesley: nothing written therein can be censured or rejected. The parenthesis is a doctrine of Scripture: it stands.

Arminian weight: he will not win the deity debate by loosening the Bible. The same unbreakable Scripture that named judges “gods” is about to name him the consecrated and sent Son. High view of the Book, high view of the Son.

Reformed

Calvin: they were authorized by God’s undoubted command; empires do not spring at random. Paul: no power but of God. This is not a general license to call every vocation “gods,” but a word about kings raised to govern. Under “Law” Christ includes the whole doctrine by which God governed the ancient Church; the Psalms are an appendage to the Law.

Reformed confession: that Scripture cannot be broken means its doctrine is inviolable. The Son who claims oneness with the Father is the same Son who will not let them pick the Psalter apart to stone him.

John 10:36

36do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, ‘You are blaspheming,’ because I said, ‘I am the Son of God’?

Arminian

Do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, You are blaspheming, because I said, I am the Son of God? Wesley: this sanctification—whereby he is essentially the Holy One of God—is mentioned as prior to his mission, and together with it implies Christ was God in the highest sense, infinitely superior to those judges.

Arminian Christology: consecrated and sent is incarnation language, not a creature’s promotion. He said “Son of God” in a room that had just heard “I and the Father are one.” The charge of blasphemy misfires because the Father himself set this One apart and sent him.

Reformed

Calvin: there is a sanctification common to all believers; here Christ claims something far more excellent—he alone was separated that the Spirit’s power and God’s majesty might be displayed in him, as he had said the Father sealed him (6:27). The two are joined: sanctified and sent into the world. Sent to bring salvation from God and to exhibit himself as the Son of God.

Reformed answer to Arian misuse: Christ is not here arguing what he is in himself as he would among disciples, but what we ought to acknowledge from his miracles in human flesh. We never comprehend his eternal Divinity unless we embrace him as Redeemer as the Father has exhibited him. He dwells on refuting slander more than on a full lecture.

John 10:37

37If I am not doing the works of my Father, then do not believe me;

Arminian

A real test: if I am not doing the Father’s works, do not believe me. He does not demand blind credulity. The stoning crowd is still offered a criterion they can look at.

Arminian sincerity: unbelief would be reasonable if the works were missing. They are not missing. The invitation to believe remains a true invitation even in a courtyard of stones. Grace argues rather than merely condemning.

Reformed

Calvin: lest they say it is vain for him to boast of sanctification, he draws them again to miracles in which there was sufficiently evident proof of his Divinity. A concession: I do not wish you bound to credit me on any other condition than that you see the fact plainly. You may safely reject me if God has not openly testified.

Reformed note: “works of my Father” means truly divine works, power that cannot be ascribed to a mere man. The test is not skepticism’s throne; it is God’s own attestation, already public, already refused in v. 25.

John 10:38

38but if I do them, even though you do not believe me, believe the works, that you may know and understand that the Father is in me and I am in the Father.”

Arminian

If I do them, even though you do not believe me, believe the works—so that you may know and understand that the Father is in me and I am in the Father. Wesley: in some a more exact knowledge precedes, in others it follows faith; “I am in the Father” and “I and the Father are one” illustrate each other.

Arminian hope: he still wants them to know and understand. Works are a mercy-path for people who will not yet take his word. Mutual indwelling is the content, not a fog. They may yet believe; the sentence is purpose, not irony.

Reformed

Calvin: they are convicted of unbelieving, sacrilegious contempt because they render no honor to what are undoubtedly God’s works. Second concession: though you doubt my doctrine, you cannot deny the miracles are from God; you therefore reject God, not a man. Rebels wish to know before they believe; God indulges so far as to prepare for faith by knowledge of his works, while knowledge of his secret wisdom comes after faith, because obedience of faith opens the door.

Reformed reading of the in-one-another: this discourse relates to manifestation of divine power in Christ’s person, from which it was evident he was sent by God—“The Father is in me” as power displayed; “I in the Father” as doing nothing but by God’s command. All tends to this: in his ministry nothing is contrary to the Father. The same oneness that keeps sheep (v. 30) is the oneness the works are meant to teach.

Key contrast Both take the works as a real witness meant to lead to knowing that the Father is in the Son and the Son in the Father. Arminians typically hear a still-open path: even hostile hearers are urged to believe the works and so come to understand. Reformed readers typically hear the same urging as God’s kindness to rebels, while holding that saving knowledge and keeping-oneness with the Father are given to the sheep who hear. Neither flattens v. 38 into “works without the person” or into a closed taunt.

John 10:39

39Again they sought to arrest him, but he escaped from their hands.

Arminian

Again they sought to arrest him; he escaped from their hands. Stones failed; seizure is next. They cannot hold him, as they cannot snatch his sheep (v. 28). His hour is not theirs to set.

Arminian note: escape is not panic. The one who lays down his life of his own accord (v. 18) will not have it taken on Dedication evening by a mob. The Shepherd walks free because he chooses when to be held.

Reformed

Calvin: undoubtedly that they might drive him out of the temple and immediately stone him; their rage was not abated by his words. Escape could be accomplished only by a wonderful exertion of divine power.

Reformed comfort: we are not exposed to the lawless passions of wicked men, which God restrains by his bridle whenever he thinks fit. The same hand that no one snatches sheep from is the hand that slips the Son from arrest until the appointed hour.

John 10:40

40He went away again across the Jordan to the place where John had been baptizing at first, and there he remained.

Arminian

He goes again across the Jordan, where John first baptized, and remains. Wesley: to the desert place where John baptized and gave so honourable a testimony of him. The forerunner’s river becomes the Shepherd’s lodging.

Arminian geography: leaving the colonnade is not abandoning Israel; it is taking the voice where it was first publicly borne. People can still come. The Door remains open east of the river.

Reformed

Calvin: he passed beyond Jordan that he might not fight continually without advantage; he teaches us to use opportunities. He did not seek solitude in order to cease from duty, but to erect a sanctuary of God in the wilderness when Jerusalem, his own abode, had obstinately driven him out.

Reformed gravity: it is a dreadful vengeance that the temple chosen by God should be a den of robbers while the Church is collected in a despised place. Remaining there is still ministry. The Shepherd gathers where the city would not.

John 10:41

41And many came to him. And they said, “John did no sign, but everything that John said about this man was true.”

Arminian

Many came. They say: John did no sign, but everything John said about this man was true. Wesley: John did no miracle—an honour reserved for him whose forerunner he was. Testimony without signs still holds; the signs belong to the One testified.

Arminian encouragement: coming happens. John’s true word is enough to send people across the Jordan after Jesus. Faith can rest on a faithful witness plus the man himself, not on a contest of wonders between prophet and Messiah.

Reformed

Calvin: the large assembly shows the wilderness sanctuary. They infer Christ is more excellent than John because of many miracles while John performed none—not that we should always judge by miracles, but that miracles united with doctrine have no small weight. The argument takes for granted that John was an eminent prophet; therefore Christ is to be preferred, and this was so by God’s providence for Christ’s sake.

Reformed pairing: two reasons induced them to believe—John’s testimony proved true, and Christ’s miracles procured greater authority. The Evangelist adds what they may not have said in full: all that John spoke was true. Forerunner and signs agree.

John 10:42

42And many believed in him there.

Arminian

Many believed in him there. Across the Jordan, away from the stones, the voice finds hearers. Sheep, in the Arminian sense of this chapter, are those who hear and follow; here many do. The Dedication refusal was not the last word on the mission.

Classical Arminian close: believing there is a real human response to a true witness and a true Shepherd. The same Christ who said they did not believe because they were not among his sheep now has many who do believe. The Door still admits. Remaining his will still mean hearing his voice—and he is still the one who gives life and from whose hand none snatches the following flock.

Reformed

Calvin’s scene: the church gathered in a despised place, John’s word vindicated, signs confirming. Many believed on him there. The gathering of other sheep (v. 16) is already in motion beyond Judea’s official fold.

Reformed close: believing there does not cancel 10:26–29; it illustrates it. Where the voice is heard, there are sheep. Jerusalem’s unbelief does not empty the Shepherd’s hand. Effectual calling can look like a riverside crowd finally agreeing that John told the truth—and the Son, one with the Father, giving them a life no one will snatch.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the sheep who hear, the life that cannot be snatched, and the oneness of Son and Father—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 10:22–42.

Arminian Notable voices

“Ye do not believe, because ye are not of my sheep—Because ye do not, will not follow me: because ye are proud, unholy, lovers of praise, lovers of the world, lovers of pleasure, not of God. My sheep are they who hear my voice by faith; are known (that is, approved) by me, as loving me; and follow me, keep my commandments, with a believing, loving heart. And to those who truly believe (observe three promises annexed to three conditions) I give eternal life. He does not say, I will, but I give. For he that believeth hath everlasting life. Those whom I know truly to love me, shall never perish, provided they abide in my love. Those who follow me, neither men nor devils can pluck out of my hand.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 10:26–29)

Reformed Notable voices

“You do not believe, because you are not of my sheep. He assigns a higher reason why they do not believe either in his miracles or in his doctrine. It is, because they are reprobate. God effectually calls all whom he has elected, so that the sheep of Christ are proved by their faith. And they shall never perish. This is a remarkable passage, by which we are taught that the salvation of all the elect is not less certain than the power of God is invincible. We infer that the statement of Christ is intended to show that the elect are absolutely certain of their salvation. I and my Father are one.
