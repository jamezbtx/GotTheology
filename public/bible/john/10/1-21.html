Passage

John 10

Verses 1–21 · ESV · Voices

Jesus answers the Pharisees of chapter 9 with a sheepfold figure: thieves climb another way; the shepherd enters by the door, calls his own by name, and they follow his voice. He is the door and the good shepherd who lays down his life of his own accord, has other sheep not of this fold, and takes his life up again. The words divide the hearers: some say he has a demon; others, these are not the words of one who is oppressed by a demon.

John 10:1

1“Truly, truly, I say to you, he who does not enter the sheepfold by the door but climbs in by another way, that man is a thief and a robber.

Arminian

Truly, truly: the fold has a door, and climbing another way is theft. Wesley: the door is Christ, the only lawful entrance; the sheepfold is the Church; a thief and robber in God’s account—such were the teachers Jesus had just been answering in chapter 9.

Arminian pastoral note: office without Christ is not shepherding. The warning is public and sincere. Hearers can tell a climber from a door-man; they are not told to stay under whoever holds the keys of the fold.

Reformed

Calvin: scribes and priests were reckoned pastors, yet Christ will not let that title bless their unbelief. Not all who outwardly claim a place in the Church are shepherds or sheep. No plague is more destructive than wolves in shepherds’ garb.

Reformed framing: the honorable name “Church” makes the distinction between true and false doctrine more urgent, not less. Weak consciences need not panic when rulers resist the Gospel; the door, not the occupancy of the fold, tells who belongs.

John 10:2

2But he who enters by the door is the shepherd of the sheep.

Arminian

The contrast is simple: the shepherd uses the door. True pastors come through Christ, not around him. Sheep are not a private brand the climber can steal by rank.

Classical Arminians hear a test for ministry. If the way in is Christ, then preaching that bypasses him—tradition as salvation, power over consciences apart from the Shepherd—is already robbery, however authorized it looks.

Reformed

Calvin’s general view: Christ compares the Church to a sheepfold and himself to a door, because there is no other entrance but by himself. They alone are good shepherds who lead people straight to Christ.

Reformed accent: belonging to the flock means devotion to Christ alone, and all of this is doctrinal. Whoever turns aside from him neither keeps the road nor enters by the door, whatever title he wears.

John 10:3

3To him the gatekeeper opens. The sheep hear his voice, and he calls his own sheep by name and leads them out.

Arminian

The gatekeeper opens; the sheep hear; he calls his own by name and leads them out. Wesley: it is not unworthy of the Father to be styled the door-keeper. Real Christians hear, listen to, understand, and obey; he counteth them his own, dearer than friend or brother, and leadeth them in paths of righteousness.

Arminian hearing: the call is personal and the response is real—hear, listen, obey. Named sheep are not deaf objects. The Shepherd’s voice is meant to be followed, and following is how the flock is known.

Reformed

Calvin will allow the porter to be God, contrasting God’s judgment with the world’s applause of pastors. He takes “calls his own by name” as the mutual consent of faith: teacher and disciple united by one Spirit, so the teacher goes before and the disciple follows.

Reformed accent: “his own” is already on the page. The sheep who hear are not a random sample of the fold’s occupants; they are the ones he calls out as his. Voice and ownership go together.

John 10:4

4When he has brought out all his own, he goes before them, and the sheep follow him, for they know his voice.

Arminian

He goes before; they follow because they know his voice. Wesley: he teaches by example as well as precept; they tread in his steps, having the witness in themselves that his words are the wisdom and power of God.

Classical Arminians put weight on following. Knowledge of the voice is not a tattoo; it is recognized and obeyed. The security of the flock is the security of those who actually go after the Shepherd who goes first.

Reformed

Calvin: we must attend to why the sheep follow—they know how to distinguish shepherds from wolves by the voice. This is the spirit of discernment by which the elect discriminate God’s truth from men’s inventions. Knowledge of the truth goes before; then an earnest desire to obey.

Reformed hearing: they not only understand what is true but receive it with warm affection. Effectual hearing shows in following. The voice creates a flock that can tell the Shepherd from a stranger.

John 10:5

5A stranger they will not follow, but they will flee from him, for they do not know the voice of strangers.”

Arminian

They will not follow a stranger; they flee; they do not know that voice. Wesley: one whom Christ hath not sent; who can constrain them? They flee as from the plague; they cannot relish it; they find nothing of God therein.

Arminian note: refusal of the stranger is a living act of the flock, not a spell. Sheep can be warned and can flee. Remaining in the flock includes a holy intolerance of voices that do not sound like the Shepherd.

Reformed

Calvin commends not only assembling at the shepherd’s voice but also not listening to strangers and not dispersing when anyone cries. The sheep of Christ are kept from the new winds that carry others off.

Reformed accent: discernment is a gift in the flock, not a hobby. That they will not follow a stranger is evidence of whose they are. The same voice that draws them out teaches them to run from counterfeits.

John 10:6

6This figure of speech Jesus used with them, but they did not understand what he was saying to them.

Arminian

The figure is used; they do not understand. Wesley: “they” are the Pharisees to whom he more immediately spoke, as the close of chapter 9 shows. Light can be spoken in a parable and still meet a closed ear.

Arminian patience: misunderstanding here is not innocence. They have just seen a blind man given sight and then been told they are blind. The next sentences will drop the figure and name the Door. Remaining baffled after that is a decision.

Reformed

Calvin: they proudly vaunt wisdom and reject the light of Christ; in a matter not very obscure they are dull of apprehension. Unbelief can wear the face of not getting the metaphor.

Reformed note: failure to understand the sheepfold is of a piece with chapter 9’s “are we also blind?” Sight is the Shepherd’s gift. The unexplained figure already sorts hearers before the “I am” sayings land.

John 10:7

7So Jesus again said to them, “Truly, truly, I say to you, I am the door of the sheep.

Arminian

He says it again, now without hiding: I am the door of the sheep. Wesley: Christ is both the Door and the Shepherd, and all things. Access is a person, sincerely set before them.

Arminian gospel: if he is the door, then entering is by him—through faith, as Wesley will say at v. 9—not by lineage, Sanhedrin, or climbing. The invitation is as wide as “I am.”

Reformed

Calvin: without this explanation the discourse would have remained allegory. The principal point of all spiritual doctrine, on which souls are fed, consists in Christ. There is but one open door; all approach another way is prohibited.

Reformed center: he invokes all who desire salvation to come to him. In vain do they wander who leave him to go to God. The Door is exclusive because he is sufficient, not because the sentence is a riddle.

John 10:8

8All who came before me are thieves and robbers, but the sheep did not listen to them.

Arminian

All who came before—Wesley: independently of me, assuming my character, rabbis over consciences, traditions as the way of salvation, running before they were sent—are thieves (temporal profit) and robbers (plundering the sheep). But the sheep did not listen.

Arminian reading: not listening is the flock’s real refusal of false voices. Sheep are those who will not take a counterfeit Christ. Moses and the prophets are not in view as thieves; those who steal the flock from the Door are.

Reformed

Calvin will not restrict this to Judas the Galilean; Christ contrasts all false doctrine with the Gospel. Moses and the prophets aimed to establish Christ’s kingdom; the Law is a preparation for the Gospel. Apart from him there is nothing but destruction.

Reformed consolation: those led away by impostors did not belong to the Church of God. Christ has always guarded his sheep so that they are not left as Satan’s prey. By nature we are lions and wolves until the Spirit tames us; we are already sheep in God’s heart before we are born, and we begin to be sheep in ourselves by the calling.

John 10:9

9I am the door. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved and will go in and out and find pasture.

Arminian

I am the door—repeated. If anyone enters by me, he will be saved, go in and out, find pasture. Wesley: as a sheep, through faith, he shall be safe from the wolf and from murdering shepherds; he shall attend the shepherds Christ has sent; pasture is food for the soul in all circumstances.

Classical Arminians rest on anyone. The Door is not a trick door. Entering is by faith in him; salvation, liberty, and pasture are promised to the one who does. The condition is real; the promise is sincere. Remaining in that safety is remaining in the Door, not climbing another way later.

Reformed

Calvin: the highest consolation of believers is that, having once embraced Christ, they learn they are out of danger; he promises salvation and happiness. Going in and out: they shall go safely wherever necessary and be fed to the full. Twofold advantage of the Gospel: pasture for famished souls, and faithful protection against wolves and robbers.

Reformed pairing: the anyone is as large as the Door; those who enter are those who actually come through Christ. The verse is comfort for the flock in him, not a claim that unaided wanderers appoint themselves sheep. Saved, going in and out, finding pasture—this is the life of those the Shepherd has taken in.

Key contrast Shared ground: Christ is the only door; entering by him means salvation, freedom, and pasture. Arminians typically hear “anyone” as a sincere, universal invitation through faith, with safety belonging to those who enter and remain in him. Reformed readers typically hear the same invitation as truly addressed, while taking the ones who enter as the sheep Christ actually gathers and keeps—pasture and protection as the fruit of being in the Door, not as a self-made lease.

John 10:10

10The thief comes only to steal and kill and destroy. I came that they may have life and have it abundantly.

Arminian

Two comings, two aims. The thief: steal, kill, destroy. Wesley: nothing else can follow a shepherd who does not enter by Christ. Jesus: I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly.

Arminian weight: abundant life is the errand of the coming, not a footnote. The “they” are sheep he came to give life to; the offer is as generous as the contrast with the thief is sharp. Life is received in the Door, not extracted from him.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ pulls our ear so that Satan’s ministers may not find us drowsy. False teachers, however mild, carry deadly poison. Then the comparison shifts: he is not only the door but the only Shepherd strictly so called; other faithful pastors are his agents, raised, endowed, and governed by him.

Reformed accent: they only who will not submit to his staff are exposed to wolves. Life is continually increased in those who do not revolt from him. Progress in faith approaches fullness of life because the Spirit, who is life, grows in the sheep.

John 10:11

11I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.

Arminian

I am the good shepherd—another “I am.” The mark is not hire but a life laid down for the sheep. The death is for them: actual, costly, intended. Wesley will later say the ransom is for the sins of the whole world (v. 18); here the flock is named as the ones he dies for as their Shepherd.

Do not hear Arminians denying that he keeps those who are his. They preach a Shepherd who truly dies to save, calls all to enter by the Door, and actually cares for the sheep who hear. “For the sheep” is love in blood, not a slogan that the world was never in view.

Reformed

Calvin: from extraordinary affection he shows how truly he is shepherd—so anxious about their salvation that he does not spare his own life. They who reject the guardianship of so kind a shepherd are exceedingly ungrateful. Augustine is right that the passage teaches what to desire, avoid, and endure in the Church’s government.

Reformed confession: he keeps the Church safe first by himself, then by his agents. The life is laid down for the sheep—his own, whom he knows (vv. 14–15). Particular love here does not make the Door a feint; it names the people his death actually saves and the flock he will not abandon to the wolf.

Key contrast Both worship the good shepherd who dies for the sheep. Arminians typically hold the death as a real ransom with the world in view (v. 18) and the flock as those who hear and enter; they still confess he actually saves and keeps his sheep. Reformed readers typically take “for the sheep” as the people given him, whom he effectually saves—without denying that thieves are warned and hearers are summoned through the Door.

John 10:12

12He who is a hired hand and not a shepherd, who does not own the sheep, sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and flees, and the wolf snatches them and scatters them.

Arminian

Hired hand, not owner: he sees the wolf, leaves, flees; the wolf snatches and scatters. Wesley: hire itself does not make a hireling—the laborer is worthy of his hire—but loving the hire more than the work. The wolf is any enemy who by force or fraud attacks a Christian’s faith, liberty, or life; he seizes some and scatters the rest.

Arminian pastoral edge: scattering is a real wound in the flock. That is why remaining with the good Shepherd matters, and why hireling ministry is a grief. The verse assumes sheep can be harmed by wolves when the wrong man is over them—hence the call to the Shepherd who will not flee.

Reformed

Calvin: hirelings may retain pure doctrine and proclaim truth to serve a purpose rather than from pure zeal; we ought still to hear them as sitting in Moses’ seat, yet the contest will prove their treachery. Christ appears almost to make all shepherds besides himself hirelings, since none of us may say the sheep are our own—yet those guided by the Spirit reckon what belongs to the Head as their charge.

Reformed note: the wolf’s harm in this verse is what happens under a fleeing hireling, not a denial of vv. 28–29. The good Shepherd’s flock is not finally the wolf’s; bad under-shepherds still do real harm, which is why Christ prefers even one sheep to his own life.

John 10:13

13He flees because he is a hired hand and cares nothing for the sheep.

Arminian

He flees because he is a hired hand: Wesley—because he loves the hire, not the sheep. Care is the difference. The good Shepherd’s opposite is not a poorly paid man but a man whose motive cannot survive a wolf.

Arminian warning: congregations can smell this. Ministry for pay-without-love is already flight in seed form. The sheep need a carer, not a contractor.

Reformed

Calvin: he flees because he cares not for the sheep—his heart is not moved by the scattering of the flock, because he thinks it does not belong to him. Looking to the hire, he may deceive in tranquillity; in the contest he shows what he is.

Reformed diagnosis: ownership and care are Christ’s, shared with agents only as they are united to the Head. A heart unmoved by scattered sheep is already outside the Shepherd’s affection, whatever the title.

John 10:14

14I am the good shepherd. I know my own and my own know me,

Arminian

Again: I am the good shepherd. I know my own; my own know me. Wesley: he knows with tender regard and special care; he is known with a holy confidence and affection. Mutual knowledge is the flock’s life, not a password for a closed caste.

Classical Arminians: to be his own is to be in this knowing—hearing, trusting, loving back. Sheep are identified by that reciprocity. Knowing him is how one remains in the flock; refusing that knowledge is how one shows oneself not his own.

Reformed

Calvin: knowledge proceeds from love and is accompanied by care. He holds out his love toward us, and in the other clause utterly disregards all who do not obey the Gospel—as he will confirm: he is known by the sheep.

Reformed accent: “my own” is already his before their answering knowledge, yet the answering knowledge is not optional. The sheep know him because they are his; those who will not obey are not excused by a vacant title of sheep.

John 10:15

15just as the Father knows me and I know the Father; and I lay down my life for the sheep.

Arminian

The knowing is measured by the Father-Son knowing. Wesley: a knowledge that implies an inexpressible union; and I lay down my life—speaking of the present, for his whole life was only a going unto death. The cross is already in the shepherding.

Arminian Christology: union with the Father grounds the death for the sheep. He does not die as a trapped victim (v. 18) but as the known Son. The flock is invited into a knowledge analogical to that union—real, not mechanical.

Reformed

Calvin refuses thorny speculation about how the Father knows his Wisdom. Christ is the bond of our union with God, placed between Him and us: it is no more possible that he forget us than that he be rejected by the Father. He employs all the power received from the Father for our protection, and wishes us obedient as he is devoted to the Father.

Reformed center: the life laid down for the sheep is the Father’s known Son dying for a known flock. Protection and death are one ministry. The comparison “just as” is pastoral assurance, not a claim that our knowing equals the inner life of God.

John 10:16

16And I have other sheep that are not of this fold. I must bring them also, and they will listen to my voice. So there will be one flock, one shepherd.

Arminian

Other sheep, not of this fold: Wesley—which he foreknew; not of the Jewish Church or nation, but Gentiles. I must bring them into my Church, the general assembly of those whose names are written in heaven. One flock (Wesley insists: not “one fold,” a false print)—no corrupt or divided flocks remaining; one shepherd who laid down his life and will leave no hireling among them. The whole flock shall hear the one shepherd.

Arminian hearing: “they will listen” is promise with a voice attached. Gentiles are truly sought; the must-bring is the Shepherd’s mission, not a shrug over Israel. Names written in heaven do not cancel the hearing by which they are gathered. One flock is the goal of a death wide enough to make one people out of two.

Reformed

Calvin has no doubt this is the calling of the Gentiles: “fold” was Israel’s enclosure of rites; other sheep lacked that mark but not the Shepherd’s claim. Augustine is true that there are wolves within and sheep without, yet here the point is the outward gathering of strangers into the kingdom. He names unbelievers “sheep” by secret election—we are already God’s sheep before we know he is our shepherd.

Reformed certainty: “I must bring them” means election will be secure, so that nothing he wishes saved shall perish. Secret purpose is manifested in effectual calling, when he regenerates by his Spirit those formerly of flesh and blood. The flock is gathered when all have one shepherd and his voice alone is heard. They will listen because he brings them.

Key contrast Both hear Gentile inclusion and one flock under one shepherd. Arminians typically take “other sheep” as those he foreknew and will bring by a voice they truly hear—gathered into the church, one flock without remaining hireling divisions. Reformed readers typically take the other sheep as already his by election before they resemble sheep, brought by effectual calling so that none he wills to save perish. Neither side should be heard as making the Gentile mission insincere or as shrinking the one Shepherd.

John 10:17

17For this reason the Father loves me, because I lay down my life that I may take it up again.

Arminian

The Father’s love is tied to this laying-down in order to take-up. Wesley: I cheerfully die to expiate the sins of men, to the end I may rise again for their justification. Death is not the end of the shepherding; resurrection is in the same sentence.

Arminian gospel: expiation and justification are the cheerful aim. The sheep get a living Shepherd, not a merely tragic one. Taking the life up again is as much the errand as laying it down.

Reformed

Calvin knows a higher reason the Father loves the Son—the beloved in whom he is well pleased—yet as he was made man for us, we need not wonder if he declares the reason to be that our salvation is dearer to him than his own life. He comforts disciples shaken by the death-word with speedy resurrection: he will not be swallowed by death but rise a conqueror.

Reformed doxology: contemplate the death and, at the same time, the glory of the resurrection. He is life because in contest with death he obtained a splendid victory. The Father’s love here is the love that gives us a dying-and-rising Shepherd.

John 10:18

18No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord. I have authority to lay it down, and I have authority to take it up again. This charge I have received from my Father.”

Arminian

No one takes it; he lays it down of his own accord. Authority both ways; a charge from the Father. Wesley: by my own free act; original power and right of myself to lay it down as a ransom and take it again after full satisfaction is made for the sins of the whole world. The Father’s commission is why he used that power—obedience—not the ground of the power itself, which he had antecedent to the commission.

Classical Arminians hear “whole world” without embarrassment, and “of my own accord” without making the Father a bystander. The death is voluntary, sufficient, aimed at a world of sinners, and still the charge of the Father. Own-accord is not independence from the Father; it is the Son’s willing ransom.

Reformed

Calvin: another consolation—he does not die by constraint but offers himself willingly for the salvation of his flock. Men have no power to put him to death except as he permits; he is free from every violence of necessity. We die for our sins; he was born mortal by voluntary submission. The death is an expiation because it was a voluntary sacrifice: by the obedience of one many were made righteous.

Reformed accent: he recalls us to the Father’s eternal purpose—such care for our salvation that he dedicated to us his only-begotten Son. “Of my own accord” and “this charge” are one will, not a tug-of-war. The flock’s redemption is a free offering inside the Father’s commandment; authority to take the life up again is already in the laying down.

Key contrast Shared ground: the death is voluntary, authoritative, and the Father’s charge; no one takes the Son’s life as if he were overpowered. Arminians (Wesley) typically stress original right in the Son and a ransom for the sins of the whole world. Reformed readers typically stress willing obedience inside the Father’s eternal purpose, an expiation for the flock he actually saves. Do not hear either camp as making the cross an accident or the Father and Son at cross-purposes.

John 10:19

19There was again a division among the Jews because of these words.

Arminian

Again a division, because of these words—door, shepherd, death of his own accord, other sheep. The same sermon splits the room. Words about a dying Shepherd do not produce a calm consensus.

Arminian note: division is not a failure of clarity. Some will slander; some will weigh the words against the opened eyes of chapter 9. The split is already a moral response to a sincere claim.

Reformed

Calvin: the discourse procured some disciples, but as the doctrine has many adversaries, they split who formerly appeared one body. If that Abraham-and-Moses profession had been sincere, Christ, the bond of charity, would not have broken their agreement. The light of the Gospel exposes hypocritical pretense.

Reformed caution: when schisms arise, inquire who revolts from God and from his pure doctrine. It is better that some separate from the wicked to be united to Christ than that all be of one mind in despising God. Blame for the tear belongs to unbelief, not to the Shepherd’s voice.

John 10:20

20Many of them said, “He has a demon, and is insane; why listen to him?”

Arminian

Many reach for the hardest slander: demon, insanity, stop listening. The good Shepherd’s claim is treated as madness. “Why listen?” is already a refusal of the voice the sheep hear.

Arminian warning: this is how a room leaves the fold without walking out—by naming the Shepherd’s voice demonic. The question is not curiosity; it is a campaign to shut ears. Listening remains the issue.

Reformed

Calvin: they employ the most offensive reproach they can devise, that all may shudder at hearing him. Wicked men, that they may not be forced to yield to God, break out with closed eyes into proud contempt and excite others to the same rage, so that not a single word of Christ is heard in silence.

Reformed diagnosis: unbelief here is not a meek maybe. It is a strategy to make the voice unsayable. The doctrine, Calvin adds, has sufficient power in itself to defend it against slanders—which is what the next verse begins to do.

John 10:21

21Others said, “These are not the words of one who is oppressed by a demon. Can a demon open the eyes of the blind?”

Arminian

Others refuse the slander: these are not the words of one oppressed by a demon. Wesley: the original word takes in actions too. Can a demon open the eyes of the blind?—chapter 9 is still in the room. Words and works sit in judgment on the charge.

Arminian close: some hear and test. The Door has already given sight; the Shepherd’s speech does not sound like oppression. Division here includes a real yes forming. The next scene (vv. 22–42) will press the same crowd toward a plainer Christ and a harder line about who his sheep are.

Reformed

Calvin: believers demand that people judge from the fact itself; truth is strong enough to maintain itself. This is the only protection of our faith: wicked men will never hinder the power, wisdom, and goodness of God from shining in the Gospel.

Reformed close: the opened eyes of the man born blind remain public evidence. Some already know these are not a demoniac’s words. The winter Dedication scene will not create a new Christ; it will show why many still will not believe—and why those who are his hear.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the door, the good shepherd, the life laid down of his own accord, and the other sheep—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 10:1–21.

Arminian Notable voices

“I am the door—Christ is both the Door and the Shepherd, and all things. If any one—as a sheep, enter in by me—through faith, he shall be safe—from the wolf, and from those murdering shepherds. I have also other sheep—which he foreknew; which are not of this fold—not of the Jewish Church or nation, but Gentiles. I must bring them likewise. I lay it down of myself—by my own free act and deed. I have an original power and right of myself, both to lay it down as a ransom, and to take it again, after full satisfaction is made, for the sins of the whole world.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 10:7–18)

Reformed Notable voices

“The good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep. From the extraordinary affection which he bears towards the sheep, he shows how truly he acts towards them as a shepherd; for he is so anxious about their salvation, that he does not even spare his own life. And I have other sheep. Them also I must bring. He means that the election of God will be secure, so that nothing of all that he wishes to be saved shall perish. For the secret purpose of God, by which men were ordained to life, is at length manifested in his own time by the calling—the effectual calling, when he regenerates by his Spirit, to be his sons, those who formerly were begotten of flesh and blood. We are already God’s sheep, before we are aware that He is our shepherd.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 10:11–16)
