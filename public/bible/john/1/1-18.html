Passage

John 1

Verses 1–18 · ESV · Voices

John’s prologue: the eternal Word with God and as God, life and light, the Baptist as witness, rejection by his own, the right to become children of God for those who receive/believe, born of God, the Word made flesh full of grace and truth.

John 1:1

1In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.

Arminian

John opens where Genesis opens, then names the One who was already there. Classical Arminians confess with the whole church: the Word is God—supreme, eternal, independent. This is no created mind and no lesser deity.

The camps do not divide on the Trinity here. They will divide on how this Word is received. The eternity of the Son is shared ground; the later call to receive him is a real invitation, not theater.

Reformed

In the beginning was the Word: before time, with God, and God. Reformed reading follows Calvin: the Evangelist asserts the eternal divinity of Christ so that faith in the Son is faith in the only God.

If the Word were not God, believing in him would be idolatry. The prologue will not let us treat Jesus as a gifted man who later became divine. He is the eternal God who will be manifested in flesh.

John 1:2

2He was in the beginning with God.

Arminian

“With God” guards distinction of person; “in the beginning” guards eternity. The Word is not the Father, yet never begins after the Father. Arminian faith needs this as much as any other: the One we receive is the everlasting Son.

Pastoral weight: prayer, trust, and obedience are offered to One who already was. Receiving him in verse 12 is not inventing a god; it is answering the God who has always been.

Reformed

The Evangelist restates verse 1 so the church cannot slip into Sabellianism or Arianism. He was with God in the beginning: distinct person, undivided essence, no start of days.

Reformed comfort: the Christ we believe is not a new actor in the drama of salvation. The same One who will tabernacle in flesh already stood with the Father before any world was made.

John 1:3

3All things were made through him, and without him was not any thing made that was made.

Arminian

Creation is through the Word—no exception. Classical Arminians hear a generous Maker, not a distant craftsman. The One through whom all things were made is the same One who later stands at the door of the human heart.

If he made all, then none of us is outside his claim or his kindness. The prologue’s later grief that “the world did not know him” is tragic precisely because the Maker came to what was his.

Reformed

Divinity is proved from works: all things were made through him, and nothing that was made stands outside that “through.” The Father works by the Son; the Son is not a creature among creatures.

Reformed accent: the Redeemer is first the Creator. Restoration will not be the rescue of a world that belongs to someone else. The Speech who made all will also renew; both works are his.

John 1:4

4In him was life, and the life was the light of men.

Arminian

Life is in the Word, and that life is light for human beings. Arminian reading takes the gift as real illumination—reason, conscience, and the further light that will shine in the gospel. Light is given so that people might see.

Prevenient grace lives near this verse. The Word does not hoard life; he sheds it. What we do with that light will be narrated in verses 10–12, where receiving and refusing both count.

Reformed

In him was life: not borrowed, not intermittent. The Speech is the source and the upholding of life, and the life bestowed on humankind is joined to the light of understanding.

Reformed reading: this light is first the gift of reason in creation, a mirror of the Speech’s power. Saving light will require a further office of the Mediator. Life is in him before any of us believes.

John 1:5

5The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it.

Arminian

The light shines—present tense, actual shining—in a real darkness that does not finally win. Classical Arminians refuse to treat the shine as a private lamp for a hidden few. It truly shines, and people may turn toward it or away.

“Has not overcome it” is hope, not fatalism. Darkness is strong; it is not sovereign. The Word’s light can be resisted, yet it is not extinguished. The invitation still stands in the dark.

Reformed

Light shines in darkness, and darkness has not overcome—or, in older English, not comprehended—it. Fallen reason still has sparks, Calvin says, yet those sparks do not find the way to God.

Reformed pastoral: the verse is not a compliment to human nature. It takes away the excuse of ignorance and still concludes that unless God grant new aid, shining is not the same as being received. The darkness needs the Mediator, not better eyesight alone.

John 1:6

6There was a man sent from God, whose name was John.

Arminian

A man, sent from God: the Baptist is not the Light, but he is no freelance voice either. Arminian reading honors the sending. God uses witnesses so that people may hear and respond.

John’s name and office are gift. The God who sends a herald is the God who sincerely wants the message heard. A sent witness assumes hearers who can believe or refuse the testimony.

Reformed

There was a man sent from God. The Baptist is introduced as ambassador, not as private enthusiast. Reformed emphasis: the authority of the witness rests on the Sender, not on John’s virtues.

God appoints a herald so that Christ may be known. The sending is providence for our sake, Calvin notes, not because the Word needed human help to be who he is.

John 1:7

7He came as a witness, to bear witness about the light, that all might believe through him.

Arminian

The purpose clause is generous: that all might believe through him. Classical Arminians take “all” as the sincere aim of the witness, not coded language for a narrower set. John’s ministry is ordered toward faith as a real response.

Prevenient grace and the preached word meet here. The Baptist points; people may believe. The “might” is not a feint. God wants the testimony to land as faith.

Reformed

John came for a testimony, that by him all might believe. Reformed reading hears the wide public of the witness and the particular fruit God gives. The herald prepares a church for Christ; the Light himself must open the heart.

“Through him” means through the Baptist’s testimony, not through John’s person as savior. Effectual faith is still the Spirit’s work when the witness is believed.

Key contrast Arminians hear “that all might believe” as God’s sincere, resistible aim in the witness; the Reformed hear a public testimony whose saving effect God grants to those he draws, not an equal outcome in every hearer.

John 1:8

8He was not the light, but came to bear witness about the light.

Arminian

John is not the light. The distinction protects both Christ and the preacher. Arminian pastoral: the best witness still only points. Saving light is a Person to be received, not a personality to be followed.

The Baptist’s humility models the church’s. We bear witness about the light so that hearers may believe in another. The pointer must not become the prize.

Reformed

He was not that light. Calvin warns that some gazed so eagerly on John that they neglected Christ—as if the dawn could replace the sun. Reformed reading keeps the only eternal source of illumination in view.

Ministers are lights in the Lord only by reflection. The Evangelist will not let even a God-sent prophet occupy the place of the Word. Witness is servant-speech.

John 1:9

9The true light, which gives light to everyone, was coming into the world.

Arminian

The true light gives light to everyone and was coming into the world. This is a classic prevenient-grace text: illumination is not a private lamp for a hidden few. The Word’s coming is light offered to all.

“Everyone” is not flattery of nature; it is the reach of grace that makes faith possible. Classical Arminians refuse to shrink this verse into a merely natural spark that never intends salvation.

Reformed

The true light enlightens every one coming into the world. Calvin reads this chiefly as the common light of nature—reason and conscience—far inferior to faith. The Spirit alone opens heaven to the elect.

Reformed caution: “everyone” is true of that creational gift, not of equal regenerating grace. The Light is personally self-shining; saving sight is still a further mercy.

Key contrast Arminians take “gives light to everyone” as saving, prevenient illumination that makes believing possible for all; the Reformed typically take it as the common light of nature, not equal regenerating grace.

John 1:10

10He was in the world, and the world was made through him, yet the world did not know him.

Arminian

Maker in his world, and the world did not know him. The ignorance is culpable, not fated in a way that erases responsibility. Arminian reading: they could have known; they did not. Grace was present; recognition was refused.

The tragedy is personal. The One through whom the world was made came near enough to be known. Not knowing him is a real failure to receive the light already given.

Reformed

He was in the world, the world was made through him, and the world knew him not. Calvin charges ingratitude: men drew from the stream and never thought of the fountain.

Reformed note: this blindness is not a small gap in information. Fallen humanity does not recognize its Maker unless God grant new aid. The guilt is ours; the cure will be the Word made flesh and the Spirit’s work.

John 1:11

11He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him.

Arminian

He came to his own—covenant nearness, household claim—and his own did not receive him. “Receive” is a real verb. Classical Arminians hear a genuine offer refused, not a pageant whose outcome was never in question.

The grief of this verse prepares verse 12. Some do not receive; some do. Both are treated as responsible responses to the One who came. Rejection is possible; so is welcome.

Reformed

He came to his own, and his own received him not. Calvin hears the desperate wickedness of those to whom the Son was especially promised. The offense of Israel’s unbelief is real; it does not cancel the Redeemer.

Reformed pastoral: the Evangelist removes a stumbling block. Christ’s rejection by his people does not unmake him. The next verse will show another people given the right of children—by God’s begetting, not by lineage.

John 1:12

12But to all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God,

Arminian

Receive and believe are joined: this is a real, grace-enabled response, not a metaphor for being processed. He gave the right—sonship is gift—to those who believed in his name. Wesley: the moment they believe, they are sons.

Prevenient grace makes receiving possible; it does not make it inevitable. Conditionality sits here without turning faith into a wage. The right is given; the receiving is not coerced. Jews or Gentiles, all who believe are welcomed.

Reformed

As many as received him—that is, who believed in his name—he gave the right to become children of God. Receiving is faith; faith is how we are engrafted into the only-begotten Son. The right is claim and gift, not a latent faculty we activate at will.

Calvin will not let “power to become” mean a suspended choice. Those who believe are already being named children. Verse 13 will trace that faith to a birth not of the will of man. Effectual grace produces the receiving this verse describes.

Key contrast Arminian readings treat receive/believe as the real, resistible response by which people obtain the right to become children of God; Reformed readings treat that receiving as the faith God produces in those he is begetting—the right of sonship is gift, not the fruit of autonomous choice.

John 1:13

13who were born, not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God.

Arminian

Three denials: not blood (Abraham’s line), not the will of the flesh (natural generation), not the will of man (human scheme or adopting power). Children of God are born of God by the Spirit. Classical Arminians gladly say new birth is God’s work, not ours.

This verse does not erase verse 12’s receiving. The Spirit begets those who believe; lineage cannot substitute for faith. The prologue names the source—God—without turning this sentence into the Nicodemus discourse of chapter 3.

Reformed

Born not of bloods, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God. The Evangelist explains verse 12 so no one credits human willing. Regeneration is the heavenly source from which faith comes; no one believes unless begotten of God.

Reformed reading keeps this as prologue, not a John 3 essay: those who receive him are those God has born. Perseverance sits nearby—the children remain his because the begetting is his. Flesh and family cannot start or finish this life.

Key contrast Arminians hear “born of God” as the Spirit’s work in those who have received Christ, excluding lineage and human scheme but not excluding believing; the Reformed hear regeneration that precedes and produces faith, so that no one believes by the will of the flesh.

John 1:14

14And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth.

Arminian

The eternal Word became flesh and pitched his tent among us. Classical Arminians hear nearness: God comes close enough to be seen, trusted, and received. Glory is not withheld; it is as of the only Son, full of grace and truth.

Incarnation is the Father’s generous self-giving. The same Word who was God now dwells with us so that believing in his name is believing a Person we can know. Grace and truth are not abstractions; they have a human face.

Reformed

The Speech was made flesh and dwelt among us. Two natures, one Person: true God, true man, neither mixed nor divided. Reformed confession follows the Evangelist’s brevity and weight. We beheld his glory—eyes the Spirit opened.

Full of grace and truth: the only-begotten is the fountain, not a cistern. The majesty of God was not annihilated by flesh; it was manifested in it. Faith rests on this One, not on a ghost or a mere man.

John 1:15

15(John bore witness about him, and cried out, “This was he of whom I said, ‘He who comes after me ranks before me, because he was before me.’”)

Arminian

John cries out so that no one confuses the herald with the Lord. He who comes after ranks before, because he was before—eternity, not mere seniority. Arminian reading: the witness is public, loud, and meant to be believed.

The Baptist keeps stepping aside. A true witness wants hearers to receive the One who was before him. Ranking and pre-existence serve the invitation, not speculation for its own sake.

Reformed

John testifies in the present tense, Calvin notes, as if the voice still sounds. Christ came after John in public ministry and before him in rank because he was before him in being.

Reformed accent: all gifts and honors must stay below this One. The Baptist surrenders his office to the eternal Word. Witness that does not lower itself before Christ’s priority is no longer witness.

John 1:16

16For from his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace.

Arminian

From his fullness we have all received—grace stacked on grace. Classical Arminians hear a fountain that is not rationed by a secret few. Fullness is in Christ; receiving is real; more grace meets those who keep coming.

“All” in the believing community still names a generous Christ. Empty people are supplied. The pastoral tone is gratitude and further asking, not anxiety that the well might be for someone else.

Reformed

Out of his fullness have we all received. Calvin: we are destitute; the abundance in Christ supplies our lack; we must not hunt a drop of good anywhere else. He is ready to flow to us—and faith is the channel God himself opens.

Grace upon grace: one gift answering another from the same fullness. Reformed comfort: the fountain is inexhaustible for those in Christ. Perseverance is not scraping a cistern; it is staying at this fullness.

John 1:17

17For the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Law through Moses; grace and truth through Jesus Christ. Arminian reading does not despise Moses; it refuses to treat the law as a rival savior. What the law required and shadowed, Christ brings as gift.

Grace and truth “came”—they arrived in a Person. The invitation of the prologue is to receive that Person, not to climb Moses into sonship. Faith is how empty people take what has come.

Reformed

The law was given by Moses; grace and truth came by Jesus Christ. Calvin: the law, by itself, wanted both. Shadows were there; the body is in Christ. Forgiveness and renewed heart are found in the Son, not in the bare letter.

Reformed note: this is not scorn for Moses. It is the insistence that if you separate the law from Christ, empty figures remain. All spiritual good is located in the Word made flesh.

John 1:18

18No one has ever seen God; the only God, who is at the Father's side, he has made him known.

Arminian

No one has seen God; the only God at the Father’s side has made him known. Revelation is personal and generous. Classical Arminians hear an unveiling meant to be trusted—God shown so that people may receive the Son and, in him, the Father.

The bosom of the Father is opened in the gospel. We are not left to guess at God. The right response to this making-known is the believing of verses 12–13, enabled by grace and still truly ours.

Reformed

No one has ever seen God; the only-begotten, in the Father’s bosom, has declared him. God dwells in inaccessible light; he is known in Christ, his living image. Reformed faith does not seek a naked majesty behind the Son.

Calvin: we have the breast of God, as it were, laid open in the gospel. All blessings are entered by this knowledge. The Word who was God now makes the Father known—so that believing in Christ is not departure from the only God.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the eternal Word made flesh and the right to become children of God—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 1:1–18.

Arminian Notable voices

“In the beginning — When all things began to be made by the Word: in the beginning of heaven and earth, and this whole frame of created beings, the Word existed, without any beginning. And the Word was God — Supreme, eternal, independent. But as many as received him — Jews or Gentiles; that believe on his name — The moment they believe, they are sons. Who were born — not of blood — Not by descent from Abraham, nor by the will of the flesh — By natural generation, nor by the will of man — Adopting them, but of God — By his Spirit.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 1:1, 12–13)

Reformed Notable voices

“In the beginning was the Speech. In this introduction he asserts the eternal Divinity of Christ, in order to inform us that he is the eternal God, who was manifested in the flesh. By these words the Evangelist assures us that we do not withdraw from the only and eternal God, when we believe in Christ.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 1:1)
