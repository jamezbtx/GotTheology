Passage

John 1

Verses 1–18 · WEB · Voices

John’s prologue announces that the Word was with God and was God, the agent of creation and the source of life and light. John the Baptist came to bear witness to the light. The true light, which gives light to everyone, came into the world, but the world did not know him and his own people did not receive him. Yet to all who received him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God, born not of human will but of God. The Word became flesh and dwelt among us, full of grace and truth. Both traditions affirm the deity and incarnation of Christ wholeheartedly. They differ on verses 9 and 12–13.

John 1:1

1In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.

Arminian

In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. Both traditions affirm the full deity of Christ. Arminius himself held firmly to Nicene orthodoxy, and Arminian readers see the Word as fully God and distinct from the Father.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the eternal Son, distinct in person from the Father yet sharing his divine nature. This verse is central to Trinitarian theology.

John 1:2

2The same was in the beginning with God.

Arminian

He was in the beginning with God. Arminian readers see the Word’s eternal fellowship with the Father.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the eternal relationship within the Trinity.

John 1:3

3All things were made through him. Without him, nothing was made that has been made.

Arminian

All things were made through him. Arminian readers see creation as the work of the Word, who also comes to save.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Son as agent of creation, which supports his deity.

John 1:4

4In him was life, and the life was the light of men.

Arminian

In him was life, and the life was the light of men. Arminian readers see Christ as the source of life and light for all humanity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as the source of natural life and, for the elect, spiritual life.

John 1:5

5The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness hasn’t overcome it.

Arminian

The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it. Arminian readers see the light shining for all, though some reject it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see darkness unable either to comprehend or to overcome the light.

John 1:6

6There came a man sent from God, whose name was John.

Arminian

There was a man sent from God, whose name was John. Arminian readers see God preparing the way for Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign sending of his messenger.

John 1:7

7The same came as a witness, that he might testify about the light, that all might believe through him.

Arminian

He came as a witness, that all might believe through him. Arminian readers stress “that all might believe”: the purpose of the witness was universal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see John’s witness addressed to all, through which God brings his people to faith.

John 1:8

8He was not the light, but was sent that he might testify about the light.

Arminian

He was not the light, but came to bear witness about the light. Arminian readers see the distinction between the messenger and Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the subordinate role of every human witness.

John 1:9

9The true light that enlightens everyone was coming into the world.

Arminian

The true light, which gives light to everyone, was coming into the world. Arminian readers see this as a key text for prevenient grace: Christ gives every person some light, enabling a response to God. Wesley read it this way.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the light given to everyone as general revelation, conscience, or the light of reason, not saving grace.

Key contrast Both see Christ enlightening every person in some way. Arminian readers, with Wesley, see prevenient grace given to all, enabling response to God. Reformed readers see general revelation or common grace, which leaves people without excuse but does not by itself save.

John 1:10

10He was in the world, and the world was made through him, and the world didn’t recognize him.

Arminian

He was in the world, and the world was made through him, yet the world did not know him. Arminian readers see the tragedy of a world rejecting its maker.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the blindness of fallen humanity.

John 1:11

11He came to his own, and those who were his own didn’t receive him.

Arminian

He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him. Arminian readers see Israel’s rejection as a free choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see rejection by those with the greatest privileges, showing that privilege does not save.

John 1:12

12But as many as received him, to them he gave the right to become God’s children, to those who believe in his name:

Arminian

To all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God. Arminian readers see receiving and believing as the condition for becoming God’s children, open to all.

Reformed

Reformed readers see receiving Christ as the result of new birth, which verse 13 explains.

Key contrast Both affirm that believers receive the right to become God’s children. Arminian readers see receiving and believing as the condition of that adoption. Reformed readers read verse 13 as showing that new birth from God lies behind the receiving.

John 1:13

13who were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God.

Arminian

Born not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God. Arminian readers see this as excluding natural descent and human effort as the source of new birth, while faith remains the response by which it is received.

Reformed

Reformed readers see new birth as wholly God’s act, excluding human will as its cause. They see regeneration as preceding faith.

Key contrast Both affirm that new birth comes from God. Arminian readers see this as excluding human merit and descent, while faith receives it. Reformed readers see it as excluding human will as the cause, with regeneration preceding faith.

John 1:14

14The Word became flesh and lived among us. We saw his glory, such glory as of the only born Son of the Father, full of grace and truth.

Arminian

The Word became flesh and dwelt among us, full of grace and truth. Both traditions affirm the incarnation. Arminian readers see God drawing near to all humanity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Word taking true human nature while remaining fully God, as Chalcedon confessed.

John 1:15

15John testified about him. He cried out, saying, “This was he of whom I said, ‘He who comes after me has surpassed me, for he was before me.’”

Arminian

John bore witness: he who comes after me ranks before me, because he was before me. Arminian readers see John affirming Christ’s pre-existence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the eternal Son entering history.

John 1:16

16From his fullness we all received grace upon grace.

Arminian

From his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace. Arminian readers see abundant grace given to believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see every blessing flowing from Christ’s fullness.

John 1:17

17For the law was given through Moses. Grace and truth were realized through Jesus Christ.

Arminian

The law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ. Arminian readers see the new covenant’s fuller revelation of grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law and the gospel within one covenant history, with grace fully revealed in Christ.

John 1:18

18No one has seen God at any time. The only born Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, has declared him.

Arminian

No one has ever seen God; the only Son, who is at the Father’s side, has made him known. Arminian readers see Christ revealing God to the world.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Son as the only one who can reveal the Father. Many manuscripts read “the only God,” which further affirms his deity.

Voices from both camps

On the eternal Word made flesh and the right to become children of God, a few classic voices from each tradition.

Arminian Notable voices

“In the beginning — When all things began to be made by the Word: in the beginning of heaven and earth, and this whole frame of created beings, the Word existed, without any beginning. And the Word was God — Supreme, eternal, independent.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 1:1, 12–13)

Reformed Notable voices

“In the beginning was the Speech. In this introduction he asserts the eternal Divinity of Christ, in order to inform us that he is the eternal God, who was manifested in the flesh.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 1:1)
