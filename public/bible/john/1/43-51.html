Passage

John 1

Verses 43–51 · ESV · Voices

Jesus finds Philip (“Follow me”); Philip finds Nathanael; prejudice about Nazareth yields to “Come and see”; Jesus names him an Israelite without deceit, having seen him under the fig tree; Nathanael confesses Son of God and King of Israel; Jesus promises heaven opened on the Son of Man.

John 1:43

43The next day Jesus decided to go to Galilee. He found Philip and said to him, “Follow me.”

Arminian

Jesus decided to go to Galilee, found Philip, and said, Follow me. The call is personal and plain. Classical Arminians hear a genuine command that Philip can heed. Finding is grace; following is the real response that grace enables.

“Follow me” is not theater. The same Word who invites the world in the prologue now addresses one man. Prevenient grace is already at work, yet Philip’s steps remain his. The call can be refused; this time it is not.

Reformed

He found Philip and said, Follow me. Calvin: inflamed by this single word, Philip shows the efficacy of God’s word—but not indiscriminately in all. External preaching is unfruitful unless secret grace quickens it.

Reformed reading: Jesus finds before Philip seeks. Effectual calling is this: the word lands, the senses are affected, the man is prepared to follow wherever God calls. “Follow me” creates the follower it commands.

Key contrast Arminians hear “Follow me” as a real, resistible summons that Philip freely answers under enabling grace; the Reformed hear an effectual call—secret grace quickening the word so that Philip is made willing to follow.

John 1:44

44Now Philip was from Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter.

Arminian

Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter. Geography becomes fellowship. Arminian note: God uses place and friendship. Philip is not an isolated trophy; he belongs to a town where others have already come and seen.

Hometown ties can serve the gospel or resist it. Here they serve. The call to follow does not erase Bethsaida; it sends a man from that city to find another.

Reformed

Philip was of Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter. Calvin: the name is mentioned so that God’s goodness to three apostles from a later-cursed town may be displayed—as if brought out of the lowest hell.

Reformed wonder: providence chooses from an ungodly place and then honors them as apostles. Grace is not owed to Bethsaida. The city that will hear woe also hears “Follow me.”

John 1:45

45Philip found Nathanael and said to him, “We have found him of whom Moses in the Law and also the prophets wrote, Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph.”

Arminian

Philip found Nathanael—the same finding-impulse as Andrew. “We have found him of whom Moses and the prophets wrote.” Scripture is the measure. Classical Arminians are glad the stammer includes Nazareth and Joseph: sincere witness need not be perfect to invite a real look.

The invitation is to a Person the Law already promised. Nathanael is treated as someone who can come. Imperfect words can still carry a true Christ to a neighbor.

Reformed

Philip findeth Nathanael. Calvin: the measure of Philip’s faith is small—he calls Jesus Joseph’s son and Nazareth his town, both false—yet God approves the diligence and crowns it. He leads Nathanael to no other than the Son of God born in Bethlehem.

Reformed note: the chief design of doctrine is that hearers come to Christ in some way. Stammering that holds the true Christ beats eloquent invention of a false one. God uses a faulty sentence to gather a true Israelite.

John 1:46

46Nathanael said to him, “Can anything good come out of Nazareth?” Philip said to him, “Come and see.”

Arminian

Can anything good come out of Nazareth? Wesley: guard against popular prejudices; they possessed even honest Nathanael and made him suspect Jesus for an impostor. Integrity then prevailed over the foolish bias and laid him open to evidence. Arminian reading: prejudice is resistible; so is the invitation to come and see.

Philip does not win by argument. He repeats Christ’s own words: Come and see. The honest heart is asked to move. Grace meets the man who will test his bias in the presence of Jesus.

Reformed

Can any good thing come out of Nazareth? Calvin: Nathanael was not far from shutting out all approach to Christ—by rashly believing Philip’s error about the place, then by contempt of Nazareth. Satan still uses such obstacles.

“Come and see” corrects a twofold error. Reformed pastoral: be submissive, then inquire; Christ is ready to remove doubts. The honest man is not left to his prejudice. Providence has already seen him under the fig tree.

John 1:47

47Jesus saw Nathanael coming toward him and said of him, “Behold, an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit!”

Arminian

Jesus saw him coming—Nathanael is on the way, prejudice and all. “An Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit” honors integrity without flattery. Classical Arminians hear a man whose honesty is a real moral fact, now being drawn to the truth.

No deceit does not mean no need of grace. It means he will not hide from what he sees. Wesley’s “ready to believe” is this character answering the Light. Coming toward Jesus is already a better confession than the Nazareth jab.

Reformed

Behold, one truly an Israelite, in whom there is no deceit. Calvin: Christ, under this man, holds out a general doctrine—few who take the name are true. Integrity of heart before God makes the genuine child, against hypocrisy that even deceives itself.

Reformed note: Jesus speaks before Nathanael explains himself. The verdict is the Lord’s. Knowing a heart without deceit belongs to God. The commendation will become a question that unveils the Son.

John 1:48

48Nathanael said to him, “How do you know me?” Jesus answered him, “Before Philip called you, when you were under the fig tree, I saw you.”

Arminian

How do you know me? is an honest question. Jesus answers with the fig tree: I saw you before Philip called. Arminian reading: the seeing is gracious knowledge that seeks; the call through Philip is still a call Nathanael had to answer.

Being seen does not cancel coming and seeing. Divine knowledge grounds the invitation; it does not replace the walk from under the tree to Jesus. The man known in secret is summoned in public and truly comes.

Reformed

Before Philip called thee, when thou wast under the fig-tree, I saw thee. Calvin: as it belongs to God to know men when they are not seen, so to see what is not visible to eyes. Nathanael knew this was no human look.

Reformed doctrine: when we are not thinking of Christ, we are observed by him, that he may bring us back. Philip’s call sat inside a prior seeing. Effectual grace had already located Nathanael before a neighbor spoke.

John 1:49

49Nathanael answered him, “Rabbi, you are the Son of God! You are the King of Israel!”

Arminian

Rabbi, you are the Son of God! You are the King of Israel! Wesley: happy are they that are ready to believe, swift to receive the truth and grace of God. So he acknowledges now more than he had heard from Philip. Integrity yields; prejudice dies in the presence of the Knower.

Classical Arminians hear a real confession. Nathanael receives what is shown. The titles are not forced from a mouth; they are the honest man’s yes. Swift receiving is the glory of a heart without deceit.

Reformed

Thou art the Son of God; thou art the King of Israel. Calvin: he adds confirmation to the Messiah already announced, and holds that the Son of God will not come without exercising kingship over God’s people. Faith attends to power and office, not essence only.

Reformed note: the confession is limited to the measure of faith—King of Israel, not yet of the world—and still true. Christ will strengthen it. Grace that saw him under the tree now opens his mouth.

John 1:50

50Jesus answered him, “Because I said to you, ‘I saw you under the fig tree,’ do you believe? You will see greater things than these.”

Arminian

Do you believe? You will see greater things. The question honors the faith already given; the promise invites more. Arminian pastoral: believing is real, and it is not the end. Those who receive are led on, not parked at a first wonder.

Greater things are for those who keep coming. The fig tree was enough to begin; it is not all. Faith that answered a private seeing will be asked to receive a public Son of Man.

Reformed

Because I said, I saw thee under the fig-tree, thou believest; thou shalt see greater things. Calvin: he does not reprove easy belief; he approves the faith and promises stronger arguments for him and for others.

Reformed comfort: Christ undertakes to confirm what he has begun. Perseverance is not Nathanael clinging to one sign. Greater sight is pledged because the Lord pledges it. The fig tree was particular; the next verse will be for all.

John 1:51

51And he said to him, “Truly, truly, I say to you, you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man.”

Arminian

Truly, truly: heaven opened, angels ascending and descending on the Son of Man—Jacob’s ladder fulfilled in a Person. Classical Arminians hear a promise to disciples who keep looking. The opened heaven is shown so that they may go on believing.

The Son of Man is the meeting place of God and humanity. Access is in him, to be received. The first chapter that began with the Word as God ends with the Word as the stair by which grace comes down and faith looks up.

Reformed

You shall see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of man. Calvin: not a one-time spectacle to date, but something perpetual in his kingdom. The gate once closed is opened in Christ; angels minister for his honor to the whole body.

Reformed hope: we are fellow-citizens of the saints because of this Son of Man. The ladder of Genesis 28 is fulfilled, not replaced by human climbing. What the prologue named as the Word made flesh is now the open heaven. Perseverance looks here, not to our grip.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Philip’s call and Nathanael under the fig tree—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 1:43–51.

Arminian Notable voices

“Can any good thing come out of Nazareth? — How cautiously should we guard against popular prejudices? When these had once possessed so honest a heart as that of Nathanael, they led him to suspect the blessed Jesus himself for an impostor, because he had been brought up at Nazareth. But his integrity prevailed over that foolish bias, and laid him open to the force of evidence. Nathanael answered — Happy are they that are ready to believe, swift to receive the truth and grace of God. Thou art the Son of God — So he acknowledges now more than he had heard from Philip.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 1:46, 49)

Reformed Notable voices

“When Philip was inflamed by this single word to follow Christ, we infer from it how great is the efficacy of the word of God; but it does not appear indiscriminately in all, for God addresses many without any advantage. The external preaching of the word is in itself unfruitful, except that it inflicts a deadly wound on the reprobate. But when the secret grace of God quickens it, all the senses must be affected in such a manner that men will be prepared to follow wherever God calls them. As Nathanael knew that Christ did not see him after the manner of men, but by a look truly divine, this might lead him to conclude that Christ did not now speak as a man.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 1:43, 48)
