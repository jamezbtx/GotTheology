Passage

John 4

Verses 43–54 · ESV · Voices

After two days Jesus goes to Galilee, where a prophet lacks hometown honor, yet Galileans welcome Him for feast-signs. At Cana an official from Capernaum begs Him to come down before his son dies. Jesus rebukes sign-hunger, then speaks: “Go; your son will live.” The man believes the word, meets servants on the road, matches the hour, and he and his household believe. This is the second sign from Judea to Galilee.

John 4:43

43After the two days he departed for Galilee.

Arminian

The Samaritan stay ends on the original itinerary. Two days were enough to plant a confession and still leave.

Arminian pace: remaining and departing can both be obedience. The Savior of the world does not settle as Samaria’s chaplain only.

Reformed

After the two days: He keeps the Father’s clock.

Reformed movement: the same necessity that brought Him through Samaria now sends Him on. Harvest there does not cancel the next appointment.

John 4:44

44(For Jesus himself had testified that a prophet has no honor in his own hometown.)

Arminian

John explains Galilee with a proverb Jesus had used. Home is not always the easiest field.

Arminian realism: familiarity can starve honor. The proverb prepares us not to confuse a welcome (v. 45) with the faith of Samaria.

Reformed

The narrator cites Jesus’ own testimony. Hometown dishonor is expected, not accidental.

Reformed irony incoming: Galilee will welcome signs and still need a rebuke (v. 48). Honor and faith are not the same.

John 4:45

45So when he came to Galilee, the Galileans welcomed him, having seen all that he had done in Jerusalem at the feast. For they too had gone to the feast.

Arminian

Welcome based on feast-signs. They had gone up; they had seen; they receive Him as a wonder-worker returning.

Arminian discernment: welcome is better than scorn and still may be sign-deep. John has already shown (2:23–25) that feast-faith can be something Jesus does not entrust Himself to.

Reformed

Having seen all that He had done: the ground of the welcome is miracles at the feast.

Reformed caution: this is not yet the household faith of v. 53. Galilee’s smile is sight-born; the official will be called to word-born trust.

John 4:46

46So he came again to Cana in Galilee, where he had made the water wine. And at Capernaum there was an official whose son was ill.

Arminian

Cana again: water-to-wine country. An official from Capernaum has a sick son—need traveling uphill to the sign-town.

Arminian mercy: status does not exempt from fever. A royal official will have to ask like the Samaritan woman did: without leverage.

Reformed

Cana recalled, Capernaum introduced. Illness in a powerful house.

Reformed stage: the second sign will answer a father’s desperation in the place of the first sign, teaching that the Giver, not the venue, is the point.

John 4:47

47When this man heard that Jesus had come from Judea to Galilee, he went to him and asked him to come down and heal his son, for he was at the point of death.

Arminian

Hearing, going, asking: come down before death. The prayer is specific and geographically small—Jesus must travel to the boy.

Arminian petition: this is real faith beginning, mixed with a theory of how Jesus has to work. Grace will honor the ask and correct the theory.

Reformed

Heard, went, asked: the man is not proud enough to stay in Capernaum. Point of death is the clock.

Reformed need: desperation is often God’s doorway. The request still tries to manage the method: come down.

John 4:48

48So Jesus said to him, “Unless you see signs and wonders you will not believe.”

Arminian

Plural you: Galilee’s sign-habit, not only this father. Wesley heard a rebuke of dependence on wonders. Belief that requires a show is the feast-welcome’s underside.

Classical Arminians take the warning as addressable. They can believe the word; Jesus is calling them off a demand that makes faith hostage to spectacle, while still about to give a sign.

Reformed

Calvin: He reproves eagerness for miracles. Unless you see—plural unbelief diagnosed.

Reformed sting: signs can become the price we charge God for trust. The official’s panic is folded into a people’s hunger for wonders; both need a better ground than sight.

Key contrast Both hear a rebuke of sign-conditioned faith. Arminians typically treat it as a real summons to trust the word now; Reformed readers typically treat it as exposing a heart that will not believe unless God pays in wonders—and as the backdrop for faith that must be given.

John 4:49

49The official said to him, “Sir, come down before my child dies.”

Arminian

He does not debate the rebuke; he pleads the child. Sir, come down—still method, more urgency.

Arminian prayer: sometimes the holiest reply to a hard word is to keep asking for the dying. Jesus can fix the man’s theology after He hears the father’s voice.

Reformed

No apology tour: just the boy. Come down before he dies—faith and unbelief tangled in one sentence.

Reformed compassion: the Lord let the plea stand. He will not come down, and He will still meet the dying.

John 4:50

50Jesus said to him, “Go; your son will live.” The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him and went on his way.

Arminian

Go; your son will live. Wesley: he believed the word even without a miracle, and went. The sign is promised, not yet seen; the going is the faith.

Classical Arminians linger on believed the word. Trust here is a genuine response to a spoken promise—enabled, responsible, mobile. He does not stay to negotiate a house-call.

Reformed

Calvin: the nobleman believed the word and went away; faith comes by hearing. The life of the boy is in a sentence.

Reformed word: Jesus refuses the trip and gives a promise. The man’s going is created by that word. Hearing, not seeing, is the appointed instrument.

Key contrast Shared ground: he believes the spoken word and goes before any proof. Arminians typically describe that believing as a grace-enabled, uncoerced trust in the promise. Reformed readers typically describe the same moment as faith coming by hearing—the word itself effecting the trust it commands.

John 4:51

51As he was going down, his servants met him and told him that his son was recovering.

Arminian

On the road, confirmation meets obedience. Recovering: the promise is already in progress.

Arminian encouragement: going is not a leap into silence. The God who speaks also sends servants with news; faith is often met midway.

Reformed

Going down, met, told: providence uses household staff.

Reformed timing: the recovery is underway while the father is still traveling on the word. The sign catches up with faith, not the reverse.

John 4:52

52So he asked them the hour when he began to get better, and they said to him, “Yesterday at the seventh hour the fever left him.”

Arminian

He checks the hour—not unbelief so much as wanting the word and the event to kiss. Seventh hour: yesterday, one p.m., fever gone.

Arminian reason: faith may still ask when. Matching clocks is allowed; it will become worship in v. 53, not a demand for a third sign.

Reformed

The hour is investigated and named. Fever left: sudden, datable.

Reformed precision: God lets the miracle be timestamped so the word’s authority is unmistakable. This is not vague improvement; it is the seventh hour.

John 4:53

53The father knew that was the hour when Jesus had said to him, “Your son will live.” And he himself believed, and all his household.

Arminian

He knew the hours matched. He himself believed, and all his household—Wesley notes the house with him. The first believing (v. 50) deepens into a household confession.

Arminian household: one man’s trust becomes a home’s trust without erasing anyone’s hearing. The word that saved a boy now names a family as believers.

Reformed

Calvin: the first faith was not small, but increased and confirmed by the event; then he and his whole house believed.

Reformed confirmation: sign serves word, then spreads. Household faith is the overflow of a promise God kept at the hour He spoke it.

Key contrast Both see two stages: believing the word on the road, then a confirmed, household faith. Arminians typically describe the increase as further free response to clearer light. Reformed readers typically describe it as the same divine word confirming and extending the faith it first created.

John 4:54

54This was now the second sign that Jesus did when he had come from Judea to Galilee.

Arminian

John numbers it: second sign, Judea-to-Galilee. Cana’s wine and a Capernaum fever bookend a journey through Samaria’s well.

Arminian close: signs are real and ordered, never the master. The chapter that offered living water ends with a boy alive because a father believed a sentence. That is the kind of seeing John wants.

Reformed

Second sign, geographically tagged. John the narrator keeps the score so we will not miss the pattern.

Reformed close: signs certify the Speaker of 4:50. Galilee gets a wonder, Samaria got two days of word, and both are the same Christ gathering believers as He will.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on believing the spoken word without first seeing the sign—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 4:43–54.

Arminian Notable voices

“Except ye see signs and wonders, ye will not believe. Go, thy son liveth. The man believed the word that Jesus spake—and that even without a miracle. And himself believed, and his whole house.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 4:48, 50, 53)

Reformed Notable voices

“He reproves them for being so eager for miracles… The nobleman believed the word of Christ, and went away. Faith comes by hearing. Afterwards he and his whole house believed: the first faith was not small, but it was increased and confirmed by the event.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 4:48, 50, 53)
