Passage

John 4

Verses 1–26 · ESV · Voices

Jesus leaves Judea, must pass through Samaria, and sits wearied at Jacob’s well. He asks a Samaritan woman for a drink, then offers living water—the gift of God—that becomes a spring to eternal life. He names her life without shaming her out of the conversation, relocates worship from Gerizim-versus-Jerusalem to the Father who seeks worshipers in spirit and truth, and discloses: “I who speak to you am he.”

John 4:1

1Now when Jesus learned that the Pharisees had heard that Jesus was making and baptizing more disciples than John

Arminian

The comparison John’s disciples feared is now Pharisee news. Jesus will not let rivalry become the story; He moves.

Arminian prudence: leaving is not cowardice. The mission to Samaria and Galilee matters more than winning a baptism count in Judea.

Reformed

Pharisees hear numbers. Jesus hears a cue to go.

Reformed timing: the Lord governs even hostile attention. More disciples than John is heaven’s gift (3:27) and now a reason to depart.

John 4:2

2(although Jesus himself did not baptize, but only his disciples),

Arminian

A clarifying aside: Jesus’ disciples baptize; He does not, perhaps to keep the sign from being captured as a personal brand.

Arminian church note: means of grace can be administered by servants without emptying them. The living water He will offer is not identical with the rite His disciples perform.

Reformed

John blocks a misunderstanding: Jesus was not personally performing the baptisms counted in v. 1.

Reformed care: Christ baptizes with the Spirit (ch. 1); His disciples handle water. The distinction protects both His uniqueness and their appointed ministry.

John 4:3

3he left Judea and departed again for Galilee.

Arminian

Departure is deliberate. Galilee is home country; Samaria will be the surprise in between.

Arminian mission: Jesus is willing to leave a swelling work to keep the Father’s pace. Geography becomes grace for a woman not looking for a Messiah that noon.

Reformed

He left… departed again: the itinerary is His.

Reformed providence: the shortest-looking line to Galilee will become a well-side election of a Samaritan hearer. Nothing on this road is filler.

John 4:4

4And he had to pass through Samaria.

Arminian

“Had to” can be map (Jews often did) or mission (He must). John has already taught us Jesus’ musts are saving.

Arminian wideness: necessity includes Samaria. The love of 3:16 is about to sit down at a well the devout might skip.

Reformed

He had to: John loves divine necessity. The road is appointment.

Reformed reading: the Father’s sending includes this crossing. Samaria is not a detour from the plan; it is the plan’s next hour.

John 4:5

5So he came to a town of Samaria called Sychar, near the field that Jacob had given to his son Joseph.

Arminian

Patriarchal ground: Jacob’s field, Joseph’s inheritance—shared memory between Jew and Samaritan, still not shared table.

Arminian hope: old promises sit in contested soil. Jesus will claim this memory for living water, not for a property fight.

Reformed

Sychar is named into Israel’s story. The well has a genealogy.

Reformed note: God returns to covenant geography with a gift Jacob could not give: the Messiah Himself, sitting where the fathers walked.

John 4:6

6Jacob’s well was there; so Jesus, wearied as he was from his journey, was sitting beside the well. It was about the sixth hour.

Arminian

True humanity: wearied, sitting, noon heat. The giver of living water is thirsty.

Arminian incarnation: we come to a Savior who knows fatigue. His weariness is not a barrier to giving life; it is how the gift arrives in a body.

Reformed

The Word made flesh sits tired at noon. Sixth hour: full sun, empty well-side.

Reformed wonder: the One from above (3:31) is weary from a road. Humiliation is the mode of this giving.

John 4:7

7A woman from Samaria came to draw water. Jesus said to her, “Give me a drink.”

Arminian

He speaks first, across every wall: gender, ethnicity, reputation. A request for water is already grace initiating.

Arminian initiative: she did not come seeking; He asked. Enabling grace often sounds like an ordinary sentence that opens a longer mercy.

Reformed

He starts. The woman came for water; the Lord came for her.

Reformed seeking: the next “Father is seeking” (v. 23) is already happening in this request. Divine initiative takes the form of thirst.

John 4:8

8(For his disciples had gone away into the city to buy food.)

Arminian

The aside explains the privacy. Disciples on a food run leave Jesus free for a conversation they would have complicated (v. 27).

Arminian providence-in-small-things: even lunch errands serve souls. God can empty a well-side of helpers so a woman can be heard.

Reformed

Disciples absent by design of the story. The encounter is uncluttered.

Reformed staging: the Lord needs no committee to save. Their buying food is honest hunger and, under God, a cleared space.

John 4:9

9The Samaritan woman said to him, “How is it that you, a Jew, ask for a drink from me, a woman of Samaria?” (For Jews have no dealings with Samaritans.)

Arminian

She names the scandal before the theology. How is it—you, a Jew, me, a Samaritan woman.

Arminian dignity: Jesus lets her question stand. Crossing “no dealings” is part of the gift; the gospel is already acting out 3:16’s world.

Reformed

John glosses the freeze: no dealings. Her how is social fact, not yet faith.

Reformed breach: the Messiah’s first move in Samaria is to need something from the excluded. Election here looks like a shared cup.

John 4:10

10Jesus answered her, “If you knew the gift of God, and who it is that is saying to you, ‘Give me a drink,’ you would have asked him, and he would have given you living water.”

Arminian

Two ifs of knowing: the gift, and the Speaker. Wesley: had she known, she would have asked, and He would have given. Asking is the opened hand; giving is His delight.

Classical Arminians hear sincere willingness in “he would have given.” Ignorance blocks asking; Jesus is dispelling it so she may ask. Living water is offered, not bait.

Reformed

Calvin: the gift is the Spirit as living water; had she known who spoke, she would have asked. Knowledge of Christ is the missing thing, and He is supplying it.

Reformed giving: the sentence is conditional in form and generous in heart. The Lord who must be known is the Lord who is already speaking. He would have given—and He has come to create the knowing and the asking.

Key contrast Shared ground: living water is God’s gift, tied to knowing who Jesus is, asked for and given. Arminians typically stress that she truly would have received had she asked—an offer as wide as her need. Reformed readers typically stress that Jesus is already creating the knowledge without which no one asks, so the gift and the asking both finally rest in His initiative.

John 4:11

11The woman said to him, “Sir, you have nothing to draw water with, and the well is deep. Where do you get that living water?

Arminian

Like Nicodemus, she hears in the wrong register: rope, depth, where. The question is still a question, which is more than walking away.

Arminian patience: Jesus stays with literalism. Grace talks a soul up from buckets toward springs without scorning the first misunderstanding.

Reformed

Nothing to draw with: she measures Him by gear. Where do you get it—still geography.

Reformed echo of ch. 3: flesh answers flesh. The well is deep; so is the need He is naming.

John 4:12

12Are you greater than our father Jacob? He gave us the well and drank from it himself, as did his sons and his livestock.”

Arminian

Jacob is the trump card: our father, our well, even the livestock drank. Greatness is being tested against a patriarch.

Arminian irony we are meant to feel: yes, He is greater. The gift of God outruns the gift of Jacob, and she will soon be asking for it.

Reformed

Ancestor, well, sons, livestock: a full covenant-memory inventory. Is this tired Jew greater?

Reformed answer already given in 1:1 and 3:31. Jacob gave a well; the Father is giving the Son.

John 4:13

13Jesus said to her, “Everyone who drinks of this water will be thirsty again,

Arminian

Everyone: no elite exemption from thirst. Jacob’s water is good and finite.

Arminian honesty: created goods cannot secure the heart. The sentence levels Jew, Samaritan, and livestock-owner: this water ends, and thirst returns.

Reformed

A universal of first-drink water: thirst again. The well cannot do eternal work.

Reformed limit: means that perish cannot give the life of God. He is about to name a drink that does not recycle need.

John 4:14

14but whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never be thirsty again. The water that I will give him will become in him a spring of water welling up to eternal life.”

Arminian

Whoever drinks, I will give, never thirsty, a spring in him welling to eternal life. Gift becomes inward fountain, not a one-time sip to remember.

Arminian promise: the giving is Christ’s, the drinking is real, the life is eternal. Grace does not merely dampen thirst; it relocates the spring inside the believer who asks and receives.

Reformed

I will give: the verb stays with Jesus. The water becomes a spring in the person, rising toward eternal life.

Reformed indwelling: this is Spirit-life, not improved hydration. Whoever drinks is as wide as the gospel and as particular as those in whom He actually places a well that does not fail.

John 4:15

15The woman said to him, “Sir, give me this water, so that I will not be thirsty or have to come here to draw water.”

Arminian

She asks—halfway. Give me this water, still to skip the noon walk. The ask is mixed motives and still the right verb.

Arminian hope: imperfect asking is asking. Jesus does not reject the request; He will deepen it by telling the truth about her life.

Reformed

Give me: the conditional of v. 10 is starting to happen, still in bucket-language.

Reformed kindness: He receives a half-understood prayer and will now expose the heart the spring must occupy. The gift will not skip holiness.

John 4:16

16Jesus said to her, “Go, call your husband, and come here.”

Arminian

The gift-path runs through truth about her household. Go, call, come—He will not give a spring that bypasses the actual life she lives.

Arminian integrity: grace is not anonymity. Living water and moral light arrive together; she can still come.

Reformed

A command that seems a change of subject and is not. The Husband of Israel is about to name her husbands.

Reformed piercing: the Lord who offers water also claims the story. Exposure is part of the giving.

John 4:17

17The woman answered him, “I have no husband.” Jesus said to her, “You are right in saying, ‘I have no husband’;

Arminian

A true sentence that is also a dodge. Jesus honors the truth in it without letting the dodge win.

Arminian conversation: He says “you are right” before He says the rest. Correction that begins by receiving what is true is how mercy argues.

Reformed

He ratifies her clause, then will fill it. Omniscience can be gentle in tone and total in knowledge.

Reformed seeing: nothing at the well is hidden. The Speaker of living water is the Searcher of hearts.

John 4:18

18for you have had five husbands, and the one you now have is not your husband. What you have said is true.”

Arminian

Five husbands, a sixth who is not. He names without a sneer, and repeats that she told the truth.

Arminian dignity: her sexual history is neither ignored nor used as a punchline. The Light of 3:20–21 has come to a well, and she is not driven off for having been in the dark.

Reformed

Specific, numbered, present-tense. Prophetic knowledge serves a pastoral end, not a spectacle.

Reformed holiness: the gift of God does not unsee sin. It brings it into speech so that worship in truth (v. 24) has something true to work with.

John 4:19

19The woman said to him, “Sir, I perceive that you are a prophet.

Arminian

Perception rises: from weary Jew to prophet. She is moving, if also perhaps steering toward safer religious ground.

Arminian process: faith can arrive in steps. A prophet is not yet “I am he,” but it is closer than “how is it you ask me.”

Reformed

I perceive: the first theological confession of the scene. Prophet is true and too small.

Reformed patience of Christ: He will accept the rung and climb with her toward Messiah (v. 26).

John 4:20

20Our fathers worshiped on this mountain, but you say that in Jerusalem is the place where people ought to worship.”

Arminian

Gerizim versus Jerusalem: the old fight, maybe also a deflection from husbands. Place has been the Samaritan-Jewish wound.

Arminian redirect Jesus will not fully take: He will answer the worship question because it is real, not only because it is convenient. The gospel heals rivalry, not only private shame.

Reformed

Fathers, mountain, you say, ought: competing sacred maps.

Reformed issue: worship’s where is about to yield to worship’s whom and how. The prophet she just named will relocate the argument to the Father.

John 4:21

21Jesus said to her, “Woman, believe me, the hour is coming when neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem will you worship the Father.

Arminian

Believe me: the coming hour outgrows both ridges. The Father will be worshiped without that map fight.

Arminian wideness: place-wars cannot contain the people God is gathering. The invitation is to the Father, not to a winning ZIP code.

Reformed

The hour is coming: Jesus’ eschatology in a sentence. Neither Gerizim nor Jerusalem as the last word.

Reformed hour: He speaks as Lord of worship’s future. Believe me is the required faith, because only the Son can retire both mountains.

John 4:22

22You worship what you do not know; we worship what we know, for salvation is from the Jews.

Arminian

He does not flatten history to be nice. Samaritan worship is ignorant; salvation is from the Jews—from this Jew, in fact.

Arminian honesty: grace to the nations does not erase Israel’s role. The woman is not told her fathers were equally right; she is told the gift comes along the Jewish line, now sitting at her well.

Reformed

Knowledge versus ignorance, and a salvation-source: the Jews. Jesus will not relativize revelation to keep peace.

Reformed particularity: the world’s Savior (v. 42) arrives by covenant history. From the Jews is how the Father kept the promise, not a slur and not a tie.

John 4:23

23But the hour is coming, and is now here, when the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth, for the Father is seeking such people to worship him.

Arminian

Coming and now here: true worshipers, spirit and truth, the Father seeking such. Wesley: the Father seeketh such worshippers. Seeking is the verb of a God who wants worshipers, not merely a temple.

Classical Arminians hear sincere divine desire. The Father seeks—He is not indifferent to whether people come. Spirit-and-truth worship is what He is after, and the seeking makes the well-side conversation itself a hunt of love.

Reformed

Calvin: God does not wait until he is sought, but comes forward of his own accord and offers himself. The Father is seeking: this noon is evidence.

Reformed seeking: the verb does not make God a disappointed suitor. It names His initiative in creating the very worshipers He seeks—already true in this woman being told the hour is now.

Key contrast Shared ground: the Father seeks worshipers in spirit and truth, and the hour is now. Arminians typically emphasize God’s sincere seeking of people who may yet respond. Reformed readers typically emphasize that the Seeker comes first and actually forms the worshipers He seeks, as this conversation itself displays.

John 4:24

24God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth.”

Arminian

Must: worship matches God’s nature. Spirit and truth are not optional styles. Place yields to presence and reality.

Arminian must: the command is livable by the Spirit He gives (7:37–39 still ahead). True worship is required of all who would come, and grace enables the correspondence God demands.

Reformed

God is spirit: no image, no ridge, no rival essence. Worship must correspond.

Reformed must: necessity again. The new birth of 3:3–8 sits underneath this verse; flesh cannot manufacture spirit-and-truth worship. God supplies what His nature requires.

Key contrast Both hear a non-negotiable: God is spirit, so worship must be spirit and truth. Arminians typically treat the must as a sincere requirement grace enables people to meet; Reformed readers typically treat it as a requirement only the Spirit-born can meet, because God’s nature and God’s begetting go together.

John 4:25

25The woman said to him, “I know that Messiah is coming (he who is called Christ). When he comes, he will tell us all things.”

Arminian

She reaches for the hope she has: Messiah coming, He will tell us all. Incomplete catechism, right direction.

Arminian readiness: God uses the light a Samaritan already holds. Expectation of a telling Christ is the runway for v. 26.

Reformed

Messiah is coming: Samaria’s remaining confession. He will tell us all things—authority located in a still-future figure.

Reformed irony: the Teller is already speaking. Knowledge she postpones is sitting on the well-wall.

John 4:26

26Jesus said to her, “I who speak to you am he.”

Arminian

The clearest self-identification yet, given not in Jerusalem’s court but to this woman. I who speak to you—the Speaker is the Gift.

Arminian wonder: the offer of living water was personal because the Messiah is personal. She is not a test case; she is addressed. The who-it-is of v. 10 is now spoken without riddle.

Reformed

I am he: the Messianic disclosure is Jesus’ to give, and He gives it here.

Reformed grace: revelation is not earned by five-husband-free résumés or by Gerizim winning. The Father-seeking Son names Himself to a Samaritan at noon because that is the hour He appointed.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on living water and worship in spirit and truth—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 4:1–26.

Arminian Notable voices

“If thou hadst known the gift of God—salvation by Christ—and who it is that saith… thou wouldst have asked of him, and he would have given thee living water. The Father seeketh such worshippers. God is a Spirit… and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 4:10, 23–24)

Reformed Notable voices

“If she had known the gift of God, and who it was that spoke to her, she would have asked… He calls the Spirit living water. The Father seeketh such: God does not wait until he is sought, but comes forward of his own accord and offers himself. God is a Spirit, and therefore the worship due to him must be spiritual.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 4:10, 23–24)
