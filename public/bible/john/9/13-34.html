Passage

John 9

Verses 13–34 · ESV · Voices

Neighbors bring the healed man to the Pharisees. It was Sabbath when Jesus made the mud. Division follows: this man is not from God, for he does not keep the Sabbath—or how can a sinner do such signs? The man says he is a prophet. Leaders will not believe he had been blind until they grill the parents, who fear synagogue-expulsion and defer: he is of age. A second interrogation demands, Give glory to God; we know this man is a sinner. One thing I know: I was blind, now I see. They revile him; he reasons from the unprecedented sign; they say he was born in utter sin and cast him out.

John 9:13

13They brought to the Pharisees the man who had formerly been blind.

Arminian

They bring him to the Pharisees. The miracle is now a case file. Religious court is not necessarily a search for truth.

Arminian: bringing a work of God to credentialed judges can be piety or evasion. The man’s new sight will now face people who claim they already see.

Reformed

Calvin: wicked men are so far from profiting by the works of God that the more they are urged by their power, the more they pour out the venom within. Hatred of Christ drives them to instant condemnation.

Reformed: the Pharisees are named as the zealous maintainers of the present order. Hypocrisy, proud and cruel, will try to absorb the sign into a struggle for power.

John 9:14

14Now it was a Sabbath day when Jesus made the mud and opened his eyes.

Arminian

Sabbath, when he made the mud. Wesley: anointing the eyes with any kind of medicine on the Sabbath was particularly forbidden by the tradition of the elders.

Arminian: Jesus does not treat human hedges as the Father’s rest. Mercy on the Sabbath is not a footnote; it is the Light working while it is day.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ purposely selected the Sabbath, which must have given offense; he had already met this slander in the case of the paralytic. The malicious defense would tend to magnify the power of God.

Reformed: the day is a whetstone. Following Christ will provoke enemies of the gospel; those who try to condemn every offense in order to compromise with the world are, Calvin says, altogether mad.

John 9:15

15So the Pharisees again asked him how he had received his sight. And he said to them, “He put mud on my eyes, and I washed, and I see.”

Arminian

Again they ask how. He shortens: mud, washed, I see. The facts do not improve with repetition; the will to receive them might.

Arminian: he keeps offering the same light. Not listening is already forming. The “I see” is a confession they can still join.

Reformed

Calvin: leaving the fact aside, they dispute the law of the case. They do not deny that sight was restored; they criminalize the time and assert it is not a work of God because it violated the Sabbath.

Reformed: obstinate malice chooses to go wrong. The Law forbade our works, not God’s. Their “how” is a search for a charge.

John 9:16

16Some of the Pharisees said, “This man is not from God, for he does not keep the Sabbath.” But others said, “How can a man who is a sinner do such signs?” And there was a division among them.

Arminian

Some: not from God, he does not keep Sabbath. Others: how can a sinner do such signs? Division. Wesley: “sinner” here means one living in wilful sin.

Arminian: the second group reasons from the sign toward God—right direction, still incomplete. Division shows the Light already sorting those who will follow the evidence from those who will protect a hedge.

Reformed

Calvin: it is better that men differ among themselves than that they all, with one consent, revolt from the true religion. Differences should be traced to their source: obstinate malice rending the church by not yielding to the truth of God.

Reformed: among those who fear God, miracles are pledges of the Spirit; Satan also counterfeits. The issue is not that signs magically compel, but that refusing God in his works is malicious contempt.

Key contrast Both take the Sabbath charge as a false dilemma. Arminians typically hear a real fork where some yield to the sign and others refuse available light; Reformed readers typically hear the same split as God making truth brighter while unbelief hardens itself against an obvious work.

John 9:17

17So they said again to the blind man, “What do you say about him, since he has opened your eyes?” He said, “He is a prophet.”

Arminian

What do you say? He is a prophet. Wesley: what inference do you draw? The man moves from “the man called Jesus” to prophet—light used, not hoarded.

Arminian: this is coming to the light in stages. He says what he can see. Classical Arminians honor that as genuine response, not a trick of programming.

Reformed

Calvin: they hope fear will make him reply according to their wish; the Lord disappoints them. When a poor man disregards their threatenings and boldly maintains that Christ is a Prophet, we ought to ascribe it to the grace of God—so that this boldness is another miracle.

Reformed: the confession is real, and it is given. Grace accounts for courage the Pharisees cannot scare off.

John 9:18

18The Jews did not believe that he had been blind and had received his sight, until they called the parents of the man who had received his sight

Arminian

They did not believe he had been blind and received sight until they called the parents. Denial now attacks the premise.

Arminian: when the work is too bright, unbelief relocates to “maybe he was never blind.” That is not careful history; it is refusing light because the verdict threatens their claim to see.

Reformed

Calvin: two things—they do not believe a miracle has been performed, and being wilfully blinded through perverse hatred of Christ they do not perceive what is manifest. If the reason is asked, their blindness was voluntary; inward malice keeps their eyes shut.

Reformed: Paul: the gospel is hidden to the reprobate whose minds the god of this world has blinded. Warned by such examples, we must not bring on ourselves the obstacles that drive us from faith.

John 9:19

19and asked them, “Is this your son, who you say was born blind? How then does he now see?”

Arminian

Is this your son, who you say was born blind? How then does he now see? Three questions braided to trap.

Arminian: they load the parents with insinuation—“who you say.” Intimidation dressed as inquiry. Truth-telling will cost.

Reformed

Calvin: they cunningly put a multitude of questions involved in each other, with a view to preventing a reply. The Lord not only defeats the attempt but turns it to an opposite purpose.

Reformed: providence uses even captious interrogation. The parents will be forced to confirm the birth-blindness they would rather not theologize.

John 9:20

20His parents answered, “We know that this is our son and that he was born blind.

Arminian

We know this is our son and he was born blind. The facts they cannot unsay. Parent-love at least tells this much truth.

Arminian: partial truth is still truth. They will fail the next sentence; they do not fail this one. Light received in the family is already a gift they should have celebrated.

Reformed

Hence, Calvin says, it follows that he does not see naturally, but that his eyes have been miraculously opened. That implication they will dodge.

Reformed: God extracts a confession of the sign’s premise even from frightened witnesses.

John 9:21

21But how he now sees we do not know, nor do we know who opened his eyes. Ask him; he is of age. He will speak for himself.”

Arminian

How he sees we do not know, nor who opened his eyes. Ask him; he is of age; he will speak for himself. Legal adulthood becomes a shield.

Arminian: deferring to the son can be respect; here it is also a dodge. “He is of age” is true and still a way not to stand in the light they have.

Reformed

Calvin: they pass by the miraculous restoration because it would give offense. By their silence they show ingratitude; having received so distinguished a gift, they ought to have burned to celebrate God’s name, but struck with terror they bury the grace of God as far as they can.

Reformed: a middle path of indirect testimony is still called cowardice by the Spirit. Terror is understandable; it is not therefore excused as faithfulness.

John 9:22

22(His parents said these things because they feared the Jews, for the Jews had already agreed that if anyone should confess Jesus to be Christ, he was to be put out of the synagogue.

Arminian

They said this because they feared the Jews; agreement already: confess Christ, be put out of the synagogue. Wesley: that is, be excommunicated.

Arminian: fear of religious exile is real pressure. Classical Arminians do not mock the parents as cartoon villains; they do say fear can refuse a light already given. The cost of confession is part of why coming to the light is a moral act.

Reformed

Calvin: this passage shows that the custom of excommunication is ancient, here turned against the disciples of Christ. Wicked men corrupt holy ordinances by sacrilege; the priests launch this thunder to crush truth they cannot put down by law.

Reformed: tyrannical use of the keys is not the same as Christ’s rule. The threat is social death; the deeper issue is leaders weaponizing discipline against the confession of the Messiah.

John 9:23

23Therefore his parents said, “He is of age; ask him.”)

Arminian

Therefore they said, He is of age; ask him. The narrator underlines motive. The refrain is policy, not ignorance.

Arminian: repeating the deferral after we know why they said it makes the tragedy plain. They will not take the step their son is about to take.

Reformed

Calvin: how much less excuse for those who, by treacherous denial, utterly bury Christ with his doctrine, his miracles, his power and grace.

Reformed: the verse is a warning to every age’s cautious middle. Indirect speech will not be counted as open confession.

John 9:24

24So for the second time they called the man who had been blind and said to him, “Give glory to God. We know that this man is a sinner.”

Arminian

Second calling. Give glory to God. We know this man is a sinner. They baptize a gag order with doxology.

Arminian: “Give glory to God” is right worship used as a weapon. They demand he deny the Giver in the name of God. That is claiming sight while refusing the Light.

Reformed

Calvin: a plausible preface, then they strictly forbid him to answer according to the conviction of his mind—servile obedience under the name of God. Hypocrites pretend reverence and at the same time demand a wicked oath.

Reformed: Joshua’s formula (7:19) is turned into a demand that he call Jesus a sinner. God drags their design into the light.

John 9:25

25He answered, “Whether he is a sinner I do not know. One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see.”

Arminian

Whether he is a sinner I do not know. One thing I do know: I was blind, now I see. The sentence every camp loves, and should.

Arminian: he will not pretend omniscience about Jesus’ status beyond what he has; he will not surrender the fact. Coming to the light can sound like this: stubborn honesty about mercy received.

Reformed

Calvin hears possible irony: he had already called Jesus a Prophet; perceiving he gains nothing, he suspends the person-question and plants the fact they cannot uproot.

Reformed: “I know” is given knowledge. Sight is not a theory they can vote down. The man’s certainty is the miracle still standing in the room.

John 9:26

26They said to him, “What did he do to you? How did he open your eyes?”

Arminian

What did he do? How did he open your eyes? They want the story again, not to believe it.

Arminian: asking again after refusing to listen is not inquiry. The man’s next sentence will expose the “how” as a stall.

Reformed

Calvin: they search on all sides for grounds of slander; the Lord defeats their attempts by the unshaken firmness of the blind man.

Reformed: repetition is a net. Unbelief can be industrious.

John 9:27

27He answered them, “I have told you already, and you would not listen. Why do you want to hear it again? Do you also want to become his disciples?”

Arminian

I told you already, and you would not listen. Why hear it again? Do you also want to become his disciples? Wesley: are ye also, as well as I, at length convinced and willing to be his disciples?

Arminian: “You would not listen” names culpable refusal. The invitation tucked in the sarcasm is still an invitation: they may yet become disciples. Light keeps addressing them.

Reformed

Calvin: he charges them with wicked hatred of Christ; though convinced a hundred times, they are so strongly prejudiced they will never yield. It is an astonishing display of freedom when a man of mean condition fearlessly provokes the priests.

Reformed: a small preparation for faith gave him this boldness. Grace, not social capital, explains the irony.

John 9:28

28And they reviled him, saying, “You are his disciple, but we are disciples of Moses.

Arminian

They revile him: you are his disciple; we are Moses’. False alternative. Following Jesus is not apostasy from Moses.

Arminian: identity by insult. They choose a camp rather than the sign. Claiming Moses while refusing the one Moses wrote of (5:46) is claiming sight.

Reformed

Calvin: they represent discipleship to Christ as revolt from the Law of Moses, and they lie when they say they are Moses’ disciples, having turned aside from the end of the Law.

Reformed: if Christ is the soul of the Law, as Paul says, the Law separated from him is a dead body. Hypocrites tear God in pieces to shelter under his name.

John 9:29

29We know that God has spoken to Moses, but as for this man, we do not know where he comes from.”

Arminian

We know God spoke to Moses; this man, we do not know where he comes from. Wesley: by what power and authority he does these things.

Arminian: “We know / we do not know” is selective. They have more than enough light to know whence a unique mercy comes; they will not use it.

Reformed

Calvin: they mean they have no knowledge of his calling, so as to receive him as having proceeded from God.

Reformed: pretended ignorance of origin, after the work, is not modesty. It is unbelief wearing epistemology.

John 9:30

30The man answered, “Why, this is an amazing thing! You do not know where he comes from, and yet he opened my eyes.

Arminian

Amazing: you do not know where he comes from, and he opened my eyes. Wesley: utterly illiterate as he was, and with what strength and clearness of reason! So had God opened the eyes of his understanding as well as his bodily eyes.

Arminian: the pupil catechizes the teachers. Light received is enough to expose their claimed ignorance as marvelously willful.

Reformed

Calvin: he indirectly reproves them for remaining unmoved by a miracle so illustrious, and for pretending they did not know Christ’s calling.

Reformed: God opened understanding. The illiterate man’s logic is itself a work of God displayed—another sight-giving.

John 9:31

31We know that God does not listen to sinners, but if anyone is a worshiper of God and does his will, God listens to him.

Arminian

God does not listen to sinners; if anyone worships God and does his will, God listens. Wesley: not impenitent sinners, so as to answer their prayers in this manner; even we of the populace know this. The honest courage of this man in adhering to the truth, though he knew the consequence, gives him claim to the title of a confessor.

Arminian: he argues from shared piety: a unique answer to prayer means the doer is heard by God. Confession under threat is free response, not a reflex they can dismiss as naivete.

Reformed

Calvin: those who think he merely echoed popular opinion are mistaken; “sinner” here means an ungodly person. It is the uniform doctrine of Scripture that God does not listen to any but those who call on him with truth; the man does not reason inconclusively that Christ has come from God because God lends a favorable ear to his prayers.

Reformed: his theology is being formed by the Spirit as he speaks. The confessor’s courage is the grace already noted at v. 17.

John 9:32

32Never since the world began has it been heard that anyone opened the eyes of a man born blind.

Arminian

Never since the world began has anyone opened the eyes of one born blind. He knows the uniqueness of the sign.

Arminian: unprecedented mercy raises the obligation. Greater light, greater guilt if refused.

Reformed

He magnifies the miracle from human memory: none was ever heard to do this. Malice shuts eyes on a manifest work of God.

Reformed: salvation-history uniqueness points to the Sent One, not a talented healer.

John 9:33

33If this man were not from God, he could do nothing.”

Arminian

If this man were not from God, he could do nothing. Wesley: nothing of this kind; nothing miraculous.

Arminian: the inference they asked for in v. 17 is now full. He has come to the light he has. They may still come; they will instead expel him.

Reformed

Calvin: he infers that Christ was sent by God, because he is endued with so great power of the Spirit of God to procure credit for himself and for his doctrine.

Reformed: “from God” is the only adequate cause. The inability of a mere sinner to do this is the man’s way of saying the work is God’s—exactly Jesus’ purpose in v. 3.

John 9:34

34They answered him, “You were born in utter sin, and would you teach us?” And they cast him out.

Arminian

You were born in utter sin, and would you teach us? They cast him out. Wesley: born in sin—and therefore, they supposed, born blind. They cast him out of the synagogue; excommunicated him.

Arminian: they return to the disciples’ error of v. 2, now as a weapon. Claiming sight (“would you teach us?”) they refuse the man who sees. Judgment on those who say they see is already in motion; casting him out is their decision, not his failure to be convincing.

Reformed

Calvin: they alluded to his blindness; proud men tease those in calamity. The scribes were convinced that souls entered new bodies to suffer former crimes, and so concluded he came from the womb marked by sins. They excommunicated him with the semblance of law.

Reformed: undeserved censure; pride stops their ears. Casting out will, in the next movement, become the occasion for Christ to find him. Human keys do not lock out the Shepherd.

Key contrast Both condemn the insult and the expulsion. Arminians typically emphasize the Pharisees’ culpable refusal of light they had and the man’s courageous coming-to-the-light; Reformed readers typically emphasize grace-given boldness in the poor man and the leaders’ willful, inward malice—spiritual blindness that cannot see a work of God.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the Sabbath division, the parents’ fear, and the man they cast out—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 9:13–34.

Arminian Notable voices

“The man answered—Utterly illiterate as he was. And with what strength and clearness of reason! So had God opened the eyes of his understanding, as well as his bodily eyes. Why, herein is a marvellous thing, that ye—the teachers and guides of the people—should not know, that a man who has wrought a miracle, the like of which was never heard of before, must be from heaven, sent by God. The honest courage of this man in adhering to the truth, though he knew the consequence, gives him claim to the title of a confessor. They cast him out—of the synagogue; excommunicated him.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 9:30–34)

Reformed Notable voices

“When a poor man disregards their threatenings, and boldly maintains that Christ is a Prophet, we ought justly to ascribe it to the grace of God; so that this boldness is another miracle… There are two things here which ought to be observed; that they do not believe that a miracle has been performed, and that, being wilfully blinded through a perverse hatred of Christ, they do not perceive what is manifest… For what prevents them from seeing an obvious work of God placed before their eyes… except that the inward malice of their heart keeps their eyes shut?”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 9:17–18)
