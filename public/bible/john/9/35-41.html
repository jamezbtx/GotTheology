Passage

John 9

Verses 35–41 · WEB

After the healed man is thrown out of the synagogue, Jesus seeks him out and reveals himself as the Son of Man, and the man believes and worships. Jesus then declares that he came so the blind might see and those who claim to see might become blind. The Pharisees’ question, ‘Are we also blind?’, exposes the sin of refusing light while claiming to have it.

John 9:35

35Jesus heard that they had thrown him out, and finding him, he said, “Do you believe in the Son of God?”

Arminian

Jesus heard they had thrown him out, found him, and asked: Do you believe in the Son of God? Wesley: having found him—for he had sought him.

Arminian: seeking is grace; the question is a one. “Do you believe?” treats him as one who may answer. The Light does not absorb him without a confession. Coming to the Light here is explicit faith in the Son of God.

Reformed

Calvin: if he had been allowed to remain in the synagogue he would have been in danger of becoming alienated from Christ. Christ now meets him wandering, receives and embraces him when the priests have cast him out, raises him from the ground, and offers him life after a sentence of death.

Initiative is the finding. The question is the means by which the finder grants fuller knowledge. Spiritual inability is why he must be found; the asking does not make the finding unnecessary.

Key contrast Both treasure that Jesus seeks the outcast. Arminians catch a call to believe that the man can, by grace, answer; Reformed interpreters hear the Son’s prior finding as the decisive mercy—faith asked for because Christ has already come to the man who could not come back into the synagogue on his own.

John 9:36

36He answered, “Who is he, Lord, that I may believe in him?”

Arminian

And who is he, Lord, that I may believe in him? Wesley: this implies some degree of faith already. He was ready to receive whatever Jesus said.

Arminian: “that I may believe” is desire, not delay-as-refusal. He wants the object of faith identified. Ready reception is a movement toward the Son.

Reformed

Calvin: though he had not yet attained clear or certain knowledge of Christ, he was obedient and ready to receive instruction: as soon as he is pointed out, I am ready to embrace him. He desires to be instructed by Christ as a Prophet, already convinced that Christ had been sent by God.

Readiness is itself the fruit of what God has already done in opening bodily and inner eyes. He does not at random place reliance on Jesus’ doctrine.

John 9:37

37Jesus said to him, “You have both seen him, and it is he who speaks with you.”

Arminian

You have both seen him, and it is he who speaks with you. The Seer is the seen. Sight given in v. 7 now looks at its Giver.

Arminian: revelation is personal and sufficient. He is not asked to climb to heaven; the Son of Man is speaking. Unbelief after this would be refusing a present Light.

Reformed

Calvin: by these words the man could not yet be carried to the full articles of the faith—atonement, resurrection, the Spirit as earnest of adoption—but Calvin has no doubt that Jesus intended to be acknowledged as the Christ, that from this beginning he might lead him forward to more intimate knowledge.

The Speaker grants what the question needs. Progressive illumination is the Lord’s tutoring, not the man’s self-schooling.

John 9:38

38He said, “Lord, I believe!” and he worshiped him.

Arminian

Lord, I believe, and he worshiped him. Wesley: what an excellent spirit was this man of—of so deep and strong an understanding, and yet of so teachable a temper.

Arminian: this is the end of coming to the light in the story’s personal arc: confession and worship. Not a slogan; a man on his face. Judgment will now address those who will not make this move.

Reformed

Calvin: the word denotes something rare and uncommon—far more honor than to an ordinary man or even a prophet. Calvin does not think that at that time he had made such progress as to know Christ was God manifested in the flesh; convinced that Jesus was the Son of God, he nearly lost the command of himself and, in rapturous admiration, bowed down.

Faith and worship are the proper effect of being found. The man does not complete himself; he yields to the One who identified himself.

John 9:39

39Jesus said, “I came into this world for judgment, that those who don’t see may see; and that those who see may become blind.”

Arminian

For judgment I came, that those who don’t see may see; and that those who see may become blind. Wesley: the consequence of my coming will be that, by the just judgment of God, while the blind in body and soul receive their sight, they who boast they see will be given up to still greater blindness than before.

Arminian: judgment is not an arbitrary split; it is what Light does when some come and some claim sight and refuse. The man who knew he was blind now sees; teachers who say they see go darker. That is moral consequence, and it is just.

Reformed

Calvin: “judgment” here cannot mean only punishment; it includes the grace of illumination. Christ restores to order what was disordered, by a wonderful purpose of God. The blindness does not proceed so much from Christ as from the fault of men: by his nature he is the light of the world, and it is an accidental result that some are made blind by his coming. The reprobate desire to extinguish that light; diseased eyes are dazzled.

We are all born blind, yet hypocrites think their sight uncommonly acute. When brightness is brought near they rise in open war, and God in vengeance puts out what remnant they had. Giving sight to those who don’t see is strictly the cause of his coming.

Key contrast Where they meet: the coming sorts sight and blindness. Arminians emphasize judgment as the just result of coming to or refusing the Light—especially those who boast they see; Reformed interpreters stress that giving sight to the unseeing is Christ’s proper work, while the blinding of the self-wise is judicial, rooted in native blindness and malice, not in a defect in the Light.

John 9:40

40Those of the Pharisees who were with him heard these things, and said to him, “Are we also blind?”

Arminian

Pharisees near him: Are we also blind? The “also” bristles. They hear insult, not diagnosis.

Arminian: the question is already an answer. They will not take the place of the man who said he was blind. Claiming not to need sight, they refuse the only Eye-opener.

Reformed

Calvin: indignation, and a haughty contempt of the grace of Christ: we do not choose to receive sight from you on the condition of admitting that we have been hitherto blind. No man is qualified to be a disciple of Christ until he has been divested of self, and they were very far from being so.

Hypocrisy is full of pride and venom. Distinguished men, intoxicated, almost forget they are men. The wound pointed out is treated as an injury Christ inflicted.

John 9:41

41Jesus said to them, “If you were blind, you would have no sin; but now you say, ‘We see.’ Therefore your sin remains.

Arminian

If you were blind, you would have no sin; but now you say, We see. Therefore your sin remains. Wesley: invincibly ignorant—if ye had not had so many means of knowing—ye would have had no sin comparatively; ye yourselves acknowledge, Ye see; therefore your sin remaineth—without excuse, without remedy.

This is the chapter’s verdict on claimed sight. Means of knowing were many; remaining in guilt is refusing the Light while advertising vision. Coming to the Light remains a demand; judgment rests on those who will not.

Reformed

Calvin: if you would acknowledge your disease, it would not be altogether incurable; now because you think you are in perfect health, you continue in a desperate state. “No sin” does not excuse ignorance as harmless; he means the disease may be cured when it is truly felt. When a blind man is desirous to obtain deliverance, God is ready to assist him; they who, insensible to their diseases, despise the grace of God, are incurable.

Spiritual inability is not an excuse for the man who says “We see.” The proud claim of sight is the incurable posture. The found man is the opposite: he knew he was blind, and the Son gave him light.

Key contrast Both hear remaining guilt in the claim “We see.” Arminians take this as heightened responsibility where light was offered and refused—no invincible ignorance left; Reformed readers take it as the incurability of those who will not confess blindness, while the truly blind whom God assists are the ones Christ came to give sight.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
