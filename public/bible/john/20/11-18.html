Passage

John 20

Verses 11–18 · ESV · Voices

Mary stays weeping at the tomb after the men go home. Angels ask why; she wants a body. She turns, mistakes Jesus for the gardener, and hears her name. “Rabboni”—then “Do not cling”: he is ascending to my Father and your Father, my God and your God. She is sent to the brothers with that news. The first Easter preacher is a woman who came for a corpse and left announcing, “I have seen the Lord.”

John 20:11

11But Mary stood weeping outside the tomb, and as she wept she stooped to look into the tomb.

Arminian

But Mary stood—Wesley: with more constancy. The men have gone home; she weeps and stoops again. Constancy is not yet insight. She is still here, still looking, still broken over a missing Lord.

Arminian pastoral: remaining is a real act. Grief that will not leave the tomb is the soil in which the next word can fall. She is not rewarded for theology she does not have; she is met because she stayed and kept seeking.

Reformed

The contrast with verse 10 is sharp: disciples to their homes, Mary at the tomb. Calvin will soon say Christ displayed the boundless treasures of his grace in choosing women as first witnesses—weak vessels, and Mary once possessed by seven demons.

Reformed note: providence parks her here. Weeping is not the efficient cause of Easter; it is where the Lord has appointed to call her by name. Constancy is fruit of a love he had already placed in her.

John 20:12

12And she saw two angels in white, sitting where the body of Jesus had lain, one at the head and one at the feet.

Arminian

Two angels in white, head and feet of the vacant place. Heaven bookends where the body had been. She is given more light than the running men, and she still wants a corpse.

Arminian reading: signs multiply, and the will can still misread them. Angels are not irresistible. The placement is almost ark-like, a mercy to be received; she will talk past them toward the missing body.

Reformed

The place that held him is now an angelic seat. God interprets the vacancy before Mary can. Head and feet mark a real space in history, not a vision detached from the slab.

Reformed accent: revelation comes in an order Mary does not control. Angels first, then the Lord. The Father staffs the tomb she thought was robbed. Glory sits where death had been.

John 20:13

13They said to her, “Woman, why are you weeping?” She said to them, “They have taken away my Lord, and I do not know where they have laid him.”

Arminian

Why are you weeping? is a real question, not a taunt. Her answer repeats verse 2, now my Lord and I do not know. Love names him Lord while locating him among the dead.

Classical Arminians: she tells heaven the truth she has. Incomplete faith still speaks. The question invites her to reconsider the tears; she may; she does not yet. Grace reasons with a weeper.

Reformed

Angels catechize her grief. The question exposes a weeping that does not match the facts God has already arranged. She answers with theft, not rising.

Reformed note: “my Lord” is seed; “laid him” is unbelief’s picture. God lets her sentence stand long enough to turn her toward the gardener. The why is mercy: tears are not self-authenticating when the tomb is empty by design.

John 20:14

14Having said this, she turned around and saw Jesus standing, but she did not know that it was Jesus.

Arminian

She turns and sees him—and does not know him. Presence is not yet recognition. The risen Lord can stand in front of love and still be unread.

Arminian weight: seeing Jesus is not the same as knowing him. The will and the mind can miss the One they seek. He does not coerce recognition; he will speak a name. The failure to know is hers; the standing is his patience.

Reformed

Calvin on the next verse: Christ allowed Mary a short time to fall into a mistake, useful for confirming her faith. Not-knowing is appointed, not accidental.

Reformed accent: the Lord is there before she has eyes. Recognition will be his gift when he calls. Flesh, even loving flesh, does not infer Easter from a figure in the garden. He must make himself known.

John 20:15

15Jesus said to her, “Woman, why are you weeping? Whom are you seeking?” Supposing him to be the gardener, she said to him, “Sir, if you have carried him away, tell me where you have laid him, and I will take him away.”

Arminian

The same why, now from Jesus, plus whom are you seeking? She supposes a gardener and offers to haul the body herself. Devotion without knowledge volunteers logistics for a corpse.

Arminian pastoral: the questions are genuine addresses. She might have answered “the Lord” and looked again. Instead she bargains with a stranger. Seeking is not yet finding; the seeker is still responsible for the whom.

Reformed

He takes up the angels’ question and adds seeking. The good Shepherd probes the lost sheep before he names her. Her gardener-plan is holy and absurd: she will carry what she cannot lift, to a grave that cannot hold him.

Reformed reading: whom-seeking is the right diagnostic. She seeks a dead Jesus and therefore cannot see the living one. The mistake is useful; it will make the name in verse 16 unmistakable.

John 20:16

16Jesus said to her, “Mary.” She turned and said to him in Aramaic, “Rabboni!” (which means Teacher).

Arminian

Mary—Wesley: with his usual voice and accent. One word turns her. Rabboni is personal allegiance, Teacher and more. The name she has worn all her life becomes the sacrament of recognition.

Classical Arminians: the call is particular and sincere, and her turn is a real response. He speaks so as to be heard; she yields. This is not an anonymous force. The same Jesus names other sheep; the naming is grace that expects an answer, not a spell that bypasses the person.

Reformed

Calvin: the Shepherd calls his own sheep by name (John 10:3). That voice enters Mary’s heart, opens her eyes, arouses her senses; she surrenders. In Mary, a lively image of our calling: we are admitted to true knowledge of Christ when he first knows us and then familiarly invites us—not by the ordinary voice that sounds indiscriminately in all ears, but by the voice with which he especially calls the sheep the Father has given him.

Reformed accent: Rabboni is the fruit of effectual naming. The turn is hers because the call was his, particular as John 10, not a general garden-noise she happened to use well.

Key contrast Shared: one personal name opens her eyes; she answers as a disciple. Arminians hear a sincere, particular call that expects and receives a free turn—grace that names and can be answered. Reformed readers (Calvin) hear John 10 in the garden: the Shepherd’s own voice to the sheep the Father gave him, not the indiscriminate sound in every ear; knowledge of Christ begins with his knowing us.

John 20:17

17Jesus said to her, “Do not cling to me, for I have not yet ascended to the Father; but go to my brothers and say to them, ‘I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God.’”

Arminian

Do not cling—Wesley: detain me not; she held his feet (Matthew 28:9); other opportunities will come. Go to my brothers: he intimates forgiveness of their fault without naming it. My Father and your Father, my God and your God: the only-begotten has unique fellowship with God; a resembling fellowship he bestows on his brethren. He will not say our God—no creature is raised to equality; the Father is his incommunicably, ours through him as a creature can bear.

Arminian gospel: brothers is mercy to deserters, and the message is for them to receive. Shared sonship is gift, not absorption into the Son’s essence. The church is sent news it did not earn; the terms remain mine and yours, not a flattened our.

Reformed

Calvin: they were not forbidden to touch until eagerness to keep him on earth became excess. Ascending names the point of the resurrection: not lingering in the world but taking the kingdom at the Father’s right hand. Brothers are the disciples, Psalm 22:22, not merely cousins. He is Son by nature; we are sons by adoption—and that grace is so firm it cannot be shaken so as to hinder us from calling him Father.

Reformed weight: do not cling to half a resurrection. Raise minds upward. My Father/your Father is union with the fountain of blessing, not a denial of the Son’s uniqueness. Mary’s commission is extraordinary; the treasure is the news, not a standing office of women as apostles.

Key contrast Both refuse “our God” as if the Son and the redeemed stood on one rung, and both hear brothers as forgiven disciples. Arminians (Wesley) stress the bestowed, creature-fitting fellowship and the implicit pardon the deserters may receive. Reformed readers (Calvin) stress nature versus adoption, the ascent to reign, and a calling that draws the given sheep to the same Father without confusing the only-begotten with the adopted.

John 20:18

18Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, “I have seen the Lord”—and that he had said these things to her.

Arminian

She goes and announces. I have seen the Lord—plus the words he gave her. The first Easter sermon is obedience to go, not clinging. Wesley’s “immediately” is the right pace: she does not wait for a second confirmation.

Arminian close: a woman once known for demons is trusted with the brothers’ gospel. The disciples may treat it as an idle tale (Luke 24); that will be their resistance, not a defect in the sending. Testimony is sincere; receiving it is now the church’s act.

Reformed

Calvin: she fully obeyed; therefore “brothers” were the disciples. Christ used weak vessels to display his power, and the apostles deserved the school of the women after such sluggish unbelief. The occurrence is extraordinary, not a law that women baptize or take the apostolic office.

Reformed close: the announcement is the Lord’s doing through her. “I have seen the Lord” is eyewitness; “these things” is doctrine of the ascent. The men will still need Jesus among them (vv. 19–23). Her faithfulness does not generate their faith; it delivers the word he wrote on her tongue.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Mary’s constancy, the Shepherd’s naming, and the ascent to my Father and your Father—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 20:11–18.

Arminian Notable voices

“But Mary stood—with more constancy. Jesus saith to her, Mary—with his usual voice and accent. Touch me not—or rather, Do not cling to me (for she held him by the feet). Detain me not now. You will have other opportunities of conversing with me. For I am not ascended to my Father—I have not yet left the world. But go immediately to my brethren—thus does he intimate in the strongest manner the forgiveness of their fault, even without ever mentioning it. To my Father and your Father, to my God and your God—this uncommon expression shows that the only-begotten Son has all kind of fellowship with God. And a fellowship with God the Father, some way resembling his own, he bestows upon his brethren. Yet he does not say, Our God: for no creature can be raised to an equality with him; but my God and your God: intimating that the Father is his in a singular and incommunicable manner; and ours through him, in such a kind as a creature is capable of.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 20:11, 16–17)

Reformed Notable voices

“He now assumes the character of the Master and addresses his disciple by name, as the good shepherd calleth to him by name every sheep of his flock. That voice of the shepherd enters into Mary’s heart, opens her eyes, arouses all her senses, and affects her in such a manner that she immediately surrenders herself to Christ. Thus in Mary we have a lively image of our calling; for the only way in which we are admitted to the true knowledge of Christ is when he first knows us, and then familiarly invites us to himself, not by that ordinary voice which sounds indiscriminately in the ears of all, but by that voice with which he especially calls the sheep which the Father hath given to him. He imparts to us the very fountain of blessings: He who is the God of Christ is their God, and He who is the Father of Christ is their Father. He is the Son of God by nature, while we are the sons of God only by adoption.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 20:16–17)
