Passage

John 20

Verses 19–23 · ESV · Voices

Evening of that first day: locked doors, fear, and Jesus among them with peace, wounds, and gladness. Then the high-contrast charge: as the Father sent me, I am sending you; he breathes, Receive the Holy Spirit; if you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven—if you withhold, it is withheld. Sending, Spirit, and forgiveness arrive together. Neither camp owns the verses; both must speak them without flattening the other.

John 20:19

19On the evening of that day, the first day of the week, the doors being locked where the disciples were for fear of the Jews, Jesus came and stood among them and said to them, “Peace be with you.”

Arminian

Same first day, evening, locked doors, fear. He comes and stands among them with the ordinary Hebrew blessing that here is not ordinary: Peace be with you. Locked fear does not keep him out, and the greeting is a real word to frightened people.

Arminian pastoral: peace is spoken to a room that can still refuse it. They are gathered; Thomas is not (v. 24). Presence and greeting are sincere gifts. Wesley will tie the next peace to a minister’s own soul—this first peace is the Lord’s to a church that has not yet been sent.

Reformed

Calvin: it did not happen without providence that all were assembled; Christ acted gently in not keeping them in suspense past evening. Shut doors prove weakness, yet they did not scatter—an example to struggle against flesh, not indulge fear into apostasy. Thomas, like a wandering soldier, withdrew from the standard of union and is justly deprived of this favor for now.

Reformed accent: he stood among them though doors were shut—a miracle of presence, not a proof that his body is infinite or passed through planks like a ghost. Peace is “may you be well,” the risen Lord’s gift to a trembling flock he will not abandon.

John 20:20

20When he had said this, he showed them his hands and his side. Then the disciples were glad when they saw the Lord.

Arminian

Hands and side: the cross is still readable on the living man. Gladness follows sight. John will need this when Thomas demands the same wounds. The Lord offers evidence; he does not scorn their need to see.

Arminian note: joy is a fitting response to sufficient signs. They saw the Lord—the same one who died. Faith is invited by wounds, not by a different Jesus. Gladness can still deepen; it is already more than verse 10’s going home.

Reformed

Calvin: necessary confirmation, that by all methods they might be fully assured he was risen. Wounds after resurrection are not a theft of glory: he rose for us, and what confirms our faith is glorious to him. Their use was temporary, until the apostles were convinced—not a warrant for imagining a still-bleeding side in heaven.

Reformed reading: grief at his death is dispelled by his new life. Sight of the Lord is the Father’s kindness to weak faith. The same body will stand eight days later for Thomas; the church’s gladness rests on identified wounds, not on a rumor.

John 20:21

21Jesus said to them again, “Peace be with you. As the Father has sent me, even so I am sending you.”

Arminian

Peace again—then the as/even so of mission. Wesley: this peace is the foundation of a true gospel minister’s mission, peace in his own soul. Christ was the apostle of the Father (Hebrews 3:1); Peter and the rest, apostles of Christ. The analogy is real: the church is actually sent, not merely informed that the Son’s unique sending happened long ago.

Classical Arminians: sending extends through these disciples into the ministry of the gospel to all nations. The as is analogical, not identical: they do not become incarnate redeemers. But it is not theater. Ministers who have peace are truly dispatched; hearers are truly addressed. The charge is a sincere commission under which people may go or refuse to go, preach or fall silent.

Reformed

Calvin: the second peace aims at attention. Hitherto they had been heralds in Judea; now they are installed as apostles, ordinary ministers of the gospel, ambassadors to establish his kingdom. He does not resign the Father’s vesting of highest teacher-authority in the Son alone—“hear him” cannot be set aside. He does not send them to atone for sins or procure justification as he was sent. The only subject here is preaching. Succession takes nothing from Christ; he adorns the doctrine, not men.

Reformed accent: the as/so ratifies their ministry with the Father’s own sending-authority behind the word, while keeping what is peculiar to the Son. Perpetual preaching, not a short errand; Christ’s mouth now speaks by apostles, his honor unimpaired.

Key contrast Shared: the risen Christ really sends, and the as/so does not make disciples into a second incarnation or a rival atonement. Arminians (Wesley) hear a live ministerial commission grounded in the preacher’s received peace, analogical sending that continues wherever the gospel is truly ministered and can be obeyed or neglected. Reformed readers (Calvin) hear the first installation of apostles as ordinary gospel ministers, a succession of preaching that takes nothing from the only Teacher, expressly not a sending to atone; authority rests in the word Christ keeps as his own.

John 20:22

22And when he had said this, he breathed on them and said to them, “Receive the Holy Spirit.

Arminian

He breathes and says Receive. Wesley: new life and vigour; as you receive this breath from my mouth, receive the Spirit from my fullness; the Holy Ghost influencing you in a peculiar manner to fit you for the embassy; an earnest of Pentecost. Receive is gift-language that still has to be received. The Spirit is not a human tool, and the earnest is not yet the tongues of fire.

Classical Arminians: fitting for mission is grace, not native capacity—no Pelagius at Easter. They still insist the Spirit given for embassy can be grieved and that Pentecost’s fullness remains to be waited for in obedience (Luke 24:49). Breath plus word is a real offer from Christ’s fullness; it does not turn the disciples into machines. The same Lord who breathes also commands them to receive.

Reformed

Calvin: not one of the sons of men is qualified; Christ prepares them by the Spirit’s grace, and it is his glory alone to form those he appoints. He bestows the Spirit as his own, in common with the Father; the Spirit proceeds from him. They are sprinkled here, not filled until Acts 2; this breathing refers chiefly to that future magnificent sending. Sign and word are joined: they receive the Spirit because Christ promises, as sacraments depend on the testimony of the word—not because breath itself contains the Spirit, and not as a perpetual rite for mitred bishops to copy.

Reformed accent: calling is authenticated by the Spirit Christ actually gives. Empty office without the Spirit is a fraud. Effectual qualifying, from Christ, of those he sends: they have nothing from themselves (v. 23’s preface).

Key contrast Both: this is not the full Pentecost; it is from Christ’s own fullness; unaided nature cannot staff the mission. Arminians (Wesley) stress receive—breath as emblem of a gift taken from his fullness, earnest that fits for embassy and still awaits Pentecost, grace that must be received rather than assumed irresistible. Reformed readers (Calvin) stress that no one is qualified unless Christ forms him by the Spirit who proceeds from the Son; sprinkled now, filled later; the sign works by the promise, and the gift is Christ’s effectual preparation of the ministers he appoints.

John 20:23

23If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven them; if you withhold forgiveness from any, it is withheld.”

Arminian

Forgive / withhold: Wesley will not let this become priestcraft. According to the tenor of the gospel—supposing them to repent and believe, sins are remitted; supposing them impenitent, retained. Are not the truly penitent forgiven without sacerdotal absolution? Are not the impenitent retained even with it? Then the commission is: (1) authority to declare the Christian terms of pardon; (2) power of ecclesiastical censures—excluding from and re-admitting into a congregation.

Classical Arminians: the hinge is the hearer’s repenting and believing. Ministers announce terms; they do not create pardon. Withholding is not a private grudge; it is the church’s truthful application of the gospel to remaining unbelief. Forgiveness is sincerely offered; it is not applied to the unrepentant by a formula. Both sides here reject a magical ear-confession. The Arminian edge is conditionality: the gospel’s pardon meets a real response grace enables and does not coerce.

Reformed

Calvin: the sum of the gospel; do not separate this power from the office of teaching. The embassy’s meaning is reconciliation by unconditional pardon of sins—not that God ignores impenitence, but that the gospel’s principal design is to receive people by not imputing sins. Christ does not convey what is peculiar to himself; it belongs to him to forgive. He enjoins them to proclaim forgiveness in his name; he alone forgives through ministers. Lofty terms so consciences may believe God himself speaks in their person (2 Corinthians 5:20). Papists torture this into magical absolutions and whispered inventories of sins; Christ appoints preachers who seal the atonement on believers’ hearts, not confessors. The retain clause comes second, to terrify despisers; inviting all to salvation belongs to the gospel’s nature, destruction of rejecters is accidental to it. Whoever hears and will not embrace the promised forgiveness is liable to a severer sentence.

Reformed accent: the power is the Word’s, not the man’s. Forgiveness announced is God’s own pardon to those who believe; retention is God’s verdict on those who refuse. Ministers are earthen ambassadors, not authors.

Key contrast Do not caricature. Shared: God alone truly forgives; this is not a Roman magical absolution; the church may also bind and loose in discipline. Arminians (Wesley) locate the verse in gospel terms the hearer must meet—repent and believe—plus congregational censure; pardon is not tied to a priest’s formula, and impenitence is not undone by one. Reformed readers (Calvin) locate it in the preaching of reconciliation: Christ alone forgives through ministers of the word; the retain clause arms the embassy against despisers; faith is confirmed because God speaks in the gospel they preach. Neither camp should be heard as handing out a human lever over heaven, or as emptying the church of a real commission.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the risen Lord’s sending, the breathed Spirit, and the authority to forgive or withhold—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 20:19–23.

Arminian Notable voices

“Peace be unto you—this is the foundation of the mission of a true Gospel minister, peace in his own soul. As the Father hath sent me, so send I you—Christ was the apostle of the Father; Peter and the rest, the apostles of Christ. He breathed on them—new life and vigour, and saith, as ye receive this breath out of my mouth, so receive ye the Spirit out of my fulness: the Holy Ghost influencing you in a peculiar manner, to fit you for your great embassy. This was an earnest of pentecost. Whose soever sins ye remit—(according to the tenor of the Gospel, that is, supposing them to repent and believe) they are remitted, and whose soever sins ye retain (supposing them to remain impenitent) they are retained. Are not the sins of one who truly repents, and unfeignedly believes in Christ, remitted, without sacerdotal absolution? And are not the sins of one who does not repent or believe, retained even with it? What then does this commission imply? Can it imply any more than, 1. A power of declaring with authority the Christian terms of pardon; and 2. A power of inflicting and remitting ecclesiastical censures? That is, of excluding from, and re-admitting into, a Christian congregation.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 20:21–23)

Reformed Notable voices

“As the Father hath sent me, I also send you. By these words Christ instals them in the office to which he had previously appointed them. Now the Lord ordains them to be his ambassadors, to establish his kingdom in the world. He does not substitute them in his room so as to resign the highest authority as a teacher, which the Father intended to be vested in him alone. The only subject handled in this passage is the preaching of the Gospel; for Christ does not send his Apostles to atone for sins, and to procure justification, as he was sent by the Father. Receive ye the Holy Spirit: not one of the sons of men is qualified for discharging so difficult an office; Christ prepares the Apostles by the grace of his Spirit. They were only sprinkled by his grace, but were not filled with full power. To all whose sins you shall remit: our Lord has embraced the sum of the Gospel; we must not separate this power of forgiving sins from the office of teaching. While Christ enjoins the Apostles to forgive sins, he does not convey to them what is peculiar to himself. It belongs to him to forgive sins. He enjoins them, in his name, to proclaim the forgiveness of sins. Properly speaking, it is he alone who forgives sins through his apostles and ministers.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 20:21–23)
