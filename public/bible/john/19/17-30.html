Passage

John 19

Verses 17–30 · ESV · Voices

Jesus goes out bearing his own cross to Golgotha and is crucified between two others. Pilate's trilingual title stands. Soldiers divide garments and cast lots for the tunic. The women and the beloved disciple are given to one another. Knowing all is finished, Jesus thirsts, receives sour wine, says It is finished, and gives up his spirit.

John 19:17

17and he went out, bearing his own cross, to the place called The Place of a Skull, which in Aramaic is called Golgotha.

Arminian

Wesley: not the whole cross, too large and heavy, but the transverse beam they made the condemned carry. He went out—outside the city, as sin-offerings were taken outside the camp. Classical Arminians read a public, sufficient sin-bearing: the Lamb is led where the world can see him.

Arminian gospel: bearing his own cross is the purchase beginning to be paid in full view. Golgotha is not a private transaction for a hidden list only. The world that sinned is the world this going-out addresses; the application of that purchase is to those who believe.

Reformed

Calvin: we must look for righteousness through the satisfaction made by Christ. He wished both to be led out of the city and hanged on a tree, that we might be convinced atonement has been made: made a curse to redeem us from the curse of the law; made sin that we might become the righteousness of God in him.

Reformed weight: going out is not theater. The defilements laid on him are carried away. This is the appointed curse-bearing for those he actually redeems, the satisfaction by which their guilt is removed—not a mere demonstration waiting to become atonement later.

Key contrast Shared ground: he goes outside the city as a sin-offering and curse-bearer. Arminians typically take the going-out as a sufficient sacrifice directed to the world, applied to believers. Reformed readers typically take it as the Son actually bearing the curse in the place of those given to him, so that their righteousness is already located in this satisfaction.

John 19:18

18There they crucified him, and with him two others, one on either side, and Jesus between them.

Arminian

There: the Place of a Skull becomes an altar. Two others, Jesus in the middle—numbered with transgressors in the most visual way. Classical Arminians hear a worldwide shame: the middle cross is for the world's sin, not a tight circle of names on a hidden ledger.

Arminian pastoral: he is close enough to criminals that anyone may see this death is for sinners. The two others do not shrink the middle cross; they advertise its company. Whoever believes may look at this crucified one and live.

Reformed

Calvin: as if the severity were not enough, he is hanged as if he were the most detestable of the three. The executioners did nothing but what God had determined; he did not surrender his Son to lawless passions as if he had lost him, but offered him as a sacrifice according to his will.

Reformed sight: in this mirror we see the weight of wrath against sin and the goodness that did not spare the Son. Guilt is removed in no other way than the Son becoming a curse. The middle cross is effectual for those he came to save, not a general possibility left hanging.

John 19:19

19Pilate also wrote an inscription and put it on the cross. It read, “Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews.”

Arminian

Wesley: undoubtedly these were the very words, though the other evangelists do not express them at large. The title tells the truth the priests wanted hedged. Classical Arminians hear a King named to the public: Nazareth, Jews, and any reader of the board.

Arminian note: the gospel on a plank is a sincere identification. This is who he is. The world may read it and believe, or rage against it. The inscription does not whisper king-of-a-few; it names him in the open.

Reformed

Calvin: perhaps titles were customary, but in Christ the title implies no crime. Pilate meant to avenge himself on the Jews; God guided the pen to a higher object—celebrating Christ as King though the writer did not know what he wrote.

Reformed accent: providence, not Pilate's spite, is the true author. The Nazarene King of a chosen people is published at the moment of his dying. The title is gospel because God dictated it, and it stands over the sacrifice that actually saves his people.

John 19:20

20Many of the Jews read this inscription, for the place where Jesus was crucified was near the city, and it was written in Aramaic, in Latin, and in Greek.

Arminian

Wesley: Latin for the majesty of the empire; Hebrew for the nation; Greek for the Hellenists who came in great numbers to the feast. Three languages, many readers, near the city. Classical Arminians take the nearness and the languages as a real publication to the nations.

Arminian gospel: God wanted the King readable. The cross is not an in-house memo. Aramaic, Latin, and Greek are a Pentecost in nails: the finished work will be announced as widely as these tongues already run.

Reformed

Calvin: the same secret guidance of the Spirit caused publication in three languages; it is not probable this was ordinary practice. The Lord showed the time was at hand when the name of his Son should be made known throughout the whole earth.

Reformed reading: near the city and in the world's languages is preparatory arrangement, not accident. The particular Redeemer is being advertised to the nations from which he will gather those given to him. The languages serve mission because the work on the middle cross is complete.

John 19:21

21So the chief priests of the Jews said to Pilate, “Do not write, ‘The King of the Jews,’ but rather, ‘This man said, I am King of the Jews.’”

Arminian

They cannot bear the board as written. They want the kingship moved from fact to alleged claim. Classical Arminians hear unbelief trying to edit the gospel down to a rumor this man said.

Arminian note: the world still prefers Jesus-said to Jesus-is. The title was a sincere public word; the priests' amendment would make faith optional gossip. The King remains the King whether they allow the wording.

Reformed

Calvin: they feel sharply rebuked and would change the title so as not to involve the nation in disgrace, throwing the whole blame on Christ. They cannot endure even the smallest spark of truth.

Reformed note: Satan prompts servants to choke the light of God as soon as the feeblest ray appears. The request is not a small edit; it is hatred of a kingship God has already written over the sacrifice.

John 19:22

22Pilate answered, “What I have written I have written.”

Arminian

Wesley: That shall stand. A pagan refuses to unsay the truth he has posted. Classical Arminians take the standing words as providence using a stubborn governor: the King-title remains readable, and the world may still believe it.

Arminian pastoral: what God has caused to be written about his Son is not withdrawn because officials dislike it. The inscription stays. Hearers remain responsible to a King who has been publicly named.

Reformed

Calvin: Pilate's firmness must be ascribed to the providence of God; he was held by a divine hand. A heathen refuses to retract what he has justly written concerning Christ, though he did not understand what he was doing.

Reformed weight: if even Pilate's writing could not be shaken, how much more the prophets. The kingdom of the Son is shown firm at the hour of his death—the death that actually accomplishes salvation for those the Father gave him.

John 19:23

23When the soldiers had crucified Jesus, they took his garments and divided them into four parts, one part for each soldier; also his tunic. But the tunic was seamless, woven in one piece from top to bottom,

Arminian

Wesley: the vesture is the upper garment. Four parts for four soldiers, plus a seamless tunic they will not tear. Classical Arminians see ordinary greed at the foot of an extraordinary death: the world strips him while he buys the world's redemption.

Arminian note: he is naked that we might be clothed. The purchase of man's redemption is underway while men gamble for cloth. Nothing here says the nakedness is only for a few; it is the public shame of the world's Savior.

Reformed

Calvin, with the other Evangelists: four soldiers part all but the coat, which, being without seam, they will cast lots for. Let us learn that Christ was stripped of his garments that he might clothe us with righteousness; his naked body was exposed to insults that we may appear in glory before God's judgment-seat.

Reformed comfort: the stripping is substitutionary clothing. Those he clothes are those his death actually covers. The seamless tunic will fulfill Scripture in the next verse; the righteousness he provides is not a half-garment.

John 19:24

24so they said to one another, “Let us not tear it, but cast lots for it to see whose it shall be.” This was to fulfill the Scripture which says, “They divided my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots.” So the soldiers did these things,

Arminian

Wesley: no circumstance of David's life bore any resemblance to this, or to several other passages in Psalm 22. The prophet, personating the Messiah, spoke what the Spirit dictated. Classical Arminians hear Scripture aiming at this hour so that the world may recognize the crucified one.

Arminian gospel: fulfillment is for readers. The lots are not a code for insiders only. Psalm 22 is being acted in public so that whoever looks may know this is the one the Scriptures meant.

Reformed

Calvin: the Evangelists remind us that in this occurrence also Scripture was fulfilled. Psalm 22 is not restricted to David—I will proclaim thy name among the Gentiles must refer to Christ. What was faintly shadowed in David is beheld in Christ with the superior clearness truth ought to have compared with the figure.

Reformed accent: soldiers do these things, and God is keeping his word. The Messiah's garments go by lot because the Spirit had written it. Fulfillment here is the same faithful God who will not lose those he has given to the Son.

John 19:25

25but standing by the cross of Jesus were his mother and his mother's sister, Mary the wife of Clopas, and Mary Magdalene.

Arminian

Wesley: we do not read she had any brother; she was her father's heir and as such transmitted the right of David's kingdom to Jesus. Mary of Clopas (also Alpheus) is named with Mary Magdalene. Classical Arminians linger on the women who stay when most of the men have gone.

Arminian pastoral: standing by the cross is a real act of love under enabling grace. They are not forced into place as scenery. The dying King still has a people who can remain, and remaining is commended, not treated as a puppet motion.

Reformed

John names four at the wood: the mother, her sister, Mary of Clopas, Mary Magdalene. The church's first congregation at Golgotha is mostly women. Reformed reading: God keeps a remnant at the cross while the official world casts lots.

Reformed note: they stand because they have been kept. The mother of the King is watching the curse-bearing she cannot stop. Their presence is not the atonement; it is evidence that this death already gathers a people.

John 19:26

26When Jesus saw his mother and the disciple whom he loved standing nearby, he said to his mother, “Woman, behold, your son!”

Arminian

He sees them. The dying man still provides. Woman, behold your son—the same address as Cana, now at the hour. Classical Arminians hear love that does not cease to care for particular people while purchasing the world's redemption.

Arminian note: sufficient for the world does not mean inattentive to a mother. The cross is both cosmic and domestic. He still addresses her as a person who can receive the word, not as a prop in a decree.

Reformed

He saw them: the loved disciple and the mother, nearby. Woman, behold your son—he relocates her household while he finishes the world's salvation, in Calvin's register of a death that contains all the parts of our salvation.

Reformed accent: even the family word is spoken from the finished-work hour. He does not come down to manage the household; he provides from the cross. The beloved disciple is given a charge because this Savior actually keeps his own.

John 19:27

27Then he said to the disciple, “Behold, your mother!” And from that hour the disciple took her to his own home.

Arminian

Wesley: Behold thy mother—to whom thou art now to perform the part of a son in my place, a peculiar honor. From that hour—from the time of our Lord's death. Classical Arminians hear a real new household created by a word that can be obeyed.

Arminian pastoral: the disciple takes her. Obedience is not delayed until a secret list is published. The word creates a duty he actually performs. Love at the cross is particular and still consistent with a purchase as wide as man.

Reformed

The disciple is given a mother; the mother is given a son. From that hour he took her home. Reformed reading: the dying King orders his house because his death will not leave his people orphans.

Reformed note: this is not a distraction from atonement. The one who will say It is finished first binds a family. Those he keeps, he keeps all the way through death—the same keeping John 17 named as given to the Son.

John 19:28

28After this, Jesus, knowing that all was now finished, said (to fulfill the Scripture), “I thirst.”

Arminian

Knowing that all was now finished, he still thirsts to fulfill Scripture (Psalm 69:21). Wesley will soon gloss finished as his suffering, the purchase of man's redemption. Classical Arminians hear a completion of provision: the work that buys the world is at the last line.

Arminian weight: he does not ask drink until the purchase is essentially done. Thirst is real, not a metaphor, and still a chosen last obedience. Finished here means the redemption-price is paid in full for mankind; application remains to those who believe.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ does not ask anything to drink till all things have been accomplished; thus he testified infinite love and an inconceivable earnestness to promote our salvation. He does not desire to be freed until the justice of God has been satisfied and he has made a perfect atonement.

Reformed reading: knowing all was finished is the Son's knowledge of a complete satisfaction, not a half-built bridge. Resurrection will follow, but nothing now remains to hinder death and rising. The atonement he has made is the whole accomplishment of our salvation contained in this death.

John 19:29

29A jar full of sour wine stood there, so they put a sponge full of the sour wine on a hyssop branch and held it to his mouth.

Arminian

Wesley: a stalk of hyssop, which in those countries grows exceeding large and strong (Psalm 69:21). Hyssop is also Passover's brush. Classical Arminians hear the last detail of the Lamb's death matching Israel's book so that the world may recognize him.

Arminian note: they hold it to his mouth. He receives what David said the sufferer would receive. The sour wine does not cheapen the purchase; it completes the written cup. The provision stands; thirsty sinners are still invited to the living water he once offered a woman at a well.

Reformed

Calvin agrees hyssop in that country grows like small shrubs, so a sponge could be raised to his mouth. He refuses metaphorical games that make thirst mean only bitterness; John says he asked when near death, from which it is evident he did not desire luxuries.

Reformed accent: Scripture is being filled to the brim so that faith may rest on a finished, written work. The hyssop that once marked doors with lamb's blood now touches the true Lamb's mouth. The sign and the thing signified meet in one death.

John 19:30

30When Jesus had received the sour wine, he said, “It is finished,” and he bowed his head and gave up his spirit.

Arminian

Wesley: It is finished—My suffering: the purchase of man's redemption. He delivered up his spirit to God. Classical Arminians take tetelestai as a completed price, sufficient for the world. The suffering that buys mankind is done; it is applied to believers who receive the purchased redemption, not automatically credited to every person without faith.

Arminian gospel: finished does not mean everyone is already saved, nor that the cross was only a gesture. The purchase is real and worldwide in provision. Whoever believes has the finished work applied. Unbelief does not prove the price was never meant for them; it proves they will not have the Redeemer who bought them. No extra Mass, merit, or self-atonement can add to a purchase he has already called finished.

Reformed

Calvin: this word shows that the whole accomplishment of our salvation, and all the parts of it, are contained in his death. Everything which contributes to the salvation of men is to be found in Christ—the perfection of salvation is contained in him. He contrasts his death with ancient sacrifices: now the true salvation is exhibited. If we assent to this word, we ought to be satisfied with his death alone.

Reformed gospel: finished is not a potential waiting on our ratification. The work actually saves those the Father gave to the Son (John 17:2, 6, 9). He bowed his head and gave up his spirit as the one who had authority to lay it down. The elect's debt is not left half-paid; it is paid. Faith receives a salvation already accomplished for those given to him, rather than activating a mere possibility. Calvin also hears comfort for believers who die with Christ: they commit the soul to a God who will not suffer to perish what he has undertaken to preserve.

Key contrast HIGH CONTRAST—do not flatten It is finished. Shared ground: the suffering is complete; no further sacrifice of Christ is needed; salvation is not a human add-on. Classical Arminians, with Wesley's “purchase of man's redemption,” take the finished work as sufficient for the world and applied to believers who receive it. Reformed readers, with Calvin's “whole accomplishment of our salvation contained in his death,” take the finished work as actually saving those given to the Son—definite, effectual atonement, not a general possibility left idle. Fairness: Arminians are not saying the cross is incomplete pending our effort; Reformed readers are not saying the invitation “whoever believes” is a feint. They differ on whether finished means a worldwide sufficient purchase personally applied by faith, or a complete salvation accomplished for the given people the Son actually redeems.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the cross where he said It is finished—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 19:17–30.

Arminian Notable voices

“It is finished—My suffering: the purchase of man's redemption. He delivered up his spirit—To God. Bearing his cross—Not the whole cross (for that was too large and heavy), but the transverse beam of it, to which his hands were afterward fastened.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 19:17, 30)

Reformed Notable voices

“This word well deserves our attention; for it shows that the whole accomplishment of our salvation, and all the parts of it, are contained in his death. The meaning is that every thing which contributes to the salvation of men is to be found in Christ, and ought not to be sought anywhere else—that the perfection of salvation is contained in him. We must look for righteousness through the satisfaction made by Christ. In no other way could our guilt be removed than by the Son of God becoming a curse for us.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 19:17, 30)
