Passage

John 18

Verses 28–40 · ESV · Voices

At dawn they bring Jesus from Caiaphas to Pilate yet will not enter, lest they be defiled for Passover. Death-power is Rome’s, fulfilling the kind of death he had said. Inside, he confesses a kingdom not of this world and a birth-purpose to witness to the truth: everyone who is of the truth listens to his voice. Pilate asks what truth is, finds no guilt, and the crowd chooses Barabbas.

John 18:28

28Then they led Jesus from the house of Caiaphas to the governor’s headquarters. It was early morning. They themselves did not enter the governor’s headquarters, so that they would not be defiled, but could eat the Passover.

Arminian

Early morning, Caiaphas to the governor. They will not enter, lest they be defiled and miss Passover. Wesley: a house not purged from leaven. They keep a threshold and break the commandment against innocent blood.

Arminian warning: scrupulous feet, unclean hands. The Lamb is delivered at dawn by men protecting their feast. Religious caution can escort a murder and still call itself purity. Eat the Passover; refuse the Passover standing in front of you.

Reformed

Calvin: two heinous faults—they ignore the pollution already in their hearts, and they strain at the small while swallowing the great. Full of malice, they fear only a Gentile floor. Hypocrisy prefers men’s traditions to God’s weightier things.

Reformed edge: they observe the shadow of Passover with false reverence and, as far as they can, bury the true Passover. Defilement is not the praetorium; it is delivering the innocent while counting the calendar.

John 18:29

29So Pilate went outside to them and said, “What accusation do you bring against this man?”

Arminian

Pilate comes out. A pagan asks for an accusation. Process, at least as a question, starts in the street because they will not come in.

Arminian note: even a compromised governor can require a charge. “This man” is still owed a sentence that names a deed. The leaders have brought a body; they have not yet brought a case.

Reformed

Calvin: this heathen will humor a superstition he despises, yet in the main he performs a judge’s duty by ordering them to bring an accusation if they have one.

Reformed reading: John lets a Gentile ask the question Israel’s court skipped. The contrast is not that Pilate is righteous; it is that the accusers want a verdict without a charge. The hour still moves through this reluctant door.

John 18:30

30They answered him, “If this man were not doing evil, we would not have delivered him over to you.”

Arminian

If he were not doing evil, we would not have delivered him. The charge is their sincerity. Trust us: we only prosecute malefactors.

Classical Arminians: this is pride dressed as evidence. Doing evil is asserted, not shown. Light they had in his open teaching (vv. 20–21) is refused; they offer their delivery as the proof. Bad-faith process is still a choice.

Reformed

Calvin: they complain that Pilate does not rely on their integrity. Wicked men raised high, blinded by greatness, do whatever they choose. They wish him reckoned a malefactor for no other reason than that they accuse him.

Reformed irony: what evil shall we find—healing, driving out demons, giving sight? They knew the facts and, intoxicated with pride, would not judge soundly. Delivery becomes the evidence of guilt. That is not law; it is will.

John 18:31

31Pilate said to them, “Take him yourselves and judge him by your own law.” The Jews said to him, “It is not lawful for us to put anyone to death.”

Arminian

Take him yourselves—Pilate would rather not own this. They answer: it is not lawful for us to put anyone to death. Wesley: capital power had been taken that very year; the scepter departed from Judah.

Arminian note: they want a death they will not legally do. The limitation they cite is real politics; the hunger for a cross is still theirs. They will use Rome to finish what their law, on their telling, may not finish.

Reformed

Calvin hears irony in “take him”: this is not how nations or God’s law condemn a man unheard. They reply knowing he mocked; they insist he is the judge, so he must do the office.

Reformed accent: they will not enter a Gentile house, but they will borrow a Gentile sword. “Not lawful” is suddenly scrupulous where blood is concerned—only because they want a particular kind of death, as the next verse will say.

John 18:32

32This was to fulfill the word that Jesus had spoken to show by what kind of death he was going to die.

Arminian

John hears Jesus’ own word about the kind of death—lifted up, not a Jewish stoning (3:14; 12:32–33). Wesley: crucifixion was Roman; without the governor he could not have been crucified.

Shared: the manner of death is not a surprise. Arminian reading: his prediction is true; their politics serve a word he already spoke. Knowing the kind of death, he still came forward (v. 4) and still drinks the cup (v. 11). The fulfillment does not cancel their guilt in handing him over.

Reformed

Calvin: if we read Christ’s death as only men’s caprice, faith is confounded. Look to God’s eternal purpose: by this condemnation our condemnation is blotted out, the Father’s method of reconciling mankind. In each part of the narrative, turn the eyes to God as Author of redemption.

Reformed accent: “kind of death” is appointed lifting-up. Rome’s monopoly on crucifixion is the road he named. The leaders’ “not lawful” is a wheel in a chariot they did not build.

Key contrast Shared: he had said how he would die; this legal deadlock fulfills it. Arminians typically keep the prediction together with the leaders’ real, resistible choice to use Rome. Reformed readers typically lift the eyes to the Father’s purpose in the same facts. Do not flatten into a death God merely foresaw, or into puppets who never willed the delivery.

John 18:33

33So Pilate entered his headquarters again and called Jesus and said to him, “Are you the King of the Jews?”

Arminian

Inside, away from the crowd: Are you the King of the Jews? The political charge has arrived even if they did not quite file it. Pilate goes to the title that could get a man killed.

Arminian pastoral note: Jesus will be asked this for real. Kingship is not a metaphor he dodges; it is a truth he will define. The question is an opening; a governor could still hear.

Reformed

Calvin: John dwells on Pilate’s laborious inquiry whether the accusation was just. Before a seditious crowd nothing could be done but riotously; so he goes in again, intending to acquit, while Christ, to obey the Father, presents himself to be condemned and is sparing in replies.

Reformed reading: a favorable judge is not used as an escape. He considers why he came into the world. The king-question is the one the passion must answer, not the one he must wriggle out of.

John 18:34

34Jesus answered, “Do you say this of your own accord, or did others say it to you about me?”

Arminian

He asks whether the title is Pilate’s own thought or a borrowed charge. The question probes the conscience: are you seeking, or only repeating?

Classical Arminians: even here the Lord addresses a hearer. Pilate is not a prop. Own-accord hearing would be a different path than managing a file. The distinction still matters; he can answer as a man, not only as Rome.

Reformed

The question sorts rumor from conviction. Calvin: the tendency of the answer is to show there is no ground for the accusation of sedition.

Reformed accent: he will confess kingship (v. 37) without letting Pilate’s categories own it. First he unmasks whether this Gentile is asking, or merely echoing accusers. Truth-telling begins by locating the questioner.

John 18:35

35Pilate answered, “Am I a Jew? Your own nation and the chief priests have delivered you over to me. What have you done?”

Arminian

Am I a Jew? Distance as defense. Your nation and priests delivered you; what have you done? He wants a deed, not a theology.

Arminian note: delivery by one’s own people is the wound. Pilate’s shrug (“Am I a Jew?”) tries to stay above the quarrel and still has a man in his headquarters. “What have you done?” is the right judicial question; he will not wait for its full answer.

Reformed

Calvin: Pilate takes the question amiss and angrily locates all the evil in Jesus’ own nation. I sit here as judge; it is not foreigners but your countrymen who accuse you. You raise disturbances; I am reluctantly compelled to bear a part.

Reformed reading: a governor who will not enter the question of truth will reduce the King to a local quarrel. “What have you done?” still assumes the problem is a crime-sheet. The next sentence will relocate the kingdom.

John 18:36

36Jesus answered, “My kingdom is not of this world. If my kingdom were of this world, my servants would have been fighting, that I might not be delivered over to the Jews. But my kingdom is not from the world.”

Arminian

Shared confession: his kingdom is not of this world, not from the world. Wesley: not an external but a spiritual kingdom. Peter’s sword is already the exhibit: if it were of this world, servants would be fighting to prevent delivery. They are not.

Classical Arminians: he is a king, and the kingship is real, and it does not advance by cohort or blade. The origin is not Rome, not the Sanhedrin, not the garden skirmish. Hearers are still summoned into this kingdom by truth (v. 37), not by enlistment in a revolt. Do not hear “not of this world” as “not among us”; hear “not from here” as to source and weapons.

Reformed

Calvin: he acknowledges he is a king and clears the calumny of sedition. No disagreement between his kingdom and political order. If it were earthly it would be frail; being heavenly it is perpetual. It is not said the kingdom is not in this world—it has its seat in the heart—but while it dwells in us it is a stranger to the world’s condition. Who belong? Those renewed by the Spirit, contemplating the heavenly life.

Reformed accent: servants not fighting is evidence of origin, not of weakness. The cup he drinks is how this king takes a people. Godly magistrates may later defend the church in their office; this verse is not, Calvin says, a ban on all use of the sword by princes. The kingdom itself is founded on the Spirit’s doctrine, not on Peter’s steel.

Key contrast Shared: the kingdom is not of this world and is not established by fighting to stop the delivery. Arminians typically stress a spiritual kingship sincerely offered, not a hidden political coup. Reformed readers typically add that those who belong are the Spirit-renewed, and that “not of this world” is origin and nature—not absence from the world. Do not flatten into quietism on the one hand or a merely earthly Christendom on the other.

John 18:37

37Then Pilate said to him, “So you are a king?” Jesus answered, “You say that I am a king. For this purpose I was born and for this purpose I have come into the world—to bear witness to the truth. Everyone who is of the truth listens to my voice.”

Arminian

You say that I am a king. Born, and come into the world, to bear witness to the truth. Wesley: Thou sayest—the truth; human origin named, divine intimated in “I came.” Everyone of the truth—that is, a lover of it—hears my voice: a universal maxim. Every sincere lover of truth will hear him so as to understand and practise what he saith.

Classical Arminians take the listening as sincere hearing. The witness is public; Pilate is truly addressed. “Of the truth” is not a locked census read off a hidden list in this sentence; it is a real description of those who love the truth and therefore listen. The voice can be heard; refusing it (Pilate’s next word) is culpable. Grace enables such hearing; it is not a feint toward people who cannot possibly listen.

Reformed

Calvin: the doctrine of the kingdom is of no ordinary importance, hence so solemn an affirmation. He was born and sent to bear witness to the truth; there is no danger in trusting him. “Everyone who is of the truth listens”: he does not mean they naturally know the truth, but that they are directed by the Spirit of God.

Reformed accent: those of the truth are not native knowers. In this Gospel they are those the Father has given, who hear the Shepherd’s voice (10:26–27; 18:9). The same verse that invites all within earshot to the truth also names a people the Spirit directs. Pilate hears the sounds and does not listen. That failure is of a piece with not being of the truth—not a surprise that breaks the purpose of the coming.

Key contrast Do not flatten v. 37. Shared: he was born and came to bear witness to the truth; everyone who is of the truth listens to his voice. Arminians (Wesley) typically take “of the truth” as sincere love of truth—a universal maxim, real hearing that understands and practices. Reformed readers (Calvin) typically take “of the truth” as those directed by the Spirit, not those who naturally know—in Johannine terms, those the Father has given. Both refuse to make the witness insincere; both refuse to make unbelief a small misunderstanding. The camps split on whether “of the truth” names a sincere, enableable love in the hearer, or a Spirit-wrought belonging that explains who actually listens.

John 18:38

38Pilate said to him, “What is truth?” After he had said this, he went back outside to the Jews and told them, “I find no guilt in him.

Arminian

What is truth? Wesley: a courtier, perhaps meaning what does truth signify—is it worth hazarding your life for? He leaves, to plead Jesus as an innocent but weak man. The question is not a seminar; it is an exit.

Arminian grief: a sincere witness was spoken; a hearer shrugs. “What is truth?” can be asked to evade listening (v. 37). Finding no guilt is right as far as it goes and still not the same as coming to the Light. He can declare innocence and still not be of the truth.

Reformed

Calvin: not so much curiosity as disdain—Pilate thinks himself insulted when treated as destitute of the truth. A common disease: few will confess ignorance; most reject true doctrine. He goes out at once. Wicked men never reject the gospel so spitefully as not to be somewhat moved; there is inward compunction without becoming humble and teachable.

Reformed accent: “What is truth?” is mockery that still feels a wound. Then the judge’s mouth speaks a true verdict—no guilt—while the man himself will not stay inside with the Truth. Innocence declared is not yet faith given.

John 18:39

39But you have a custom that I should release one man for you at the Passover. So do you want me to release to you the King of the Jews?”

Arminian

A custom: one released at Passover. Do you want the King of the Jews? Pilate tries a middle path—perhaps they will take the title as a joke and take the man as a gift.

Arminian note: calling him King here is half-sneer, half-offer. A crowd is asked to choose. The question is real in the street; so will the answer be. Custom cannot baptize the coming exchange.

Reformed

Calvin: Pilate ponders how to save Christ’s life and tries a middle path to allay fury—dismissed as a malefactor, marked with ignominy, perhaps enough. He selects a comparison (Barabbas) to soften their hatred.

Reformed reading: the custom itself, meant to honor the day, is a shameful profanation: acquitting the guilty is abomination (Prov. 17:15). Trying to serve God by invention ends in insult. The King will not be extracted from the cup by a holiday loophole.

John 18:40

40They cried out again, “Not this man, but Barabbas!” Now Barabbas was a robber.

Arminian

Not this man, but Barabbas—a robber (or insurrectionist). The crowd prefers a man of this world’s violence to the king whose servants would not fight. Passover purity that would not enter the praetorium now shouts for a bandit.

Arminian close: the choice is public and guilty. They had light enough—open teaching, no guilt found, a spiritual kingdom on offer. They refuse the voice of truth and take Barabbas. The tragedy is chosen preference, not a missing footnote. The same voice still calls; this scene shows what refusing it looks like in a chorus.

Reformed

Calvin: that they preferred Barabbas did not happen without singular providence. It would have been unbecoming for the Son of God to be rescued by so dishonorable a price. By his death he is thrown into deepest ignominy, crucified between robbers, having taken the sins of all which could not be expiated otherwise; the glory of the resurrection will make the death a splendid triumph.

Reformed close: a robber is released; the Innocent is kept. The cup of v. 11 is filling in public. Their cry does not dethrone the King whose kingdom is not from the world. It exhibits the world from which that kingdom does not come.

Key contrast Shared: they choose Barabbas; Jesus is the rejected king with no guilt in him. Arminians typically stress the crowd’s real, culpable preference against sufficient light. Reformed readers typically stress the same cry as also under providence—the dishonorable path by which the appointed cup is drunk. Do not flatten the shout into either “God made them prefer a robber” without guilt, or into a surprise that escaped the Father’s hand.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the kingdom not of this world, the witness to the truth, and those who listen—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 18:28–40.

Arminian Notable voices

“My kingdom is not of this world—Is not an external, but a spiritual kingdom. Thou sayest—The truth. To this end was I born—Speaking of his human origin: his Divine was above Pilate's comprehension. I came into the world, that I might witness to the truth. Every one that is of the truth—That is, a lover of it, heareth my voice—A universal maxim. Every sincere lover of truth will hear him, so as to understand and practise what he saith.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 18:36–37)

Reformed Notable voices

“By these words he acknowledges that he is a king, but he declares that there is no disagreement between his kingdom and political government or order. I have preached about the kingdom of God; but that is spiritual. We learn also who they are that belong to this kingdom: those who, having been renewed by the Spirit of God, contemplate the heavenly life in holiness and righteousness. Every one that is of the truth heareth my voice. When he says that they are of the truth, he does not mean that they naturally know the truth, but that they are directed by the Spirit of God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 18:36–37)
