Passage

John 18

Verses 12–27 · ESV · Voices

The band arrests and binds the One who just dropped them with a word. He is led first to Annas, then toward Caiaphas—the counselor of expedient death. Peter follows, is brought in, and three times says “I am not.” Between denials Jesus answers openly; an officer strikes him; a rooster crows.

John 18:12

12So the band of soldiers and their captain and the officers of the Jews arrested Jesus and bound him.

Arminian

They arrest and bind the man who just spoke them off their feet. Bonds are real: ropes on the Word made flesh. He allows what he could have refused.

Arminian pastoral note: binding does not cancel the I AM of vv. 5–8. He is not overpowered; he is handed over. The same will that drank the cup now drinks arrest. Disciples will need this: the Lord bound is still Lord.

Reformed

Calvin: it might seem strange that he who laid them prostrate now allows himself to be taken. The miracle was to take away the offense—we should not think he yielded as one overcome—and to prove that in dying he was altogether voluntary.

Reformed confession: the body of the Son of God was bound that our souls might be loosed from the cords of sin and of Satan. The captain’s knots are real; they are not the last word about who holds whom.

John 18:13

13First they led him to Annas, for he was the father-in-law of Caiaphas, who was high priest that year.

Arminian

First Annas, father-in-law of Caiaphas, high priest that year. Wesley: Annas had been high priest; they still deferred to his age and experience, so Christ is brought to him first.

Arminian note: family power and retired office still steer the night. The trial begins in a household, not only in a hall. Jesus is led; he does not bargain for a better venue.

Reformed

Calvin: the other evangelists omit this because little of substance happened at Annas; perhaps convenience of place. “That year” does not mean the office was meant to be annual—Rome’s shuffling of priests had made it so in practice.

Reformed accent: even the route is under providence. Father-in-law, son-in-law, a borrowed title—yet this is the path to the cup already accepted. Human nepotism does not dethrone the hour.

John 18:14

14It was Caiaphas who had advised the Jews that it would be expedient that one man should die for the people.

Arminian

John reminds us of 11:50: expedient that one die for the people. Caiaphas meant politics; God meant more. The counselor of death is still on the stage.

Classical Arminians: a wicked sentence can carry a truth bigger than the speaker. One man dying for the people is the gospel’s shape—and still Caiaphas is guilty of the counsel. Expedience is not innocence.

Reformed

Calvin: God employed the foul mouth of a wicked high priest to utter a prediction, as he once guided Balaam’s tongue against his wish.

Reformed reading: John wants Caiaphas’s logic audible while Jesus is bound. The death for the people is not Caiaphas’s invention that God then hijacked; it is the appointed cup, spoken even through an enemy. Unwitting prophecy does not pardon the prophet.

John 18:15

15Simon Peter followed Jesus, and so did another disciple. Since that disciple was known to the high priest, he entered with Jesus into the courtyard of the high priest,

Arminian

Peter follows; another disciple—known to the high priest—goes in with Jesus. Love is still moving. Following after the sword-rebuke is not nothing.

Arminian pastoral reading: zeal that sinned in the garden is not yet finished with Jesus. Following is a real grace-enabled act. The danger is that following without prayer-watch will become standing by a fire among accusers. Stay close, but not in your own boast.

Reformed

Calvin: some guess the unnamed disciple is John; he doubts a mean fisherman’s intimacy with a proud high priest, and thinks it more probable this “disciple” is a believer not of the Twelve. Godly zeal induced both to follow; yet Christ had already spared Peter from a trial beyond his strength.

Reformed caution: the man so weak would have done better to groan in a dark corner than to walk into a confession he will not make. Following can be earnest and still unwise when it outruns the Lord’s release.

John 18:16

16but Peter stood outside at the door. So the other disciple, who was known to the high priest, went out and spoke to the servant girl who kept watch at the door, and brought Peter in.

Arminian

Peter is outside; a word to the doorkeeper brings him in. Access is a gift from a friend. The threshold is a servant girl.

Arminian note: kindness gets Peter into the courtyard where his mouth will fail. Helping a brother in can still land him in a trial he is not ready to speak through. The door is not neutral; it is the first question’s porch.

Reformed

Admission was not for anyone who chose; the known disciple asked a personal favor. Peter is fetched from the cold into the danger.

Reformed accent: providence and folly braid. He is brought in by friendship into the place where a maid’s voice will undo last supper’s vows. The Lord who kept him from arrest does not keep him from this courtyard; the sifting has another shape.

John 18:17

17The servant girl at the door said to Peter, “You also are not one of this man’s disciples, are you?” He said, “I am not.”

Arminian

A girl at the door, a question that Wesley thinks is without design to hurt him—and Peter says, I am not. Hours ago: I am he. Now the disciple’s mouth is the opposite word.

Classical Arminians: this is a real, culpable denial, not a slip of phrasing. A true follower can refuse the Name under pressure. The fall is his; the earlier keeping (v. 9) was not a charm against speech-sin. Grace still meets cowards, but the “I am not” is a decision, and it will deepen if he warms himself in it.

Reformed

Calvin: at the first step the foolishness of his boasting is exposed. A valiant champion, confounded by a single maid, her voice without threat. All human strength is smoke a breath drives away. This is not quite a full legal brief against Christ, but shrinking from being his disciple amounts to saying he has nothing to do with him.

Reformed gravity: learn not to be brave in any other than the Lord. The I AM of the garden and the I am not of the door belong in one chapter so that no one trusts Peter’s sinew. Indirect denial is still denial.

Key contrast Shared: Peter’s “I am not” is real, shameful, and his. Arminians typically treat it as a true disciple’s resistible, culpable fall—kept from arrest, not kept from lying—and a warning that following can still refuse the Name. Reformed readers typically treat it as the collapse of self-trust: smoke-strength, the Lord’s later look still to come. Do not flatten into “he was never given” or into a harmless social fib.

John 18:18

18Now the servants and officers had made a charcoal fire, because it was cold, and they were standing and warming themselves. Peter also was with them, standing and warming himself.

Arminian

Cold night, charcoal fire, officers and servants. Peter is with them, warming himself. Belonging shifts from the garden’s “these men” to the courtyard’s “them.”

Arminian pastoral warning: comfort among the wrong company hardens a first denial. He is cold and he is afraid; both are human. Staying at the fire without turning is how “I am not” becomes a place to live for an hour. Leave the warmth if it costs the Name.

Reformed

Calvin: how great Peter’s stupidity, that without concern he warms himself with a multitude of wicked men after denying his Master—unless fear of another danger kept him from going out.

Reformed note: John locates him at the fire to connect the denials. The charcoal will appear again at the breakfast of restoration (21:9). For now the fire is fellowship with the arresters. Warmth is not neutrality when you have just said you are not his.

John 18:19

19The high priest then questioned Jesus about his disciples and his teaching.

Arminian

Inside, the high priest questions Jesus about disciples and teaching—while a disciple at the fire is disowning both. The inquiry treats him as a movement-founder and a possible false prophet.

Arminian reading: they want a handle on the school. Jesus will not sell the eleven or recant the public word. The contrast with Peter is already loud: the Lord speaks; the disciple hides.

Reformed

Calvin: he is interrogated as if he had split the church by collecting disciples and corrupted the faith by new doctrine. Having discharged the teacher’s office, he will not enter a new defense, yet he will not abandon the cause of truth.

Reformed accent: the question is already a verdict hunting a pretext. Disciples and teaching are the two things the night wants to criminalize. He will point them back to an open record, not a secret cell.

John 18:20

20Jesus answered him, “I have spoken openly to the world. I have always taught in synagogues and in the temple, where all Jews come together. I have said nothing in secret.

Arminian

Openly, always, in synagogue and temple, nothing in secret. Wesley: manner, time, place; no point of doctrine untaught in public.

Arminian weight: the gospel of this book has been a public witness. Night-courts that pretend they need a private confession are refusing light already given. Hearers in Israel had the teaching; the arrest is not for lack of disclosure.

Reformed

Calvin: not a ban on private exposition under tyranny; he puts down Caiaphas’s insolent malice. “Nothing in secret” refers to the substance—always the same—though the form of teaching varied. He did not keep a second gospel for a clique.

Reformed reading: the world has heard. What is about to be condemned is not a hidden plot but the open word. The secret is on the accusers’ side, not his.

John 18:21

21Why do you ask me? Ask those who have heard me what I said to them; they know what I said.”

Arminian

Why ask me? Ask the hearers; they know. Wesley: why askest thou me—whom thou wilt not believe?

Classical Arminians: he sends the court to the public. Testimony already given is enough to obligate. Unbelief here is not a missing lecture; it is refusal of a word the crowds could repeat. The question is fair process and a rebuke together.

Reformed

He will not play along with a fishing expedition. The hearers know; the high priest knows they know.

Reformed edge: truth that has been spoken in the temple does not become unsaid because night has come. He stands on witness already in the world. The coming blow (v. 22) will answer his legality with a hand.

John 18:22

22When he had said these things, one of the officers standing by struck Jesus with his hand, saying, “Is that how you answer the high priest?”

Arminian

A standing officer strikes him: is that how you answer the high priest? Violence fills the hole where evidence should be. Wesley hears the charge as lack of reverence.

Arminian pastoral note: open speech is treated as insolence. The bound Teacher is hit for asking that witnesses be called. Disciples who warm themselves outside should see what the truth costs in the room they fear.

Reformed

Calvin: see the rage and the discipline of that priesthood—judges like beasts; a single officer dares, in the midst of proceedings, to strike a man not found guilty. No wonder the doctrine is condemned by so barbarous an assembly.

Reformed accent: the slap is the court’s theology. When process fails, the hand preaches. The Lamb receives what the cup included; the officer’s piety (“the high priest”) is a cover for the blow.

John 18:23

23Jesus answered him, “If what I said is wrong, bear witness about the wrong; but if what I said is right, why do you strike me?”

Arminian

If wrong, bear witness; if right, why strike? He does not return the blow. He asks for the lawful thing: testimony, not a fist.

Arminian reading: this is not a contradiction of turning the other cheek. He suffers, refuses revenge, and still names the injustice. Patience can speak. The call to bear witness remains a real call even in a crooked room.

Reformed

Calvin: if I have sinned, accuse me in order; this is not lawful procedure. Christian patience is not always mute: endure, put away revenge, overcome evil with good. A man may expostulate when wronged if the mind is free of rancor and the hand of revenge.

Reformed note: the rightness of his words stands; the strike has no witness behind it. He holds the court to its own forms while walking the appointed cup. The question “why strike?” is truth, not a bid to escape the hour.

John 18:24

24Annas then sent him bound to Caiaphas the high priest.

Arminian

Still bound, he is sent to Caiaphas. Wesley: as implied in v. 13; bound as in v. 12. Annas’s house was not the end.

Arminian note: the night has stages. Being passed along bound is part of the drinking. No speech of his unbinds him; the cup is still the Father’s, not Annas’s to empty.

Reformed

Calvin reads the verb as pluperfect in sense: Annas had sent him—John reminding us the narrative’s center is the high priest’s house, not that we missed a second trial’s transcript.

Reformed accent: the bonds do not come off between fathers-in-law. The expedient counselor of v. 14 now receives the One he already voted to spend. The transfer is administrative; the decree is older.

John 18:25

25Now Simon Peter was standing and warming himself. So they said to him, “You also are not one of his disciples, are you?” He denied it and said, “I am not.”

Arminian

John cuts back to the fire: same stance, same question, same I am not. The second denial is easier than the first. Warmth and company do their work.

Classical Arminians: repetition is not fate; it is habit forming. A disciple can deny twice. The warning of 13:38 is coming true in choices, not in a spell. Get up from the charcoal before the third. Grace still calls; he is still saying no.

Reformed

Calvin: shocking stupidity—after denying, no repentance, but hardening by the very indulgence in the sin. If each had asked in turn, he would have denied a thousand times. Satan hurries wretched men once they are degraded.

Reformed warning: first fault, then habit, then a conscience laid asleep. The other evangelists add cursing and swearing. Do not wait to be “the old Peter” again; the second I am not is already a deeper hole. Only the Lord will stop this slide.

John 18:26

26One of the servants of the high priest, a relative of the man whose ear Peter had cut off, asked, “Did I not see you in the garden with him?”

Arminian

Now it is not a vague maid: a relative of Malchus saw him in the garden. The sword has a family. Recognition is specific.

Arminian note: yesterday’s zeal becomes tonight’s evidence. Violence done “for Jesus” does not protect the Name; it names you to the wrong people. The garden that held the I AM now supplies a witness against Peter’s lie.

Reformed

The ear Peter cut off has a kinsman at the fire. Providence lets the garden follow him indoors.

Reformed accent: the man who would not be known as a disciple is known as a swordsman. Flesh-help (v. 10) cannot be hidden when you also want anonymity. The question is almost mercy—a chance to tell the truth about the garden—and he will not take it.

John 18:27

27Peter again denied it, and at once a rooster crowed.

Arminian

A third denial; at once a rooster crows. The word of 13:38 lands. John does not here record the weeping; he lets the sound hang. Shared with every camp: this is a true disciple’s ruinous hour, and it is not the end of Peter’s story in this Gospel.

Classical Arminians: he fell by his own mouth, not because the Father’s giving in v. 9 was a fiction. Kept from being lost with Judas is not the same as kept from this crowing. The fall is real; restoration will also be real (ch. 21). Do not use this verse to say either that saints cannot fall terribly, or that this fall is the same loss as Judas’s.

Reformed

Calvin: the crowing is mentioned so we know Peter was warned by God at the very time. Luke adds that the crowing itself did not restore him until Christ looked at him. Once a person has begun to fall through Satan’s suggestions, no voice or sign will bring him back until the Lord casts his eyes upon him.

Reformed hope: the rooster is the Lord’s appointed alarm, not luck. Peter’s collapse proves smoke-strength; the look and the later charcoal breakfast will prove keeping. The crowing fulfills Jesus’ word as surely as the arrest fulfilled the cup. He is sifted, not discarded.

Key contrast Shared: three denials, a rooster, a real fall of a real disciple, and (in this Gospel’s arc) a real restoration still to come. Arminians typically hear a warning that the given may stumble grievously by their own refusal—kept from Judas’s loss, not from this sin. Reformed readers typically hear the failure of self-trust and a recovery that waits on the Lord’s look, not on Peter’s remaining nerve. Do not flatten Peter into an unbeliever, or the crowing into a small social embarrassment.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Annas, the open teaching, and Peter’s denials—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 18:12–27.

Arminian Notable voices

“Annas had been high priest before his son-in-law Caiaphas. And though he had for some time resigned that office, yet they paid so much regard to his age and experience, that they brought Christ to Annas first. Art thou also—as well as the others, one of this man's disciples—She does not appear to have asked with any design to hurt him. I spake openly—as to the manner: continually—as to the time: in the synagogue and temple—as to the place. In secret have I said nothing—no point of doctrine which I have not taught in public.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 18:13–20)

Reformed Notable voices

“When he stumbles so shamefully at the first step, the foolishness of his boasting is exposed. He had boasted that he would prove a valiant champion; and now, at the voice of a single maid, and that voice unaccompanied by threatening, he is confounded and throws down his arms. All the strength that appears to be in men is smoke, which a breath immediately drives away. Let us therefore learn not to be brave in any other than the Lord. How shocking the stupidity of Peter, who, after having denied his Master, not only has no feeling of repentance, but hardens himself by the very indulgence he takes in sinning. Immediately the cock crew, that Peter was warned by God at the very time.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 18:17–27)
