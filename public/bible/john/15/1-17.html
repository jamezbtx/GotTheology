Passage

John 15

Verses 1–17 · ESV · Voices

Farewell discourse after Judas has gone: Jesus is the true vine, the Father the vinedresser. Fruitless branches are taken away; fruit-bearing branches are pruned. Already clean by his word, the Eleven must abide in him—apart from him they can do nothing; the non-abiding branch is thrown away and burned. Abide in his love by keeping his commandments; love one another as he has loved, even to laying down life. He calls them friends, not servants. They did not choose him; he chose and appointed them to go and bear fruit that abides.

John 15:1

1“I am the true vine, and my Father is the vinedresser.

Arminian

True vine, as true bread in chapter 6: the genuine plant, not a rival spirituality. Israel was a vineyard in the prophets; Jesus now names himself the vine in whom fruit actually grows. The Father is vinedresser—care, pruning, and removal belong to him, not to self-tending branches.

Classical Arminians start where the image starts: life is in the Son. Nothing here is Pelagian self-fruit. The question the chapter will press is whether those now in him must still abide—and whether a branch can fail to.

Reformed

True vine: barren nature yields nothing until it is in him. Calvin: the vital sap proceeds from Christ alone; men toil in vain looking elsewhere. The Father is first author, planting with his hand; life begins as we take root in the Son.

Reformed hearing already leans toward special grace: this life is not a native universal implant. Shared ground with Arminians is total dependence on the vine. How one is in him, and what v. 6’s fire means, will divide the readings without erasing the image.

John 15:2

2Every branch in me that does not bear fruit he takes away, and every branch that does bear fruit he prunes, that it may bear more fruit.

Arminian

Two treatments from the same Father: fruitless branches taken away; fruitful ones pruned for more fruit. “In me” is Jesus’ own phrase for the taken-away branch—not a later gloss. Wesley: purity and fruitfulness help each other; more fruit is among the noblest rewards of obedience.

Arminian pastoral weight: being “in me” does not make fruit automatic, and pruning is not punishment only. The vinedresser’s knife is real in both directions. Remaining fruitless is not a harmless season; it is the path toward removal.

Reformed

The vinedresser will not leave unfruitful wood on the vine. Calvin’s question is honest: can one engrafted into Christ be without fruit? His answer: many are supposed to be in the vine according to human opinion who have no root in it—as Israel could be called God’s vine by outward profession.

Pruning, for those who do bear, is incessant culture. Adoption once received is not a store of grace we spend down; the Father’s hand must keep applying. Fruit is the vine’s evidence, not a human add-on.

John 15:3

3Already you are clean because of the word that I have spoken to you.

Arminian

Spoken to the Eleven after Judas has gone out (13:30–31). Wesley: purged from the guilt and power of sin by the word, applied by the Spirit. They are not being told they have no need to abide; they are told they already stand in a cleansing the word has done.

Arminian note: cleanliness here is gift-through-word, not a boast. The same men will be commanded to abide. Present cleanness does not cancel future warning; it is why the warning is addressed to friends, not strangers.

Reformed

They have already tasted what the parable describes: planted and, in measure, pruned. Calvin: the means of pruning is doctrine—the outward word, made efficacious as Christ works by the Spirit. He does not mean they are sinless; he holds out their experience so they will learn that continuance of grace is necessary.

Reformed accent: the word that cleansed them is the same word that must remain in them (v. 7). Cleansing is Christ’s work; it is not a finished independence from the vine.

John 15:4

4Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit by itself, unless it abides in the vine, neither can you, unless you abide in me.

Arminian

Command plus impossibility: abide—and a branch cannot fruit from itself. Wesley: addressed to those now pure by living faith, which alone is being in him. Mutual indwelling is promised (“and I in you”), not as a trance but as ongoing union.

Classical Arminians hear a real imperative. If abiding were an automatic property of being chosen, the command would be theater. Fruitlessness is what happens when the branch treats union as a past event instead of a present remaining.

Reformed

The carelessness of the flesh must be aroused. Calvin: Christ means to keep us, as a hen her chickens, lest indifference carry us to destruction. He did not begin salvation to leave it unfinished; the one who abides bears fruit.

Reformed reading does not empty the command. Abiding is the necessary path of those in the vine. The cannot of the detached branch is ontological, not a hint that fruit might be self-grown with a little help.

John 15:5

5I am the vine; you are the branches. Whoever abides in me and I in him, he it is that bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing.

Arminian

Identity, then inability: he the vine, they the branches. The abider is the one who bears much fruit. “Apart from me you can do nothing” is total—no rival holiness, no freelance apostleship. Wesley: this whole passage speaks of no branches but such as are, or at least were once, united to him by living faith.

Classical Arminians confess the nothing without embarrassment. Prevenient grace is not a private vine. Fruit is Christ’s life in remaining people; the command to abide is how that life is not refused. Nothing here lets a branch boast, and nothing here makes the warning of v. 6 a bluff.

Reformed

Calvin takes this as the parable’s conclusion: separate from him, we bear no fruit acceptable to God, for we are unable to do anything good. He presses it against any scheme in which we have a native remainder that, aided by grace, cooperates as a second source. “Without me” means except from me.

Reformed anthropology: we begin to be branches when united to him; before that we are dry wood. Much fruit is the mark of living root, not a surplus we add. The nothing is real; so is the much fruit of those in whom he abides.

Key contrast Both refuse a self-fruiting Christianity: apart from Jesus the branches can do nothing, and much fruit belongs to mutual abiding. Arminians typically take the nothing as true inability met by a real command to remain in living faith—union that can, as v. 6 will say, be forsaken. Reformed readers typically take the nothing as having no power except from him, and much fruit as what living root necessarily yields—the warning still stands, without turning the vine into a partner who supplies only part of the sap.

John 15:6

6If anyone does not abide in me he is thrown away like a branch and withers; and the branches are gathered, thrown into the fire, and burned.

Arminian

The warning is as horticultural as the comfort. Non-abiding is not a slight wilt; it is thrown away, withered, gathered, burned. Wesley: if any one abide not in him by living faith—not by church communion only—he is cast out of the vineyard, the invisible church. Therefore he was in it once.

Classical Arminians will not sand this into a hypothetical aimed only at people who were never united. The same Jesus who will say “I chose you” (v. 16) here says anyone who does not abide is firewood. Abiding is a real remaining they can fail; the fire is not a metaphor for a slightly less fruitful year.

Reformed

Calvin hears punishment of ingratitude and a goad to perseverance. Being cut off, they wither, because strength’s beginning and continuance are from Christ. “Not that it ever happens that any one of the elect is dried up,” he adds—but many hypocrites flourish green for a time and then, when fruit is due, show the opposite of what the Lord demands.

Reformed reading: v. 6 is a real warning, not stage thunder. Outward place in the vine is not the same as root. The Shepherd’s choice is not made uncertain by the fire; the fire is how false profession ends, and how true branches are told not to trifle.

Key contrast Do not flatten the verse. Shared ground: non-abiding branches are thrown away, wither, and are burned; the warning is meant to be heard. Arminians typically take this as possible apostasy of those once united by living faith (Wesley: therefore he was in it once). Reformed readers typically take the burned branches as those who appeared in the vine without root—hypocrites, not elect sheep the Shepherd loses—while still treating the warning as a genuine means by which those he chose are kept abiding.

John 15:7

7If you abide in me, and my words abide in you, ask whatever you wish, and it will be done for you.

Arminian

Abiding is not wordless mysticism. His words remaining in them shape the wishing. Wesley: prayers themselves are a fruit of faith and produce more fruit. Petition is promised, not as a blank check for the detached, but as the conversation of those who stay.

Arminian pastoral note: the condition is real. Ask-whatever sits inside abide-in-me. Prayer that ignores his words is not the prayer this verse underwrites; remaining is how desire is taught to ask what the vine would bear.

Reformed

Words-in-you is how they abide in him. The promise of asking is tied to that remaining, not to raw wanting. Fruit and prayer are not two careers; the branch that draws sap also speaks to the Father.

Reformed comfort: this is not flattery of apostolic whim. Wishes formed by his indwelling word are the wishes he binds himself to do. The same grace that keeps them in the vine teaches the asking.

John 15:8

8By this my Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit and so prove to be my disciples.

Arminian

The Father’s glory is much fruit, and fruit is how they show themselves disciples. Wesley: so shall you be my disciples—worthy of the name. Discipleship is not a title from a past choosing only; it is proven in remaining fruit.

Arminian weight: “prove” is public and moral, not a hint that fruit earns the vine. The Eleven are already his; they must still bear. A fruitless claim to be a disciple is the contradiction v. 2 and v. 6 have already named.

Reformed

Much fruit glorifies the Father and marks discipleship. Proof here is evidence, not a self-made diploma. The vine’s life becomes visible; the vinedresser is honored.

Reformed accent: they do not become disciples by fruit in the sense of meriting union. They prove to be what grace has made them. Still, a profession without fruit has no right to calm; the verse will not baptize barrenness.

John 15:9

9As the Father has loved me, so have I loved you. Abide in my love.

Arminian

The measure of his love for them is the Father’s love for the Son—then the command: abide in that love. Wesley: keep your place in my affection; see that you do not forfeit that invaluable blessing. How needless a caution, he asks, if it were impossible for them not to abide therein?

Classical Arminians take the caution as proof that remaining is not automatic. The love is prior and costly; the abiding is still commanded. They do not deny he loved them first. They deny that “I loved you” makes “abide” a decorative extra.

Reformed

The analogy is staggering: as the Father loved the Son, so the Son has loved them. Abide in that love is the only sane response. Calvin’s larger reading of the chapter treats such exhortations as the way Christ keeps those he has begun, not as evidence that his love is a temporary lease.

Reformed pastoral note: the command is urgent because the flesh is careless, not because the Son’s love is fickle. They are told to remain where they have been placed. Love that large is the atmosphere of discipleship, not a prize for the already fruitful.

John 15:10

10If you keep my commandments, you will abide in my love, just as I have kept my Father’s commandments and abide in his love.

Arminian

Keeping and abiding are tied with an if, patterned on the Son’s own obedience to the Father. Wesley: on these terms, and no other, you remain the objects of my special affection. The parallel is not that they become co-redeemers, but that remaining in love has an obedient shape.

Arminian reading: this is not legalism replacing the vine. It is how abiding looks. A love-talk that will not keep commandments is not the remaining Jesus describes. The if is as real as the love.

Reformed

The Son’s own life is the pattern: he kept the Father’s commandments and abides in the Father’s love. Their keeping is not a ladder into the vine; it is the form of remaining in a love already spoken.

Reformed care: “if you keep” does not turn the gospel into a wage. It names the path of those he loves. As the Son’s obedience does not make the Father’s love uncertain, so their keeping is the fruit and proof of abiding—not a threat that the vine may change its mind on a whim.

John 15:11

11These things I have spoken to you, that my joy may be in you, and that your joy may be full.

Arminian

Purpose of the discourse: his joy in them, their joy full. Wesley: the same joy he feels in loving the Father and keeping his commandments. Warnings about fire and commands to abide are not joy-killers; they are how joy is kept from becoming a slogan.

Arminian pastoral tone: full joy is promised to remaining friends, not to the detached. The chapter that threatens burning also aims at joy. Both are Jesus’ words to the same Eleven.

Reformed

He has spoken these things—vine, knife, fire, and love—so that his joy may be in them. The aim is not anxiety as an end. Fullness of joy sits on the far side of honest warning.

Reformed hearing: joy is Christ’s own, shared, not a mood they manufacture. The God who prunes is not at cross-purposes with the God who gladdens. Remaining in the vine is where that joy lives.

John 15:12

12“This is my commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you.

Arminian

The commandment is singular and social: love one another as he has loved them. Fruit is not only private piety; it is mutual love in the circle he is about to leave. Wesley: your joy will be full if you so love one another.

Arminian note: as-I-have-loved sets the measure before v. 13 names the death. The command is addressable. They can refuse this love (the chapter has already imagined non-abiding). They are not told to feel an affection they cannot; they are told to remain in a love that produces the same toward each other.

Reformed

Love one another is not a second vine. It is the commandment that grows from being loved as the Father loved the Son. The as is the gospel before it is the ethic.

Reformed accent: this love will be impossible apart from him (v. 5). It is commanded because it is the fruit the vinedresser wants, not because the branches have a native store of it. The Eleven’s life together is where the parable becomes visible.

John 15:13

13Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends.

Arminian

The measure of v. 12 is now defined: greater love is life laid down for friends. Wesley notes he speaks here of friends—the soon-to-be-cross is friendship, not a cold decree. They are about to watch this sentence happen.

Arminian gospel: the laying down is for friends he names, and the friendship of v. 14 will be conditional in form (“if you do”). Both can be said without canceling either. The death is gift; remaining in that love still looks like obedience.

Reformed

The greatest love is not advice; it is a death. He is hours from laying his life down. Friends is his word for them in this hour.

Reformed center: this is the love in which they are told to abide. Election debates must not mute the cross as the content of “I have loved you.” The vine will be cut; the sap of their remaining is this laid-down life.

John 15:14

14You are my friends if you do what I command you.

Arminian

Friendship has an if. Wesley: on this condition, not otherwise—a thunderbolt for antinomianism. Who then dares assert that God’s love does not at all depend on man’s works? He is aiming at cheap grace, not at denying that Jesus chose them (v. 16 still stands).

Classical Arminians: the if is as serious as the cross is gracious. They do not become friends by hiring Jesus. They remain in a friendship whose shape is obedience. The verse will not let “I chose you” mean “commandments are optional.”

Reformed

Friends if you do: the relationship has a recognizable obedience. This is not Jesus putting friendship up for bid after the foot-washing. It is how friends of this Lord look.

Reformed caution: Wesley’s anti-antinomian edge is one the Reformed should not dodge. Doing what he commands is not the origin of the choice in v. 16; it is the evidence and path of those he calls friends. Grace that produces no doing is not the grace of this verse.

John 15:15

15No longer do I call you servants, for the servant does not know what his master is doing; but I have called you friends, for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you.

Arminian

Status shift: not servants in the dark, but friends who have been told the Father’s things. Wesley: all things which might be of service to you. Knowledge here is gift, not gnosis for an elite; it is why they can be commanded as friends rather than driven as hirelings.

Arminian note: being told the Father’s will increases responsibility. Light received (as 15:22 will say of the world) removes excuse. Friendship is privilege and obligation together—already chosen, still commanded to love.

Reformed

He has made known what he heard from the Father. Friendship is revelation, not a promotion they earned. Servants wait for orders without the counsel; friends are brought into the mission’s inside.

Reformed accent: the revealing is his act. They know because he told them, as they will fruit because they abide. Dignity does not create independence from the vine; it deepens the debt of grace Calvin will stress at v. 16.

John 15:16

16You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit and that your fruit should abide, so that whatever you ask the Father in my name, he may give it to you.

Arminian

The sentence is first of all historical: you (the Eleven) did not choose me; I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit. Wesley: my apostles—as the sacred history clearly shows; appointed for this end, that you may go and convert sinners; fruit remaining to the world’s end, even to eternity. Arminians do not deny that Jesus chose these men. They deny that this verse, by itself, settles the decretal election of all believers versus others.

Appointment to go and bear fruit is mission-language. Abiding remains a real command they can fail (v. 6). Remaining in his love is still required (vv. 9–10). The choosing is his; the fruit is the aim; the warning is not canceled by the appointment. This is not a slogan that the apostles chose Christ first, nor a proof-text that every later hearer’s salvation was decided in this upper-room sentence.

Reformed

They did not choose him; he chose and appointed, and fruit and abiding fruit flow from that prior choice. Calvin grants the immediate subject: this is not, in the first instance, the ordinary election by which believers are adopted, but the special election by which he set the disciples to preach. Then the a fortiori: if apostleship was free gift, much more is the election by which we become God’s children of free grace. The contrast “I chose you, I was not chosen by you” claims for Christ alone what is usually divided between Christ and man.

Reformed readers do not ignore the command to abide. Fruit and asking in his name are the appointed path of those he chose. Verse 6 remains a real warning; it does not make the Shepherd’s choice uncertain. This is not a slogan that “I chose you” has no apostolic office in view, nor a claim that abiding is optional because choice is his.

Key contrast Treat both sides honestly. Shared: Jesus chose and appointed these disciples; fruit that abides is the aim; remaining in his love is commanded; they did not put themselves in the vine. Arminian reading: first of all the Eleven’s historical election to office and mission (“appointed you that you should go and bear fruit”); the verse does not by itself decide decretal election of all believers versus others; abiding can still be failed (v. 6). Reformed reading: the saying displays Christ’s prior, sovereign choice of his own; fruit and appointment flow from his choosing, not theirs; Calvin himself starts with apostolic office and then argues from it to free grace in salvation; abiding is the necessary path of those he chose, and v. 6 warns without rendering that choice unsure. Do not claim Arminians deny he chose the apostles, or that Reformed readers ignore the command to abide.

John 15:17

17These things I command you, so that you will love one another.

Arminian

The unit closes where v. 12 opened: these things—vine, abide, choose, appoint—are commanded so that they will love one another. Election to office is not an escape from the love-command. Wesley’s thread holds: fruit, remaining, and mutual love belong together.

Arminian close: the Eleven are chosen, appointed, warned, and commanded. None of those verbs is decoration. Love is the purpose of the commands, not a mood that might follow if they feel like remaining.

Reformed

Calvin: this is added so that apostles may know mutual love among ministers is demanded above all, that they may build the church with one accord. Without brotherly intercourse they may raise heaps, but there will be no building.

Reformed close: the God who chose them commands the love that matches the choice. Fruit that abides includes this love. The next paragraph will say the world hates them; this paragraph says they must not hate one another.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the true vine, abiding, and the choosing that appoints fruit—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 15:1–17.

Arminian Notable voices

“Ye—My apostles, have not chosen me, but I have chosen you—as clearly appears from the sacred history: and appointed you, that ye may go and bear fruit—I have chosen and appointed you for this end, that ye may go and convert sinners: and that your fruit may remain—that the fruit of your labours may remain to the end of the world; yea, to eternity.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 15:16)

Reformed Notable voices

“True, the subject now in hand is not the ordinary election of believers, by which they are adopted to be the children of God, but that special election, by which he set apart his disciples to the office of preaching the Gospel. But if it was by free gift, and not by their own merit, that they were chosen to the apostolic office, much more is it certain that the election, by which we become the children of God, is of free grace. That contrast, I chose you, I was not chosen by you, claims, exclusively, for Christ alone what is usually divided between Christ and man; as if he had said, that a man is not moved of his own accord to seek Christ, until he has been sought by him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 15:16)
