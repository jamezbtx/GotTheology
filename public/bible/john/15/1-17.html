Passage

John 15

Verses 1–17 · WEB · Voices

On the night before his death, Jesus describes himself as the true vine and his Father as the vinedresser. Branches that bear no fruit are taken away; fruitful ones are pruned to bear more. The disciples must abide in him, because apart from him they can do nothing. Anyone who does not abide is thrown away like a withered branch and burned. Jesus calls them to abide in his love by keeping his commands, above all to love one another as he loved them. He calls them friends and reminds them that they did not choose him; he chose them and appointed them to bear lasting fruit. Verses 2 and 6 are central to the perseverance debate, and verse 16 to the election debate.

John 15:1

1“I am the true vine, and my Father is the farmer.

Arminian

I am the true vine, and my Father is the vinedresser. Arminian readers see Christ as the true Israel and source of life for all who are joined to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as the true vine replacing unfruitful Israel (Isaiah 5), with the Father tending his people.

John 15:2

2Every branch in me that doesn’t bear fruit, he takes away. Every branch that bears fruit, he prunes, that it may bear more fruit.

Arminian

Every branch in me that does not bear fruit he takes away, and every branch that does bear fruit he prunes. Arminian readers see branches “in me” that are removed as real believers who fail to remain fruitful and are finally cut off.

Reformed

Reformed readers see branches with outward connection to Christ but no living union, such as Judas. Others read “takes away” as “lifts up” for restoration.

Key contrast Both read the warning about fruitless branches seriously. Arminian readers see true branches in Christ that can be removed. Reformed readers see outward branches without living union, or read the verb as lifting up for care.

John 15:3

3You are already pruned clean because of the word which I have spoken to you.

Arminian

You are already clean because of the word I have spoken to you. Arminian readers see the disciples cleansed through receiving Christ’s word.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the cleansing as a completed work of grace through the word.

John 15:4

4Remain in me, and I in you. As the branch can’t bear fruit by itself unless it remains in the vine, so neither can you, unless you remain in me.

Arminian

Abide in me, and I in you. The branch cannot bear fruit by itself. Arminian readers see abiding as a command believers must keep obeying.

Reformed

Reformed readers see abiding as both a command and a gift: those truly in Christ will abide, because he abides in them.

John 15:5

5I am the vine. You are the branches. He who remains in me and I in him bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing.

Arminian

I am the vine; you are the branches. Apart from me you can do nothing. Arminian readers see total dependence on Christ, with believers cooperating by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see complete human inability apart from Christ. All fruit is his work.

John 15:6

6If a man doesn’t remain in me, he is thrown out as a branch and is withered; and they gather them, throw them into the fire, and they are burned.

Arminian

If anyone does not abide in me he is thrown away like a branch and withers, and the branches are gathered and burned. Arminian readers see a real warning that someone once in Christ can cease to abide and be finally lost.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the warning describing those whose connection was never living. It also serves as God’s means to keep true disciples abiding.

Key contrast Both take the warning with full seriousness. Arminian readers see a branch that truly abided and then stopped, ending in loss. Reformed readers see a branch whose connection was only outward, or a warning that God uses to keep his people abiding.

John 15:7

7If you remain in me, and my words remain in you, you will ask whatever you desire, and it will be done for you.

Arminian

If you abide in me and my words abide in you, ask whatever you wish, and it will be done. Arminian readers see prayer answered within an abiding relationship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see prayer shaped by Christ’s words, asking according to his will.

John 15:8

8“In this my Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit; and so you will be my disciples.

Arminian

By this my Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit and so prove to be my disciples. Arminian readers see fruit as evidence of genuine discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see fruit as the evidence that someone truly belongs to Christ.

John 15:9

9Even as the Father has loved me, I also have loved you. Remain in my love.

Arminian

As the Father has loved me, so have I loved you. Abide in my love. Arminian readers see a love believers are called to remain in.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an unchanging love that keeps believers, which they are called to enjoy.

John 15:10

10If you keep my commandments, you will remain in my love, even as I have kept my Father’s commandments and remain in his love.

Arminian

If you keep my commandments, you will abide in my love. Arminian readers see obedience as the condition of remaining in Christ’s love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience as the way believers experience and enjoy his love, not the cause of it.

Key contrast Both join obedience and abiding in love. Arminian readers see keeping Christ’s commands as a condition of remaining in his love. Reformed readers see it as the way believers enjoy a love that rests on Christ, not on their performance.

John 15:11

11I have spoken these things to you, that my joy may remain in you, and that your joy may be made full.

Arminian

These things I have spoken so that my joy may be in you and your joy may be full. Arminian readers see full joy as part of the holy life Wesleyans emphasize.

Reformed

Reformed readers see joy as a fruit of union with Christ.

John 15:12

12“This is my commandment, that you love one another, even as I have loved you.

Arminian

Love one another as I have loved you. Both traditions see Christ’s love as the pattern for believers. Arminian readers see love as the heart of holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see love as the fruit of grace, modeled on Christ.

John 15:13

13Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends.

Arminian

Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends. Arminian readers see Christ’s love extending to all, with his friends as those who receive it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ laying down his life for his friends, which they connect with a definite atonement.

John 15:14

14You are my friends if you do whatever I command you.

Arminian

You are my friends if you do what I command you. Arminian readers see obedience as the mark and condition of friendship with Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience as the evidence of friendship, which Christ established first.

John 15:15

15No longer do I call you servants, for the servant doesn’t know what his lord does. But I have called you friends, for everything that I heard from my Father, I have made known to you.

Arminian

I have called you friends, for all I have heard from my Father I have made known to you. Arminian readers see intimacy with Christ offered to believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see revelation as the privilege of those Christ has chosen.

John 15:16

16You didn’t choose me, but I chose you and appointed you, that you should go and bear fruit, and that your fruit should remain; that whatever you will ask of the Father in my name, he may give it to you.

Arminian

You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit. Arminian readers see this as Jesus’ choice of the Eleven for apostleship and fruitful mission, not a statement about individual election to salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a principle that applies to all believers: Christ’s choice comes before and causes the disciple’s response. The fruit that remains flows from that choice.

Key contrast Both see Christ’s choosing as prior. Arminian readers see it as the choice of the apostles for mission. Reformed readers see it as reflecting God’s sovereign election, which precedes and produces faith and fruit.

John 15:17

17“I command these things to you, that you may love one another.

Arminian

These things I command you, so that you will love one another. Arminian readers see love as the summary of Christ’s commands.

Reformed

Reformed readers see love as the goal and fruit of Christ’s choosing and appointing.

Voices from both camps

The true vine, abiding, and the choosing that appoints fruit: older Arminian and Reformed writers, each in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Ye—My apostles, have not chosen me, but I have chosen you—as clearly appears from the sacred history: and appointed you, that ye may go and bear fruit—I have chosen and appointed you for this end, that ye may go and convert sinners: and that your fruit may remain—that the fruit of your labours may remain to the end of the world; yea, to eternity.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 15:16)

Reformed Notable voices

“True, the subject now in hand is not the ordinary election of believers, by which they are adopted to be the children of God, but that special election, by which he set apart his disciples to the office of preaching the Gospel. But if it was by free gift, and not by their own merit, that they were chosen to the apostolic office, much more is it certain that the election, by which we become the children of God, is of free grace.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 15:16)
