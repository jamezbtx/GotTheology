Passage

John 2

Verses 1–12 · ESV · Voices

At a wedding in Cana the wine runs out. Jesus, whose hour has not yet come, has the stone jars of Jewish purification filled; the water becomes wine. This first sign manifests his glory, and his disciples believe in him. Afterward he goes down to Capernaum with his mother, his brothers, and his disciples for a few days.

John 2:1

1On the third day there was a wedding at Cana in Galilee, and the mother of Jesus was there.

Arminian

The scene is ordinary grace: a village wedding, a mother present, joy about to be tested by lack. Jesus comes where people actually live. Cana is not a distraction from ministry; it is already ministry—the Son among neighbors, ready to be received.

Classical Arminians hear an invitation that can be welcomed. Mary’s being there is family nearness, not a claim to command the Son. The third day simply locates the story; the deeper question is whether the guests—and we—will trust the One who is about to act.

Reformed

The third day and Cana of Galilee fix the first sign in the Father’s time and place. Jesus is not a random guest; providence has brought Him to this feast so that His glory may be manifested when the hour arrives.

Reformed reading: the wedding is honored because the Son is there, not because the feast confers glory on Him. Mary’s presence and the coming sign both sit under the Father’s appointment. What follows will be His work, on His timetable.

John 2:2

2Jesus also was invited to the wedding with his disciples.

Arminian

Wesley’s note is pastoral: Christ does not take away human society, but sanctifies it. The invitation is real. Jesus and His disciples may come; wine at a feast is not refused. Grace meets people in celebration, not only in crisis.

The disciples are already with Him—followers who have begun to trust. Arminian reading: their being invited is a genuine human welcome that Jesus accepts. Signs later will invite firmer faith; already the relationship is personal, not mechanical.

Reformed

The disciples accompany the Lord who will soon manifest His glory. Their place at the table is gift and placement. Calvin later notes the high honor given to marriage: Christ not only deigned to be present but honored it with His first miracle.

Reformed accent: He is not made a guest by human worthiness. The invitation is real, and the power about to be shown is His. Those who sit with Him are being prepared to believe when the sign is given.

John 2:3

3When the wine ran out, the mother of Jesus said to him, “They have no wine.”

Arminian

Need is named, not hidden. Mary brings the lack to Jesus—either asking help or urging a quiet departure, as Wesley allows. Either way, she treats Him as the one to tell. Empty cups can be spoken before the Lord.

Arminian pastoral note: people may ask. The request does not coerce the Son, yet it is a real human turning toward Him. Grace meets want; the coming sign will invite trust in the One who hears.

Reformed

The wine fails under providence so that there is room for the miracle. Mary’s words do not create the hour. The shortage will display His glory; it is not merely a social embarrassment waiting on a clever son.

Reformed comfort: lack is not outside the Father’s care. What runs out becomes the occasion of a sign. The mother’s concern is human and understandable; the remedy will be Christ’s, when He wills.

John 2:4

4And Jesus said to her, “Woman, what does this have to do with me? My hour has not yet come.”

Arminian

“Woman” is the address He will use at the cross; Wesley hears a mild reproof of untimely interposal, not contempt. Jesus is free. His hour is the Father’s, not Mary’s to schedule. Human love may ask; it may not command.

Arminian reading: the hour will come, and He will act. Faith waits on His timing rather than directing “the time or manner in which he shall appear for us,” as Wesley warns. The door is not slammed; it is kept in His hand.

Reformed

The hour belongs to the Father’s appointment. Calvin: Christ understands what it is necessary for Him to do, and yet He will not act at His mother’s suggestion. Divine glory must not be obscured by transferring to Mary what belongs to God.

Reformed accent: delay is not neglect. He holds the time for displaying His power. The sign will arrive when He wishes to make Himself known agreeably to the Father’s command—not when affection presses Him.

John 2:5

5His mother said to the servants, “Do whatever he tells you.”

Arminian

Mary yields. Gathering that He is about to do something extraordinary (Wesley), she points the servants to Jesus, not to herself. “Do whatever he tells you” is the posture of living faith: hear the word and obey it.

The servants’ coming obedience is a genuine human act the sign will meet. Classical Arminians gladly say: grace enables; people still do what He says. The miracle will not bypass the filling of jars.

Reformed

Mary steps back; authority is Christ’s. Calvin hears her disclaiming usurped power and ascribing the whole to the Son. Those who want anything from Christ must depend on Him alone and do whatever He commands.

Reformed reading: the servants will fill jars because He commands. Obedience is the appointed means; the power is His. The word to the servants is already a small manifestation of His lordship.

John 2:6

6Now there were six stone water jars there for the Jewish rites of purification, each holding twenty or thirty gallons.

Arminian

The jars belong to Jewish purification—washings before eating, as Wesley notes. Jesus does not scorn the vessels; He will fill them and surpass them. Old rites become the stage of new wine.

The quantity is generous. Arminian note: abundance fits a grace sincerely given and large enough to see. What was set for cleansing will overflow as gift, inviting the disciples to believe.

Reformed

Number and size serve to prove the miracle, Calvin argues: so many large jars leave little room for smuggling wine. Superstition had multiplied washings beyond the Law’s simplicity; Christ will turn that water into His first sign.

Reformed accent: the vessels are prepared for a display of divine power. Purification pots cannot purify the world; the Son who fills them can manifest glory. The scale of the sign matches the glory it is meant to show.

John 2:7

7Jesus said to the servants, “Fill the jars with water.” And they filled them up to the brim.

Arminian

The command is ordinary; the filling is complete. Servants do what they can—fill with water. Faith acts on the word before anyone tastes wine. Brim-full jars leave no honest claim that wine was mixed in.

Arminian pastoral weight: they obey without yet seeing the sign. The invitation is to trust His word. Human action is real; the transformation will be His. Disciples watching servants fill jars are being drawn toward firmer belief.

Reformed

Calvin: the Lord often acts so that His power is more illustriously displayed by an unexpected result. Filling to the brim removes suspicion. When wine is later drawn from water-filled vessels, the sign stands.

Reformed reading: the servants are instruments; the act is Christ’s. Obedience does not manufacture glory. It is the means God uses so that, when the water has become wine, no one can say the disciples slipped in the gift.

John 2:8

8And he said to them, “Now draw some out and take it to the master of the feast.” So they took it.

Arminian

They draw before tasting. Trust moves on His word a second time. The sign is not a private vision for the in-group; it is sent to the master of the feast to be received in public.

Arminian note: “So they took it” is genuine obedience. The servants who know the water’s origin (v. 9) are already in a position to believe. Signs invite faith in those who do what He says.

Reformed

Christ sends the portion to the master first so the miracle is attested apart from His own circle. Calvin notes the servants’ readiness and the reverence in which Jesus was held. The sign is arranged to manifest glory, not to win a private cheer.

Reformed accent: even the route of the cup is His. Drawing and carrying are means; the change is the Son’s. Glory will be tasted by a man who does not know where it came from.

John 2:9

9When the master of the feast tasted the water now become wine, and did not know where it came from (though the servants who had drawn the water knew), the master of the feast called the bridegroom

Arminian

Knowledge is split: the master tastes excellence without knowing the source; the servants who obeyed know. Those who did what He told them are positioned to recognize the gift. The sign is public and checkable.

Arminian reading: the water now become wine is given to be received. Some taste without tracing it to Jesus; some know and may believe. Glory is manifested so that genuine faith can arise, not so that spectators are forced.

Reformed

The master’s ignorance and the servants’ knowledge together certify the sign. No collusion with the feast’s official is possible. Calvin had already marked this arrangement: the flavor is tried before Christ or the guests drink.

Reformed accent: God attests His Son whether or not the master understands. The wine is Christ’s work. Glory is manifested; faith, where it comes, is the proper fruit of that manifestation.

John 2:10

10and said to him, “Everyone serves the good wine first, and when people have drunk freely, then the poor wine. But you have kept the good wine until now.”

Arminian

The best is kept until now. Jesus gives better than custom. John, Wesley notes, relates the master’s words without endorsing every implication about drinking. The point is the gift: unexpected excellence from the One they invited.

Arminian pastoral note: such kindness draws disciples to trust Him more. The “until now” is grace arriving in time for the feast. The sign invites joy and faith together—not a trick, but a welcome.

Reformed

Abundance of the very best wine is, Calvin says, a trial of sobriety in plenty, not a license. The “until now” marks the hour of His first public proof. What men usually reverse, the Son sets right.

Reformed reading: the good wine is His glory arriving on time. The master speaks better than he knows. The feast’s surprise is a demonstration of divine power, aimed at manifesting the Son.

John 2:11

11This, the first of his signs, Jesus did at Cana in Galilee, and manifested his glory. And his disciples believed in him.

Arminian

The first sign is given so that people may believe. “His disciples believed in him”—Wesley: more steadfastly. Faith already begun is strengthened by what they see. The sign invites genuine trust; it does not coerce. Glory is manifested to be received.

Classical Arminians hear Cana as a pattern: Jesus shows Himself, and disciples really believe. That believing is a living response to enabling grace, capable of growth. The same Gospel will later warn that not every sign-faith is adequate (2:23–25); here the faith of the disciples is treated as true.

Reformed

He manifested His glory: Calvin calls it a striking proof that He is the Son of God. All His miracles are demonstrations of divine power, done to manifest glory when the Father’s time had come. The end of miracles is that glory—not spectacle for its own sake.

The disciples’ believing, Calvin adds, is the confirmation and progress of a faith still small: they begin to devote themselves to Him as Messiah. Faith is gift and growth, the advantage of miracles, not an independent human achievement that causes the glory.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically hear the disciples’ believing as a genuine, grace-enabled response to a sign meant to invite faith. Reformed readings typically hear the same believing as the fruit of a glory Christ manifests on the Father’s timetable; faith is confirmed and given progress, not produced by independent human insight.

John 2:12

12After this he went down to Capernaum, with his mother and his brothers and his disciples, and they stayed there for a few days.

Arminian

After the sign, ordinary life continues—family, brothers, disciples, a few days in Capernaum. Faith that believed at Cana now walks with Him. The miracle does not end discipleship; it sends it on.

Arminian note: they stay with Him. Believing (v. 11) becomes accompanying. The first sign has done its inviting work; the days that follow are the test of a faith that can grow more steadfast still.

Reformed

The descent to Capernaum is the Lord’s movement, not a pause in His office. Those He has begun to confirm in faith stay with Him. Mother, brothers, and disciples are gathered around the One whose glory has been manifested.

Reformed comfort: the sign’s end is not the feast but following the Son. A few days in Capernaum are still His days. The faith given at Cana is kept by remaining with the One who showed His glory.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the wedding at Cana—wine gone, jars filled, water become wine, the first sign manifesting his glory, and the disciples believing—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 2:1–12.

Arminian Notable voices

“Jesus and his disciples were invited to the marriage — Christ does not take away human society, but sanctifies it. Water might have quenched thirst; yet our Lord allows wine; especially at a festival solemnity. Such was his facility in drawing his disciples at first, who were afterward to go through rougher ways. And his disciples believed — More steadfastly.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 2:2, 11)

Reformed Notable voices

“And manifested his glory; that is, because he then gave a striking and illustrious proof, by which it was ascertained that he was the Son of God; for all the miracles which he exhibited to the world were so many demonstrations of his divine power. The proper time for displaying his glory was now come, when he wished to make himself known agreeably to the command of his Father. Hence, also, we learn the end of miracles; for this expression amounts to a declaration that Christ, in order to manifest his glory, performed this miracle.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 2:11)
