Passage

John 2

Verses 13–25 · ESV · Voices

At Passover Jesus goes up to Jerusalem and drives the traders from his Father’s house. The disciples remember the zeal text. Asked for a sign, he answers, “Destroy this temple,” speaking of his body; later they believe the Scripture and his word. Many believe when they see the signs, but Jesus does not entrust himself to them, knowing what is in man.

John 2:13

13The Passover of the Jews was at hand, and Jesus went up to Jerusalem.

Arminian

Jesus goes up to the feast. The pilgrimage is real; He enters Israel’s worship rather than standing apart from it. What He will do in the temple is zeal for the Father, and it calls for a response.

Arminian note: going up is an invitation in motion. Those who meet Him in Jerusalem will see a claim on the house and a word about His body. The feast can be received as a place to trust the Son—or to demand a sign on our terms.

Reformed

The Passover hour is appointed. He goes up not as a tourist of religion but as the Son whose body will be the true temple. The feast is the Father’s stage for both the cleansing and the riddle of three days.

Reformed reading: “went up” is mission, not impulse. The same Lord who manifested glory at Cana now claims the house in Jerusalem. What happens next will serve that glory, including the resurrection He is about to name.

John 2:14

14In the temple he found those who were selling oxen and sheep and pigeons, and the money-changers sitting there.

Arminian

Trade had filled the courts. Wesley: oxen, sheep, and doves used for sacrifice; money-changers serving travelers. Convenience had become commerce in the place of meeting God.

Arminian pastoral weight: Jesus finds what should not be there. The finding is itself a call—repent, make room, stop treating worship as a market. People can still take these things away (v. 16) when He says so.

Reformed

Corruption in the temple does not surprise the Son. He finds what the Father’s house has become. The scene prepares both the whip and the word about His body.

Reformed accent: the house is His Father’s before it is Israel’s facility. Sitting money-changers picture a worship captured by use. The One who will raise the true temple first exposes the false peace of the old courts.

John 2:15

15And making a whip of cords, he drove them all out of the temple, with the sheep and oxen. And he poured out the coins of the money-changers and overturned their tables.

Arminian

Wesley: a scourge of rushes; He drove all out of the court; it does not appear He struck even the animals, much less the men—yet “a terror from God, it is evident, fell upon them.” The action is holy, not a loss of temper.

Arminian reading: zeal can be seen and should awaken conscience. The traders could have yielded. Driving out is judgment that still functions as a call: leave the house of trade and honor the Father.

Reformed

One unarmed man against a multitude succeeds, Calvin notes on this scene, because they were stunned by the hand of God. This is the Son’s authority over the house. The sign they will demand is already among them.

Reformed accent: overturned tables are not a technique; they are a claim. Coins poured out show that merchandise cannot stay where His Father is honored. The power at work is God’s, preparing the word about the temple of His body.

John 2:16

16And he told those who sold the pigeons, “Take these things away; do not make my Father's house a house of trade.”

Arminian

“My Father’s house” is a claim and a plea. Worship is not merchandise. The command can be obeyed: take these things away. The Father’s house is meant for meeting God, not for profit.

Arminian note: He speaks even to those who sold pigeons—perhaps the poorer trade—because the issue is the house, not the size of the stall. People are addressed; they may still hear and clear the courts.

Reformed

He calls God “my Father”—filial right over the house. Trade is not a small fault; it profanes what is His. Authority to cleanse rests in who He is, not in a committee’s permission.

Reformed reading: the word interprets the whip. This is the Son in His Father’s dwelling. The same One will presently speak of another temple—His body—which no trader can occupy and which God will raise.

John 2:17

17His disciples remembered that it was written, “Zeal for your house will consume me.”

Arminian

The disciples remember Scripture (Psalm 69). Zeal is love for the Father’s honor that costs the Son. Remembering the word is how faith grows—connecting what they see with what is written.

Arminian pastoral note: memory here is a living response. They are learning to read Jesus by the Book. Later they will remember again (v. 22) and believe. Zeal that consumes is not a mood; it is the Messiah fulfilling what was written.

Reformed

Psalm 69 is fulfilled in Him. Zeal consumes; it will take Him to death. The disciples’ memory is later faith’s raw material; God writes the Son into the psalm.

Reformed accent: they remembered—yet full believing waits for the resurrection (v. 22). Scripture is God’s prior word; the consuming zeal is Christ’s own. The house He loves will finally be His body, raised by God’s act.

John 2:18

18So the Jews said to him, “What sign do you show us for doing these things?”

Arminian

Wesley: they require a miracle to confirm a miracle. The demand can be honest curiosity or a stall. Jesus will answer, but not on their terms. Faith does not postpone obedience until extra proof is granted.

Arminian reading: a sign is asked; a word is given (v. 19). Hearers may still receive it. The tragedy is not that God hides; it is that people can stand in a cleansed court and still refuse the Son unless He performs on cue.

Reformed

Calvin: they refuse His calling unless a miracle is performed, laying down a law to God. Unbelief demands a sign while standing in the wreckage of His authority. He will give a sign they cannot control: His death and resurrection.

Reformed accent: the request is not humble seeking. It treats the Son as a petitioner in His Father’s house. The sign He names next will be God’s act in raising the temple of His body—not a display fitted to feeble capacity.

John 2:19

19Jesus answered them, “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.”

Arminian

Wesley: “Doubtless pointing, while he spoke, to his body, the temple and habitation of the Godhead.” The riddle is an invitation: look beyond stone to the Son. “I will raise it up” is His promise; hearers may trust or twist it.

Arminian note: the saying is given in public. It can be misunderstood (v. 20) and still later believed (v. 22). Destroying is what they will do; raising is what He pledges. The word seeks faith, even when it comes veiled.

Reformed

Calvin: this is an allegorical reply to people He knew were unworthy of a direct sign; yet He declares a sign of no ordinary value—His resurrection. He claims for Himself the glory of that raising, which Scripture also ascribes to the Father: the two agree in the one divine work.

Reformed reading: “I will raise it up” is God’s act in the Son, not a human reconstruction project. The body is the true temple because the fullness of the Godhead dwells in Him. The sign they demanded is the cross and the third day.

John 2:20

20The Jews then said, “It has taken forty-six years to build this temple, and will you raise it up in three days?”

Arminian

They hear stone, not flesh. Wesley notes Herod’s long reparation still underway. Misunderstanding is possible; the word is still given. Three days will prove what forty-six years of building cannot.

Arminian pastoral weight: unbelief often reduces Jesus to what we can measure. The question is scorn, yet it leaves the saying in the air for later memory. People may still come to believe Scripture and His word (v. 22).

Reformed

Calvin: they receive nothing which does not correspond to the feeble capacity of man. They want a sign of divine power and yet do not wish that it should be divine. Forty-six years is their yardstick; three days is God’s.

Reformed accent: the mistake is not lack of data. It is refusal to receive a Prophet on God’s terms. The temple they count in decades will not be the temple He raises. Resurrection remains God’s act, beyond their arithmetic.

John 2:21

21But he was speaking about the temple of his body.

Arminian

John tells us the meaning so we may believe. The body of Jesus is where God dwells—Wesley’s “habitation of the Godhead.” Destroyed by men, it will be raised. That is good news faith can receive.

Arminian note: the Evangelist does not leave the riddle locked. He interprets so that readers may trust the Son. The temple of His body is offered to faith now, even before the three days have come in the story.

Reformed

Calvin: the body of Christ is justly called a temple. Ours are tabernacles because a soul dwells in them; His was the abode of His Divinity—the eternal majesty dwelling in the flesh as in a sanctuary. In Him the fullness of the Godhead dwells bodily.

Reformed comfort: this temple is unique. Raising it is God’s act and the Son’s glory together. The true house is not Herod’s stone but the crucified and risen body of the Lord.

John 2:22

22When therefore he was raised from the dead, his disciples remembered that he had said this, and they believed the Scripture and the word that Jesus had spoken.

Arminian

Faith can grow after the fact. Wesley: they believed the Scripture and the word—concerning His resurrection. They remembered and believed. The raising confirms what they had heard; believing is a real act, now made steadfast.

Arminian reading: Scripture and Jesus’ word together invite trust. Memory becomes faith. The same disciples who believed at Cana (2:11) believe again here with fuller light. Grace gives the word; people truly believe it.

Reformed

Calvin: the doctrine appeared to vanish into air, then produced fruit in its time. By comparing the Scripture with the word of Christ they were aided in making progress in faith. Belief here follows God’s act: He was raised from the dead.

Reformed accent: resurrection is first, remembering and believing after. Faith is gift that ripens when God raises His Son. The disciples do not create the temple’s rising; they receive the word that the raising has now made plain.

John 2:23

23Now when he was in Jerusalem at the Passover Feast, many believed in his name when they saw the signs that he was doing.

Arminian

Seeing signs, many believed in His name. Wesley: that He was a teacher sent from God. Signs do invite faith—Cana proved that (2:11). Yet John is about to show that some belief can be inadequate. Not every crowd-faith is the disciples’ trust.

Arminian pastoral note: the invitation is real, and so is the warning. People may truly begin and still need a deeper yielding than marvel at miracles. The gospel does not sneer at sign-faith; it calls it onward into a trust Jesus can entrust Himself to.

Reformed

Calvin: a cold faith, only the shadow of faith—convinced perhaps that He was a great Prophet, even Messiah, yet exclusively directed to earthly things, unaccompanied by the true feelings of the heart. It depended solely on miracles and had no root in the Gospel.

Reformed reading: not all who admire power actually believe so as to subject themselves wholly to Christ. True faith depends on the Spirit of regeneration. Signs assist the children of God; they do not amount to actual believing when they only produce amazement.

John 2:24

24But Jesus on his part did not entrust himself to them, because he knew all people

Arminian

Jesus does not commit Himself to inadequate belief. Knowing all, He is not naive. Wesley: learn a wise medium—not universal suspicion, not the easiness that makes us the property of every pretender. Some belief (v. 23) is not yet the trust that receives Him.

Arminian reading: the contrast with 2:11 is pastoral, not cynical. Disciples believed and He received them; this crowd believed at signs and He did not entrust Himself. The difference is the quality of faith—a real human difference people are called to press beyond by grace.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ did not reckon them genuine disciples, but volatile and unsteady. Not all who profess to be His followers are such in His estimation. He knew them all. His judgment, not ours, decides who belongs to Him.

Reformed accent: knowledge of persons is divine. He does not entrust Himself because He knows—not because He is guessing. Faith that is only a shadow is not the faith God gives as a living root. The Son’s refusal is truth-telling, not mood.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically distinguish genuine faith (2:11) from inadequate sign-faith (2:23–24) as a real human difference people are called, by grace, to press beyond. Reformed readings typically locate the difference in Christ’s own knowledge and gift: He does not entrust Himself to a faith He has not made genuine, because He knows all.

John 2:25

25and needed no one to bear witness about man, for he himself knew what was in man.

Arminian

Wesley: He—to whom all things are naked—knew what was in man: a desperately deceitful heart. The diagnosis is honest, not cynical. Knowing what is in us, He still came, still spoke, still offered signs that invite real trust.

Arminian pastoral close: inadequate belief does not surprise Him; it calls us to a deeper faith than signs alone produce. We need no other witness about our hearts than His. The proper response is to believe Him as the disciples did at Cana—steadfastly—not merely to admire what He does.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ perceived everything in men concealed from our view, so that He could on His own authority distinguish among them. He had no need of anyone to inform Him what sort of men they were. He knew their disposition, and justly regarded them as persons who did not belong to Him.

Reformed close: heart-knowledge is the Son’s. Faith is not a surface profession He must take on credit. What is in man is known; new life, when it comes, will be God’s gift, not a verdict we render on ourselves. The chapter ends with His knowledge, not ours.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the temple cleansing, the zeal that consumes, the temple of his body, and the many who believed the signs while Jesus knew what was in man—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 2:13–25.

Arminian Notable voices

“Having made a scourge of rushes — (Which were strewed on the ground,) he drove all out of the temple, (that is, the court of it,) both the sheep and the oxen — Though it does not appear that he struck even them; and much less, any of the men. But a terror from God, it is evident, fell upon them. This temple — Doubtless pointing, while he spoke, to his body, the temple and habitation of the Godhead.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 2:15, 19)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Evangelist rather means, in my opinion, that Christ did not reckon them to be genuine disciples, but despised them as volatile and unsteady. It is a passage which ought to be carefully observed, that not all who profess to be Christ’s followers are such in his estimation. Christ, therefore, who knows the hearts, had no need of any one to inform him what sort of men they were. He knew them to have such a disposition and such feelings, that he justly regarded them as persons who did not belong to him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 2:24–25)
