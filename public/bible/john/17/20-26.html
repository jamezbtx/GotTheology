Passage

John 17

Verses 20–26 · ESV · Voices

The prayer widens: not for these only, but for those who will believe through their word—that they all may be one, as Father and Son are in one another, so that the world may believe the Father sent the Son. He has given them glory for that oneness; perfected unity so the world may know the sending and the love. He desires that those given him be with him and see the glory given because the Father loved him before the foundation of the world. The world does not know the righteous Father; these do. He will keep making the Name known, that the Father’s love may be in them, and he in them.

John 17:20

20“I do not ask for these only, but also for those who will believe in me through their word,

Arminian

Not for these only, but also for those who will believe through their word. Wesley: for them who will believe—in all ages. The circle expands by believing the apostolic word. Future believers are defined by faith through the word, not by a prior hidden list named apart from believing.

Classical Arminians take v. 20 as the reason v. 9 cannot mean Jesus never cares for those still in the world: some who now stand in that world will believe. This petition is still for the church-to-come; it proves the earlier “not for the world” was this-prayer-for-believers, while the world remains the field of the word.

Reformed

Calvin: a wider range, to all disciples of the gospel to the end of the world. If we believe through that doctrine, we are already gathered with the apostles into his protection, so that not one shall perish. A safe harbour. Faith is directed to him; “through their word” means faith springs from hearing—God the Author, men the ministers.

Reformed accent: particular intercession now explicitly includes the rest of the elect not yet converted. “Those who will believe” are not an open maybe; they are those who will, in fact, believe—the given still to be gathered by the apostolic word. None of them perish.

Key contrast Both hear a prayer that outruns the Eleven. Arminians typically take “those who will believe” as the open class of future believers, proof that v. 9’s “not for the world” was this petition for the church, not a refusal of the world’s invitation. Reformed readers typically take the same clause as the rest of the elect who will be brought to faith by that word, gathered into the same particular protection so that none perish.

John 17:21

21that they may all be one, just as you, Father, are in me, and I in you, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that you have sent me.

Arminian

That they all may be one, as the Father is in the Son and the Son in the Father; that they may be in us; so that the world may believe the Father sent the Son. Wesley: similitude, not sameness or equality. “That the world may believe—Here Christ prays for the world.” Sum of the prayer: receive me into glory; let the apostles share; and all other believers; and let all the world believe.

Arminian weight: unity is missionary. The oneness of those who have believed is aimed at a world still invited to believe. Do not flatten v. 9 into a denial of this petition. The world is not the church; it is still addressed, still capable of the faith v. 20 names.

Reformed

Unity is the end of our happiness; the ruin of the race is alienation and scattering. Restoration is being one body. Calvin cautions: “one with the Father” here is mediatorial and headship language, not a detached speculation on hidden deity. The power of that unity must be diffused through the whole body of believers.

Reformed accent on “the world may believe”: some take “world” as elect still dispersed; Calvin refuses, because world in this chapter denotes the reprobate. “Believe” is used for perceiving—unbelievers may be convinced of Christ’s glory yet, believing, not believe; the conviction does not penetrate the heart. The given are one; the world may see, and still not be given.

Key contrast Both want visible unity that witnesses to the sending of the Son. Arminians typically take “that the world may believe” as Jesus actually praying for the world to come to faith. Reformed readers (with Calvin) typically keep “world” in this chapter as those not given, so the world’s “believing” is at most an outward perceiving of Christ’s glory, not the inward faith of the elect. Do not flatten v. 21 into v. 9, or v. 9 into v. 21.

John 17:22

22The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one even as we are one,

Arminian

The glory given him he has given them, that they may be one as the Father and Son are one. Wesley: the glory of the only begotten shines in all the sons of God. How great is the majesty of Christians. Participated glory is for unity, not for private display.

Shared high Christology, Arminian voice: this is not equality of essence (Wesley already guarded v. 21). It is the Son’s given glory shared with believers so that the world has something to see (v. 21, 23). Oneness has a source: his glory, not our talent for getting along.

Reformed

Christ had nothing that belonged peculiarly to himself; he was rich in order to enrich believers. Our happiness is the restored image of God. The likeness of the Father’s glory is engraved even on his human nature, to form his members to that resemblance. No one should be reckoned a disciple unless that glory is impressed on him as with a seal.

Reformed reading: the given glory is the bond of the given people. “Even as we are one” is the pattern; the gift is from the Son who already had glory with the Father before the world existed (v. 5). Unity is glory shared, not a committee result.

John 17:23

23I in them and you in me, that they may become perfectly one, so that the world may know that you sent me and loved them even as you loved me.

Arminian

I in them, you in me: perfected oneness, so that the world may know the sending and the love. Wesley paired “believe” (v. 21) and “know” (v. 23)—the world is the intended audience of the church’s unity. Loved as the Father loved the Son: staggering, and held out as what the world should come to recognize.

Arminian pastoral note: this is the second time the world is named as the aim of oneness. The given are in the Son; the world is not thereby written off. Knowing that the Father sent the Son, and that he loves believers as he loves the Son, is still a true errand toward the world.

Reformed

Fullness concealed in God is manifested in Christ and imparted to his people, as water from a fountain. Calvin: the world is compelled to feel, whether it will or not, the love of God toward believers when the Spirit sends forth rays of holiness. “As thou hast loved me” means because: the title Well-beloved belongs to Christ alone, and that love is extended to the members; he loves none but in Christ.

Reformed accent: “the world may know” does not convert the world into the given. It is the public pressure of holiness on those not given. The love with which the Father loves the church is the love with which he loves the Son—particular, in Christ, before we were born, and now manifested.

John 17:24

24Father, I desire that they also, whom you have given me, may be with me where I am, to see my glory that you have given me because you loved me before the foundation of the world.

Arminian

He desires that those given him be with him and see the glory given because the Father loved him before the foundation of the world. Wesley: he returns to the apostles; “I will” as a Son with a right to be heard; beholding that glory is heaven’s happiness. Pre-existent love and glory are shared high Christology, not a Reformed exclusive.

Arminian reading of “given”: still those who believe—the apostles, and by v. 20 those who will believe through their word. The hope of seeing his glory is the believers’ hope. Before-the-foundation love is the Father’s love of the Son, the ground of the life offered to faith—not a roll-call that makes believing a mere symptom.

Reformed

“I will” is desire, prayer not command. Calvin: he will not be satisfied until they are received into heaven; they now see glory as through small chinks, and he wants the full brightness. “For thou lovedst me” fits the Mediator: the Father loved him before creation that he might be the person in whom the Father would love his elect.

Reformed accent: particular eschatology of those given (vv. 2, 6, 9). They will be with him. The glory they see is the glory of v. 5, loved into the Son before the world was. Election’s end is not a chart; it is this sight, this presence, this beloved Son.

Key contrast Both confess the Son’s pre-temporal glory and the Father’s love before the foundation of the world, and both hear a real desire that the given be with him. Arminians typically keep “those given” as believers (the apostles and those who will believe, v. 20) promised that sight. Reformed readers typically hear the eschatological gathering of the elect, loved in the Son whom the Father loved before creation. Shared Christology; unflattened “given.”

John 17:25

25O righteous Father, even though the world does not know you, I know you, and these know that you have sent me.

Arminian

Righteous Father: the world does not know him; the Son does; these know the sending. Wesley: the admission of believers to God through Christ flows even from God’s justice. The split is knowledge of the Sent One—believers versus the unknowing world.

Arminian care: the world that does not know is the same world of vv. 21 and 23 that may believe and know. Present ignorance is real and culpable; it is not a decree that the world must stay ignorant. “These” are those who have received the word (v. 8) and those who will (v. 20).

Reformed

He compares the disciples with the world to show the Father’s favor more fully. Those who know God, whom the whole world rejects, are rightly distinguished. “Righteous Father” defies the world’s malice: proud rejection takes nothing from him. Faith must be founded on God so that, though the whole world oppose, it does not fail.

Reformed weight: two steps—the Son knows the Father; the disciples know he was sent. Given-versus-world is knowledge granted, not a temporary misunderstanding. The world does not know; these do—because they were given, and the Name was efficaciously made known.

John 17:26

26I made known to them your name, and I will continue to make it known, that the love with which you have loved me may be in them, and I in them.”

Arminian

He made the Name known and will continue to make it known, that the Father’s love for the Son may be in them, and he in them. Wesley: the new, best name of love—that the Father and his love, and the Son and his love, may be in them, that they may love him with that love.

Arminian close: ongoing making-known is the church’s life in the world. Love is meant to dwell in believers, and through their unity (vv. 21–23) to be seen. The prayer that began with pre-existent glory ends with the Son in his people—high Christology at both ends, and a world still in view of that love.

Reformed

He taught efficaciously, not by voice alone but by the Spirit’s secret revelation. Calvin: their faith was then weak, so he promises greater progress and more abundant grace. The only way we are included in that love is that Christ dwells in us. Apart from him we are hated; the Father begins to love us as children when we are united to the beloved Son’s body.

Reformed close: he was loved on our account that we might be partakers of the same love forever. The Father cannot look on his Son without seeing the whole body; if we wish to be beheld in him, we must be actually his members. The given end in his indwelling, not in a bare decision.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the prayer for future believers, the world’s believing, and the glory loved before the foundation of the world—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 17:20–26.

Arminian Notable voices

“For them who will believe—In all ages. That the world may believe—Here Christ prays for the world. Observe the sum of his whole prayer: Receive me into thy own and my glory; Let my apostles share therein; And all other believers; And let all the world believe. The glory which thou hast given me, I have given them—The glory of the only begotten shines in all the sons of God. How great is the majesty of Christians.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 17:20–26, especially 17:20–22)

Reformed Notable voices

“He now gives a wider range to his prayer; he extends it to all the disciples of the Gospel, so long as there shall be any of them to the end of the world. If we believe in Christ through the doctrine of the Gospel, we ought to entertain no doubt that we are already gathered with the apostles into his faithful protection, so that not one of us shall perish. Some explain the word world to mean the elect, who, at that time, were still dispersed; but since the word world, throughout the whole of this chapter, denotes the reprobate, I am more inclined to adopt a different opinion. With such a love did the Father love him before the creation of the world, that he might be the person in whom the Father would love his elect.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 17:20–26, especially 17:20–21, 24)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
