Passage

John 17

Verses 6–19 · ESV · Voices

Jesus turns from himself to those the Father gave him out of the world. He manifested the Name; they kept the word, received the words, and believed the sending. He prays for them—not, in this petition, for the world. He asks that they be kept in the Name, made one, guarded (none lost except the son of destruction), given his joy, kept from the evil one, and sanctified in the truth. As the Father sent him, he sends them; for their sake he consecrates himself.

John 17:6

6“I have manifested your name to the people whom you gave me out of the world. Yours they were, and you gave them to me, and they have kept your word.

Arminian

The Name is manifested to people given him out of the world. Wesley heard the apostles here: they were the Father’s by creation and Abrahamic descent; given by giving them faith in what Jesus had spoken. They have kept the word—the given are identified as keepers, not as a roll that never had to believe.

Classical Arminians take “out of the world” as real separation by faith, not as proof the rest of the world is unprayed-for forever. Those given are believers who received what was manifested. The world remains the field from which they came—and, later, the field still invited to believe.

Reformed

There was no lack of diligence to call all; among the elect only was the labor efficacious. Preaching of the Name was common; inward teaching of the Spirit was not. Calvin ascribes the difference to election: they were given to him. They always belonged to God; he distinguished them from the reprobate by pure grace, then committed them to the Son’s guardianship.

Reformed accent: keeping the word is the third step—election, then gift into Christ’s keeping, then faith taking root. The word flows out to the reprobate; it takes root in the elect. “Yours they were” is eternity before believing, not believing read backward.

Key contrast Both hear a people given to the Son out of the world who kept the word. Arminians typically take the giving as the Father’s granting of faith to those who received Jesus’ words. Reformed readers typically take the giving as eternal election unto the Son, of which faith and keeping the word are effects. Neither side should be talked into the other’s “given.”

John 17:7

7Now they know that everything that you have given me is from you.

Arminian

They now know that everything given the Son is from the Father—and so, Wesley said, right and true. Knowledge here is the disciples’ recognition, following the keeping of the word. The given are knowers, not a silent list.

Arminian pastoral note: this knowing is the fruit of manifestation received. It is not a boast that they climbed into heaven; it is confession that the Son’s words and works are the Father’s. Such knowledge is how believers are marked in this prayer.

Reformed

Calvin locates the chief part of faith here: believing in Christ so that faith does not rest in the flesh but perceives his divine power. Believers feel that all they possess is heavenly. If we do not perceive God in Christ, we remain in hesitation.

Reformed reading: this knowledge is not a native insight. It is the elect’s recognition that the Son’s entire endowment is from the Father—the same giving that placed them in his hand.

John 17:8

8For I have given them the words that you gave me, and they have received them and have come to know in truth that I came from you; and they have believed that you sent me.

Arminian

He gave them the Father’s words; they received them; they know in truth he came from the Father; they have believed the sending. Wesley: they received by faith. Verse 8 is the identifying mark of the given: receivers and believers, not a hidden company irrespective of the word.

Classical Arminians rest on the verbs. Received, known, believed—these are the human acts grace enables and the gospel actually seeks. Those given in this prayer are those of whom this verse is true; that is why the next verse’s “not for the world” is a petition for the church, not a slammed door on invitation.

Reformed

The words are the Father’s; Christ is the faithful witness, so their faith is founded on God’s truth. Calvin: the receiving arose from his having efficaciously manifested the Name by the Spirit. He pairs know and believe: nothing of God is known aright except by faith, and faith has such certainty it is justly called knowledge.

Reformed accent: reception is not a native power deciding to agree. It is the Spirit’s effectual work in those given. They believed because they were given so as to believe; the verbs describe the fruit of the gift, not the cause of it.

Key contrast Both glory in received words and true faith in the Sent One. Arminians typically treat v. 8 as the definition of the given: believers who received the word. Reformed readers typically treat receiving as the fruit of being given, wrought by the Spirit in the elect. Do not flatten “received” into either Pelagian self-start or a faith that never actually believes.

John 17:9

9I am praying for them. I am not praying for the world but for those whom you have given me, for they are yours.

Arminian

He is praying for them—not, in this petition, for the world, but for those given, who are the Father’s. Wesley: not in these petitions, which are adapted to believers only. He prays for the world at vv. 21 and 23, that they may believe. This no more proves he never prays for the world than praying for the apostles in vv. 6–19 proves he does not pray for future believers in v. 20.

Arminian pastoral weight: “not for the world” means this intercession is for the church. The world is still invited to believe. Particular petitions for saints do not cancel the wider mercy of the gospel, any more than praying for your children means the neighbors are beyond hope.

Reformed

He asks what agrees with the Father’s will: he pleads for those the Father himself loves. Calvin: he has no solicitude but about his own flock received from the Father. They are given so as to believe; faith flows from the giving; election precedes faith in order and time. Those God wishes saved out of the world are elected by free grace.

Reformed accent: particular intercession. If we want the Son to plead for our salvation, we must believe election—not as an excuse for indolence, but as the ground of this very prayer. The world, in this verse, is distinguished from those given, not invited in the same petition.

Key contrast Both refuse to pretend v. 9 does not distinguish. Arminians typically limit “I am not praying for the world” to this churchly petition, insisting Jesus still prays that the world believe (vv. 21, 23) and that the world remains invited. Reformed readers typically hear particular intercession for the elect given by the Father, the world being those not so given. Neither reading should be talked into the other.

John 17:10

10All mine are yours, and yours are mine, and I am glorified in them.

Arminian

All mine are yours, and yours mine: Wesley called these expressions too grand for any mere creature, implying that divine nature, perfections, and operations are the common property of Father and Son. That shared deity is the ground of their property in the persons given to the Mediator. He is glorified in them as they believe.

Shared high Christology, Arminian voice: the church does not inflate Jesus; he speaks as only the Son can. Being glorified in believers is not a threat to the Father’s honor. It is the fruit of words received (v. 8) in people who are now his.

Reformed

The Father will hear, because the Son pleads for none but those the Father acknowledges as his; nothing is separated between them. The Son cares for the elect because they are the Father’s. We must not seek salvation anywhere but in Christ—and we shall not be satisfied with Christ if we do not know that we possess God in him.

Reformed consolation: he will not cease to care for our salvation, since he is glorified in us. The unity of Father and Son in this verse is the believers’ security, not a puzzle for later councils only.

John 17:11

11And I am no longer in the world, but they are in the world, and I am coming to you. Holy Father, keep them in your name, which you have given me, that they may be one, even as we are one.

Arminian

He is leaving; they remain in the world; he asks the Holy Father to keep them in the Name, that they may be one as the Father and Son are one. Wesley: keep through power, mercy, wisdom; one with us and with each other—one body, separate from the world; “as we are” by resemblance, not equality.

Arminian note on unity: oneness is real, analogical, and prayed for because the church in the world needs keeping. Shared with the Reformed: the pattern is the Father–Son unity, not a club’s mood. The world is the place they stay, not the identity they keep.

Reformed

So long as he dwelt with them he cherished them; now he commits them to the Father’s hands. Calvin: those the Heavenly Father has decreed to keep, he brings together in a holy unity of faith and of the Spirit. “As we are”—unity that bears the image of Father and Christ as wax takes the seal.

Reformed accent: bodily absence does not worsen their condition if the Father’s guardianship takes them up. Keeping in the Name is preservation of those given, and unity is the way that keeping looks. He is one with the Father as Head and Mediator, not only as a later metaphysical thesis.

John 17:12

12While I was with them, I kept them in your name, which you have given me. I have guarded them, and not one of them has been lost except the son of destruction, that the Scripture might be fulfilled.

Arminian

He kept and guarded them in the Name; not one lost except the son of destruction, that Scripture might be fulfilled. Wesley: so one even of them whom God had given him is lost—so far was even that decree from being unchangeable. “Son of perdition” means one who deservedly perishes.

Arminian pastoral warning: Judas is not a footnote that leaves perseverance automatic. Office among the given is not the same as indefectible standing. Christ’s guarding is faithful; it does not turn apostasy into an illusion or make the lost man’s sin a puppet-show.

Reformed

Judas is excepted, Calvin says, not as a lost elect but as one whose office gave him the appearance of the flock. “Son of perdition” means his ruin was known to God long before; a man devoted to destruction. Eternal election is not overturned by his damnation. Scripture is fulfilled; the prediction did not force the revolt.

Reformed accent: particular preservation. Of those truly given, none is lost. The exception is grammatical accommodation to ordinary opinion of Judas’s place—not a hole in the Father’s gift. The Son’s guarding of his own will not fail.

Key contrast Both take Judas’s loss with full moral weight and the fulfillment of Scripture. Arminians typically hear that one of those given was lost, so the giving is not an unchangeable decree of perseverance. Reformed readers typically deny Judas was ever of the true given flock—he had the office’s appearance; the Son lost none of those the Father truly gave him.

John 17:13

13But now I am coming to you, and these things I speak in the world, that they may have my joy fulfilled in themselves.

Arminian

He is coming to the Father, and he speaks these things in the world so that they may have his joy fulfilled in themselves. Wesley: “in the world” means before he leaves it; the joy is the joy he feels at going to the Father.

Arminian pastoral note: the prayer is spoken aloud for their sake. Joy is not a private mystical extra; it is the Son’s own gladness toward the Father, meant to be fulfilled in the believers he is about to leave in a hating world.

Reformed

He prays in their hearing not because he needs words, but to remove their doubt. Calvin: his joy—he is its Author, Cause, and Pledge. In us there is alarm and uneasiness; in Christ alone peace and joy. Their salvation was already placed in the Father’s hands.

Reformed consolation: the spoken prayer is a remedy for anxiety, not a sign that the outcome is in doubt. Fulfilled joy is Christ’s gift to those he kept, not a mood they must manufacture after he goes.

John 17:14

14I have given them your word, and the world has hated them because they are not of the world, just as I am not of the world.

Arminian

He gave them the Father’s word; the world hated them because they are not of the world, as he is not. The line is word-reception and allegiance. “Not of the world” describes people who received the words (v. 8), not a metaphysical caste created irrespective of faith.

Arminian care: the world that hates is still the world of vv. 21 and 23, invited to believe. Hatred does not rewrite the invitation into a feint. They are distinct from the world because of the word they were given—and they are sent back into that same world (v. 18).

Reformed

He pleads their need: the world’s hatred. The cause is that they embraced the word the world cannot receive. Calvin: they are not of the world because he regenerates them by his Spirit and separates them. God will not suffer his sheep to wander among wolves without showing himself their shepherd.

Reformed accent: given-versus-world is not mood. It is new birth and belonging. The word creates a people the world hates; that hatred is evidence they are Christ’s, and a reason the Father should keep them.

John 17:15

15I do not ask that you take them out of the world, but that you keep them from the evil one.

Arminian

Not taken out—not yet—but kept from the evil one who reigns in the world (Wesley). Remaining is the point: the church’s place is still the world that is to be invited. Protection is requested because the danger is real and resistible only by the Father’s keeping.

Arminian pastoral note: holiness is not evacuation. They will be sent (v. 18) into the same world he is not, in this petition, praying for as he prays for them. Kept from the evil one, they can still speak so that some of that world believe.

Reformed

Safety is not ease. Calvin: they continue safe through God’s assistance in the midst of dangers. He does not wish them slothful; he delivers them from evil so they are not overwhelmed. They are to fight; they are not to be mortally wounded.

Reformed accent: the prayer teaches us not to ask for immediate rest, but for a certain issue from the contest. Preservation (already sounded in v. 12) here means being kept from the evil one while still in the world—the given remain, the world remains hostile, the Father keeps.

John 17:16

16They are not of the world, just as I am not of the world.

Arminian

The identity is repeated: not of the world, as he is not. Likeness to Jesus, not fusion of essence. Wesley’s “as we are” logic from v. 11 holds: resemblance, not equality with the Son’s unique unworldliness.

Arminian note: this is why v. 9’s petition fits believers. They share his distinction from the world by the word they received. The world is still the place they inhabit (v. 15) and the audience of their sending (v. 18)—hated, not uninvited.

Reformed

Calvin repeats the plea so the Father may be the more disposed to help: the world hates them, and the hatred does not arise from their fault but because the world hates God and Christ.

Reformed weight: “just as I am not” is union-language for the given flock. Their unworldliness is derivative of his. The distinction of vv. 6, 9, and 14 is not rhetorical; it is the same line the Shepherd draws around those the Father gave him.

John 17:17

17Sanctify them in the truth; your word is truth.

Arminian

Sanctify them in the truth; the Father’s word is truth. Wesley: consecrate them by the Spirit’s anointing to their office, and perfect them in holiness by means of the word. Sanctification is real holiness, by a true word, for people about to be sent.

Shared ground, Arminian voice: no fanatic holiness that neglects the word, and no chatter about truth that ignores Scripture. The church is made holy as it is true—set apart by the gospel it received (v. 8) and will speak (v. 20).

Reformed

Sanctification is God renewing by his Spirit, confirming the grace of renewal to the end. Calvin: consecrate them entirely to himself; the means is the truth, and the truth is not found anywhere but in the word—the doctrine of the gospel. Outward preaching does not of itself accomplish this; the reprobate profane it. Christ speaks of the elect whom the Spirit efficaciously regenerates by the word.

Shared ground, Reformed voice: sanctify-in-truth is not instant on the first day. Progress runs the whole course of life until the garment of flesh is taken away. The given are made holy by the same word that marked them off from the world.

John 17:18

18As you sent me into the world, so I have sent them into the world.

Arminian

As the Father sent the Son into the world, so the Son sends them into the world—the same world he is not, in v. 9, praying for in that petition. Mission implies the world is addressable. They are not of it (vv. 14, 16); they are sent into it.

Arminian pastoral weight: particular prayer for the church does not cancel the errand to the world. The Sent One makes senders. If the world were a write-off, this verse would be theater. It is not.

Reformed

The calling of Christ and of the apostles is the same calling. Calvin: he appoints them to an office he has held by the Father’s command; therefore they must be furnished with the Spirit’s power for so weighty a charge.

Reformed reading: sent into a hostile world, not withdrawn from it. The given go as the Son went—under authority, into enmity, with a word that will gather the rest of those the Father is giving. Sending does not turn v. 9 into a universal intercession.

John 17:19

19And for their sake I consecrate myself, that they also may be sanctified in truth.

Arminian

For their sake he consecrates himself, that they may be sanctified in truth. Wesley: he devotes himself as a victim to be sacrificed. The self-consecration of the Son is for the believers he is sending; the truth remains the means of their holiness.

Arminian close of the range: high priestly offering and holy people belong together. He sets himself apart so that those who received the word may be set apart in it. Shared with the Reformed: no sanctification of the church that bypasses this consecrated Son.

Reformed

Calvin: our sanctification flows from his consecrating himself; as the blessing on the firstfruits is spread over the harvest, so the Spirit cleanses us by the holiness of Christ. Not by imputation only—he is made to us righteousness and also sanctification. We are presented in his person and renewed by his Spirit.

Reformed close: the highest illustration is the sacrifice of death, the true High Priest consecrating temple, altar, vessels, and people. For their sake—the given—he sanctifies himself, that they may be sanctified in truth.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on those given out of the world, and on the prayer that is—and is not—for the world—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 17:6–19.

Arminian Notable voices

“I pray not for the world—Not in these petitions, which are adapted to the state of believers only. (He prays for the world at John 17:21, 23, that they may believe—That they may know God hath sent him.) This no more proves that our Lord did not pray for the world, both before and afterward, than his praying for the apostles alone, John 17:6–19, proves that he did not pray for them also which shall believe through their word, John 17:20. They were thine—By creation, and by descent from Abraham. And thou hast given them me—By giving them faith in what I have spoken.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 17:6–19, especially 17:6, 9)

Reformed Notable voices

“He openly declares that he does not pray for the world, because he has no solicitude but about his own flock, which he received from the hand of the Father. Christ expressly declares that they who are given to him belong to the Father; and it is certain that they are given so as to believe, and that faith flows from this act of giving. If the origin of faith is this act of giving, and if election comes before it in order and time, what remains but that we acknowledge that those whom God wishes to be saved out of the world are elected by free grace? Now since Christ prays for the elect only, it is necessary for us to believe the doctrine of election, if we wish that he should plead with the Father for our salvation.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 17:6–19, especially 17:9)
