Passage

John 17

Verses 1–5 · ESV · Voices

After the farewell words, Jesus lifts his eyes to heaven. The hour has come: he asks the Father to glorify the Son so the Son may glorify the Father. Authority over all flesh is for giving eternal life to those the Father has given him. Eternal life is to know the only true God and Jesus Christ the Sent One. He has finished the given work on earth and asks to be glorified in the Father’s presence with the glory he had before the world existed.

John 17:1

1When Jesus had spoken these words, he lifted up his eyes to heaven, and said, “Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you,

Arminian

The farewell teaching becomes prayer. “Father”—Wesley heard the only-begotten’s own simplicity—and the appointed hour. Glorify your Son so that the Son may glorify you: the cross-hour is mutual honor, not a private transaction hidden from the world he will still name.

Arminian pastoral note: the Son who prays is already the Revealer. This opening petition is for his glorification in the work of life-giving; the prayer will soon include believers and, at last, the world’s believing. High Christology first: only this Son can ask this of this Father.

Reformed

Having spoken, he seals the words with prayer. The hour is the Father’s appointed hour, not men’s timetable. Father and Son glorify one another: the death that looks least glorious is, Calvin said, the hidden triumph of atonement, reconciliation, and Satan’s defeat.

Reformed framing: doctrine has no power unless efficacy comes from above. The Mediator prays according to the office given him. What is about to happen is decreed, and still he asks—because promises are meant to become petitions.

John 17:2

2since you have given him authority over all flesh, to give eternal life to all whom you have given him.

Arminian

Authority over all flesh answers “glorify your Son”; giving eternal life answers “that the Son may glorify you.” Wesley took “all whom you have given him” as all believers—and as clear proof the sacrifice was designed to avail for all: as the Father gave him power over all flesh, so he gave himself a ransom for all.

Classical Arminians refuse to shrink “all flesh.” The given are those who believe; the Son’s jurisdiction is as wide as the humanity he rules. Eternal life is his to give, and the giving is not a feint over a secretly tiny field.

Reformed

Christ is not Governor in order to bestow life on all without distinction. Calvin limits this grace to those given him: only the elect belong to the peculiar flock he shepherds. The kingdom extends to all men; it brings salvation to none but the elect who follow the Shepherd’s voice.

Reformed accent: authority over all flesh is real; the saving gift of life is particular. “Those given” here are the Father’s gift to the Son (vv. 6, 9, 24), not a post-facto label for whoever happens to believe.

Key contrast Both confess the Son’s authority over all flesh and that eternal life is his to give. Arminians typically take “those given” as believers, and the all-flesh authority as evidence the ransom is for every man. Reformed readers typically take “those given” as the elect handed to the Son, so the life-gift is particular even while the kingdom extends over all. Do not flatten either “all flesh” or “those given.”

John 17:3

3And this is eternal life, that they know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.

Arminian

Eternal life is defined as knowing: the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom he has sent. Wesley: to know by loving, holy faith—not excluding Son and Spirit, but excluding the false gods; Christ as prophet, priest, and king. This knowledge is both the way to, and the essence of, everlasting happiness.

Arminian weight: life is not a cipher. It is personal knowledge of the Father in the Sent One, held out as the life the Son is authorized to give. High Christology stands in the definition itself: you cannot have the life without this Jesus Christ.

Reformed

Calvin: we are all in death until God, who alone is life, enlightens us. This is not every kind of knowledge, but the knowledge which is faith—engrafting into Christ, adoption, inheritance. The only true God is known in the face of Jesus Christ; to separate the Son from the Father’s deity is to invent a strange god.

Reformed accent: the manner of bestowing life is the illumination of those given him. The gospel is offered; the secret, efficacious teaching by which children of God are drawn to faith is not common to all. Life consists in this knowing.

John 17:4

4I glorified you on earth, having accomplished the work that you gave me to do.

Arminian

The Son has already glorified the Father on earth by finishing the given work. Wesley: thus he laid the foundation of the kingdom on earth. The accomplished work is public mission, not a hidden ledger of names.

Arminian pastoral note: the hour still includes the cross, yet he speaks as one who has done what he was sent to do. The work given him is the work that makes the Father known—so that flesh may have a true God to know, and a Sent One to believe.

Reformed

He has completed the course of his calling. Calvin includes more than teaching: the sacrifice of death is so near that he speaks as if he had already endured it. The request is that the Father put him in possession of the kingdom and display, by the Spirit, the fruit of all he did on earth.

Reformed reading: the work is the Father’s assignment, done in the form of a servant. Philippians 2 sits under this verse: humiliation first, then the name above every name. Accomplished work is not independent of the eternal purpose that appointed the hour.

John 17:5

5And now, Father, glorify me in your own presence with the glory that I had with you before the world existed.

Arminian

He asks for the glory he had with the Father before the world existed. Wesley: he does not say “received”—he always had it, until he emptied himself in the days of his flesh. Pre-existent glory is the Son’s own, not a created award handed to a later hero.

Shared confession, Arminian voice: the one about to die is the one who was with the Father before there was a world. Kenosis does not cancel deity. The church that will be prayed for in this chapter is prayed for by the pre-existent Son, not by a mere prophet at the end of his strength.

Reformed

Calvin called this remarkable: Christ is not a newly contrived God; if his glory was eternal, himself has always been. A manifest distinction of persons stands here—the eternal Word, begotten of the Father before all ages—now asking that divine majesty be displayed in the Mediator’s flesh.

Shared confession, Reformed voice: he desires nothing that is not already his. Earthly fading honor is not the point; the glory with the Father before the world was. High Christology is not a later church upgrade of John 17; it is the prayer’s premise.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the Son’s pre-existent glory and the gift of life to those given him—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 17:1–5.

Arminian Notable voices

“To all whom thou hast given him—To all believers. This is a clear proof that Christ designed his sacrifice should avail for all: yea, that all flesh, every man, should partake of everlasting life. For as the Father had given him power over all flesh, so he gave himself a ransom for all. The glory which I had—He does not say received—He always had it, till he emptied himself of it in the days of his flesh.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 17:1–5, especially 17:2, 5)

Reformed Notable voices

“Christ does not say that he has been made Governor over the whole world, in order to bestow life on all without any distinction; but he limits this grace to those who have been given to him. It is only the elect who belong to his peculiar flock, which he has undertaken to guard as a Shepherd. So then, the kingdom of Christ extends, no doubt, to all men; but it brings salvation to none but the elect. This is a remarkable passage, which teaches us that Christ is not a God who has been newly contrived, or who has existed only for a time; for if his glory was eternal, himself also has always been.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 17:1–5, especially 17:2, 5)
