Passage

John 6

Verses 41–59 · WEB · Voices

The crowd grumbles at Jesus’ claim to be bread from heaven, since they know his family. Jesus answers that no one can come to him unless the Father who sent him draws him, and he will raise that person up on the last day. Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes to him. He then presses further: he is the living bread, and the bread he will give for the life of the world is his flesh. Unless they eat his flesh and drink his blood, they have no life. Verse 44 is one of the most debated verses between Arminian and Reformed readers. The language of eating and drinking also raises questions about the Lord’s Supper.

John 6:41

41The Jews therefore murmured concerning him, because he said, “I am the bread which came down out of heaven.”

Arminian

The Jews grumbled because he said he was the bread from heaven. Arminian readers see resistance to Jesus’ claims as a choice people make.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the grumbling as the natural response of the unregenerate heart.

John 6:42

42They said, “Isn’t this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know? How then does he say, ‘I have come down out of heaven’?”

Arminian

“Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph?” Arminian readers see familiarity blinding people to the truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human reasoning unable to grasp spiritual truth without God’s work.

John 6:43

43Therefore Jesus answered them, “Don’t murmur among yourselves.

Arminian

Do not grumble among yourselves. Arminian readers see Jesus redirecting the crowd toward faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus explaining the cause of their unbelief in the next verse.

John 6:44

44No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him; and I will raise him up in the last day.

Arminian

No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him, and I will raise him up on the last day. Arminian readers see the drawing as a universal work of grace. They connect it with 12:32, where Jesus says he will draw all people to himself. The drawing enables but does not compel, and it can be resisted.

They note that inability is overcome by grace offered to all.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the drawing as effectual. The one who is drawn is the one raised up on the last day, so drawing results in salvation. They read 12:32 as all kinds of people, Gentiles as well as Jews.

Calvin and later Reformed writers saw here the doctrine of irresistible grace.

Key contrast Both affirm that no one comes to Christ apart from the Father’s drawing. Arminian readers see a universal drawing, connected with 12:32, that enables a free response and can be resisted. Reformed readers see an effectual drawing given to the elect, since the one drawn is the one raised on the last day.

John 6:45

45It is written in the prophets, ‘They will all be taught by God.’ Therefore everyone who hears from the Father and has learned, comes to me.

Arminian

“They will all be taught by God.” Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes to me. Arminian readers see the Father teaching all, with those who listen and learn coming to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see everyone taught by the Father actually coming to Christ, which confirms that the drawing is effectual.

John 6:46

46Not that anyone has seen the Father, except he who is from God. He has seen the Father.

Arminian

Not that anyone has seen the Father except he who is from God. Arminian readers see Christ as the only one who can reveal the Father.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Son’s unique knowledge of the Father.

John 6:47

47Most certainly, I tell you, he who believes in me has eternal life.

Arminian

Whoever believes has eternal life. Arminian readers see eternal life offered to everyone who believes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the present possession of eternal life by every believer.

John 6:48

48I am the bread of life.

Arminian

I am the bread of life. Arminian readers see Christ as the provision God offers to all.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as the sole source of spiritual life.

John 6:49

49Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness and they died.

Arminian

Your fathers ate the manna and died. Arminian readers see the limits of the old provision.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the manna as a type that could not give eternal life.

John 6:50

50This is the bread which comes down out of heaven, that anyone may eat of it and not die.

Arminian

This is the bread that comes down from heaven, so that one may eat of it and not die. Arminian readers see an invitation for anyone to eat.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the bread giving life to all who eat it by faith.

John 6:51

51I am the living bread which came down out of heaven. If anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever. Yes, the bread which I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.”

Arminian

The bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh. Arminian readers see Christ’s death given for the whole world, a key text for universal atonement.

Reformed

Reformed readers see “the world” as the world of humanity across nations. They argue that Christ’s death secures life for all who eat by faith.

Key contrast Both see Christ giving his flesh for the life of the world. Arminian readers see this as his death for every person. Reformed readers see it as his death for people from every nation, effective for those who believe.

John 6:52

52The Jews therefore contended with one another, saying, “How can this man give us his flesh to eat?”

Arminian

“How can this man give us his flesh to eat?” Arminian readers see the crowd taking Jesus’ words literally.

Reformed

Reformed readers see misunderstanding that Jesus addresses in the next verses.

John 6:53

53Jesus therefore said to them, “Most certainly I tell you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you don’t have life in yourselves.

Arminian

Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you. Arminian readers generally read eating and drinking as believing in Christ’s death. Many link it secondarily with the Lord’s Supper.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it as feeding on Christ by faith. Calvin saw a real spiritual communion with Christ, which the Supper signifies and seals.

John 6:54

54He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up at the last day.

Arminian

Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up. Arminian readers see continuing faith as feeding on Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer’s secure possession of eternal life and future resurrection.

John 6:55

55For my flesh is food indeed, and my blood is drink indeed.

Arminian

My flesh is true food and my blood is true drink. Arminian readers see Christ as the real nourishment of the soul.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ truly given to believers, received by faith through the Spirit.

John 6:56

56He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood lives in me, and I in him.

Arminian

Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in him. Arminian readers see abiding as an ongoing relationship that believers must maintain.

Reformed

Reformed readers see mutual indwelling as union with Christ, which cannot be broken.

John 6:57

57As the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so he who feeds on me will also live because of me.

Arminian

Whoever feeds on me will live because of me. Arminian readers see life sustained by continuing to feed on Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as the source of the believer’s life.

John 6:58

58This is the bread which came down out of heaven—not as our fathers ate the manna and died. He who eats this bread will live forever.”

Arminian

Whoever feeds on this bread will live forever. Arminian readers see eternal life promised to those who feed on Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the promise of eternal life as certain for believers.

John 6:59

59He said these things in the synagogue, as he taught in Capernaum.

Arminian

Jesus said these things in the synagogue at Capernaum. Arminian readers see the public offer of the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the discourse delivered as public teaching, which sifted true and false disciples.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What older interpreters in each camp said about the Father’s drawing and the bread that is Christ’s flesh.

Arminian Notable voices

“No man comes to me, unless my Father draw him—No man can believe in Christ, unless God give him power: he draws us first, by good desires. Not by compulsion, not by laying the will under any necessity; but by the strong and sweet, yet still resistible, motions of his heavenly grace.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 6:44)

Reformed Notable voices

“No man will ever of himself be able to come to Christ, but God must first approach him by his Spirit; and hence it follows that all are not drawn, but that God bestows this grace on those whom he has elected. True, indeed, as to the kind of drawing, it is not violent, so as to compel men by external force; but still it is a powerful impulse of the Holy Spirit, which makes men willing who formerly were unwilling and reluctant.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 6:44)
