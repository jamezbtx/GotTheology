Passage

John 6

Verses 41–59 · ESV · Voices

The crowd grumbles that Jesus, whose parents they know, claims to have come down from heaven. He answers that no one can come unless the Father draws, cites the prophets that they will all be taught by God, and names himself the living bread whose flesh is given for the world’s life. Dispute over eating his flesh leads to the hard synagogue teaching at Capernaum: unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life.

John 6:41

41So the Jews grumbled about him, because he said, “I am the bread that came down from heaven.”

Arminian

The complaint is about heavenly origin, not yet about flesh. They stumble that this man claims to have come down from heaven. Grumbling is a wilderness echo (Exod. 16; Num. 11).

Classical Arminians: unbelief here is a real, responsible refusal of light already given—the signs, the feeding, the “I am.” They are not forced to murmur; they treat the claim as intolerable.

Reformed

Grumbling repeats Israel’s wilderness pattern against manna and Moses. The offense is the descent: he claims heaven as origin.

Reformed accent: unbelief is not a surprise that threatens the gospel. It is what happens when hearers meet a claim their nature will not receive. The next verses will name why they cannot come unaided.

John 6:42

42They said, “Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know? How does he now say, ‘I have come down from heaven’?”

Arminian

They think they have him catalogued: Joseph’s son, known parents. Incarnation is misread as disproof of descent. The “how” is historically sincere and still culpable—they have seen enough to ask a humbler question.

Arminian pastoral note: familiarity with Jesus’ household is not the same as knowing the Father who sent him. High Christology is not in dispute: he did come down. Their knowledge of Mary and Joseph is being used to close the ear.

Reformed

Familiarity becomes a weapon. They measure the Sent One by the household they know. “This Jesus” is placed under village epistemology.

Reformed reading: flesh judges by sight. What they know of his parents is made to cancel what he says of the Father. The offense of the incarnation is already at work; v. 44 will explain why such hearers do not come.

John 6:43

43Jesus answered them, “Do not grumble among yourselves.

Arminian

He checks the murmuring (Wesley) rather than litigating the genealogy. Grumbling is not a path into truth. The command is real: they must stop the wilderness posture if they would hear.

Classical Arminians take the prohibition as a genuine address. Unbelief talking to unbelief will not yield faith; they remain responsible to cease the complaint and listen.

Reformed

He will not let Joseph’s household be the court of appeal. Murmur among yourselves is unbelief conferring with unbelief.

Reformed accent: he redirects from complaint to the Father’s drawing. The next word will not flatter their capacity; it will name their inability and God’s gift.

John 6:44

44No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him. And I will raise him up on the last day.

Arminian

“No one can come” is real inability: no one comes unaided. The Father who sent the Son must draw. Wesley: not by compulsion or a necessity laid on the will, but by the strong and sweet, still resistible motions of heavenly grace; he draws first by good desires. Verse 45 will interpret the drawing as being taught by God—heard and learned.

Drawing is necessary; it is not irresistible. Those who do come are promised resurrection. The second clause comforts the comer; it does not turn the drawing into a force that cannot be refused. Grace enables coming without canceling a genuine human response.

Reformed

“No one can come” is inability of nature. Coming is impossible unless the Father draws. Calvin: no man will ever of himself be able to come; God must first approach by his Spirit; all are not drawn, but this grace is bestowed on the elect. The drawing is not violent external force, yet it is a powerful impulse that makes the unwilling willing.

The second clause interprets the first as effectual: the one drawn is the one Jesus will raise on the last day. Faith does not arise from an undrawn will; it is God’s gift. The gospel is preached widely; this drawing is savingly particular.

Key contrast Both confess that no one comes unaided: the Father must draw. Arminians typically take drawing as necessary—often via teaching (v. 45), heard and learned—yet resistible; grace enables coming without guaranteeing it. Reformed readers typically take “no one can come” as real inability met by an effectual drawing: those the Father draws do come, and Jesus raises them on the last day.

John 6:45

45It is written in the Prophets, ‘And they will all be taught by God.’ Everyone who has heard and learned from the Father comes to me—

Arminian

Isaiah 54:13: they will all be taught by God. Drawing is unpacked as teaching. Wesley: every man that has heard—the secret voice of God—he, and he only, believes. Classical Arminians stress both clauses: the Father teaches, and the hearer must hear and learn—a real, grace-enabled response.

Those who learn come; those who shut the ear resist the drawing. The prophecy does not erase responsibility. Being taught is how the Father draws; hearing and learning are how that teaching is received.

Reformed

The prophecy names the new-covenant people as God-taught. “Everyone who has heard and learned… comes”—not some of the truly taught. Hearing and learning are God’s work in those he draws, not a separable human contribution that might fail after the lesson.

Calvin on this verse: as men are not fitted for believing until they have been drawn, so the grace by which they are drawn is efficacious, so that they necessarily believe. The God-taught are the ones who come.

Key contrast Arminians typically read v. 45 as the manner of the drawing: the Father teaches, and those who hear and learn come—teaching that can still be refused. Reformed readers typically read “everyone who has heard and learned… comes” as proof that the teaching is effectual: the God-taught do come.

John 6:46

46not that anyone has seen the Father except he who is from God; he has seen the Father.

Arminian

Taught-by-God is not a claim to have seen the Father. Only the One who is from God has seen him. High Christology is shared: Jesus uniquely sees and reveals.

Arminian note: the teaching that draws is the Father’s, through the Son we can hear. No private vision replaces coming to Christ. The invitation still runs through the Incarnate Word.

Reformed

Exclusive vision belongs to the Son. No mystic shortcut around the incarnate Word. Those taught by God are taught concerning, and by, this Seer of the Father.

Reformed accent: revelation is Christ-shaped. The Father draws people to the One who has seen him, not to a nameless inner light that bypasses the Sent One.

John 6:47

47Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever believes has eternal life.

Arminian

The invitation stays wide: whoever believes has life now. Believing is the appointed means; life is present possession, not only a future hope.

Classical Arminians: the “whoever” is sincere. The inability of v. 44 does not shrink the offer; it names our need of drawing so that we may believe. The promise meets every actual believer.

Reformed

Whoever believes has life—and believing is what the drawn do. The universal term invites all who believe and cuts off excuse, while faith itself is not native to all.

Reformed comfort: life is present in the believer because it is Christ’s life given. The same people drawn (v. 44) are the believers here and the raised on the last day.

John 6:48

48I am the bread of life.

Arminian

He repeats the claim of v. 35. He has and gives life. Coming, believing, and (soon) eating are overlapping invitations to the same Christ.

Arminian pastoral weight: the bread is a person to be received, not a theory to admire. The grumblers are still being offered the loaf they resent.

Reformed

The I AM of life-bread stands after the drawing-saying. He is the food the Father gives and the One to whom the Father draws.

Reformed accent: identity first. Eating will interpret faith-union with this I AM. The bread is not a thing detachable from the Son.

John 6:49

49Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died.

Arminian

Manna was real gift and still not the life-bread. Wilderness fathers ate and died—mortality, and for many, judgment. Do not rest in a past miracle as if it were the Son.

Classical Arminians: types sincerely fed the body; they did not give the life now offered. The crowd’s nostalgia for Moses’ bread is a responsible misreading of a greater gift.

Reformed

The type is honored and limited. Manna fed bodies; it did not raise the dead or confer the life of the age to come.

Reformed reading: the contrast prepares the claim that this bread is of another order—the flesh of the One from heaven. Providence gave manna; the same God now gives the Son.

John 6:50

50This is the bread that comes down from heaven, so that one may eat of it and not die.

Arminian

Purpose clause: so that one may eat and not die. Eating is believing (Wesley on v. 51). The offer is that a person may eat—real possibility under drawing grace.

Not dying is eternal life, not a promise that the body will never see the grave. The loaf is given to be eaten; refusal is still possible, and tragic.

Reformed

Heaven’s bread has a saving purpose manna never carried. “One may eat” is the appointed means; those who eat do not die the death that held the wilderness generation.

Reformed accent: the Son himself is the food of immortality. The purpose is not frustrated in those the Father draws to this eating.

John 6:51

51I am the living bread that came down from heaven. If anyone eats of this bread, he will live forever. And the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.”

Arminian

Living bread; anyone who eats (believes) lives forever. Wesley: he that believes to the end shall be saved. The bread given is his flesh—the passion, given “for the life of the world.”

This whole discourse refers directly to his passion, and but remotely, if at all, to the Lord’s Supper (Wesley). Classical Arminians hear world-wide saving intent: the flesh is given for the world’s life. Eating remains a genuine response to a sincere gift—not cannibalism, but faith receiving the crucified Son.

Reformed

Descent, gift, and flesh cohere: the incarnate Son will give himself in death. “For the life of the world” names the world as the theater and the peoples for whom the bread is given, without making every individual an eater.

Reformed reading: the gift of flesh is public in proclamation and particular in application. Eating is faith receiving the crucified—not a physical chewing. The living bread is the Son whose flesh will hang on the cross.

John 6:52

52The Jews then disputed among themselves, saying, “How can this man give us his flesh to eat?”

Arminian

They hear butchery. The how is carnal, like Nicodemus’s womb. Dispute is unbelief refusing the spiritual sense. They remain responsible for the mishearing.

Arminian note: Jesus will not recant the gift. He will intensify it. The scandal of flesh given is meant to drive them to faith, not to a literal meal of tissue.

Reformed

“This man”—contempt. They stumble at the incarnation’s lowliness and at a gift they will not receive spiritually. The quarrel proves they need the drawing of v. 44 even to understand the bread.

Reformed accent: without the Spirit, the cross-shaped gift sounds like cannibalism. The how is the wrong first question; the need is to be drawn to the crucified Giver.

John 6:53

53So Jesus said to them, “Truly, truly, I say to you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you.

Arminian

He does not soften the scandal; he intensifies it. Wesley: unless you eat spiritually—draw continual virtue from him by faith. Eating his flesh is only another expression for believing.

No cannibalism; no life without faith-union with the crucified Son. The unless is a genuine warning: refuse this food and you have no life. Blood named beside flesh points to the passion, the life laid down.

Reformed

Calvin: they despise the mean appearance of his flesh, yet that flesh contains life. Eating and drinking are faith’s reception of the atoning death—flesh given, blood shed. The Lord’s Supper corresponds to this doctrine as seal; the life is in union with the crucified, not in a physical chewing.

Without this eating there is no life. The necessity matches v. 44: as no one can come unless drawn, so no one has life unless joined to the Son in his death.

Key contrast Shared ground: this is faith-union with the crucified Son, not cannibalism. Arminians typically equate eating and drinking with believing—a responsible, ongoing drawing of virtue from Christ. Reformed readers typically treat this eating as the faith of those united to the crucified by the Father’s effectual gift—necessary, and the appointed way the drawn live.

John 6:54

54Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up on the last day.

Arminian

Feeds—ongoing, not a single nibble. Present possession of life plus resurrection. The eater is the believer. The same raise-up promise as v. 44 is now tied to feeding.

Arminian pastoral note: persevering faith-union is the path. He who believes to the end shall be saved (Wesley on v. 51). The promise is sincere to every feeder.

Reformed

The feeder has life and will be raised—the same people as those drawn in v. 44. The last-day raising is certain for those who feed.

Reformed comfort: faith-union with the crucified is not a maybe. It is the life of those the Father gives the Son. Resurrection is the Son’s pledged act, not a wage the eater earns.

John 6:55

55For my flesh is true food, and my blood is true drink.

Arminian

Wesley: meat and drink indeed—with which the soul of a believer is as truly fed as the body with meat and drink. True, not theatrical.

The soul’s hunger meets a real banquet in Christ. Pastoral tone: he is enough. You are not sent to chew what cannot nourish; you are invited to a food that actually gives life.

Reformed

True food and true drink: not a metaphor that empties the thing, but a naming of what actually nourishes unto life. Calvin: food and drink together so the life is complete, not half; the Supper corresponds as a twofold pledge.

Reformed accent: never a part of life in Christ until entire life in him. The crucified flesh and shed blood are the believer’s full provision.

John 6:56

56Whoever feeds on my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in him.

Arminian

Mutual indwelling: the eater abides in Christ and Christ in the eater. Abiding is living union, maintained by ongoing faith—not a physical lodging of tissue.

Classical Arminians: this communion is real and must be continued. The verse is promise and call together. Feeding is how we stay in the Son who gave his flesh.

Reformed

Mutual abiding is the heart of union with Christ. The feeder is in the Son; the Son is in the feeder. This is the faith-union the drawing created—not cannibalism, but personal communion with the crucified and living Lord.

Reformed comfort: this abiding is the secure communion of those united to Christ. He does not lodge as a guest who may be finally shown the door by frailty; he abides in his own.

John 6:57

57As the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so whoever feeds on me, he also will live because of me.

Arminian

Wesley: I live by the Father—being one with him. He shall live by me—being one with me. Amazing union! The analogy is life-from-life.

The eater’s life is derivative of the Son as the Son’s is of the Father. High Christology and real human feeding: we live because of him, by feeding on him in faith.

Reformed

Sent by the living Father, the Son lives because of the Father; feeders live because of the Son. Trinitarian life flows down.

Reformed reading: union with the Son is participation in that life, not imitation from outside. The “whoever” is the one who actually feeds—those drawn into this life by the Father who sent the Son.

John 6:58

58This is the bread that came down from heaven, not like the bread the fathers ate, and died. Whoever feeds on this bread will live forever.”

Arminian

Wesley: I am the bread, not like the manna your fathers ate, who died notwithstanding. The contrast is complete: wilderness bread versus the Son.

Whoever feeds lives forever—a sincere promise to eaters. The synagogue still hears an open “whoever.” Forever is the life of faith-union with the crucified, not a second helping of manna.

Reformed

Type and antitype. Manna and mortality; this bread and forever. The feeder’s forever is as sure as the descent.

Reformed accent: the same whoever as the drawn (v. 44) and the raised. Heaven’s loaf does what wilderness bread could not, because the loaf is the Son himself.

John 6:59

59Jesus said these things in the synagogue, as he taught at Capernaum.

Arminian

Public synagogue teaching, not a secret riddle for insiders only. Capernaum hears and must answer. The hardness of the saying does not cancel the sincerity of the teaching.

Arminian pastoral close: the Father still draws by teaching. Those who hear and learn may yet come. Grumbling is not the last possible word.

Reformed

John locates the discourse: synagogue, Capernaum. The Bread of Life is synagogue doctrine, then a crisis of discipleship (vv. 60–71).

Reformed accent: Israel’s Teacher has spoken covenant words, not a private mystic. The Father’s drawing and the Son’s flesh given are public truth; unbelief in the pew does not make them untrue.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the Father’s drawing and the bread that is Christ’s flesh—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 6:41–59.

Arminian Notable voices

“No man comes to me, unless my Father draw him—No man can believe in Christ, unless God give him power: he draws us first, by good desires. Not by compulsion, not by laying the will under any necessity; but by the strong and sweet, yet still resistible, motions of his heavenly grace.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 6:44)

Reformed Notable voices

“No man will ever of himself be able to come to Christ, but God must first approach him by his Spirit; and hence it follows that all are not drawn, but that God bestows this grace on those whom he has elected. True, indeed, as to the kind of drawing, it is not violent, so as to compel men by external force; but still it is a powerful impulse of the Holy Spirit, which makes men willing who formerly were unwilling and reluctant.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 6:44)
