Passage

John 6

Verses 16–21 · WEB · Voices

After refusing forced kingship, Jesus’ disciples start across the sea toward Capernaum in the dark. A strong wind rises; they see him walking on the water; he says, “It is I. Don’t be afraid,” and the boat is immediately at the land.

John 6:16

16When evening came, his disciples went down to the sea.

Arminian

Evening comes; the disciples go down to the sea as Jesus had ordered (Wesley on v. 15). They leave the mountain of his solitude and obey in the darkening hour.

Arminian interpreters hear responsible discipleship: they go because he sent them, even though Matthew says they were unwilling to leave him. Obedience here is a human act under his word, not drift.

Reformed

The evening departure is of a piece with the withdrawal of v. 15. He hides from the king-making crowd; the disciples take the water-path he assigned.

The timing is his. Night and lake will become the next theater of his identity. They go down not because the story slipped, but because the Son orders both the hiding and the crossing.

John 6:17

17They entered into the boat, and were going over the sea to Capernaum. It was now dark, and Jesus had not come to them.

Arminian

They embark for Capernaum. It is dark, and Jesus has not yet come. John lets us feel the absence: the Provider of the loaves is not in the boat.

“Not” is delay, not desertion. Disciples may obey and still pass through hours when he has not come. The delay tests trust; it does not mean he has withdrawn the care he showed on the grass.

Reformed

Capernaum is the destination; darkness and the Lord’s absence are the condition. John will later note that the crowd saw he had not entered the boat—so this crossing also sets up the next day’s seeking.

“Not” is appointed. He will come at the hour that confirms their faith. The dark is not evidence that providence failed; it is the setting in which “It is I” will be heard.

John 6:18

18The sea was tossed by a great wind blowing.

Arminian

The sea roughens under a strong wind. Creation’s turmoil meets a small boat of disciples who already lack their Master’s visible presence.

Storms are trials in which faith may falter or look for him. The wind does not nullify their responsibility to receive him when he comes; it makes that receiving costly and glad (v. 21).

Reformed

Wind and rough water are not outside the Son’s rule. The Lord who multiplied barley now lets the lake rise. John is spare; the other Gospels add hours of rowing against the wind.

The Lord often lets his people fall into alarming dangers, Calvin says, that they may more plainly recognize him in deliverance. The gale serves the revelation.

John 6:19

19When therefore they had rowed about twenty-five or thirty stadia, they saw Jesus walking on the sea and drawing near to the boat; and they were afraid.

Arminian

After three or four miles they see him walking on the sea, coming near—and they are frightened. The approach of help is misread as threat. Only God treads the waves; their fear shows how unready the heart can be for glory.

Seeing Jesus is not yet trusting Jesus. The sign is sincere; the fright is theirs. They will need his word before they will be glad to take him in. High Christology is not in dispute: the walker on the sea is no mere prophet still on the mountain.

Reformed

Walking on the sea is a theophany-shaped sign (Job 9:8). They see him coming near and are frightened—Matthew and Mark explain they thought a ghost. Without the word, Calvin says, a demonstration of divinity drives us into idols and terror.

The same Christ who feeds also rules chaos. Their fear does not make the sign untrue; it shows how helpless sight is until he speaks. The boat, the miles, and the walker are all under his purpose.

John 6:20

20But he said to them, “It is I. Don’t be afraid.”

Arminian

“It is I. Don’t be afraid.” The Greek can bear the overtone of “I am,” yet here it is also plain identification: not a ghost, but Jesus. Wesley hears the same comfort in the parallel: be of good cheer; it is I.

Classical Arminians take the word as a sincere address to frightened disciples. He comes toward them before they can come to him—prevenient mercy on the water. They must still receive the comfort; fear is not in doubt, and so is the command not to be afraid. The saying is gospel-shaped: the present Christ is enough for the storm.

Reformed

Calvin: in Christ’s presence alone we have abundant grounds of confidence. Believers, knowing he is given to make propitiation, take courage as if raised from death to life. The same words “It is I” later strike down the arrest party (18:6); here they steady his own.

This is high Christology and particular comfort. He is not a vague force on the lake; he is the I AM who speaks. He not only says “Don’t be afraid” but, Calvin adds, removes the cause of terror by stilling the scene. Presence is effectual for those who are his.

Key contrast Both hear divine self-identification and comfort. Arminians stress a sincere word to disciples who must still trust and receive him in the boat. Reformed Christians press that this presence belongs to Christ’s own—the same “It is I” that comforts disciples overwhelms the wicked—and that he effectually removes the terror, not only advises against it.

John 6:21

21They were willing therefore to receive him into the boat. Immediately the boat was at the land where they were going.

Arminian

They are glad to take him in; immediately the boat is at the land. Receiving him ends the crisis. The gladness is a human welcome after fear.

The One they receive is the very Lord who fed the five thousand and refused a forced crown. Willing reception here pictures the coming invitation: him who comes to me I will in no way throw out. The landing is gift; the taking-in is their act under his word.

Reformed

Glad reception and immediate arrival: his presence does what miles of rowing could not. John compresses the stilling and the landing into one stroke of sovereignty.

They take him in because he has already come and spoken. The boat’s sudden arrival is his work. The sign confirms the disciples for the hard bread-speech ahead: this is the Lord of sea and bread, not a candidate for an earthly throne.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Arminian Notable voices

“He constrained his disciples — Who were unwilling to leave him. The fourth watch — The Jews (as well as the Romans) usually divided the night into four watches, of three hours each.”

Reformed Notable voices
