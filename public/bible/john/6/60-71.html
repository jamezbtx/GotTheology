Passage

John 6

Verses 60–71 · ESV · Voices

Many disciples call this a hard saying. Jesus traces unbelief to the Father’s grant (6:65); many turn back and no longer walk with him (6:66); Peter confesses the Holy One; Jesus names Judas, one of the twelve, as a devil who will betray him.

John 6:60

60When many of his disciples heard it, they said, “This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?”

Arminian

Many of his disciples—not only hostile “Jews”—call it a hard saying: who can listen? Wesley: hard to the children of the world, sweet to the children of God. Scarce ever did he speak more sublimely, even to the apostles in private. Who can hear—endure it?

Classical Arminians: they heard and found it intolerable. The hardness is in refusing spiritual eating of the crucified Son. Disciples can still walk away; the saying is not softened to keep a crowd. Their question is a decision forming, not a request for a glossary only.

Reformed

Calvin: the harshness lay in their hearts, not in the saying. The reprobate form stones out of the word to dash themselves upon. Whoever submits with true humility finds nothing harsh; to unbelievers it is a hammer that breaks the rock.

Reformed accent: “Who can listen?” already excuses the self and indicts the Son. Many who wore the name disciple now show what they were. The hard saying is the same gospel that is life to the teachable.

John 6:61

61But Jesus, knowing in himself that his disciples were grumbling about this, said to them, “Do you take offense at this?

Arminian

He knows in himself they are grumbling—no spy needed—and asks if they take offense. The question is a real opening: they may still drop the scandal and stay.

Arminian reading: omniscience of their murmur does not cancel the pastoral appeal. He names the offense so they can repent of it. Grumbling disciples are still being addressed as people who might listen.

Reformed

Calvin: he knew in himself because they had not yet declared openly; they murmured secretly and so shut the way to instruction. The question means they have no reason to be offended, or at least that the ground of offense is not in the doctrine.

Reformed accent: he tries whether any offended hearer is not yet beyond cure, and stops the mouths of the rest. Knowledge of hearts is his divinity; the question still sifts.

John 6:62

62Then what if you were to see the Son of Man ascending to where he was before?

Arminian

What if you see the Son of Man ascending where he was before? Wesley: how much more incredible will it then appear that he should give you his flesh to eat? If descent and flesh offend, ascent will not be easier for a carnal ear.

Shared high Christology: he was before—preexistence—and will ascend. Classical Arminians: the question presses them to interpret flesh-eating in light of a returning-to-heaven Son, not a cannibal meal. They can still learn this; many will not.

Reformed

Calvin: the mean condition of the flesh prevented them from submitting to his divine power; he calls them to behold heavenly glory. Resurrection and ascension display that he is the Son of God. “Where he was before” does not apply strictly to the human nature alone; the two natures are one person.

Reformed accent: offense at incarnation will not be cured by shrinking Christ. Ascension confirms the descent. The flesh they must eat is the flesh of One who returns to glory—faith-union, not a corpse-meal.

John 6:63

63It is the Spirit who gives life; the flesh is no help at all. The words that I have spoken to you are spirit and life.

Arminian

The Spirit gives life; the flesh is no help. Wesley: the spiritual meaning by which God gives life; the bare carnal literal meaning profits nothing. The words are spirit—to be taken spiritually—and when so understood are life, a means of spiritual life to the hearers.

Shared with Reformed: this kills crude cannibalism. Eating flesh is not chewing tissue. Classical Arminians: the words are a means of life to those who receive them spiritually. Hearers may still take them grossly and die in the hearing. The Spirit’s life-giving does not erase the call to understand and believe the words.

Reformed

Calvin: if the flesh is separated from the Spirit it is useless—not because Christ’s flesh cannot quicken, but because its power is spiritual. We must bring the spiritual mouth of faith. He then calls his words spirit and life: spiritual, calling us upward to seek Christ in heavenly glory by faith, not carnal perception; life to those who receive them spiritually, death to others.

Reformed accent: Spirit-given life is why faith is not native sagacity (v. 65). The same verse that denies flesh-help affirms that the crucified flesh, taken spiritually, is food. No help from unaided flesh; all help from the Spirit in the words.

John 6:64

64But there are some of you who do not believe.” (For Jesus knew from the beginning who those were who did not believe, and who it was who would betray him.)

Arminian

Some of you do not believe—and so receive no life, Wesley says, because they take the words in a gross literal sense. Jesus knew from the beginning of his ministry who would not believe and who would betray him. Wesley: therefore God does foresee future contingencies; “but his foreknowledge causes not the fault, which had no less proved certain unforeknown.”

Classical Arminians: foreknowledge is real; it does not author their unbelief. Judas and the unbelieving disciples remain guilty. The warning is still a place to repent. Knowing from the beginning is Christ’s (and the Father’s) knowledge, not a cause that makes their refusal a puppet show.

Reformed

Calvin: he imputes blame—being destitute of the Spirit they corrupt the doctrine. The Evangelist adds that Jesus knew from the beginning, lest anyone think he judged at random. Many professed his flock; sudden apostasy exposed hypocrisy; their treachery was well known to Christ while unknown to others.

Reformed accent: this knowledge is peculiar to his divinity. The leaving of v. 66 and the betrayer of vv. 70–71 are not surprises that thwart the mission. Unbelief was known from the beginning; those who do not believe were not given (v. 65). John also teaches us not to judge hearts until fruit shows—our knowledge is not his.

Key contrast Both take Jesus’ knowledge of unbelievers and the betrayer as real from the beginning. Arminians (Wesley) insist foreknowledge does not cause the fault—contingent refusals are still theirs. Reformed readers hear the same knowledge as the Son’s divine certainty: apostasy exposes what he already knew; it does not break the Father’s giving of the rest.

John 6:65

65And he said, “This is why I told you that no one can come to me unless it is granted him by the Father.”

Arminian

This is why I told you: no one can come unless it is granted by the Father. Wesley’s actual reading: “Unless it be given—and it is given to those only who will receive it on God’s own terms.”

Coming is impossible unaided—again, not Pelagian. The grant is the Father’s enabling (drawing, teaching, v. 45). It is given to those who will receive it on God’s terms: faith-union with the crucified Son, not loaf-politics or carnal eating. The crowd about to leave proves the grant is not an irresistible seizure; they had light, signs, words of spirit and life, and they will refuse. “This is why” explains unbelief without making the invitation insincere. Grace is given that can be received or refused.

Reformed

Calvin’s actual reading: faith is an uncommon gift of the Spirit. We must not think more meanly of the gospel because the whole world does not assent. Why so few believers? No one, however acute, arrives at faith by his own sagacity; all are blind until illuminated; only those the Father deigns partake. If this grace were bestowed on all without exception, it would have been unseasonable to mention it here.

He now uses give instead of draw: God draws because out of free grace he loves us; what we obtain by gift, no one procures by industry. “This is why” points at the unbelievers of v. 64: they do not come because it has not been granted. The leaving crowd were not given. Effectual grant, not a resistible voucher waiting on independent consent, is why some remain and many will not.

Key contrast Both: no one can come unaided; the Father must grant. Arminian (Wesley): the grant is given to those who will receive it on God’s terms—enabling grace that can be refused; the crowd’s departure is resisted drawing. Reformed (Calvin): grant replaces draw to show free, electing love; no one arrives by sagacity; if all received this grace the saying would not explain few believers; the leavers were not granted. Neither side should be heard as denying either inability or the call to come.

John 6:66

66After this many of his disciples turned back and no longer walked with him.

Arminian

After this many disciples turned back and no longer walked with him. Do not skip this. Wesley: from this time he began to purge his floor; the proud and careless were driven away; those remained who were meet for the Master’s use.

Classical Arminians treat this as the exhibit of resistible grace in John 6. They had been disciples in some real sense—followers, eaters of loaves, hearers. They then quit. Drawing, teaching, and granting were not irresistible in their case. The hard saying did not mechanically eject them; they turned back. Walking with Jesus can be begun and abandoned. The tragedy is theirs.

Reformed

Calvin: a dreadful, monstrous thing that so kind an invitation alienated many who had professed to belong to him. Some would say the sermon should never have been preached; we ought to think otherwise—Christ is the stone of stumbling. We must not offend by our fault, yet the doctrine will offend many; the reprobate suck venom from wholesome food.

Reformed reading: “walked no more with him” is not a harmless cooling of acquaintance; John condemns them as apostates. We cannot go back a foot without danger of denying the Master. Their leaving does not mean they were given and then dropped; it confirms vv. 64–65: they did not believe, and coming had not been granted. Preach it anyway.

Key contrast Arminians read the mass turning-back as proof that disciples can resist the Father’s drawing—real followers who refuse the hard saying. Reformed readers read it as apostasy that exposes the reprobate among professing disciples: they were not granted to come; the wholesome invitation became their stumbling stone. Both refuse to skip v. 66 or to treat it as unimportant attrition.

John 6:67

67So Jesus said to the twelve, “Do you want to go away as well?”

Arminian

He asks the twelve if they want to go away as well. The question is sincere: they could walk. Remaining is a real response under pressure, not a trance.

Arminian pastoral weight: he does not chain the twelve to the boat. The same Lord who will not cast out a comer (v. 37) lets them feel the cost of staying. The question is grace, not a taunt—an opening to confess and remain.

Reformed

Calvin: as the apostles’ faith might be shaken by seeing so small a remnant, he directs the question to them to confirm them—exhibiting himself that they may remain, exhorting them not to become companions of apostates. Faith founded on Christ will not depend on men.

Reformed accent: deprived of nearly all disciples he retains the twelve (as Isaiah sealed the law among the disciples). Each believer is taught to follow God even without a companion. The question steadies; it does not imply that the given will in fact desert.

John 6:68

68Simon Peter answered him, “Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life,

Arminian

Peter answers for the group: Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life. Wesley: thou, and thou alone, speakest the words which show the way to life everlasting.

Classical Arminians: this is a real confession under the option of leaving. They stay because they have found life-words in him, not because they cannot imagine their legs working. The contrast with v. 66 is voluntary remaining enabled by grace.

Reformed

Calvin: Peter speaks for all (Judas excepted in sincerity). Two clauses: they feel his doctrine wholesome and quickening; and if they leave Christ, only death remains. “Words of life” means quickening—the gospel the power of God for salvation. The Law also contains life but kills transgressors; here life is offered by free grace, not imputing sins.

Reformed accent: he ascribes the words of eternal life to Christ as belonging to him alone. Certain destruction awaits all who, not satisfied with this Teacher, fly to human inventions.

John 6:69

69and we have believed, and have come to know, that you are the Holy One of God.”

Arminian

We have believed and come to know that you are the Holy One of God. Wesley: we who have been with you from the beginning, whatever others do, have known—are absolutely assured—that you are the Christ.

Arminian reading: believing and knowing are their acts, grace-enabled, in contrast to the crowd. The Holy One title is high Christology shared with the Reformed. They remain by a living confession, not by being unable to leave.

Reformed

Calvin: a brief summary of faith. The twelve did not at once comprehend all he had taught; it is enough that according to the capacity of their faith they acknowledge him as Author of salvation and submit in all things. Believe is put first—faith the eye of understanding—then knowledge, which distinguishes faith from false opinion.

Reformed accent: we believe and know. The Holy One of God is the confession that holds when flesh-eating language offended others. Faith here is the fruit of remaining under the Father’s grant, sealed in knowledge.

John 6:70

70Jesus answered them, “Did I not choose you, the twelve? And yet one of you is a devil.”

Arminian

Did I not choose you, the twelve? And yet one of you is a devil. Wesley: even you have not all acted suitable to this knowledge. Have I not chosen or elected you twelve?—but they might fall even from that election. On this gracious warning Judas ought to have repented; he is now influenced by a devil.

Do not skip Judas. Classical Arminians: the choice of the twelve is real, and one of the chosen is a devil. The warning is grace Judas should have received. Election-language here is not an unlosable inner circle; a chosen apostle can be a devil. Remaining in the confession of vv. 68–69 is still required.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ replies to all, so all spoke by Peter’s mouth. He fortifies the eleven against the coming offense of Judas. “Have not I chosen you twelve?” does not refer to the eternal purpose—it is impossible that any predestinated to life shall fall away—but to the apostolic office, in which they ought to have surpassed others in piety.

“One of you is a devil”: the name holds Judas up to detestation. God allows Satan more power over wicked ministers. The honored rank is not despised because one occupant is diabolical; the profanation is fearfully punished. Judas does not overturn the giving of v. 37; office-choice is not life-election.

Key contrast Both hear a real choosing of the twelve and a real devil among them. Arminians (Wesley): they might fall even from that election; the warning is a gracious call for Judas to repent. Reformed (Calvin): “chosen” here is apostolic office, not eternal predestination—those predestinated to life cannot fall; Judas’s devilry does not mean the Father’s given ones are lost. Neither camp should skip the verse or pretend the twelve were a pure band.

John 6:71

71He spoke of Judas the son of Simon Iscariot, for he, one of the twelve, was going to betray him.

Arminian

John names him: Judas, son of Simon Iscariot, one of the twelve, going to betray him. The tragedy is specific, historical, inside the circle.

Classical Arminians: betrayal is Judas’s act. He had the warning (v. 70), the words of life, the same loaves and sea. Foreknowledge (v. 64) did not force his hand. He ought to have repented; he is going to betray. The chapter that began with five thousand ends with a chosen apostle on the path of ruin—and with Peter’s confession still standing for those who will receive the grant on God’s terms.

Reformed

Calvin: though Judas had a bad conscience, we do not read that he was moved. Hypocrites are so stupid they do not feel their sores, and in the presence of men have hardened effrontery.

Reformed close: John will not let the reader miss it—one of the twelve, going to betray. Jesus knew from the beginning (v. 64). The devil-disciple does not cancel the bread of life, the Father’s giving, or the keeping of those truly given. The Holy One remains; betrayal is underway; the passion that makes his flesh true food is already in view.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the hard saying, the Father’s grant, the many who turned back, and Judas among the twelve—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 6:60–71.

Arminian Notable voices

“This is a hard saying — Hard to the children of the world, but sweet to the children of God. Unless it be given — And it is given to those only who will receive it on God’s own terms. From this time many of his disciples went back — So our Lord now began to purge his floor: the proud and careless were driven away. Have I not chosen or elected you twelve? — But they might fall even from that election. Yet one of you — On this gracious warning, Judas ought to have repented; is a devil — Is now influenced by one.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 6:60–70)

Reformed Notable voices

“Therefore have I told you that no man can come to me, unless it be given to him by my Father. Christ assigns a reason why there are so few believers, namely, because no man, whatever may be his acuteness, can arrive at faith by his own sagacity; for all are blind, until they are illuminated by the Spirit of God, and therefore they only partake of so great a blessing whom the Father deigns to make partakers of it. He now uses the word give instead of the word which he formerly used, draw; by which he means that there is no other reason why God draws, than because out of free grace he loves us. From that time many of his disciples went back. It is a dreadful and monstrous thing that so kind and gracious an invitation of Christ could have alienated the minds of many, and especially of those who had formerly professed to belong to him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 6:65–66)
