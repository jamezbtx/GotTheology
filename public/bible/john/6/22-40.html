Passage

John 6

Verses 22–40 · WEB · Voices

The day after the feeding of the five thousand, the crowd tracks Jesus to Capernaum. He tells them they seek him for loaves, not signs, and urges them to work for food that endures to eternal life. When they ask what work God requires, he answers that the work of God is to believe in the one he sent. Jesus declares that he is the bread of life: whoever comes to him will never hunger, and whoever believes will never thirst. He adds that all the Father gives him will come to him, that he will never cast out anyone who comes, and that it is the Father’s will that he lose none of those given him and that everyone who looks to the Son and believes should have eternal life. This passage is at the center of the Arminian and Reformed debate over election, grace, and perseverance.

John 6:22

22On the next day, the multitude that stood on the other side of the sea saw that there was no other boat there, except the one in which his disciples had embarked, and that Jesus hadn’t entered with his disciples into the boat, but his disciples had gone away alone.

Arminian

The crowd notices that Jesus did not leave in the disciples’ boat. Arminian readers see people genuinely drawn to Jesus, even if their motives are mixed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the crowd’s curiosity as the setting for Jesus to expose the difference between outward interest and true faith.

John 6:23

23However, boats from Tiberias came near to the place where they ate the bread after the Lord had given thanks.

Arminian

Boats from Tiberias came near the place where they ate the bread. Arminian readers note John’s careful setting of the scene.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the narrative detail connecting the sign to the discourse.

John 6:24

24When the multitude therefore saw that Jesus wasn’t there, nor his disciples, they themselves got into the boats and came to Capernaum, seeking Jesus.

Arminian

The crowd goes to Capernaum seeking Jesus. Arminian readers see seeking that could become true faith if rightly directed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see seeking that is not yet saving, since it is driven by physical need.

John 6:25

25When they found him on the other side of the sea, they asked him, “Rabbi, when did you come here?”

Arminian

“Rabbi, when did you come here?” Arminian readers see a question that opens a conversation about deeper realities.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the crowd missing the significance of what Jesus has done.

John 6:26

26Jesus answered them, “Most certainly I tell you, you seek me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate of the loaves and were filled.

Arminian

You seek me not because you saw signs, but because you ate the loaves. Arminian readers see Jesus confronting wrong motives in order to call people to true faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus exposing the natural heart, which seeks gifts rather than the Giver.

John 6:27

27Don’t work for the food which perishes, but for the food which remains to eternal life, which the Son of Man will give to you. For God the Father has sealed him.”

Arminian

Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures to eternal life, which the Son will give. Arminian readers see a genuine invitation to everyone in the crowd.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the food as a gift the Son gives, not a wage earned. The command reveals human need and God’s provision.

John 6:28

28They said therefore to him, “What must we do, that we may work the works of God?”

Arminian

“What must we do to be doing the works of God?” Arminian readers see a sincere question about how to respond to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a question that assumes salvation comes by human works.

John 6:29

29Jesus answered them, “This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.”

Arminian

This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent. Arminian readers see faith as the one response God asks, which is not a meritorious work.

Reformed

Reformed readers often read “the work of God” as the work God does in people, producing faith. Others read it as the work God requires.

Key contrast Both agree that faith is not a meritorious work. Arminian readers see faith as the response God requires, enabled by grace. Many Reformed readers see faith here as the work God himself performs in the believer.

John 6:30

30They said therefore to him, “What then do you do for a sign, that we may see and believe you? What work do you do?

Arminian

“What sign do you do, that we may see and believe?” Arminian readers see unbelief demanding more evidence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that signs alone do not produce faith without the Spirit’s work.

John 6:31

31Our fathers ate the manna in the wilderness. As it is written, ‘He gave them bread out of heaven to eat.’”

Arminian

Our fathers ate the manna in the wilderness. Arminian readers see the crowd comparing Jesus unfavorably with Moses.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the manna as a type of Christ, the true bread from heaven.

John 6:32

32Jesus therefore said to them, “Most certainly, I tell you, it wasn’t Moses who gave you the bread out of heaven, but my Father gives you the true bread out of heaven.

Arminian

It was not Moses who gave you the bread from heaven, but my Father gives you the true bread. Arminian readers see the Father offering the true bread to the crowd.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Father as the source of salvation, giving his Son.

John 6:33

33For the bread of God is that which comes down out of heaven and gives life to the world.”

Arminian

The bread of God is he who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world. Arminian readers see “the world” as all humanity, to whom Christ offers life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ giving life to the world in the sense of people from every nation, not only Israel.

John 6:34

34They said therefore to him, “Lord, always give us this bread.”

Arminian

“Lord, give us this bread always.” Arminian readers see a request that could become true faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a request still misunderstanding the nature of the bread.

John 6:35

35Jesus said to them, “I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will not be hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty.

Arminian

I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst. Arminian readers see an open invitation to everyone to come and believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a sure promise to all who come, which God fulfills in those he draws.

John 6:36

36But I told you that you have seen me, and yet you don’t believe.

Arminian

You have seen me and yet do not believe. Arminian readers see unbelief as a refusal of what was clearly shown.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that seeing Christ does not produce faith apart from the Father’s gift, as verse 37 explains.

John 6:37

37All those whom the Father gives me will come to me. He who comes to me I will in no way throw out.

Arminian

All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out. Arminian readers see the Father giving to the Son those who respond in faith, so that the giving follows the Father’s foreknowledge of faith. The second half is an unconditional welcome to everyone who comes.

They stress that the promise not to cast out is open to all.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Father’s giving as prior to and the cause of coming. Everyone the Father gives will certainly come. The welcome of every comer is the means by which the given ones arrive.

They see in this verse both unconditional election and effectual grace.

Key contrast Both affirm that everyone who comes will be received. Arminian readers see the Father giving to the Son those he foreknows will believe. Reformed readers see the Father’s gift as the cause of their coming, so that all who are given will certainly come.

John 6:38

38For I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will, but the will of him who sent me.

Arminian

I have come down from heaven to do the will of him who sent me. Arminian readers see Christ’s perfect obedience to the Father’s saving purpose.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Son carrying out the Father’s eternal plan of redemption.

John 6:39

39This is the will of my Father who sent me, that of all he has given to me I should lose nothing, but should raise him up at the last day.

Arminian

This is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all he has given me, but raise it up on the last day. Arminian readers see the Father’s will that none be lost, which does not override human freedom to leave. They point to Judas (17:12).

Reformed

Reformed readers see a guarantee that every person the Father has given to the Son will be raised up. None will be lost, because Christ will not fail to do the Father’s will.

Key contrast Both see Christ committed to raising those the Father has given him. Arminian readers see the Father’s will that none be lost, without excluding the possibility that someone might turn away. Reformed readers see an unconditional guarantee that none of the given will be lost.

John 6:40

40This is the will of the one who sent me, that everyone who sees the Son and believes in him should have eternal life; and I will raise him up at the last day.”

Arminian

Everyone who looks on the Son and believes in him should have eternal life, and I will raise him up. Arminian readers see “everyone who believes” as the defining condition of salvation, open to all.

Reformed

Reformed readers see verse 40 explaining verse 39 from the human side. The given ones are those who look and believe.

Voices from both camps

What earlier interpreters on each side wrote about John 6:22–40.

Arminian Notable voices

“I am the bread of life — Having and giving life: he that cometh — he that believeth — Equivalent expressions: shall never hunger, thirst — Shall be satisfied, happy, for ever. All that the Father giveth me — All that feel themselves lost, and follow the drawings of the Father, he in a peculiar manner giveth to the Son: will come to me — By faith.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 6:22–40, especially 6:37)

Reformed Notable voices

“All that the Father giveth me. That their unbelief may not detract anything from his doctrine, he says, that the cause of so great obstinacy is, that they are reprobate, and do not belong to the flock of God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 6:22–40, especially 6:37)
