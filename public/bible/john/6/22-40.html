Passage

John 6

Verses 22–40 · ESV · Voices

The next day the crowd that ate the loaves crosses to Capernaum seeking Jesus. He exposes their motive, names himself the bread of life, and promises that all the Father gives will come, and that whoever comes he will never cast out.

John 6:22

22On the next day the crowd that remained on the other side of the sea saw that there had been only one boat there, and that Jesus had not entered the boat with his disciples, but that his disciples had gone away alone.

Arminian

The crowd stayed because there had been only one boat, and they saw that Jesus had not embarked (Wesley). They remain on the scene of the loaves, still wanting the Provider they think they can find by ordinary means.

Classical Arminians hear responsible seekers. The night’s absence did not cancel the day’s mercy. They will soon misread why they seek him, yet the seeking itself is a real human motion toward a Savior who still lets himself be found.

Reformed

Calvin: circumstances from which the multitude might conclude he had crossed by divine power. One boat left; Jesus was not a passenger. The next day’s hunt is already framed by a sign they have not yet understood.

Reformed accent: providence, not luck, stages the Capernaum sermon. The empty shore and the missing Lord are the Father’s setup for the bread-of-life word, not a gap in the story.

John 6:23

23Other boats from Tiberias came near the place where they had eaten the bread after the Lord had given thanks.

Arminian

Other boats arrive near the feeding place. John again notes that the Lord had given thanks: the meal remains gift, not a trick of sharing. Means of travel now stand at the grass where they ate.

Arminian pastoral note: ordinary boats become the way they may go after him. Grace often arrives in common things. They can take the passage or stay; the opportunity is sincere.

Reformed

Calvin prefers that the ships reached the shore near that place, sailing along where the crowd stood. The second mention of thanks is not idle: Christ obtained by prayer that few loaves fed many.

Reformed reading: the boats are appointed ferries. Tiberias, the feeding ground, and Capernaum are under the same hand that multiplied barley. Travel serves the coming discourse.

John 6:24

24So when the crowd saw that Jesus was not there, nor his disciples, they themselves got into the boats and went to Capernaum, seeking Jesus.

Arminian

They see he is not there, take the boats, and go to Capernaum seeking Jesus. Seeking is not yet believing, but it is not nothing. Wesley will soon correct the motive, not the motion of going after him.

Classical Arminians: the invitation to find him is still open. Crowds may come for mixed reasons and still be honestly summoned to a better hunger. Capernaum will test whether they want the Giver or only more loaves.

Reformed

Calvin pictures them blockading the shore, then sailing when other ships appear. Seeking Jesus is not yet the faith the sermon will require. Movement toward him can still be unbelief in motion.

Reformed accent: Capernaum is the appointed classroom. The Son who hid from a crown now lets the seekers arrive so that the word, not the stomach, may sift them.

John 6:25

25When they found him on the other side of the sea, they said to him, “Rabbi, when did you come here?”

Arminian

They find him and ask, “Rabbi, when did you come here?” Wesley: he will not satisfy their curiosity. The title is respectful; the question is small. They could have asked who he is, or how they ought to live after the sign.

Arminian note: finding him is a real mercy. How they use the finding is theirs. A sincere Teacher will turn the when into a why—and the why will still be an address they can receive or refuse.

Reformed

Calvin: “the other side” is popular speech; Capernaum sits on a winding shore, not a straight opposite bank. He does not answer the when, because the how would only feed wonder at a miracle they already mean to misuse.

Reformed reading: Rabbi is true and still too small. The One they found walked the sea and is about to name himself bread from heaven. The question exposes how little they have yet seen.

John 6:26

26Jesus answered them, “Truly, truly, I say to you, you are seeking me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate your fill of the loaves.

Arminian

Truly, truly: they seek him not because they saw signs but because they ate their fill. Wesley: he corrects the wrong motive—merely for temporal advantage. Hitherto he had been gathering hearers; now he tries their sincerity.

Classical Arminians take the rebuke as a real kindness. Mixed motives do not make the later invitation theater. He wants them to seek him for himself. The sign was sincere; their use of it is culpable, not programmed.

Reformed

Calvin: they seek Christ for the belly, not for the miracles rightly used. They would have him as a giver of ease in this world, not as Messiah who forms them anew by the Spirit and clothes them with righteousness.

Reformed accent: to rest in conveniences is to rob him of his chief power. The doubled “truly” uncovers hypocrisy that can look like zeal. Seeing signs without surrender is unbelief with a full stomach.

John 6:27

27Do not work for the food that perishes, but for the food that endures to eternal life, which the Son of Man will give to you. For on him God the Father has set his seal.”

Arminian

Do not work for perishable food, but for food that endures to eternal life, which the Son of Man will give. Wesley: not for bodily food only or chiefly; labour for everlasting life—work for life as well as from life, from faith and love. The Father sealed him by this miracle and by his whole testimony.

Arminian reading: the command is a genuine summons. They must strive after the lasting food; the Son will give what they cannot earn. Seal means authenticity: this Giver is the Father’s marked envoy. Grace enables the labour; it does not cancel it.

Reformed

Calvin: he looses them from earthly cords before pointing them to eternal life. “Labour” is metaphor, not merit—Christ himself giveth the food; what we obtain by his gift no man procures by industry. The Father’s seal marks the Son as the authorized dispenser of incorruptible food.

Reformed accent: the heavenly life is preferred to the earthly, yet the food is gift. Sealing is commission, not a stamp we add by effort. Those who present their souls to this sealed Son will not be disappointed.

John 6:28

28Then they said to him, “What must we do, to be doing the works of God?”

Arminian

They hear “work” and ask for tasks: the works of God—Wesley: works pleasing to God. The question is not wicked in itself. It is still too much “do” and not yet “believe,” but it is a real inquiry he will answer with a real command.

Classical Arminians: people often want a list. He will not mock the question; he will relocate it. The door they are knocking on is not closed; it opens onto faith in the Sent One.

Reformed

Calvin: they partly mistake the kind of labor and talk without a definite object, ignorant of grace. “Works of God” means what God demands and approves. They still think in performances he will itemize.

Reformed reading: the question exposes a heart that has not yet learned to receive. He will name one work, and even that work will bring nothing to God but empty hands.

John 6:29

29Jesus answered them, “This is the work of God, that you believe in him whom he has sent.”

Arminian

This is the work of God: that you believe in him whom he has sent. Wesley: the work most pleasing to God, and the foundation of all others. He says it first properly, later figuratively. Faith is the commanded response—enabled by grace, yet the human act God is pleased with.

Arminian pastoral weight: the invitation to believe is genuine for these hearers. Believing the Sent One is not a hidden assignment for a few. It is the one “work” the crowd is actually called to do. Other works are not despised; they live inside this faith.

Reformed

Calvin: he reminds them of one work, faith; without it all is vain. Those who infer from this verse that faith is here named as God’s producing gift are mistaken—Christ shows what God requires, not yet what God works in us. Yet faith is no meritorious wage: it is, Calvin says, a passive work that brings nothing and receives Christ.

Reformed accent: believe the Sent One. Verse 37 will soon locate the power to do so in the Father’s giving. Requirement and gift will stand together without turning faith into a paycheck.

Key contrast Both name believing the Sent One as the required “work.” Arminians typically hear a sincere command the hearers can, by assisting grace, obey. Reformed readers typically hear faith as what God requires, yet not a wage—and they will soon (v. 37) locate the power to believe in the Father’s giving.

John 6:30

30So they said to him, “Then what sign do you do, that we may see and believe you? What work do you perform?

Arminian

They ask for a sign after the loaves. Wesley: Amazing, after what they had just seen! Unbelief can demand new evidence while standing on yesterday’s miracle. The request does not cancel the sincerity of Jesus’ call to believe; it shows how they are using their freedom poorly.

Classical Arminians: seeing and believing are being bargained. He has already given a sign; they remain responsible for treating it as insufficient. Grace keeps speaking; they keep raising the price.

Reformed

Calvin: this wicked generation seeks a sign. Sudden forgetfulness of the feeding is ingratitude. When he will not flatter the flesh, they begin to treat him as unknown. Asking for proof after a public miracle is already a verdict on the heart.

Reformed accent: unbelief is not lack of data. The same crowd that would have crowned him now cross-examines him. Signs serve the Son; they do not become a vending machine for faith.

John 6:31

31Our fathers ate the manna in the wilderness; as it is written, ‘He gave them bread from heaven to eat.’”

Arminian

They cite manna: He gave them bread from heaven. Wesley: this sign Moses gave; bread from the lower, sublunary heaven, against which Jesus will set the highest heaven. The Scripture is true; the use is a bargain—match Moses and we will believe.

Arminian note: they remain accountable for that bargain. A sincere gift (the loaves, and now the true bread) can be turned into a demand. The fathers’ manna does not obligate the Son to feed bellies on their terms.

Reformed

Calvin: they extol manna to bury his rebuke of belly-religion. When the Spirit calls manna bread of heaven, it is not so that God might fatten a herd and give nothing more valuable. They weaponize Psalm 78 against the true bread now offered.

Reformed reading: Scripture quoted against the Word made flesh is still unbelief. The wilderness gift was real; it was never the last word. The contrast they want (Moses vs. Jesus on groceries) is the contrast he will refuse.

John 6:32

32Jesus then said to them, “Truly, truly, I say to you, it was not Moses who gave you the bread from heaven, but my Father gives you the true bread from heaven.

Arminian

Not Moses but the Father gives the true bread from heaven. Present tense: gives. Wesley: it was not Moses who gave the manna; the Father now gives the true bread. The gift is being offered to these hearers now.

Classical Arminians hear a real giving of true bread to the crowd, not a private inventory. The Father’s generosity is on the table. Whether they will receive the loaf as a Person remains their responsible answer.

Reformed

Calvin: manna is “bread of heaven” only by comparison; it fed the body. The bread that is truly heavenly is what gives spiritual life. He names the Father as Giver so that Christ is received as God’s minister feeding to eternal life—not Moses versus God, but Moses versus Christ.

Reformed accent: the true bread is given from above. The present “gives” is the Father’s act in sending the Son. What they demand as menu he will soon identify as himself.

John 6:33

33For the bread of God is he who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world.”

Arminian

The bread of God is he who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world. Wesley: not, like the manna, to one people only, and that from generation to generation. He does not yet say “I am that bread,” else they would not have answered so respectfully (v. 34).

Arminian reading: “world” is wide. Life is actually given in the descending Son. High Christology is shared: this is no mere teacher still on the mountain. The world’s life is sincerely offered in him.

Reformed

Calvin: heavenly bread has come down from heaven to give life to the world; manna had nothing of this sort. The whole world is dead to God except so far as Christ quickens it. Life will be found nowhere else than in him.

Reformed accent: two things in the descent—we have divine life in Christ because he came from God; and that life is near, so we need not fly the clouds. The bread of God is a Person, already among them.

John 6:34

34They said to him, “Sir, give us this bread always.”

Arminian

“Sir, give us this bread always.” Wesley: they still mean it in a literal sense, yet seem now not far from believing. The request can be the start of real faith if they will take him as he is, not as a standing bakery.

Classical Arminians hear a door still ajar. Respectful speech is not yet coming and believing (v. 35), but it is not a sneer either. He will answer the ask by giving them himself.

Reformed

Calvin hears irony: they accuse him of vain boasting when he said he could give the bread of life. Wretched men reject the promise and then put Christ in the dock for their unbelief.

Reformed reading: whether naïve or mocking, they still want bread more than the Bread. “Always” can mean a supply line. He will not become their manna machine; he will name himself the loaf.

John 6:35

35Jesus said to them, “I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger, and whoever believes in me shall never thirst.

Arminian

I am the bread of life: whoever comes shall not hunger; whoever believes shall never thirst. Wesley: having and giving life; coming and believing are equivalent expressions; they shall be satisfied, happy, forever. Shared ground: Jesus himself is the loaf; looking to him in faith ends the soul’s famine.

Classical Arminians stress the open “whoever.” Coming is a genuine invitation to hungry people. The promise is not a decoy; those who come and believe are truly filled. Grace draws; the coming remains a real human act under that drawing, not a formality after a closed list.

Reformed

Calvin: two parts of the doctrine—whence we ought to seek life, and how we may enjoy it. Coming and believing here mean the same: we fly to Christ driven by hunger. It is of no avail to unbelievers that he is the bread of life; they remain empty. He becomes our bread when we come as hungry persons that he may fill us.

Reformed accent: shared confession—he is the bread of life; faith embraces him so that he dwells in us. The “whoever” is as wide as coming. Verse 37 will say who actually comes: those the Father gives. The filling is real for all who do.

Key contrast Both confess that Jesus is the bread of life and that coming/believing ends hunger and thirst. Arminians typically stress the open “whoever” as a genuine invitation any hearer may, by resistible grace, accept. Reformed readers typically agree the hungry are filled, but read “whoever comes” with v. 37: those who come are those the Father gives.

John 6:36

36But I said to you that you have seen me and yet do not believe.

Arminian

They have seen him and do not believe. Wesley points back to v. 26. Seeing is not the same as coming. Their unbelief is culpable; the bread has been set before their eyes.

Arminian pastoral note: refusal is a real human no, not evidence that they were never addressed. The invitation of v. 35 stands even as he names their unbelief. Light refused is still light.

Reformed

Calvin: wicked contempt of a gift they might have known. If he had not shown his power, ignorance might have lessened the guilt; they reject the doctrine of him they had treated as Messiah. Unbelievers shut their eyes; malice prevented knowledge.

Reformed accent: this sight-without-faith sets up v. 37. Unbelief does not empty his doctrine. Not all who see are his; the children of God will embrace the word the rest refuse.

John 6:37

37All that the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never cast out.

Arminian

All that the Father gives will come; whoever comes he will never cast out. Wesley: all who feel themselves lost and follow the Father’s drawings he in a peculiar manner gives to the Son; they will come by faith; the one who thus comes is in nowise cast out—pardon, holiness, and heaven, if he endure to the end.

Classical Arminian reading: the Father’s giving is consistent with drawings that are strong and sweet yet resistible (Wesley on v. 44). Those who feel their lostness and yield are the ones given. “Will come” describes those who do follow, not a force that makes coming automatic. Faith is the condition of receiving. The second clause is a sincere promise to all who come: none who actually come are turned away. “Never cast out” is gospel welcome, not a comfort reserved for a prior closed list. The invitation of v. 35 is not canceled here; it is confirmed.

Reformed

Calvin: their unbelief must not detract from his doctrine. The obstinate are reprobate and not of his flock; he distinguishes elect and reprobate so that the authority of the word remains, though many do not believe. All whom the Father gives come: faith is not a random act of human will; God elects those he hands over to the Son. “Whatever is given comes” implies that all do not come. Give means: those the Father has chosen he regenerates and gives to the Son, that they may obey the gospel. The Spirit’s efficacy is such that not one of them falls away.

Coming is the fruit of being given, not the cause. “Whoever comes I will never cast out” is consolation for the godly who do come: free access by faith, gracious reception, a good and faithful Teacher. It is the comfort of the elect who, having been given, actually arrive—not a pledge that every hearer will. Shared ground remains: the coming ones are never cast out, and Jesus is still the bread they come to eat.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take “whoever comes” as a genuine invitation, the Father’s giving as consistent with resistible grace, and faith as the condition of receiving—“never cast out” a sincere promise to all who come. Reformed readings typically take “all the Father gives will come” as effectual: coming is the fruit of being given, those given are preserved, and “never cast out” is the comfort of the elect who do come.

John 6:38

38For I have come down from heaven, not to do my own will but the will of him who sent me.

Arminian

He came down from heaven not to do his own will but the will of the One who sent him. The descent is incarnation—shared high Christology. The Son’s will is not a rival decree against a wide welcome; it is obedience that makes the promise of v. 37 trustworthy.

Arminian pastoral note: those who come are not risking a Son who might refuse them. The Father’s will, as v. 40 will say, is that everyone who looks and believes should have life. The down-coming is for that will, not against it.

Reformed

Calvin: confirmation that we do not seek him in vain. Faith is a work of God by which he shows we are his people and appoints the Son protector of our salvation. The Son’s only design is to fulfill the Father’s commands; he will never reject those the Father has sent. Faith will never be useless.

Reformed accent: no imagined split between Father and Son can produce hesitation. He has been manifested to ratify what the Father decreed concerning salvation. The descent serves that decree.

John 6:39

39And this is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but raise it up on the last day.

Arminian

The Sender’s will: lose nothing of all given, but raise it up on the last day. Wesley: of all already given (cf. John 17:6, 12)—if they endure to the end. But Judas did not. Preservation is promised to those who remain in the given company by enduring faith, not an unconditional lock irrespective of continuance.

Classical Arminians hear a real last-day hope for true believers and a real warning in Judas. The Son is no careless shepherd; he means to keep those who keep coming. “Lose nothing” does not erase the call to endure. Shared: the given are aimed at resurrection, not abandonment.

Reformed

Calvin: this is the Father’s design, that believers find salvation secured in Christ. He is not guardian for a day but to the end, conducting from commencement to the last resurrection. Believers, supported by Christ’s hand, advance to that day; without that hand the world’s salvation might be ruined at every moment.

Reformed accent: effectual preservation of all the Father has given. The Son does not fail. Those who do not profit by the gospel willingly draw ruin on themselves; the gospel still gathers the elect. The last-day raising is certain for that given people—the same “all” who, in v. 37, will come.

Key contrast Arminians typically hear “lose nothing” with Wesley’s proviso—endure to the end; Judas shows that one “given” in a wider sense may fall. Reformed readers typically hear effectual preservation of all the Father has given: the Son does not fail, and the last-day raising is certain for that given people.

John 6:40

40For this is the will of my Father, that everyone who looks on the Son and believes in him should have eternal life, and I will raise him up on the last day.”

Arminian

The Father’s will: everyone who looks on the Son and believes should have eternal life, and Jesus will raise him. Wesley: the sum of vv. 37–39; this is the whole of what he has said—the eternal, unchangeable will of God. Every one who truly believes shall have everlasting life. The Jews saw and yet believed not. He will perform the raising effectually.

Shared ground: looking and believing has eternal life; the last day belongs to the Son. Classical Arminians treat looking/believing as the appointed, grace-enabled condition any hearer may fulfill. The “everyone” is as wide as the invitation. Eternal life is genuinely offered to those who look. The promise is not bait; it is the Father’s will.

Reformed

Calvin: the way to obtain salvation is to obey the gospel of Christ. Election is hidden; God manifests it by calling. Do not seek salvation in the whirlpool of predestination while ignoring the way exhibited. To every man his faith is sufficient attestation of God’s eternal predestination. Seeing and believing, joined, mark the sons of God—against v. 36, where some saw and did not believe.

Reformed accent: shared preaching—everyone who looks and believes has eternal life and will be raised. That looking is the fruit of the Father’s giving (v. 37) and the revealed path by which the elect are saved and assured. The Son executes the Father’s will; the raising matches v. 39’s “lose nothing.”

Key contrast Both preach that everyone who looks on the Son and believes has eternal life and will be raised. Arminians typically treat looking/believing as the resistible, grace-enabled condition any hearer may fulfill. Reformed readers typically treat that looking as the fruit of the Father’s giving (v. 37) and as the revealed path by which the elect are saved and assured.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jesus as the bread of life and the promise that all the Father gives will come—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 6:22–40.

Arminian Notable voices

“I am the bread of life — Having and giving life: he that cometh — he that believeth — Equivalent expressions: shall never hunger, thirst — Shall be satisfied, happy, for ever. All that the Father giveth me — All that feel themselves lost, and follow the drawings of the Father, he in a peculiar manner giveth to the Son: will come to me — By faith. And him that thus cometh to me, I will in nowise cast out — I will give him pardon, holiness, and heaven, if he endure to the end. Of all which he hath already given me — If they endure to the end. But Judas did not. This is the eternal, unchangeable will of God. Every one who truly believeth, shall have everlasting life.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 6:22–40, especially 6:37)

Reformed Notable voices

“All that the Father giveth me. That their unbelief may not detract anything from his doctrine, he says, that the cause of so great obstinacy is, that they are reprobate, and do not belong to the flock of God. He says that all whom the Father giveth him come to him; by which words he means, that faith is not a thing which depends on the will of men, so that this man and that man indiscriminately and at random believe, but that God elects those whom he hands over, as it were, to his Son; for when he says, that whatever is given cometh, we infer from it, that all do not come. Again, we infer, that God works in his elect by such an efficacy of the Holy Spirit, that not one of them falls away; for the word give has the same meaning as if Christ had said, “Those whom the Father hath chosen he regenerates, and gives to me, that they may obey the Gospel.” And him that cometh to me I will not cast out. This is added for the consolation of the godly, that they may be fully persuaded that they have free access to Christ by faith, and that they will be graciously received by him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 6:22–40, especially 6:37)
