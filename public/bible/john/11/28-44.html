Passage

John 11

Verses 28–44 · ESV · Voices

Martha calls Mary in private: the Teacher is here and is calling for you. Mary rises quickly; mourners follow, thinking she goes to the tomb. She falls at Jesus’ feet with the same if-you-had-been-here. He is deeply moved, asks where they laid him, and weeps. At the cave he says, Take away the stone. Martha names the four-day odor. If you believed, you would see the glory of God. He thanks the Father aloud so that the crowd may believe the Father sent him, then cries, Lazarus, come out. The dead man comes bound; Jesus says, Unbind him, and let him go.

John 11:28

28When she had said this, she went and called her sister Mary, saying in private, “The Teacher is here and is calling for you.”

Arminian

The Teacher is here and is calling for you. Martha’s first act after confessing is to fetch her sister. The call is personal and private—Mary by name, not a crowd slogan.

Arminian pastoral note: those who have said Yes, Lord often become messengers. The same Lord who asked Martha “Do you believe?” now calls Mary. Being called is a real address that expects her to rise.

Reformed

Calvin: Master shows how these women ranked him. They had not profited as they might, yet they were devoted as disciples.

Reformed note: the call to Mary is Christ’s. Martha carries it, but the Teacher summons. Secret providence will also draw the Jews after her to the tomb they did not mean to see opened.

John 11:29

29And when she heard it, she rose quickly and went to him.

Arminian

Heard—rose quickly—went. Mary’s slowness in the house becomes haste at his call. Wesley will note that falling at his feet makes amends for coming late.

Classical Arminians hear a faith-response in motion. The call is effectual in the sense that she actually comes; it is still a call she hears and answers, not a shove without her will.

Reformed

Sudden departure, Calvin said, proved no ordinary reverence. The sheep knows the Teacher’s voice even in grief.

Reformed accent: she rises because he calls. Quick obedience here is the fruit of a word, the same pattern soon made visible when a dead man hears his name.

John 11:30

30Now Jesus had not yet come into the village, but was still in the place where Martha had met him.

Arminian

He waits outside. The meeting-place of v. 20 becomes Mary’s meeting-place too. He does not force a scene in the packed house first.

Arminian note: the Lord can be met on the road. Location is pastoral. He stays where a sister can reach him without yet turning the village into a riot.

Reformed

He remains where Martha met him. Caution and purpose together: the audience will be those who follow Mary, thinking they go to weep.

Reformed framing: even the spot on the path is appointed. Witnesses will be led, as Calvin said of the Jews, where they did not intend to go.

John 11:31

31When the Jews who were with her in the house, consoling her, saw Mary rise quickly and go out, they followed her, supposing that she was going to the tomb to weep there.

Arminian

They misread the haste as a trip to weep at the grave. Consolers become accidental witnesses.

Arminian observation: motives in a funeral crowd are mixed and ordinary. God will still set life in front of them. What they do with the sight remains their act—believe, or walk to the Pharisees.

Reformed

Calvin: they think she goes to inflame grief, a common disease. They even accompany her toward excess rather than restrain it. Yet Christ had another design: that they might be witnesses of the miracle.

Reformed note: secret providence leads men in darkness where they did not mean to go. The crowd’s supposition is wrong; their feet are still on time.

John 11:32

32Now when Mary came to where Jesus was and saw him, she fell at his feet, saying to him, “Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died.”

Arminian

Wesley: she fell at his feet—this Martha had not done; so she makes amends for slowness in coming. Same sentence as Martha’s, different posture.

Arminian pastoral note: identical words can carry different worship. Falling down is faith’s body-language. The if-clause still hurts; it is spoken from the ground at his feet, not from a stand-off.

Reformed

Calvin: falling at his feet honors him beyond ordinary custom; she would not have done it unconvinced he was the Son of God. The if-you-had-been-here still wrongly limits his power to bodily presence.

Reformed care: reverence and a cramped Christology can travel together. He will answer her grief with his own tears and then with a call that does not need to have “been here” on time.

John 11:33

33When Jesus saw her weeping, and the Jews who had come with her also weeping, he was deeply moved in his spirit and greatly troubled.

Arminian

He sees her weeping and the crowd weeping, and he is deeply moved and troubled. Wesley: he groaned, restraining tears at first; “he troubled himself”—affections not passions out of control, but voluntary emotions in his power, full of order and reason.

Arminian weight: the Lord who is resurrection still chooses to feel with mourners. Sympathy is not weakness in the invitation. The One who will ask faith also enters the tears of those he calls.

Reformed

Calvin: he groaned in spirit, conforming to mourners so far as to weep with them—proof of sympathy—and contemplated the general misery of the race he was sent to free. His feelings were sinless, bounded, founded on reason, unlike our unrestrained grief.

Reformed confession: the Son clothed himself with human feelings, sin excepted, that we might have a Mediator who has endured distresses in himself. Trouble here is holy, aimed at the tyranny of death he is about to break.

John 11:34

34And he said, “Where have you laid him?” They said to him, “Lord, come and see.”

Arminian

Where have you laid him? He asks what he could know. Come and see—the mourners become guides.

Arminian note: he involves them. They must lead him to the cave, later take the stone, later unbind. Faith’s Lord does not flatten human action; he commands it.

Reformed

He asks the place of burial as one walking with them, not as a magician skipping the grave.

Reformed note: the question draws the crowd to the exact spot of the sign. Come and see will become see the glory. Their knowledge of the tomb will certify that the man who comes out is the man they laid in.

John 11:35

35Jesus wept.

Arminian

Two words. Wesley: out of sympathy with those in tears around him, and from a deep sense of the misery sin had brought upon human nature.

Arminian pastoral note: the shortest verse is not a shrug. The Lord of life cries at a friend’s grave. An invitation to believe is not icy. He is for the weeping, not above it.

Reformed

Calvin located the feeling in what he saw—Mary and the rest weeping—and in the higher sight of the race’s misery. He shows he is as much affected by our distresses as if he had endured them himself.

Reformed comfort: tears here are the champion’s, not the victim’s collapse. He weeps as he goes to war with death’s tyranny, a brother in feeling and Lord in power.

John 11:36

36So the Jews said, “See how he loved him!”

Arminian

They read the tears as love, and they are not wrong. See how he loved him—the sisters’ first message vindicated in public.

Classical Arminians take the crowd at this better word. Love is visible. Some will stop here, as if love were only affection without power; others will follow love into the next sign.

Reformed

Calvin: this first opinion ascribes to him only what may belong to a man, yet it is more candid than the next verse’s slander. They speak with modesty they will not all keep.

Reformed note: love is truly seen. It is not the whole of what the tears mean. The love that weeps is the love that delayed for glory and will soon call a corpse by name.

John 11:37

37But some of them said, “Could not he who opened the eyes of the blind man also have kept this man from dying?”

Arminian

Wesley: they never dreamed he could raise him again—what a strange mixture of faith and unbelief. They remember the blind man and still shrink the miracle to prevention.

Arminian warning: last chapter’s light does not automatically become this chapter’s faith. The same people can grant he opened eyes and still not expect a grave to empty. Unbelief often sounds like a disappointed compliment.

Reformed

Calvin: they maliciously slander him for not hindering death, applauding his power only to reproach its pause. Ingratitude complains, Why does he who used to help now disappoint?

Reformed diagnosis: two diseases—subjecting God to the flesh’s wishes, and rude impatience that hurries him. They want a timely prevention; he intends a resurrection.

John 11:38

38Then Jesus, deeply moved again, came to the tomb. It was a cave, and a stone lay against it.

Arminian

Deeply moved again, he comes to the tomb. A cave, a stone—ordinary Judean burial, like the patriarchs’ caves Wesley recalled.

Arminian note: he approaches the place of death rather than calling from a distance only. The stone is a real barrier he will have others move. Glory will use hands.

Reformed

Calvin: he does not come as an idle spectator but as a champion preparing for contest; he groans because the violent tyranny of death is before his eyes.

Reformed framing: the cave and stone will magnify the power about to be shown. Four days, a sealed tomb, a crowd—then a voice. The wrestling is with death, not with the mourners’ manners only.

John 11:39

39Jesus said, “Take away the stone.” Martha, the sister of the dead man, said to him, “Lord, by this time there will be an odor, for he has been dead four days.”

Arminian

Take away the stone—a command to bystanders. Martha, who just confessed him as Christ, now names decay. Wesley: thus did reason and faith struggle together.

Arminian honesty: real faith can flinch at a smell. Her yes in v. 27 was not fake because her nose now objects. He does not discard her; he reminds her of the word she already heard.

Reformed

Calvin: this is distrust, measuring God’s infinite power by the perception of the flesh. Nothing is more inconsistent with life than putrefaction, so she infers no remedy—and so far as she can, would keep Lazarus in the tomb.

Reformed realism: we stretch one hand for aid and repel it with the other. Confused faith, even in the excellent, needs a second and third word before the stone moves.

John 11:40

40Jesus said to her, “Did I not tell you that if you believed you would see the glory of God?”

Arminian

Wesley: it appears Christ had said more to Martha than is recorded. If you believed, you would see the glory of God—belief as the way of seeing, not as a trick condition.

Classical Arminians hear a genuine if. Glory is not withheld from a sincere believer to prove a point; it is promised to faith. Martha is still being invited to trust the word she has, four days and odor notwithstanding.

Reformed

Calvin: he reproves her for not forming a hope vigorous enough from the promise. Something more was said than John relates; this was meant when he called himself the resurrection and the life.

Reformed pairing: faith opens eyes to see God’s power, and faith prepares the way for that power to be displayed toward us. Unbelief shuts God’s hands, as far as men can; he is not bound by their caprice, yet he often withholds the manifestation they have refused.

Key contrast Shared ground: believing and seeing the glory belong together, as Jesus told her. Arminians typically take the if as a sincere condition addressed to a responsible sister who may yet trust. Reformed readers typically take believing as the gift-way God has appointed for seeing glory—Martha blamed for weak hope, faith itself the Spirit’s work that does not originate in the smell-test of the flesh.

John 11:41

41So they took away the stone. And Jesus lifted up his eyes and said, “Father, I thank you that you have heard me.

Arminian

They take the stone; he lifts his eyes. Wesley: not as if he applied for assistance—there is not the least show of that. He wrought the miracle with an air of absolute sovereignty, as Lord of life and death, thanking the Father for the providential opportunity of showing forth his praise.

Arminian hearing: sovereignty here is the Son’s own. Thanksgiving is public, so the crowd may know the Father’s fellowship with the Son who is about to call. Hands moved the stone because he said so.

Reformed

Calvin: Martha, hearing the Son of God, permits the stone to be removed, relying on his authority while she still sees nothing. He thanks the Father, having been heard—glory of deity in the flesh, accommodated to dull senses.

Reformed note: the lifted eyes train others to the Father. He is not an independent wonder-worker; the sign will certify that the Father sent him, even as the voice that follows is his own divine power.

John 11:42

42I knew that you always hear me, but I said this on account of the people standing around, that they may believe that you sent me.”

Arminian

Always heard—yet he speaks aloud for the bystanders, that they may believe the Father sent him. The prayer is missional, aimed at a crowd that can still believe.

Arminian weight: “that they may believe” is a sincere purpose toward people standing around a grave. The sign is not a private seal for an inner circle only. Whoever in that crowd who believes will have done what the spoken thanks was for.

Reformed

Calvin: he anticipates the thought that he might not stand high enough with the Father to do as many miracles as he chose. Agreement is total; he need not pray as if uncertain, but he calls on the Father so men may be sure the work is divine.

Reformed limit: why not raise all the dead? A fixed limit was assigned to miracles, sufficient for confirming the gospel. The purpose “that they may believe” does not make faith a native power in the crowd; it sets the appointed means by which God creates it.

John 11:43

43When he had said these things, he cried out with a loud voice, “Lazarus, come out.”

Arminian

Wesley: he cried with a loud voice that all present might hear. Lazarus, come forth—he called him out as easily as if he had been not only alive but awake. The name keeps the call particular; the loudness keeps it public.

Arminian note: a command to a dead man is still a word meant to be heard. The God who sincerely invites the living “whoever believes” here shows that his voice can reach even a corpse. Ease is not coldness; it is sufficiency. The same Lord calls the spiritually dead by the gospel with a word that can be refused by the living and cannot be refused by Lazarus.

Reformed

Calvin: by not touching with the hand, but only crying with the voice, divine power is more fully shown. How did he restore life but by the word? In raising Lazarus he exhibited a visible token of the spiritual grace we experience when his voice gives life.

Reformed center: this is effectual call. “Lazarus, come out” does not wait on the corpse’s cooperation. The voice that created now recreates. What the tomb displays, regeneration secretly answers: the dead hear the Son and live (John 5:25). Life is given by the call, not offered to a merely sleepy will.

Key contrast Shared ground: Jesus’ loud, named command actually raises Lazarus; the word does the work. Arminians typically keep this unique, irresistible miracle distinct from the gospel’s sincere call to living hearers, which can be refused. Reformed readers typically take the scene as the public token of always-effectual spiritual calling: the same voice gives life to the elect dead, and Lazarus is the picture, not the exception.

John 11:44

44The man who had died came out, his hands and feet bound with linen strips, and his face wrapped with a cloth. Jesus said to them, “Unbind him, and let him go.”

Arminian

The dead man comes out still bound. Wesley noted the wrappings: if buried Egyptian-style, the napkin might leave the face able to see the way. Unbind him, and let him go—others must finish what they can finish.

Arminian close: life is Jesus’ gift; unwrapping is the community’s task. Those who watched are commanded to handle the miracle. New life is not meant to stay in graveclothes, and the command to unbind is a real charge to the living.

Reformed

Calvin: napkin and bandages prove he came out as he was laid in. Christ could have dropped the bindings himself; he intended to employ the spectators’ hands as witnesses.

Reformed doxology: they must touch the work they saw. The man lives because the voice lived in him; their unbinding certifies, it does not cause, the resurrection. So the church looses what Christ has already made alive.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the Teacher’s tears, the stone, and a loud call that wakes the dead—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 11:28–44.

Arminian Notable voices

“Jesus wept—Out of sympathy with those who were in tears all around him, as well as from a deep sense of the misery sin had brought upon human nature. Jesus lifted up his eyes—Not as if he applied to his Father for assistance. There is not the least show of this. He wrought the miracle with an air of absolute sovereignty, as the Lord of life and death. He cried with a loud voice—That all who were present might hear. Lazarus, come forth—Jesus called him out of the tomb as easily as if he had been not only alive, but awake also.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 11:35, 41, 43)

Reformed Notable voices

“By not touching with the hand, but only crying with the voice, his Divine power is more fully demonstrated. At the same time, he holds out to our view the secret and astonishing efficacy of his word. For how did Christ restore life to the dead but by the word? And therefore, in raising Lazarus, he exhibited a visible token of his spiritual grace, which we experience every day by the perception of faith, when he shows that his voice gives life.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 11:43)
