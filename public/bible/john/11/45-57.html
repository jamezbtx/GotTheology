Passage

John 11

Verses 45–57 · WEB · Voices

Many of the Jews who came to Mary and seen what Jesus did believed in him; some went to the Pharisees. The council panics over signs and Rome. Caiaphas, high priest that year, says it is better that one man die for the people than that the nation perish. Now he didn’t say this of his own accord: he prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation, and not for the nation only, but to gather into one the scattered children of God. From that day they plot to kill him. Jesus no longer walks openly, but stays at Ephraim. Passover is near; they look for him in the temple; orders are out for his arrest.

John 11:45

45Therefore many of the Jews who came to Mary and saw what Jesus did believed in him.

Arminian

Wesley: many believed, and so the Son of God was glorified, as he had said in v. 4. Sight of the sign becomes faith in the person. The therefore is moral, not mechanical: they had come with Mary, they saw, they believed.

Classical Arminians take this believing as a response to sufficient light. The same raising that some will report as a threat, these receive. “Whoever believes” in vv. 25–26 is already gathering names in Bethany.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ did not let the miracle be without fruit; by it he drew some to faith. Miracles prepare for faith or confirm it; here they produce admiration that submits to be taught. Bare miracle could not have been enough; believe means willingness to embrace his doctrine.

The fruit is Christ’s. Many believing is the glory appointed in v. 4, given through a sign, received as faith the Lord himself yields.

Key contrast Either reading: many believed because of what they saw. Arminians emphasize a free, sincere response to public evidence. Reformed interpreters usually stress that the sign is God’s appointed means by which he actually draws people—miracle plus the Son’s power to give the faith the sign aims at.

John 11:46

46But some of them went away to the Pharisees and told them the things which Jesus had done.

Arminian

Wesley: a dreadful confirmation—if they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded though one rose from the dead. Same funeral, opposite errand.

The split is in the will, not in the data. They saw what the many saw. Reporting to hostile clergy is a decision grace still confronts. Resurrection-evidence does not abolish responsible unbelief.

Reformed

Calvin: detestable ingratitude, horrible rage. Hearts must be purified before miracles profit; those without fear of God could see heaven and earth mingled and still reject sound doctrine.

Impiety infects even this work with poison. The report to the Pharisees is not a neutral news-cycle; it is blindness choosing a venue. God still uses their feet to move the plot toward the appointed cross.

John 11:47

47The chief priests therefore and the Pharisees gathered a council, and said, “What are we doing? For this man does many signs.

Arminian

Wesley: What do we?—What? Believe. Yea, but death yields to the power of Christ sooner than infidelity. They confess the signs and still ask for a strategy, not for repentance.

“Many signs” is enough light to obligate. The council’s question treats the Messiah as a problem of public order. Refusal here is culpable, not a missing inner chapter of evidence.

Reformed

Calvin: monstrous blindness. They assemble to bury the glory of God they are constrained to be astonished at. They do not openly say they war with God, yet they cannot extinguish Christ without overturning the power that shines in the miracles.

They admit he does many miracles, then plot to crush the power of God in them. Infidelity grows haughty until it raises horns against heaven; God laughs until the day of wrath (Psalm 2).

John 11:48

48If we leave him alone like this, everyone will believe in him, and the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation.”

Arminian

Wesley: all will believe and receive him as Messiah, giving Rome umbrage to subvert temple and nation. Were they afraid, or was it color only? Certainly no more—they could not but know that he who raised the dead was able to conquer the Romans.

They fear “everyone will believe”—the very width of vv. 25–26—as a political threat. Unbelief can name the universal draw of Christ and still try to stop it. Place and nation become idols that veto the sign.

Reformed

Calvin: they cloak wickedness as zeal for public good; tyranny is what they fear, temple and people what they pretend. Hypocrisy wraps even a private consultation. They confessed miracles, then dread Rome as if God’s present power were not protection.

They resolve to remove Christ so inconvenience will not arise. First question should have been, What does God command? Their prudence is unbelief with a budget. What they dread will, in history, still come.

John 11:49

49But a certain one of them, Caiaphas, being high priest that year, said to them, “You know nothing at all,

Arminian

Wesley: that memorable year in which Christ was to die—last of Daniel’s seventy weeks, fortieth before Jerusalem’s fall. Caiaphas was high priest before and after; the year is marked because of the death. You know nothing—he rebukes slow deliberations in a “clear” case.

Office without fear of God is brutal certainty. He silences debate toward murder. Rank does not make the verdict righteous; it only makes it loud.

Reformed

Calvin: a short consultation; Caiaphas will not let them hesitate. The title “that year” is not an annual job but the purchased, Roman-shuffled dignity the Law meant to last until death.

God is about to speak through a wicked mouth because he looks at the priesthood he instituted more than at the man. The scorn You know nothing will become, against Caiaphas’s intent, a setup for prophecy.

John 11:50

50nor do you consider that it is advantageous for us that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation not perish.”

Arminian

Wesley: God overruled his tongue; he spoke not by his own spirit only but by the spirit of prophecy—unaware, as clear a testimony to Christ’s priestly office as Pilate later to his kingly. Better that one die: the substitution they mean as expediency God means as atonement.

The emphasis is on a historical death for the people, a priestly work sincerely sufficient for the world that will be invited to believe. Caiaphas means a scapegoat to save chairs; the gospel means a Lamb to save sinners who trust him.

Reformed

Calvin: one way of purchasing safety—slay an innocent man. Wickedness plans from the flesh, expecting advantage from what God does not permit. What he meant: provoke God’s wrath in order to be happy.

Never separate useful from lawful. A people is no more protected by an unjust death than a body is protected by cutting its throat. Yet God will take the sentence and make it true in a higher key.

Key contrast Caiaphas speaks murder, and God means a death that truly saves. Arminians typically stress the priestly testimony as wide as the people and the world invited to believe. Calvinists stress God’s sovereign overruling of a wicked tongue, the death itself particular in design toward those he will actually gather.

John 11:51

51Now he didn’t say this of himself, but being high priest that year, he prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation,

Arminian

Not of his own accord: John will not let the sentence be only politics. The high priest that year prophesied—Jesus would die for the nation. The office, not the man’s virtue, is the channel.

God can hijack a murderer-sentence into gospel without making the murder good. The death is for the nation in a saving sense the council refuses. Hearers remain accountable for killing the One who dies for them.

Reformed

Calvin: not that Caiaphas was mad and did not understand his own opinion; a higher impulse guided his tongue. Two tongues: he vomits the cruel design, and God turns the words into a prediction. The heavenly oracle proceeds from the high priest’s seat so the Jews have less excuse.

The Spirit can use a foul mouth as an organ because God regards the instituted priesthood. Wickedness of the speaker does not cancel the prophecy; it does not license treating every later papal word as oracle either, Calvin warns.

John 11:52

52and not for the nation only, but that he might also gather together into one the children of God who are scattered abroad.

Arminian

Wesley: that he might gather into one church all the children of God scattered through all ages and nations. Not for the nation only—John will not let Israel’s privilege stop the sentence. The death aims at a gathered people wider than Judea.

The passage carries the worldwide intent of the cross: one death, a sincere gathering call to the scattered. Children of God names those who believe and are brought in, from every nation, as the gospel is actually preached and believed—not a roll frozen before the invitation.

Reformed

Calvin: the whole of salvation consists in Christ assembling us into one, reconciling us to the Father, the fountain of life. Why call wandering enemies children? God had in his breast children who in themselves were lost sheep—or wolves. It is by election that he reckons as children, even before they are called, those who at length are manifested by faith.

The death gathers a people already named in God’s breast. Scattered does not mean merely unevangelized volunteers; it means elect sheep not yet called, whom the cross and the gospel will actually bring in.

Key contrast This reading does not flatten “not for the nation only.” Jesus dies to gather scattered children of God into one. Arminians read the gathering as the wide, sincere aim of the atonement toward all nations, children identified as those who believe the preached gospel. Reformed commentators often read children of God as those God already has in his breast by election, gathered by the death from every place—called in time, named before they believe.

John 11:53

53So from that day forward they took counsel that they might put him to death.

Arminian

From that day: the sign of life becomes the occasion of a death-plot. They choose a plan. Glory in Bethany does not compel Jerusalem’s rulers.

Light resisted hardens. The same week’s evidence that made many believe makes this council murderous. Responsible unbelief can sit in official session.

Reformed

The prophecy they will not hear becomes the policy they will. God meant a death for the nation; they mean a hit.

Their plan is wicked and, in the secret counsel, on schedule. The hour of the cross is not their triumph over the sign; it is the sign’s road into its true meaning.

John 11:54

54Jesus therefore walked no more openly among the Jews, but departed from there into the country near the wilderness, to a city called Ephraim. He stayed there with his disciples.

Arminian

He no longer walks openly. Withdrawal to Ephraim with the disciples is wisdom, not fear emptying the mission. The hour is not yet the feast-hour.

The Lord who walked into Judea in v. 7 now steps back. Timing is moral and missional. He still stays with the disciples who were told “let us go”; they are not discarded for Thomas’s gloom or the council’s vote.

Reformed

Open walking ends because the plot is now policy. He chooses wilderness-adjacent Ephraim until Passover draws the hour.

The twelve hours of the day (v. 9) include retreat. He is not seized before the Father’s time. Staying with the disciples is the quiet before the public week of his death.

John 11:55

55Now the Passover of the Jews was at hand. Many went up from the country to Jerusalem before the Passover, to purify themselves.

Arminian

Wesley: many went up to purify themselves, to remove hindrances to eating the Passover. Calendar and cleansing fill the city while the true Passover stays in Ephraim a little longer.

They purify for a feast whose Lamb they will reject. Ritual readiness without receiving the One the feast names is the old danger of light unused.

Reformed

Passover at hand: John stamps the hour. Crowds for purification become the pool of lookers in the next verse.

God fills Jerusalem with pilgrims who will see the death Caiaphas named. Their washings cannot do what the gathered children need; the Lamb they do not yet recognize is about to be killed.

John 11:56

56Then they sought for Jesus and spoke with one another as they stood in the temple, “What do you think—that he isn’t coming to the feast at all?”

Arminian

Temple talk: will he come or not? Looking for Jesus can be hope or a hunt. The question treats his coming as a rumor, not yet as worship.

Speculation in holy space is still addressable. He will come to the feast. The looking is a chance to believe the One who is resurrection and life, not only to bet on logistics.

Reformed

They seek him with a question that already half-expects absence. The temple is full of inquiry without allegiance.

Seeking here is the restless crowd of 7:11 again. Some of this looking will become Hosanna, some a net for arrest. The feast will not miss him; the hour has his name on it.

John 11:57

57Now the chief priests and the Pharisees had commanded that if anyone knew where he was, he should report it, that they might seize him.

Arminian

Orders out: inform, that they might seize him. The council’s plan has become a warrant. Knowing his location is now a test of loyalty.

The chapter that began with a loved friend’s illness ends with a manhunt. Many believed; the leadership legislates unbelief. The sincere call of vv. 25–26 still stands over a city preparing to seize the Life it was offered.

Reformed

An arrest order is the human last word of the chapter. They will need a Judas and a night; they already have a policy.

God has already spoken the truer last word in v. 52—die to gather the scattered children. Their warrant runs on his timetable. The One they hunt is the Resurrection, and the grave they mean for him will not hold any more than Lazarus’s did.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on many who believed, a high priest’s double tongue, and one man dying to gather the scattered, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Many believed on Him—And so the Son of God was glorified, according to what our Lord had said, John 11:4. But some of them went to the Pharisees—What a dreadful confirmation of that weighty truth, If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded though one rose from the dead! It is expedient that one man should die for the people—So God overruled his tongue, for he spake not of himself, by his own spirit only, but by the spirit of prophecy. And thus he gave unawares as clear a testimony to the priestly, as Pilate did to the kingly office of Christ.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 11:45–46, 50)

Reformed Notable voices

“Caiaphas, therefore, might be said, at that time, to have two tongues; for he vomited out the wicked and cruel design of putting Christ to death, which he had conceived in his mind; but God turned his tongue to a different purpose, so that, under ambiguous words, he likewise uttered a prediction… How comes it that they who, in consequence of being wretchedly scattered and wandering, became the enemies of God, are here called the children of God? I answer… God had in his breast children, who in themselves were wandering and lost sheep… It is therefore by election that he reckons as the children of God, even before they are called, those who at length begin to be manifested by faith both to themselves and to others.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 11:51–52)
