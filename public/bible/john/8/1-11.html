Passage

John 8

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

ESV prints 7:53–8:11 as a bracketed unit: “They went each to his own house” (7:53) after Nicodemus stalled the council, then “but Jesus went to the Mount of Olives.” A heading warns that the earliest manuscripts do not include 7:53–8:11; a footnote notes that some copies omit it, others place it here, after 7:36, after 21:25, or after Luke 21:38, with variations. The double brackets [[ ]] enclose the pericope adulterae. This file numbers only 8:1–11; 7:53 belongs in this context, not as a chapter-8 verse. Still the canonical ESV text is to be read pastorally: at dawn Jesus teaches in the temple; scribes and Pharisees thrust a woman caught in adultery into the midst to trap him between Moses’ sentence and his mercy; he writes on the ground, sends the sinless-first stone, and tells her, “Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more.”

John 8:1

1but Jesus went to the Mount of Olives.

Arminian

ESV continues the sentence from 7:53: they went each to his own house, but Jesus went to the Mount of Olives. The night is his, not the council’s. He withdraws without abandoning the city that will see him at dawn.

Classical Arminians hear a Savior still available. The textual brackets around 7:53–8:11 belong in the apparatus; the printed Lord still spends the night where he may be found again by people, not by a plot.

Reformed

The ESV’s verse 1 is a continuation, not a new scene invented for chapter 8. Jesus’ night on the Mount is the same sovereignty John has already named: his hour had not yet come.

Reformed readers take the canonical line pastorally without pretending every early copy had it. The Son’s steps are not dictated by the Sanhedrin’s adjournment; he will sit and teach when he chooses.

John 8:2

2Early in the morning he came again to the temple. All the people came to him, and he sat down and taught them.

Arminian

Dawn, temple, a seated Teacher, and all the people coming. Mercy begins as public instruction, not as a trap. Whoever will come may hear him before the scribes arrive with their case.

Arminian pastoral note: Jesus returns to the same house of God they tried to control. Grace is early and open. Teaching is already gift before any woman is dragged into the midst.

Reformed

He came again: the temple is his Father’s, and the hour for arrest has not struck. Sitting to teach is rabbinic authority in the open, under God’s protection.

Reformed accent: the people gather because the Word is there. What follows will test whether the teachers of Israel want instruction or a charge to file.

John 8:3

3The scribes and the Pharisees brought a woman who had been caught in adultery, and placing her in the midst

Arminian

They bring a woman and place her in the midst—object, exhibit, bait. Adultery is real sin; so is the theater. The missing man in the act already shows the case is not even-handed justice.

Classical Arminians refuse to romanticize her. She is guilty enough to need pardon. They also refuse to baptize the trap: using a sinner as a weapon against Jesus is itself wickedness he will expose.

Reformed

Calvin: they bring her by agreement, to lay traps. Pharisees are named as chief among the scribes. The midst is a courtroom they have improvised around the Teacher.

Reformed diagnosis: the woman’s guilt is not denied by noting their malice. Both are on the floor. Christ will deal with hypocrites first, then with the sinner they meant to spend.

John 8:4

4they said to him, “Teacher, this woman has been caught in the act of adultery.

Arminian

Teacher: the title is courtesy covering a snare. Caught in the act is meant to close every loophole. They want him on record before Moses, not before mercy.

Arminian hearing: they still have to speak to him. The Word is in the temple, and even enemies address him as Teacher. Light is present; they will use it to hunt.

Reformed

The charge is specific: in the act. They present facts as if facts were their god. What they withhold—the male partner, their own intent—already indicts the proceeding.

Reformed note: truth can be spoken as bait. Jesus will not deny the Law; he will unmask the speakers.

John 8:5

5Now in the Law, Moses commanded us to stone such women. So what do you say?”

Arminian

Wesley: if they spoke accurately, Moses specified stoning for a betrothed woman (Deut. 22:23); other adulteresses were to die, manner not always named. “So what do you say?” is the real payload: force him to choose Law or leniency.

Classical Arminians love Moses and the Messiah together. The question is not whether adultery is sin; it is whether these men may weaponize Moses against the One Moses wrote about.

Reformed

Calvin: they do not disguise that they have a plain commandment, hence they act maliciously in putting a question as if it were doubtful. They mean to bind Christ to condemn, or to brand him enemy of Moses.

Reformed care: the Law’s holiness is not the problem. Using a holy sentence to destroy the preacher of grace is. Christ will not set Moses aside; he will not let Moses be hijacked.

Key contrast Both camps keep the Law’s verdict on adultery and refuse the trap. Arminians typically stress that Jesus’ next word sincerely offers life rather than a courtroom sentence; Reformed readers typically stress that he will not overturn political justice even as he refuses their snare and later sends her to repent.

John 8:6

6This they said to test him, that they might have some charge to bring against him. Jesus bent down and wrote with his finger on the ground.

Arminian

John names the motive: test, charge. Wesley: accuse him of usurping a judge’s office if he condemns, or of enmity to the Law if he acquits. He writes on the ground—God wrote once in the Old Testament, Christ once in the New—to fix hurrying thoughts and to show he came not then to condemn but to save.

Arminian pastoral reading: silence can be mercy. He will not play their game on their clock. Consciences need a pause before a stone is lifted.

Reformed

Calvin declines guesses at the letters. The stoop is disdain for the trap: as if a man drew lines while another prated, showing he is not attending to their cavil. Delays that only hinder the gospel are wisely disregarded.

Reformed accent: Christ is not cornered. The finger that wrote the Law now writes in dust, and the testers are already being judged without a formal bench.

John 8:7

7And as they continued to ask him, he stood up and said to them, “Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her.”

Arminian

They keep asking; he stands and speaks. Wesley: without sin means not guilty—conscience judging—of the same sin or one nearly resembling it; let him, as a witness, cast first. The Law already required witnesses to start the execution (Deut. 17:7). He drives that home into their chests.

Classical Arminians do not hear a blanket ban on all courts. They hear hypocrites forbidden to play executioner. The word is a real call to drop the stone; they can still refuse, and some hearts do awaken.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ said this according to the Law’s custom that witnesses put malefactors to death. He is not emptying the bench of every imperfect judge; he is reproving hypocrites who flatter their own vices and act the felon in censuring others.

Reformed precision: “without sin” here unmasks this posse, not all magistracy. Every accuser ought first to sit as witness and judge against himself. Hate the sin; do not hunt the person as sport.

Key contrast Shared ground: the first stone belongs to a clean witness, and these men are not that. Arminians typically hear a searching, resistible word that can still awaken conscience to drop the stone. Reformed readers typically hear Christ exposing hypocrisy without abolishing lawful judgment, summoning each man to self-judgment before God.

John 8:8

8And once more he bent down and wrote on the ground.

Arminian

He writes again. The second stoop gives the sentence time to work. He will not gloat while they squirm; he returns to the dust and lets the word do what shouting would not.

Arminian patience: grace often waits after it speaks. The offer to leave is as real as the exposure. No one is forced to stay and repent, and no one is mocked for walking away under conviction.

Reformed

The repeated gesture is the same holy disregard. He has said enough. The Spirit of wisdom will now use one sentence as a hammer on proud consciences.

Reformed note: Christ is not fishing for a better legal opinion from them. He has already judged the proceeding. What remains is whether they will stand in the light they hate.

John 8:9

9But when they heard it, they went away one by one, beginning with the older ones, and Jesus was left alone with the woman standing before him.

Arminian

Hearing does the work: they leave, older first. Wesley: left alone by the scribes and Pharisees who proposed the question; many others remained. Conscience can still function in unregenerate men enough to drop a stone they should never have picked up.

Classical Arminians: this is not saving faith yet. It is light doing what light does—making hiding harder. They could have stayed and confessed; they chose exit. The woman is left not with them but with Jesus, which is the safer court.

Reformed

Calvin: we perceive how great is the power of an evil conscience. Shame puts them to flight. Beginning from the eldest: rank made the sting quicker. This conviction is not yet true repentance, which would go to the Judge for pardon rather than seek a place to hide.

Reformed gravity: they leave the presence they cannot bear. The woman remaining with Christ is the better picture—brought as guilty to his tribunal, to surrender to his government.

John 8:10

10Jesus stood up and said to her, “Woman, where are they? Has no one condemned you?”

Arminian

He stands and addresses her as Woman—dignity, not slur. Wesley: has no judicial sentence been passed upon thee? He makes her speak. She is no longer only a prop in their test.

Arminian pastoral care: the Lord who silenced accusers now honors her agency. Mercy talks to the sinner, not merely about her. The question is kind and true: the human court has emptied.

Reformed

The same Jesus who wrote in the dust now looks at her. The accusers’ absence is his doing by the word. He will not pretend a Roman or Mosaic sentence has been formally executed.

Reformed reading: she is still a sinner in need of grace, not a mascot of cheap acquittal. The empty circle is providence. The next sentence will pair pardon’s tone with a command.

John 8:11

11She said, “No one, Lord.” And Jesus said, “Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more.”

Arminian

No one, Lord: she names him rightly. Wesley: neither do I take upon me to pass any such sentence; let this deliverance lead thee to repentance. “From now on sin no more” is not a footnote. Pardon is real, and so is the call to leave the life that led her here.

Classical Arminians refuse both a harsh Christ who only stones and a soft Christ who only shrugs. He does not condemn her in this trap; he does send her into a new walk. Grace that cannot say “sin no more” is not the grace of this verse.

Reformed

Calvin: we are not told that Christ absolutely acquitted her, but that he allowed her to go at liberty. Mindful of his calling to gather lost sheep, he exhorts to repentance and comforts by a promise of grace. He does not overturn political order; popish “law of grace” that frees adulterers from due penalty abuses the text.

Reformed close: the design of Christ’s grace is that the reconciled sinner honor the Author of salvation by a holy life. Forgiveness and “sin no more” arrive in the same breath. That is gospel, not laxity.

Key contrast Shared ground: Jesus does not stone her, and he does command a break with sin. Arminians typically hear a sincere, wide mercy that delivers and then truly calls her to repent—grace that can be received or later despised. Reformed readers typically hear the Shepherd’s own errand: liberty plus repentance, without turning the episode into a repeal of lawful penalties or a Christ who never judges.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the woman caught in adultery, the unthrown stone, and “go, and from now on sin no more”—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 8:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“That they might have to accuse him—Either of usurping the office of a judge, if he condemned her, or of being an enemy to the law, if he acquitted her. Jesus stooping down, wrote with his finger on the ground—By this silent action he, 1, fixed their wandering, hurrying thoughts, in order to awaken their consciences: and, 2, signified that he was not then come to condemn but to save the world. He that is without sin—He that is not guilty (his own conscience being the judge) either of the same sin, or of some nearly resembling it; let him—as a witness, cast the first stone at her. Neither do I condemn thee—Neither do I take upon me to pass any such sentence. Let this deliverance lead thee to repentance.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 8:6–11)

Reformed Notable voices

“It is plain enough that this passage was unknown anciently to the Greek Churches; and some conjecture that it has been brought from some other place and inserted here. But as it has always been received by the Latin Churches, and is found in many old Greek manuscripts, and contains nothing unworthy of an Apostolic Spirit, there is no reason why we should refuse to apply it to our advantage. … We are not told that Christ absolutely acquitted the woman, but that he allowed her to go at liberty. … Mindful of his calling, he exhorts the woman to repentance, and comforts her by a promise of grace. … Go, and sin no more. Hence we infer what is the design of the grace of Christ. It is, that the sinner, being reconciled to God, may honor the Author of his salvation by a good and holy life.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 8:3–11)
