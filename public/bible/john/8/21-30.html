Passage

John 8

Verses 21–30 · ESV · Voices

Still in the temple, Jesus says he is going away; they will seek him and die in their sin; where he goes they cannot come. They joke about suicide. He contrasts below and above, this world and not of this world, and warns that unless they believe that “I am he” they will die in their sins. They ask who he is. He speaks of the true Sender and of being lifted up as Son of Man—then they will know that I am he. The Father has not left him alone, for he always does what pleases him. As he says these things, many believed in him.

John 8:21

21So he said to them again, “I am going away, and you will seek me, and you will die in your sin. Where I am going, you cannot come.”

Arminian

Again: the going-away word of 7:33–34 returns with a sharper edge—die in your sin. Seeking too late is not the same as coming now. Where he goes they cannot come while they remain in unbelief.

Classical Arminians hear a real window closing, not a pretend offer. He freely speaks while he is here; refusal now can become a seeking that never arrives. The warning is mercy in a severe key: come before the departure.

Reformed

Calvin: perceiving he does no good among obstinate men, he threatens destruction—the end of all who reject the Gospel, which breathes life or death. Christ freely offers himself; they will feel the loss too late, with no more room for repentance.

Reformed gravity: the accepted time is short. He knocks; delay is not neutral. Going away is the Father’s timetable, and dying in sin is the stated end of remaining rebellion.

John 8:22

22So the Jews said, “Will he kill himself, since he says, ‘Where I am going, you cannot come’?”

Arminian

They turn a death-warning into a suicide joke. Mockery is easier than self-examination. They will not imagine a destination they cannot reach by pedigree.

Arminian note: scorn is a decision. The same mouths could have asked how to come. Light is still speaking; they spend it on a taunt.

Reformed

Calvin: fearless scorn and effrontery. They regard his absence as no loss and bid him begone. Shocking stupidity: Satan infatuates the reprobate to throw themselves into the flame of wrath with brutal indifference.

Reformed realism: the joke is a symptom. Inward wounds may still be there, but the public face is laughter at judgment. That is how dying in sin often sounds before it is silent.

John 8:23

23He said to them, “You are from below; I am from above. You are of this world; I am not of this world.

Arminian

Wesley: he passes their interruption and proves v. 21. From beneath—from the earth; from above—from heaven. Origin explains the gap: they cannot follow because they remain earth-bound in allegiance, not merely in birth.

Classical Arminians: “of this world” is moral location that grace still addresses. He tells them the truth so they may believe and be lifted from below. Remaining of this world after this word is stubbornness, not a missing map.

Reformed

Calvin: under world and beneath he includes all men naturally possess. Gospel is heavenly wisdom; our mind grovels on the earth. No man is qualified to become a disciple until Christ has formed him by his Spirit—hence faith is so seldom found, because mankind is naturally averse except those he elevates by special grace.

Reformed anthropology: the contrast is natures, not etiquette. From-below cannot come where from-above is going unless the Spirit raises them.

John 8:24

24I told you that you would die in your sins, for unless you believe that I am he you will die in your sins.”

Arminian

Plural sins now, and a unless: believe that I am he—or, as Wesley hears it, I AM, the divine name of Exodus 3:14. They treat it as an unfinished sentence and will soon ask, Who are you? The condition is faith in this identity, sincerely required.

Classical Arminians do not soften the unless. Dying in sins is the stated alternative to believing. Grace enables that faith; unbelief remains the condemning act, not a secret non-invitation.

Reformed

Calvin: unbelief is the source that drives us from Christ, from whom deliverance from all sins is to be expected. If you do not believe that I am—there is no other way for lost men to recover salvation but to betake themselves to Christ. Until his grace is manifested to deliver us, a boundless mass of evils reigns in us.

Reformed pairing: the unless is as large as the need. He freely offers himself; faith is the appointed means; dying in sins is what remains when that means is refused.

Key contrast Shared ground: only believing that he is who he is saves from dying in sins. Arminians typically take the unless as a genuine, grace-enabled condition addressed to these hearers. Reformed readers typically take the same warning as the only way of salvation, while tracing actual believing to the Spirit who forms disciples from above.

John 8:25

25So they said to him, “Who are you?” Jesus said to them, “Just what I have been telling you from the beginning.

Arminian

Who are you? pretends he has not been clear. Wesley: even what I say to you from the beginning—one tenor from the first time he spoke to them. The identity has been on the table; they want a different Christ.

Arminian patience and edge: he keeps answering. Ignorance here is not lack of sermons. It is refusal to receive the same word he has been telling all along.

Reformed

Calvin rejects “I am the beginning” as a divinity proof-text here. He reads from the beginning: not a sudden pretender, but the one promised and now public. He speaks in accord with the covenant made in all ages; they reject both prophets and Gospel.

Reformed note: they boasted of Moses and the eternal covenant while refusing the Christ promised from the beginning. The question Who are you? is already answered in their own Scriptures.

John 8:26

26I have much to say about you and much to judge, but he who sent me is true, and I declare to the world what I have heard from him.”

Arminian

Wesley: much to say concerning your inexcusable unbelief; the Sender is true whether you believe or no. He delivers what he was charged to speak to the world—public, not esoteric.

Classical Arminians: inexcusable is the right word. The world-declaration is sincere. Unbelief does not falsify the Sender; it leaves the hearer under a judgment already queued.

Reformed

Calvin: he is in the position of one who sings to the deaf, so he will not pursue the discourse farther, but declares that God will defend the despised doctrine. If we deny him, he remains faithful. He utters nothing but what he received from the Father.

Reformed comfort for preachers: authority is the Sender’s truth, not the crowd’s credit. The same word that judges unbelief is the only word a minister may speak.

John 8:27

27They did not understand that he had been speaking to them about the Father.

Arminian

John interprets: they missed that the Sender is the Father. Wesley: therefore in vv. 28–29 he will speak plainly of the Father and again claim I AM. Dullness here is moral, not a puzzle God refused to solve.

Arminian warning: light can be unexplained away. They had “Father” in their creed and still did not hear it in Jesus’ mouth. Understanding is tied to willingness (7:17).

Reformed

Calvin: how stupid those whose understandings Satan possesses. Nothing could be plainer than that they were summoned to God’s judgment-seat—yet they are altogether blind. Such blindness instructs us to walk with fear.

Reformed note: this not-understanding is not an innocent IQ gap. It is the below-mind meeting above-speech. Next he will tie knowledge to the lifting up of the Son of Man.

John 8:28

28So Jesus said to them, “When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he, and that I do nothing on my own authority, but speak just as the Father taught me.

Arminian

Lifted up: the cross, as in 3:14. Wesley: ye shall know—and so many of them did—that I AM, God over all, and that he does nothing of himself, being one with the Father. The crucifixion they intend will become the place of recognition for some.

Classical Arminians: “when you have lifted up” includes their guilty act and God’s saving use of it. Knowledge after the cross is still a call to trust the I AM; many did, which Wesley will not reduce to a formality.

Reformed

Calvin: exalt points to his death. They mean to plunge him into hell; he says the event will be contrary. The word vexes them: lifting up is their crime and his triumph—cross, then gospel, then resurrection and session.

Reformed sight: “that I am” here is office more than a bare essence-claim: the sent One who does nothing from himself. Some knowledge of the reprobate will come too late, as horror; some, like those who later believe, will know unto life.

Key contrast Both hear the cross in “lifted up” and a real knowing that he is I am he. Arminians typically emphasize that many actually did come to that knowledge, the crucified Son sincerely set before them. Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s turning of their murder into exaltation, with knowledge unto life given to some and too-late knowledge to others.

John 8:29

29And he who sent me is with me. He has not left me alone, for I always do the things that are pleasing to him.”

Arminian

Wesley: the Father has not left him alone—never from the moment he came into the world. Presence is tied to a pleasing life, not to crowd applause. The sent One is never a freelance savior.

Arminian discipleship: always doing what pleases the Father is the Son’s own path, and it is the path he will soon require of those who abide in his word. Union with the Sender is moral as well as ontological.

Reformed

Calvin: God, under whose guidance he does everything, will assist him so he shall not labor in vain. The Spirit’s power accompanies the ministry. He is not left alone because he is wholly devoted to the Father’s will—always, not in part.

Reformed courage: when the nation gives almost no support, the Father is enough. Teachers who subject their whole reason to God’s word may count on the same presence; senses on the throne make labor fruitless.

John 8:30

30As he was saying these things, many believed in him.

Arminian

Many believed—John’s own verb—while he was still speaking of going away, dying in sins, and being lifted up. Faith can begin under a severe sermon. The next paragraph will test whether this believing abides.

Classical Arminians take the report at face value as a real motion toward him, without pretending it is finished holiness. Belief can be initial and still need the “if you abide” of v. 31. That condition is not cynicism; it is pastoral honesty.

Reformed

Calvin: God did not permit the seed to be entirely lost even in dry soil. Yet the Evangelist inaccurately calls faith what was only a sort of preparation for faith: they were disposed to receive the doctrine. The next warning is for that very crowd.

Reformed caution: many-believed is not the same as finished discipleship. John himself will show some “believers” seeking to kill him. True faith perseveres; what v. 30 names must be proved by abiding.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on dying in sin, the divine “I am he,” and the lifted-up Son of Man—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 8:21–30.

Arminian Notable voices

“If ye believe not that I AM—Here (as in ver. 58) our Lord claims the Divine name, I AM, Exod. iii, 14. But the Jews, as if he had stopped short, and not finished the sentence, answered, Who art thou? When ye shall have lifted up—On the cross, ye shall know—And so many of them did, that I AM—God over all; and that I do nothing of myself—Being one with the Father. The Father hath not left me alone—Never from the moment I came into the world.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 8:24–29)

Reformed Notable voices

“Perceiving that he is doing no good among these obstinate men, he threatens their destruction; and this is the end of all those who reject the Gospel. For it is not thrown uselessly into the air, but must breathe the odour either of life or of death. … The wicked will at length feel how great loss they have suffered by rejecting Christ, when he freely offers himself to them. They will feel it, but it will be too late. … If you do not believe that I am. For there is no other way for lost men to recover salvation, but to betake themselves to Christ. … While he spoke these things, many believed in him. … the Evangelist inaccurately gives the name of faith to that which was only a sort of preparation for faith. For he affirms nothing higher concerning them than that they were disposed to receive the doctrine of Christ.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 8:21–30)
