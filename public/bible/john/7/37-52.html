Passage

John 7

Verses 37–52 · ESV · Voices

On the last, great day of Tabernacles, Jesus stands and cries: if anyone thirsts, let him come and drink; whoever believes, as Scripture said, out of his heart flow rivers of living water. John interprets this of the Spirit, to be received after Jesus is glorified. The crowd divides—Prophet, Christ, or not from Galilee (they cite David and Bethlehem and miss his birth). Some would arrest; no hands. Officers return empty: no one ever spoke like this man. Pharisees scorn: have any authorities believed? The crowd that does not know the law is accursed. Nicodemus, one of them, asks whether the law judges without a hearing. They retort: are you from Galilee too? Search and see that no prophet arises from Galilee. (John 7:53 belongs with 8:1–11, not this range.)

John 7:37

37On the last day of the feast, the great day, Jesus stood up and cried out, “If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink.

Arminian

Wesley: greatest concourse; they fetched Siloam water, poured it on the altar, singing, With joy shall ye draw water from the wells of salvation (Isa. 12:3). They commemorated water from the rock and prayed for rain. Against that rite he cries, If anyone thirsts—come to me. ‘Anyone’ matches the feast’s public water. Coming is the condition; drinking is the promise.

Classical Arminians: the cry is as wide as thirst. Prevenient grace is what makes a dry soul feel thirst and able to come; it does not drag every thirsty hearer irresistibly. Remaining dry beside Siloam’s theater is refusal of a sincere, resistible invitation, not proof they were never addressed. He stood and cried so that ignorance would not be the defense.

Reformed

Calvin: plots did not terrify him; courage rose with danger. Officers were likely ready to seize him; God restrained rage. The loud cry means the invitation is not for one or two, nor a whisper—none are ignorant of it but those who shut their ears of their own accord. He exhorts all, provided they desire help from conviction of poverty. All are poor; not all are roused to seek relief. Many wither without stirring. It belongs to the Spirit to cause us to desire his grace. None are called to the Spirit’s riches but those who burn with that desire. He alludes to Isaiah 55:1: what the prophet ascribes to God is fulfilled in Christ. Come direct to him; anywhere else, even a little alleviation, is labor in vain. ‘Let him drink’ contains a promise: he is not a dry cistern but an inexhaustible fountain.

Reformed pairing: the cry is truly universal in address; thirst is not native. The Spirit kindles the hunger the invitation assumes. Those who will not hear have shut their own ears—and that shutting is the unregenerate will, which only the Spirit makes thirsty.

Key contrast Both preach a loud, wide, Christ-only invitation at the water rite. Arminians typically take ‘if anyone thirsts, let him come’ as a sincere, preveniently enabled call any hearer may heed or refuse. Reformed readers typically take the same cry as genuinely public, while holding that the thirst itself is Spirit-given: all are poor, not all are roused, and only the thirsty—those the Spirit makes to burn—actually drink.

John 7:38

38Whoever believes in me, as the Scripture has said, ‘Out of his heart will flow rivers of living water.’”

Arminian

Wesley: he that believes answers to let him come. Whosoever comes by faith, his inmost soul is filled with living water—peace, joy, love—which flows from him to others. Not one proof-text only, but the whole prophetic fountain-language for the Spirit’s effusion (Zech. 14:8). ‘Whoever believes’ keeps the condition named in v. 37.

Classical Arminians: rivers are promised to believers, not to a hidden list beneath the word whoever. Faith is the appointed instrument; grace enables it and does not coerce it. Overflow toward others is holiness spreading, not an automatic pipe detached from ongoing trust.

Reformed

Calvin: coming is not with the feet but by faith—or rather, to come is to believe, if we define believe rightly: embracing Christ as the gospel holds him out, full of the Spirit’s gifts. Rivers, plural, denote diversified graces needed for the soul’s life; perpetuity and abundance are promised. Simpler sense: the believer has a fountain springing in himself (John 4:14). Not that the first day ends all hunger; enjoyment kindles new desire. The Spirit is a continually flowing fountain, though we still carry remains of sin. Each partakes according to the measure of faith; we lack perfect fullness now. Small capacity means the graces come by drops that would flow as rivers if faith gave due admission to Christ. ‘As the Scripture saith’ is the ordinary prophetic doctrine of living waters aimed at Messiah’s kingdom. We draw from his fullness (John 1:16). Those called so kindly who wander elsewhere deserve to perish miserably.

Reformed note: whoever believes will overflow; faith is not common to all, though the promise is held out to believers without restriction.

John 7:39

39Now this he said about the Spirit, whom those who believed in him were to receive, for as yet the Spirit had not been given, because Jesus was not yet glorified.

Arminian

Wesley: the Holy Ghost was not yet given—those fruits were not yet given even to true believers in that full measure. John ties water to the Spirit and gift to glorification (cross, resurrection, exaltation). Believers are the named recipients.

Arminian reading: the delay is redemptive-historical, not a hint that some believers were never meant to drink. When the Spirit is poured out, the same ‘whoever believes’ still stands. Conditionality remains: the gift is for those who believed in him, a class one enters by coming.

Reformed

Calvin: water here is not mainly purity but sap and moisture of life—without the Spirit we have none; he waters us with secret vigor. Under one word, all parts of life. Unregenerate men, whatever life they pretend, are dead. ‘Not yet given’ is comparative: so long as Christ was in servant-form, the post-resurrection grace was not openly manifested—as when the Spirit is promised to the elect as if never given to the Fathers. Disciples already had first-fruits; whence faith, if not from the Spirit? Not that grace was absent before the death of Christ, but it was not yet so bright. Highest ornament of Christ’s kingdom: he governs by the Spirit, entering solemn possession when exalted. The question is the power by which we are born again. Present famine of blessing while Christ sits in glory is to be imputed to our sloth and small faith.

Reformed frame: the gift follows glorification in fullness, and regeneration is this water in its proper sense—not merely gifts, but new creation.

John 7:40

40When they heard these words, some of the people said, “This really is the Prophet.”

Arminian

Wesley: the prophet we expect as forerunner of the Messiah. The water-cry produces a verdict, still one size too small. Honoring him as Prophet without drinking is unfinished hearing.

Arminian note: a true step can stop too soon. They could have come and drunk. Titles without thirst still leave the heart dry.

Reformed

Calvin: fruit of the last sermon—some thought one thing, some another. He speaks not of open deadly enemies but of the common people, who ought to have had more integrity. Three classes. The first acknowledge him truly a Prophet, so they do not dislike the doctrine; yet the confession is light: they neither understand nor relish what he says, for they could not truly receive him as Prophet without acknowledging him Son of God and author of salvation. Still, perceiving something divine may later open an easy door to faith.

Reformed patience: God may count a thin confession as a beginning without our pretending it is saving faith already.

John 7:41

41Others said, “This is the Christ.” But some said, “Is the Christ to come from Galilee?

Arminian

A better confession—This is the Christ—meets the Galilee objection. Geography again (v. 27) does the work unbelief wants. They could have asked where he was born.

Classical Arminians: the division is the word doing what it always does: some receive, some raise a hometown veto. The veto is a choice. Bethlehem was in their Bible (v. 42); Galilee was in their prejudice.

Reformed

Calvin: the second opinion is more correct; the third rise against them, hence debate. Do not think it strange if men are divided by controversies. Christ’s sermon produced a schism in the midst of the Church, in its chief seat. Shall doctrine be blamed as the cause of disturbance? Nay, though the world be in commotion, the word is so precious we should wish it received at least by a few. Divisions do not properly originate in the gospel; firm agreement exists only in undoubted truth. Peace among those who know not God is stupidity more than agreement. The seed of difference already lay in men; the sun of the word makes the vapors appear.

Reformed comfort: a Christ-split in the temple does not mean the cry of v. 37 failed. It means it landed.

John 7:42

42Has not the Scripture said that the Christ comes from the offspring of David, and comes from Bethlehem, the village where David was?”

Arminian

Wesley: how could they forget that Jesus was born there? Had not Herod given terrible reason to remember it? Micah 5:2. They wield the right texts against the right man because they will not ask the right question of his history.

Arminian tragedy: Scripture used to avoid the Christ of Scripture. The condition of coming (v. 37) is refused with a genealogy they have not checked. Ignorance here is culpable; the registers and the massacre were public.

Reformed

Calvin: they fortify themselves by testimony of Scripture, and though they do violence to the passage by turning it against Christ, they have some appearance of truth. Wrong in this only: they make him a Galilean. Whence this ignorance but from contempt? If they had taken the trouble to inquire, they would have seen both titles—born in Bethlehem, son of David. In small matters we are ashamed of indolence; in the mysteries of the kingdom we slumber. Diligent to find an excuse to turn aside, astonishingly dull to receive sound doctrine. Out of the Scriptures that lead to Christ, men make obstacles so as not to come.

Reformed note: right verses plus contempt still miss Bethlehem. The mind without the Spirit can quote Micah and stay in Galilee.

John 7:43

43So there was a division among the people over him.

Arminian

John names the effect: schism over him, not over a side issue. Living water does not produce polite consensus. The feast ends with a split crowd.

Arminian pastoral: division is the cost of a sincere public offer. Some thirst; some argue origins. Neither side is a puppet. The cry still stands while they argue.

Reformed

Calvin has already said the sermon produced a schism in the Church’s chief seat. The word is not to be blamed. Hidden seeds of unbelief and of faith become visible when Christ is cried aloud.

Reformed pairing: the same rivers (v. 38) that fill believers expose dry ground. God is not surprised by Tabernacles dividing over his Son.

John 7:44

44Some of them wanted to arrest him, but no one laid hands on him.

Arminian

Will to arrest returns; hands still fail. The hour (v. 30) has not become the hour of the cross. Desire to seize coexists with inability to touch.

Arminian note: failed arrest is more time to come and drink. Judgment delayed is not permission to keep plotting. The water is still being offered over their heads.

Reformed

Calvin: they not only despised him; rejection was accompanied by cruelty. Superstition is always cruel. That efforts failed we ascribe to providence: his hour not yet come; guarded by the Father, he surmounts dangers.

Reformed echo of v. 30: the decree that will give him up tomorrow’s Friday still binds hands today. Officers will confirm it.

John 7:45

45The officers then came to the chief priests and Pharisees, who said to them, “Why did you not bring him?”

Arminian

The machine demands its prisoner. ‘Why not’ assumes that speech can be arrested on command. They treat failure as insubordination, not as a sign.

Arminian hearing: authority without right judgment (v. 24) becomes this question. They could have asked what he said. They ask why the body is missing.

Reformed

Calvin: blind arrogance. They admire their own greatness so as to trample morality and religion. If anything happens contrary to their wish, they would mingle heaven and earth. Haughty priests ask why he was not brought, magnifying their power as if nothing ought to oppose their command.

Reformed note: the gospel’s liberty to be heard is already a judgment on benches that think they own the temple.

John 7:46

46The officers answered, “No one ever spoke like this man!”

Arminian

Police become unwitting witnesses. The speech itself disarmed the arrest. They do not yet confess him as Christ; they confess uniqueness. That can be a step toward drinking or a compliment that never comes.

Classical Arminians: the word is able to halt a mission without violating anyone’s will. They could have returned as captors; they returned as hearers. Light reached the officers. Remaining in the Pharisees’ employ without coming to him is still an unfinished response.

Reformed

Calvin: they acknowledge themselves vanquished by the word, yet do not repent or give due honor. If never man spoke like this man, why did that power not touch them so as to devote themselves wholly to God? Isaiah 11:4: he will prostrate the wicked by the breath of his mouth. Later, those attempting to kill him fall backward at his voice (18:6). Learn that Christ’s doctrine can terrify the wicked; as that tends to their destruction, take care to be softened, not merely broken. Many today are reluctantly drawn to admire the gospel and yet remain in the enemy’s camp; others slander what they know in their hearts is from God, to keep favor with the wicked.

Reformed caution: admiration is not regeneration. The Spirit must take the vanquishing word inside.

John 7:47

47The Pharisees answered them, “Have you also been deceived?

Arminian

Deceived—the same charge as ‘leads the people astray’ (v. 12). They protect the officers by shaming them back into the party line. Coming to drink is recoded as being fooled.

Arminian warning: social contempt is a standard tool against a sincere call. The officers heard more truly than their betters. Rank does not guarantee thirst.

Reformed

Calvin: while they reprove the officers they try to keep them in subjection. Unreasonable, they imply, that these should not remain steady though the whole people revolt. See on what they rest when they insult Christ so haughtily—the next verse’s appeal to rulers.

Reformed note: fear of being ‘also’ deceived is not discernment. It is loyalty to a bench that will not come to the water.

John 7:48

48Have any of the authorities or the Pharisees believed in him?

Arminian

Wesley: men of rank or eminence, or of learning or religion—have they believed? The implied answer is no, and therefore you should not. Argument from elite unbelief. Nicodemus is about to complicate the statistic.

Classical Arminians: unbelief in high places does not cancel ‘if anyone thirsts.’ Prevenient grace is not bound to Sanhedrin majorities. Using authorities as a veto is an appeal to men, not to the Sender. One may believe against the poll.

Reformed

Calvin: ‘none on his side but low and ignorant men; rulers and persons of distinction are opposed.’ The objection has plausibility: if rulers do not retain authority, order fails. God did give judgment to the high priest (Deut. 17), but not to decide except by the Law. All pastoral authority is subject to the word, that God alone may be exalted. When mere human authority is supported without God’s word, it is vain boasting. Wicked men often rule in the Church; beware giving them authority as soon as they depart from the word. Prophets were tormented by the same magnificent titles. Papists still rage with that armor. Horrible blindness when a mortal opposes God. Cherish such reverence for the word as extinguishes the world’s splendor. Miserable if salvation depended on princes; too unsteady if faith stood or fell by their pleasure.

Reformed comfort: 1 Cor. 1—not many wise. God’s election is not waiting on the Pharisees’ roll.

Key contrast Both refuse to let elite unbelief decide the truth of Christ. Arminians typically add that any thirsty hearer, officer or ruler, may still come—the poll does not bind the will grace has enabled. Reformed readers typically add that God is free to hide these things from the wise and reveal them to little ones: faith does not stand or fall by princes, because calling is effectual where he pleases, not where the bench votes.

John 7:49

49But this crowd that does not know the law is accursed.”

Arminian

Wesley: this ignorant rabble are accursed—exposed by ignorance to the curse of being seduced. Contempt of the thirsty poor. The people who heard living water are damned by those who would not drink.

Arminian pastoral: knowing the law without coming to Christ is the deeper curse. The crowd’s supposed ignorance has just produced better hearing than the experts’ law. Grace is not the property of a guild.

Reformed

Calvin: first pride, the priestly title used to subject all tyrannically; next, despising others. They pronounce the populace accursed because they do not know the law—and because they think holiness exists only in their rank. True: those not instructed in the law are accursed, for by its knowledge we are sanctified; but that knowledge is not confined to a few swollen with confidence. It belongs to all the children of God, from smallest to greatest, in the same obedience of faith.

Reformed reversal: God prefers the mean and despised to those in highest power. Accursed is not the thirsty crowd but the unbelief that curses them.

John 7:50

50Nicodemus, who had gone to him before, and who was one of them, said to them,

Arminian

Wesley: having now a little more courage, present as a member of the great council. John reminds us of the night visit (ch. 3). A ruler does believe, or is on the way—enough to speak. The Pharisees’ ‘none of us’ is already false.

Classical Arminians: new birth is not instantaneous theater in every case. Nicodemus moves from night questions toward daylight procedure. Prevenient light can grow; it can also stall. His next sentence is still not a full cry of faith, but it is not nothing.

Reformed

Calvin: a neutral man, who does not venture to defend sound doctrine in good earnest, yet cannot endure to have the truth oppressed. ‘Came by night’ is partly praise, partly disgrace. Love of the doctrine made him dare the rage of wicked men; not defending Christ openly shows excessive timidity. He still hankers after night’s concealment, not a true disciple. Many now plead they resemble Nicodemus and hope to mock God with the mask.

Reformed pastoral: even granting the likeness, what help is it? He will only say Christ ought not to be condemned unheard—which might be said of a robber. He attempts to release the person and abandons the doctrine. Not yet worthy of a believer. Yet the Lord often lets doctrine that seemed to perish take a concealed root and, after long time, put forth a bud; Nicodemus’s faith acquired new vigor from the death of Christ.

John 7:51

51“Does our law judge a man without first giving him a hearing and learning what he does?”

Arminian

Wesley: do not we ourselves act as if we knew not the law, if we pass sentence before we hear him? He meets them on their own Torah. Right judgment (v. 24) in legal dress. It is not yet ‘come and drink,’ but it is a halt to the hanging.

Arminian note: law they claimed to own already required a hearing. Their curse on the crowd (v. 49) boomerangs. Nicodemus uses remaining light; they will answer with a taunt instead of a trial.

Reformed

Calvin: he says Christ ought not to be condemned until heard. The same could be said of an assassin; it is proverbial that it is better to acquit the innocent than condemn the guilty. In trying to release the person he leaves the doctrine. Not much of a believer’s part. The seed is still choked.

Reformed charity: God may use even a procedural protest to break a council (see Calvin on 7:53, which this range does not include). A weak word in season, when God blesses it, can stall persecutors. That is providence, not yet Pentecost in Nicodemus.

John 7:52

52They replied, “Are you from Galilee too? Search and see that no prophet arises from Galilee.”

Arminian

Wesley: they answered by personal reflection; the argument they could not answer, so did not attempt it. Art thou also a Galilean—one of his party? Out of Galilee arises no prophet—they could not but know the contrary: Jonah from Gath-hepher, Nahum from Galilee, Elijah’s Thisbe. They might have known Jesus was born at Bethlehem from the register and David’s genealogies. Conscious the answer would not bear examination, they prevented a reply.

Arminian close: the last word of the chapter (as we have cut it, before the 7:53–8:11 pericope) is a lie used to stop a hearing. Search-and-see was exactly what they would not do. Thirst is still the better search. The invitation of v. 37 has not been withdrawn by their geography.

Reformed

Calvin: they say all who favor Christ are from Galilee, spoken reproachfully, as if he could have no follower except from that small unknown corner. Extreme violence against Nicodemus shows the hatred they burned with against Christ; he had not avowedly undertaken defense, only said a man should not be condemned unheard. Among Papists, no token of candor on behalf of the gospel but they fly into passion and cry heretic.

Reformed close of John 7 proper: contempt of Galilee is contempt of the way God chose to hide and then reveal his Son. Search the Scriptures they cite and you find both Bethlehem and despised Nazareth. Unbelief will not search; it will sneer. The rivers still flow for whoever believes, whether officer, crowd, or a ruler who came by night.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on thirst, living water, the Spirit not yet given, and the divided crowd—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 7:37–52.

Arminian Notable voices

“On the last, the great day of the feast—On this day there was the greatest concourse of people, and they were then wont to fetch water from the fountain of Siloam, which the priests poured out on the great altar, singing one to an other, With joy shall ye draw water from the wells of salvation. … He that believeth—This answers to let him come to me. And whosoever doth come to him by faith, his inmost soul shall be filled with living water, with abundance of peace, joy, and love, which shall likewise flow from him to others. As the Scripture hath said—Not expressly in any one particular place. But here is a general reference to all those scriptures which speak of the effusion of the Spirit by the Messiah, under the similitude of pouring out water.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 7:37–38)

Reformed Notable voices

“The invitation was not addressed to one or two persons only, or in a low and gentle whisper, but that this doctrine is proclaimed to all, in such a manner that none may be ignorant of it, but those who, of their own accord shutting their ears, will not receive this loud and distinct cry. … He exhorts all to partake of his blessings, provided that, from a conviction of their own poverty, they desire to obtain assistance. For it is true that we are all poor and destitute of every blessing, but it is far from being true that all are roused by a conviction of their poverty to seek relief. Hence it arises that many persons do not stir a foot, but wretchedly wither and decay. … It belongs to the Spirit, therefore, to cause us to desire his grace. … None are called to obtain the riches of the Spirit but those who burn with the desire of them.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 7:37)
