Passage

John 7

Verses 10–24 · ESV · Voices

After his brothers go up, Jesus goes to the feast privately. Jerusalem mutters and fears. Mid-feast he teaches in the temple: his teaching is from the One who sent him; anyone whose will is to do God’s will will know the teaching’s origin. He exposes Sabbath hypocrisy over Moses’ circumcision and calls them to judge with right judgment, not appearances.

John 7:10

10But after his brothers had gone up to the feast, then he also went up, not publicly but in private.

Arminian

Wesley: this was his last journey but one to Jerusalem; the next time he went up he suffered. He does go—but not as they demanded, not in their caravan, not as a candidate.

Classical Arminians: private going is still coming. The offer will be public in the temple; the approach refuses their stage-management. He keeps the feast without keeping their script.

Reformed

Calvin has already set him coming secretly until office requires an open stand. Private is not timid; it is timed. He will teach mid-feast in the teeth of armed dislike.

Reformed reading: the Son’s itinerary is holy. He neither deserts the feast nor lets unbelief choreograph the entrance. When he appears, it will be as teacher, not as spectacle.

John 7:11

11The Jews were looking for him at the feast, and saying, “Where is he?”

Arminian

Wesley: the Jews—the men of Judea, particularly of Jerusalem. “Where is he?” can be hunger or hunt. The feast feels his absence before it hears his voice.

Arminian note: seeking can be honest or hostile. The question is already a kind of light: they know he belongs in the conversation. What they will do when he answers is still before them as responsibility.

Reformed

Calvin: the people gape for promised redemption like hungry men, yet when Christ appears they remain in suspense. “Where is he?” shows they knew the man and had not yet been earnestly moved; they stay in doubt.

Reformed realism: curiosity is not faith. A church can miss its own feast-guest and still mouth his name. The later teaching will divide this looking into muttering and, in some, the beginning of belief.

John 7:12

12And there was much muttering about him among the people. While some said, “He is a good man,” others said, “No, he is leading the people astray.”

Arminian

Wesley: much whispering among those from distant parts. The crowd splits into moral compliment and a charge of seduction—neither yet the confession of chapter 20.

Classical Arminians: division here is the sound of a real decision in process. “Good man” is too small; “leads astray” is slander. Grace has put enough of him before them that neutrality is already leaking. They may still come further—or harden the whisper into a verdict.

Reformed

Calvin: it is not a new evil that men differ about Christ even in the bosom of the Church. Rashness is great in the things of God; they hurry to judgment on the Son while they would be modest in small matters. Yet in a confused multitude some think aright, though swallowed by the bewildered many.

Reformed comfort: dissension around the Word is not proof the Word is unclear. The marks and brands of Christ include being called an impostor. Remain faithful; do not let the split unsettle you into silence.

John 7:13

13Yet for fear of the Jews no one spoke openly of him.

Arminian

Wesley: no one spoke openly in his favor, for fear of those in authority. The muttering stays in side rooms because the rulers have made his name expensive.

Arminian pastoral note: fear is a real pressure, not an excuse that erases duty. Some who whisper now will later speak; some will stay safe. The gospel still sincerely calls the frightened to open speech, without pretending the cost is light.

Reformed

Calvin: by “the Jews” here, the rulers. They burned to bury his name in oblivion. Silence under fear is gross tyranny—a wretched condition when freedom is oppressed. Yet Christ’s power will shine brighter when he makes himself heard amidst armed foes.

Reformed note: unbridled license is not the church; neither is terror. The Son will not wait for a free press. He teaches when the Father opens a mouth in a hostile temple.

John 7:14

14About the middle of the feast Jesus went up into the temple and began teaching.

Arminian

Wesley: the feast lasted eight days; this was probably a Sabbath. He went up directly into the temple, without stopping elsewhere. Mid-feast, mid-crowd, he takes the teacher’s place.

Classical Arminians: teaching is the form of the offer. He does not merely appear; he opens the Sender’s doctrine to whoever will hear. A public lesson in the temple is as wide as the courts will hold.

Reformed

Calvin: he was not so afraid as to desist from office; delay was so that he might preach to a very large assembly. We may expose ourselves to dangers, but ought never to omit an opportunity of doing good. He teaches according to ancient custom: ceremonies without doctrine are cold spectacles.

Reformed confession: the great High Priest undertakes the teacher’s office because priests were dumb and doctrine leavened. He attempts nothing but by the Father’s command.

John 7:15

15The Jews therefore marveled, saying, “How is it that this man has learning, when he has never studied?”

Arminian

Wesley: how comes he to be so acquainted with sacred literature as to expound with propriety and gracefulness, seeing he has never learned at any place of education? Their marvel is half compliment, half dismissal.

Arminian hearing: they have light enough to see that the teaching is not homemade. Instead of kneeling, they file a credentials objection. Unbelief often sounds like an admissions office.

Reformed

Calvin: those who think he is being honored are mistaken. The astonishment seeks occasion to despise him. If God uses ordinary means, the means veil his hand; if unwonted power shines, we are stunned and let it pass as a dream. They despise the grace because it exceeds their capacity.

Reformed warning: pride takes no interest in what it cannot diagram. Admonished by them, learn deeper reverence in considering God’s works.

John 7:16

16So Jesus answered them, “My teaching is not mine, but his who sent me.

Arminian

Wesley: my doctrine is not mine—acquired by any labor of learning; but his that sent me—immediately infused by him. The scandal of no school is actually a ladder to the Sender.

Classical Arminians: he gives them a testable claim. The teaching can be known as God’s. He does not ask for blind personality-cult; he points away from himself-as-self to the Father, and still holds hearers responsible for what they now hear.

Reformed

Calvin: the circumstance that offended them was a ladder to perceive God’s glory. The Father determined that the Son should go out of a mechanic’s workshop rather than the schools of the scribes, that none might think the gospel fabricated on earth. Authority of spiritual doctrine is from God alone.

Reformed accent: every teacher must be able to refer doctrine to God as author and to a sending. What is merely ours may be despised; what the Father sends may not.

John 7:17

17If anyone’s will is to do God’s will, he will know whether the teaching is from God or whether I am speaking on my own authority.

Arminian

Wesley: if any man be willing to do his will, he shall know of the doctrine—a universal rule for all persons and doctrines. He that is thoroughly willing to do it shall certainly know what the will of God is. Knowledge of origin follows a will that wants obedience, not a seminar that wants leverage.

Classical Arminians hear conditionality without Pelagius. Prevenient grace restores the capacity to be willing; the verse is a sincere address: will to do, and you will know. Remaining unsure is often unwillingness wearing the mask of intellectual caution. “Anyone” is not a trick word. The teaching can be resisted; the willing are not a closed caste.

Reformed

Calvin: sound judgment flows from fear and reverence for God. Nothing else hinders us from judging aright but that we are unruly and headstrong. If we are prepared to obey, he will illuminate by the Spirit. These words define true religion: being prepared heartily to follow God’s will, which no man can do unless he has renounced his own views. They whose hearts are right will never be deceived.

Reformed pairing: the promise is real, and the willingness is not native. Unruly hearts do not elect themselves into piety. The Spirit who illuminates also makes the heart willing; those he so prepares do know. The verse is not a ladder of unaided will; it is Christ’s diagnosis of why they cannot see.

Key contrast Both refuse a merely academic test of Jesus’ teaching. Arminians typically take “if anyone’s will is to do God’s will” as a genuine, grace-enabled condition anyone may meet or resist. Reformed readers typically take the same words as a real promise to the willing, while holding that no one is so willing unless God has already humbled and illumined the heart.

John 7:18

18The one who speaks on his own authority seeks his own glory; but the one who seeks the glory of him who sent him is true, and in him there is no falsehood.

Arminian

Wesley: there is no unrighteousness in him—no deceit or falsehood. Self-origin and self-glory travel together; the sent One aims at the Sender’s honor, and that is how you know the teaching is true.

Arminian pastoral test: weigh teachers by whose name they burnish. Christ meets his own criterion in public. Hearers who still prefer a glory of their own will call even this honesty a threat.

Reformed

Calvin: everything that displays God’s glory is holy; everything that exalts men and obscures that glory ought to be rejected. He who aims at God’s glory will not go wrong; this is the touchstone. No man can faithfully teach unless void of ambition.

Reformed note: “no falsehood” is not a mood. It is the moral signature of a messenger who has nowhere to stand but the Father’s honor. Ambition in the pulpit is already a doctrinal crack.

John 7:19

19Has not Moses given you the law? Yet none of you keeps the law. Why do you seek to kill me?”

Arminian

Wesley: ye are unrighteous; ye violate the very law ye profess zeal for. The murder-plot is not Torah-faithfulness. Moses is on Jesus’ side against their selective zeal.

Classical Arminians: he still reasons with them. The law they boast of already condemns the intent to kill. They could repent of the plot; the question “why” is not rhetorical fatalism but a door.

Reformed

Calvin: he tears the mask. They freely violate the law they claim to defend; therefore they are not moved by love of it. Drag the wicked from concealments when they fight the gospel under pious slogans.

Reformed hearing: zeal for Moses without keeping Moses is already judgment. The law they wield will not justify the desire to kill the Lawgiver’s sent Son. Their rage has another source than holiness.

John 7:20

20The crowd answered, “You have a demon! Who is seeking to kill you?”

Arminian

Wesley: thou hast a devil—a lying spirit. These, coming from distant parts, probably did not know the priests’ design. Distance can be innocence; it can also be unbelief that treats a true warning as madness.

Arminian note: not every hearer is in on the plot, but every hearer is responsible for the Speaker. Calling the warning demonic is easier than asking whether it is true. Grace still offers them a better hearing.

Reformed

Calvin: “thou art mad”—a Jewish idiom for rage or lost reason as diabolic torment. The multitude reproaches him with simplicity; the common people did not know the priests’ intent. Be cautious not to judge what you do not understand; if rashly condemned, digest the affront mildly.

Reformed realism: ignorance of a plot does not make the crowd a safe jury. They still have the teaching and the Speaker. Madness-language is how unbelief often names the truth that threatens it.

John 7:21

21Jesus answered them, “I did one work, and you all marvel at it.

Arminian

Wesley: I did—at the pool of Bethesda—one work out of many; and ye all marveled, amazed because I did it on the Sabbath. Sixteen months of anger, in his counting, sit on a healing.

Arminian pastoral point: they marvel at mercy as if it were the scandal. The work was good; the marvel is unbelief’s way of staring without repenting. He will now reason from Moses rather than merely assert power.

Reformed

Calvin: leaving their persons, he speaks of the fact. He is blamed for a single work—the Sabbath cure—while they do many like works on every Sabbath in circumcision and do not count them criminal.

Reformed note: he will not let the marvel stay aesthetic. The work is God’s; their amazement is already a kind of judgment on their inconsistency. Truth will now compare the greater good with their lesser exception.

John 7:22

22Moses gave you circumcision (not that it is from Moses, but from the fathers), and you circumcise a man on the Sabbath.

Arminian

Wesley: because Moses enjoined circumcision (though far more ancient) you think it no harm to circumcise on the Sabbath—and are angry at him for a greater good, healing a man body and soul on the Sabbath.

Classical Arminians: he honors both Moses and the fathers. Right judgment would rejoice that Sabbath already bends for covenant mercy. They could have used the same canon to welcome the healing; they prefer the appearance of rigor.

Reformed

Calvin: circumcision was enjoined so as to be necessary even on the Sabbath; thus it is demonstrated that Sabbath worship is not violated by the works of God. Moses, who rigidly demanded Sabbath-keeping, still commanded the eighth-day rite even when it fell on the day of Rest.

Reformed reading: God’s works agree with God’s works. The exception they already practice is the principle they deny in him. Their canon is not too strict; it is selective.

John 7:23

23If on the Sabbath a man receives circumcision, so that the law of Moses may not be broken, are you angry with me because on the Sabbath I made a man’s whole body well?

Arminian

From a wound of covenant sign to a whole-body making-well: the comparison is from lesser to greater. If Sabbath may host circumcision so Moses is not broken, it may host the Messiah’s mercy without being broken.

Arminian appeal: anger here is a decision. They are not forced by Torah to hate the healing. Willing to do God’s will (v. 17) would have recognized the greater good. The question still invites a better judgment.

Reformed

Calvin: both works are God’s; the cure is more excellent because its benefit extends to the whole man—body joined to spiritual advantage, not a mere bodily relief that would lose the comparison. They slander a work in which grace shines more illustriously than in the circumcision they honor.

Reformed weight: the argument is not a loophole. It is a claim that the Son’s mercy is the Sabbath’s fulfillment, not its foe. Anger at that mercy is already a verdict on their seeing.

John 7:24

24Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment.”

Arminian

Face, party, and Sabbath optics have been their court. Right judgment would have weighed the work by God’s intent, not by the embarrassment of an unauthorized healer.

Classical Arminians: the imperative assumes they can yet judge rightly. The call is sincere. Prevenient grace and the teaching now in their ears make a just verdict possible; remaining at appearances is not unfortunate dimness but refusal of the light they have.

Reformed

Calvin: having finished the defense, he reproves them for being carried by wicked dispositions rather than by fact. Circumcision on the Sabbath did not violate the Law because God’s works agree. Why not conclude the same about Christ’s work, except that prejudice against his person has preoccupied them? Judgment will never be right unless regulated by the truth of the fact—especially about heavenly doctrine, which we are prone to dislike for hatred or contempt of the man.

Reformed pastoral edge: appearances include credentials, Galilee, and crowd. Right judgment begins when God is allowed to be God in the work, even when the Worker offends our party.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on a teaching not his own, and on the will that is willing to do God’s will—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 7:10–24.

Arminian Notable voices

“My doctrine is not mine—Acquired by any labour of learning; but his that sent me—Immediately infused by him. If any man be willing to do his will, he shall know of the doctrine, whether it be of God—This is a universal rule, with regard to all persons and doctrines. He that is thoroughly willing to do it, shall certainly know what the will of God is.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 7:16–17)

Reformed Notable voices

“Christ, therefore, replies that sound judgment flows from fear and reverence for God; so that, if their minds be well disposed to the fear of God, they will easily perceive if what he preaches be true or not. … if we are prepared to obey God, he will never fail to illuminate us by the light of his Spirit, so that we shall be able to distinguish between truth and falsehood. Nothing else, therefore, hinders us from judging aright, but that we are unruly and headstrong. … these words of Christ contain a definition of true religion; that is, when we are prepared heartily to follow the will of God, which no man can do, unless he has renounced his own views.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 7:17)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
