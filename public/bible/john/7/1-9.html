Passage

John 7

Verses 1–9 · ESV · Voices

After the bread-of-life crisis, Jesus remains in Galilee because Judean leaders are seeking to kill him. At the Feast of Booths his brothers urge a public show of works; he answers that his time has not yet come, that the world cannot hate them, and that he will not go up on their timetable—then he stays in Galilee.

John 7:1

1After this Jesus went about in Galilee. He would not go about in Judea, because the Jews were seeking to kill him.

Arminian

After the hard sayings of chapter 6, he stays in Galilee. Wesley: he continued there some months after the second Passover, because those who did not believe—especially priests, scribes, and Pharisees—sought occasion to kill him. Withdrawal is not retreat from the mission; it is refusal to die on their clock.

Classical Arminians hear a Savior still using means. He does not dare the knives to prove a decree. The same Lord who will later walk into the feast keeps himself available until the hour of willing self-giving, not until a plot succeeds.

Reformed

Calvin: he remembered the commission received from the Father and confined himself within the limits that belonged to him as man. He knew the foreordained time of his death and still conducted himself as a man liable to dangers, not throwing himself at random into them. Duty, not curiosity about the decree, governs the steps.

Reformed pastoral note: piety is not always in the chief places of the Church. A despised corner of Galilee lodges the Christ whom Judea cannot endure. The hour is fixed; the path to it is still obedience.

John 7:2

2Now the Jews’ Feast of Booths was at hand.

Arminian

Booths remembers wilderness tents and God’s sheltering grace (Lev. 23). A pilgrim feast is coming; Jerusalem will be packed with people who must hear him—and with leaders who want him silent.

Arminian reading: the calendar itself is mercy. God had already given Israel a feast that preaches provision; Jesus will later stand up on its last day and offer living water. The invitation is being staged in public, not whispered to a few.

Reformed

Calvin: the feast recalled forty years in tabernacles and celebrated delivering grace. Christ would later come to it both as one subject to the Law, to redeem from its bondage, and because a great assembly gave a better opportunity of advancing the gospel.

Reformed note: even the feasts serve the Father’s timetable. Booths is not a human publicity window; it is a stage God has already set for the Word to be heard.

John 7:3

3So his brothers said to him, “Leave here and go to Judea, that your disciples also may see the works you are doing.

Arminian

Cousins, in Wesley’s Jewish idiom, push him out of obscurity: go where disciples and crowds can see. The advice sounds like mission; it is actually a demand that he market himself on their terms.

Classical Arminians: family can sincerely want a larger platform and still miss the Son. They have light enough from the works; they use it to manage him. Grace does not wait for relatives to become a publicity team.

Reformed

Calvin: under brethren the Hebrews include cousins and relations. They mock him for hiding in a mean district, as if a man who wishes to be something lacked confidence to appear. Near relations often annoy the children of God more than strangers, tempting to ambition.

Reformed hearing: Satan can speak through family strategy. Christ will repulse the foolish wishes of brethren rather than yield an inch of the Father’s hour.

John 7:4

4For no one works in secret if he seeks to be known openly. If you do these things, show yourself to the world.”

Arminian

Their logic is worldly obvious: secret works and public fame do not match. If the miracles are real, take them to the world. They treat the Messiah as a candidate who needs a better rollout.

Arminian warning: “show yourself to the world” can be unbelief wearing zeal. The world is not the court that validates the Son. He will show himself when love, not applause, requires it—and many will still refuse.

Reformed

Calvin hears either ambition—“direct the eyes of all”—or a charge that gifts are being wasted in a small circle. Either way they trample the manifest proofs of divine power they ought to have revered.

Reformed accent: indolence before God’s works is not a small fault. They have seen enough to worship; they lecture him instead. The world they want is the world’s own stage, not the Father’s.

John 7:5

5For not even his brothers believed in him.

Arminian

John states it without varnish: not even his brothers believed. Bloodline did not produce faith. Later some of these men will believe; this verse records the present refusal, not a sealed fate written in their genealogy.

Classical Arminians: proximity to Jesus is not conversion. Prevenient grace can surround a household with works and words, and relatives can still resist. Unbelief here is culpable, not a missing relative-slot in an eternal list. The same men remain addressable.

Reformed

Calvin: how small is the value of carnal relationship. The Spirit stamps with a mark of infamy relations who, convinced by so many works, did not even then believe. Whoever wishes to be in Christ must be a new creature; they who dedicate themselves to God become brother and mother to him, and he disavows the rest.

Reformed confession: flesh does not beget faith. Election and new creation, not kinship, populate the family of the Son. Their unbelief is not an accident of insufficient marketing.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat family as saving. Arminians typically hear brothers who can and do resist present light, remaining responsible and still later convertible. Reformed readers typically hear how little blood can do, and how necessary a new creation is where even Christ’s household does not believe.

John 7:6

6Jesus said to them, “My time has not yet come, but your time is always here.

Arminian

Wesley: your time is always ready—this or any time will suit you. His kairos is not their festival calendar. They can appear without cost; he cannot treat the feast as a neutral date.

Arminian pastoral note: “not yet” is real history, not theater. He truly waits, then truly goes. Hearers are not asked to decode a hidden timetable but to stop forcing the Son onto a worldly clock.

Reformed

Calvin: some wrongly make this the hour of death; it denotes the time of setting out for the feast. He differs from his relations: they may appear at all hours because the world is friendly to them; he justly dreads, because the world is his mortal enemy. They advise on what they do not understand.

Reformed weight: the world’s welcome is itself a diagnosis. Easy timing is often the timing of those the world does not hate. His hour remains the Father’s.

John 7:7

7The world cannot hate you, but it hates me because I testify about it that its works are evil.

Arminian

Wesley: the world cannot hate you—because ye are of the world. But me it hateth—and all that bear the same testimony. Hatred tracks the testimony against evil works, not mere Galilean obscurity.

Classical Arminians: “cannot hate you” names their present alignment, not an unalterable nature-code. They could yet believe, as some brothers later do. The world that hates him is still summoned by the same testimony; remaining “of the world” after this word is a choice grace makes possible to refuse.

Reformed

Calvin: he reproves them as altogether carnal; peace with the world is purchased only by wicked consent. The world denotes men not born again, who retain their natural disposition—so all not yet regenerated by the Spirit are Christ’s adversaries, because he condemns their works. Nothing right, sincere, or good proceeds from unrenewed nature. The Spirit alone softens us to endure reproof.

Reformed anthropology: hatred of Christ is not a scheduling problem. Unregenerate flesh cannot be his friend while he tells the truth about its works. New birth, not better publicity, would change the world’s verdict.

Key contrast Both take the hatred as moral, not accidental. Arminians typically treat “of the world” as a present, resistible allegiance that testimony still addresses. Reformed readers typically take “the world” as the unregenerate mass, unable to love the Christ who condemns its works until the Spirit makes them new.

John 7:8

8You go up to the feast. I am not going up to this feast, for my time has not yet fully come.”

Arminian

He sends them on without him. ESV notes that some manuscripts add “yet”; even without it, verse 10 will show a later, private going. He will not go up as they meant—openly, for display—because the hour is not full.

Arminian hearing: he is free, not trapped by their dare. “Not this feast” on their terms is still consistent with later obedience. The feast remains a place of sincere offer when he chooses to teach; they cannot conscript that hour.

Reformed

He will not move one step ahead of the Father. Avoiding their caravan is not fear of Booths; it is refusal to let unbelief set the itinerary. When he does go, it will be as sent, not as managed.

Reformed note: the “fully come” language keeps sovereignty and incarnation together. The Son empties himself within a real timetable. Neither panic nor ambition may hurry what the Father has not completed.

John 7:9

9After saying this, he remained in Galilee.

Arminian

The verse is quiet and heavy: he stayed. Galilee holds the Word a little longer while brothers go up in ordinary peace with a world that does not hate them.

Classical Arminians: remaining is still mission. The same Jesus who will cry “if anyone thirsts” first declines a false going. Delay can be love. Those who wanted a spectacle are left with a brother who will not perform on cue—and who still intends to save.

Reformed

Calvin sets two pictures: cousins who, complying with custom, pretend to worship and walk without alarm among unbelievers; and Christ, hated, who will come secretly until office requires him to show himself. Accursed be the peace purchased by abandoning the Son.

Reformed gravity: remaining in Galilee is holy. Better a despised lodging with Christ than a feast-peace that costs the truth. The hour will come; it will not be borrowed from unbelief’s calendar.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Galilee, unbelieving brothers, and a world that hates the One who testifies that its works are evil—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 7:1–9.

Arminian Notable voices

“After these things Jesus walked in Galilee—That is, continued there, for some months after the second passover. For he would not walk—Continue in Judea; because the Jews—Those of them who did not believe; and in particular the chief priests, scribes, and Pharisees, sought an opportunity to kill him. … Your time is always ready—This or any time will suit you. The world cannot hate you—Because ye are of the world. But me it hateth—And all that bear the same testimony.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 7:1–7)

Reformed Notable voices

“When he says that the world cannot hate them, he reproves them for being altogether carnal; for peace with the world can only be purchased by a wicked consent to vices and to every kind of wickedness. The world here denotes men who are not born again, who retain their natural disposition; and accordingly he declares that all who have not yet been regenerated by the Spirit are Christ’s adversaries. … the whole nature of man is so sinful and wicked, that nothing right, or sincere, or good, can proceed from it. … The Holy Spirit alone softens us, so as to endure reproofs patiently.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 7:7)
