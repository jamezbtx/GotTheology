Passage

John 21

Verses 1–14 · ESV · Voices

After the locked-room appearances, Jesus reveals himself again by the Sea of Tiberias. Seven disciples fish all night and catch nothing. At daybreak a stranger on the shore tells them to cast on the right side; the net fills. The beloved disciple names the Lord; Peter plunges into the water. Ashore they find a charcoal fire, fish, and bread. Jesus breakfasts with them. This is the third time he was revealed to the disciples after he was raised from the dead.

John 21:1

1After this Jesus revealed himself again to the disciples by the Sea of Tiberias, and he revealed himself in this way.

Arminian

The Gospel is not finished with locked rooms. Jesus reveals himself again—by a lake the disciples know, in a way they can tell. Revelation is personal and public enough to be narrated.

Classical Arminians hear a risen Lord who keeps coming near. Grace does not retire after one appearance; it seeks the same men in their ordinary place so they may recognize him and respond.

Reformed

John still labors, as Calvin says, to prove the resurrection. “Revealed himself” is the verb of divine self-disclosure, not a chance meeting on a beach.

Reformed framing: the Sea of Tiberias is an appointed stage. The Father who raised the Son now times a third manifestation to the disciples so that faith in the risen Christ is confirmed, not left to rumor.

John 21:2

2Simon Peter, Thomas (called the Twin), Nathanael of Cana in Galilee, the sons of Zebedee, and two others of his disciples were together.

Arminian

Seven named or countable witnesses: Peter, the once-doubting Twin, Nathanael of Cana, James and John, and two more. The story is not a private vision.

Arminian pastoral note: restoration and mission will happen in company. Thomas’s presence matters; a man who had to see is now among those who will see again. Community is where the Lord meets them.

Reformed

Calvin notes Thomas is listed not for flattery but because a once-obstinate witness makes the testimony harder to dismiss. Named disciples, a known lake, a numbered catch—John collects circumstances that prove the history.

Reformed accent: God furnishes witnesses. The resurrection is not a mood; it is an event attested by men the church can name.

John 21:3

3Simon Peter said to them, “I am going fishing.” They said to him, “We will go with you.” They went out and got into the boat, but that night they caught nothing.

Arminian

Peter returns to a trade he knows; the others go with him. This is not a third denial. They work, and the night yields nothing—an honest emptiness before the Lord speaks.

Classical Arminians refuse to treat ordinary labor as unbelief. Waiting for promised power (Luke 24:49; Acts 1) can include work. Empty nets are a real trial of patience, not proof that the disciples have abandoned the call.

Reformed

Calvin: by the earlier breathing Jesus had ordained Peter, yet he abstained from public apostleship until clothed with new power. Meanwhile they do what belongs to private life. God permitted the fruitless night to prove the miracle that follows.

Reformed reading: empty nets are providence, not accident. If they had caught something, the dawn’s haul would not have so clearly manifested Christ’s power. The Lord often withholds so that the blessing cannot be kissed as one’s own industry.

Key contrast Both deny that fishing here is a new apostasy. Arminians typically stress responsible, ordinary work while they wait—and a genuine night of unsuccessful toil. Reformed readers typically stress that public office is still God’s to clothe with power, and that the empty night is the Lord’s trial so the catch will be recognized as his blessing.

John 21:4

4Just as day was breaking, Jesus stood on the shore; yet the disciples did not know that it was Jesus.

Arminian

Dawn, a figure on the beach, and unrecognized Lord. Wesley thought their eyes were holden. Distance, dim light, and the ordinary can hide even a familiar voice until he acts.

Arminian note: not knowing is not yet refusing. The same men who will soon cry “It is the Lord!” are first allowed to meet him as a stranger. Grace often arrives before recognition; the invitation of the next verse will be real before they can name the speaker.

Reformed

Jesus stands on the shore at daybreak; they do not know him. Recognition will be given, not guessed from a silhouette.

Reformed accent: the risen Christ is not at the mercy of their eyesight. He will make himself known in his own way—command, catch, and then the beloved disciple’s confession. Unknowing disciples do not delay his purpose.

John 21:5

5Jesus said to them, “Children, do you have any fish?” They answered him, “No.”

Arminian

He calls them children and asks a question they can truly answer. “No” is not a trick; it is the night’s result spoken aloud.

Classical Arminians hear a Lord who deals with them as responsible people. He elicits the confession of lack before he fills the net. Grace does not skip the honest no; it uses it.

Reformed

The question exposes emptiness the night has already taught. He does not ask because he lacks knowledge of their boat.

Reformed reading: the No is catechesis. Christ draws from them the admission that their labor, without his word, has produced nothing—so that the coming catch cannot be credited to a change of luck.

John 21:6

6He said to them, “Cast the net on the right side of the boat, and you will find some.” So they cast it, and now they were not able to haul it in, because of the quantity of fish.

Arminian

They obey a stranger’s advice after a wasted night. Wesley heard both power and kindness: food for families waiting in Jerusalem, and an emblem of success as fishers of men. The promise “you will find some” is sincere; they find more than some.

Arminian mission note: the same Lord who once called them from nets now fills nets again. The emblem is a real call to keep casting where he says. Quantity is gift, and the gift is meant to be hauled—labor under a word they can trust.

Reformed

Calvin: he does not yet command as Master with a name they know, but advises like one of the people; they obey because they are worn out, not because they already believe it is Jesus. Then two proofs of divine power: the draught, and the net that will not tear.

Reformed accent: success is timed to his word. Toil without his blessing is empty; toil at his word is suddenly more than they can haul. The miracle proves the resurrection by doing what only the Lord of the lake can do.

Key contrast Both see a miracle of provision and a picture of mission. Arminians typically take the catch as a sincere emblem of the success that should attend them as fishers of men—an invitation to keep casting at his word. Reformed readers typically take the empty night and sudden plenty as the Lord’s trial and blessing, so that the power of the risen Christ—not their industry—is what the catch manifests.

John 21:7

7That disciple whom Jesus loved therefore said to Peter, “It is the Lord!” When Simon Peter heard that it was the Lord, he put on his outer garment, for he was stripped for work, and threw himself into the sea.

Arminian

John names him; Peter moves. Wesley: he girt his coat, reverencing the Lord, and threw himself in to swim to him. “The love of Christ draws men through fire and water.” Recognition issues in a real act of going to Jesus.

Classical Arminians love this sequence: a word, a heart that answers, a body in the water. Peter’s plunge is not theater. Restored affection is already showing itself before the charcoal-fire conversation. Love that has failed can still run.

Reformed

Calvin: John’s holy recognition of unexpected blessing led him to the knowledge of Christ; he does not first see a face but infers the hand of God in the haul. John goes before in faith; Peter afterwards excels in zeal, disregarding danger.

Reformed note: both faith and zeal are the Lord’s to allot in his own order. Peter’s plunge is real courage, and it is still a response to a name John was given to speak: “It is the Lord.”

Key contrast Both honor John’s sight and Peter’s plunge. Arminians typically stress the drawing love that Peter truly answers by going through the water to Jesus. Reformed readers typically stress that recognition itself is given—John sees first by reading the blessing—and that zeal is Christ’s to distribute as he wills.

John 21:8

8The other disciples came in the boat, dragging the net full of fish, for they were not far from the land, but about a hundred yards off.

Arminian

Not everyone swims. The rest bring the boat and the gift, about a hundred yards offshore. Different paces, one Lord on the beach.

Arminian pastoral note: love has more than one faithful shape. Dragging the net is obedience too. The haul is for the group, not only for the one who jumped.

Reformed

Calvin is content that all come to Christ at length, while Peter is actuated by peculiar zeal. Distance is modest; the net is heavy with what Christ gave.

Reformed accent: the same Lord meets swimmer and rowers. Variety of zeal does not split the gift. Providence has placed them near enough that both the plunge and the drag make land.

John 21:9

9When they got out on land, they saw a charcoal fire in place, with fish laid out on it, and bread.

Arminian

A charcoal fire—the same kind of fire where Peter warmed himself and denied (18:18). Fish and bread already waiting: the Lord has provided before they land their 153.

Arminian reading: memory is invited, not weaponized. The fire can become a place of table instead of a place of failure. He prepares a meal for deniers and doubters before he asks about love.

Reformed

Coals, fish, bread: Calvin lists these among the circumstances that display Christ’s power. The shore is already a table the disciples did not set.

Reformed framing: the Lord who filled the net has also kindled the fire. Provision precedes their haul. The echo of the courtyard fire is real, and it is the risen Christ who chooses that echo as the setting for what he will next restore.

John 21:10

10Jesus said to them, “Bring some of the fish that you have just caught.”

Arminian

He has fish already; he still says bring some of yours. Their labor under his word is not discarded. He lets them contribute what he first gave.

Classical Arminians hear partnership without pride. The catch is his gift and their haul. Disciples are truly involved; they are not props around a meal he could have served alone.

Reformed

Calvin: though the net filled in a moment without great labor, Christ still ascribes the taking to them—as we call daily bread ours and still ask that it be given.

Reformed accent: human “our” and divine gift are not rivals. He commands them to bring what his power put in the net, training them to receive blessing without erasing means.

John 21:11

11So Simon Peter went aboard and hauled the net ashore, full of large fish, 153 of them. And although there were so many, the net was not torn.

Arminian

Peter, wet from the swim, hauls. The number is counted: 153 large fish, net intact. Wesley treated the unbroken net as part of the Lord’s kind supply and the emblem of a catch that would not be lost.

Arminian note: particular numbers honor particular fish. Mission is not vague plenty; it is people who can be counted. The net that holds is a picture of a gospel that can bear the weight of many without tearing—if they stay with the Lord’s taking.

Reformed

Calvin refuses deep mystery in the 153: Augustine’s law-and-gospel arithmetic he calls childish trifling if pressed. Two proofs remain: the draught, and the concealed power that kept the net whole.

Reformed reading: count the fish, then ascribe the unbroken net to Christ. The church’s gathering is his preservation, not the quality of the twine. Exactness serves history; it does not license a cipher.

John 21:12

12Jesus said to them, “Come and have breakfast.” Now none of the disciples dared ask him, “Who are you?” They knew it was the Lord.

Arminian

Come and eat: the risen Christ hosts. Wesley: he needed no food; none presumed a needless question. They know, and the knowledge is enough for the table.

Arminian pastoral note: communion with Jesus is offered as breakfast, not as an exam. Knowing it is the Lord, they come. The invitation is sincere; awe does not cancel the meal.

Reformed

Calvin: they do not ask because they fear doing him wrong; the signs by which he made himself known were too plain. Uncertainty would have asked; reverence does not.

Reformed accent: knowledge here is given recognition, not a vote. He is the Lord; they know it because he has shown it. Breakfast is fellowship on his terms.

John 21:13

13Jesus came and took the bread and gave it to them, and so with the fish.

Arminian

He comes, takes, gives—the same verbs as the feedings and the supper. The host is the gift. Disciples receive from his hands again.

Classical Arminians see a table meant to be received. The Lord who gave bread to thousands now gives breakfast to seven. Taking from his hand is the posture of restored followers, not of spectators.

Reformed

The risen one distributes bread and fish as he did before the cross. The gesture identifies him more surely than a lecture would.

Reformed comfort: the same Lord who multiplied loaves now feeds witnesses of the resurrection. Giving is his office. They eat what he places in their hands, a mercy that will soon become a charge to feed his lambs.

John 21:14

14This was now the third time that Jesus was revealed to the disciples after he was raised from the dead.

Arminian

John numbers the appearances to the disciples as a group (Wesley: the third time to so many of the apostles together). Faith is given history to stand on: not one flash, but repeated, shareable revealing.

Arminian close of the scene: the Lord has shown himself enough for them to know him, eat with him, and—next—answer him. Revelation aims at a real response still to come around the coals.

Reformed

Calvin: “third” refers to intervals of time; more than seven appearances can sit inside fewer “times” if a day’s events are counted as one manifestation. Christ was seen at intervals to confirm their belief of the resurrection.

Reformed close: the self-revealing is God’s work, paced for confirmation. What follows (vv. 15–19) will be the same Lord appointing a fallen apostle—on the ground of a resurrection already attested three times to the band.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the empty nets, the catch, and the risen Lord at breakfast—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 21:1–14.

Arminian Notable voices

“They were not able to draw it for the multitude of fishes—This was not only a demonstration of the power of our Lord, but a kind supply for them and their families, and such as might be of service to them, when they waited afterward in Jerusalem. It was likewise an emblem of the great success which should attend them as fishers of men. Peter… threw himself into the sea—To swim to him immediately. The love of Christ draws men through fire and water.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 21:6–7)

Reformed Notable voices

“God permitted them to toil to no purpose during the whole night, in order to prove the truth of the miracle… In the same manner, also, God often tries believers, that he may lead them the more highly to value his blessing. As John goes before Peter in faith, so Peter afterwards excels him in zeal, when, disregarding personal danger, he throws himself into the lake.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 21:3, 7)
