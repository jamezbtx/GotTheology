Passage

John 21

Verses 15–25 · ESV · Voices

After breakfast Jesus thrice asks Simon, son of John, whether he loves him, and thrice charges him to feed his lambs and sheep—meeting the threefold denial with restored love and office. He then shows by what death Peter will glorify God and says, Follow me. When Peter asks about the beloved disciple, Jesus answers: If it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you? You follow me. The Gospel closes with that disciple’s true testimony and the world’s inability to contain all Jesus did.

John 21:15

15When they had finished breakfast, Jesus said to Simon Peter, “Simon, son of John, do you love me more than these?” He said to him, “Yes, Lord; you know that I love you.” He said to him, “Feed my lambs.”

Arminian

Breakfast first, then the question. He uses Simon, son of John—the name of the great confession (Wesley) and of the man who fell. “More than these” recalls the old boast; Peter now answers only, I love you, without ranking himself above the rest. The yes is a real yes from a restored heart.

Classical Arminians take this as genuine restored love, not a script Peter is made to recite. Jesus asks because love is the living condition for feeding lambs; Peter can deny again in principle, and he can love again in fact. Grace has brought him back to a sincere affection that must now do something: feed the weakest of the flock.

Reformed

Calvin: the Evangelist relates how Peter was restored to the honor from which he had fallen. Denial had rendered him unworthy of apostleship; now liberty and authority to teach are restored, and Christ blots out the remembrance of the apostasy. The Lord who knows him still calls him Simon, son of John, and appoints: Feed my lambs.

Reformed accent: the question is not a gap in Jesus’ knowledge. The knowing Lord restores and appoints. “You know that I love you” already leans on Christ’s knowledge; the charge that follows is Christ’s reinvestment of a fallen apostle, so that neither Peter nor his hearers need treat the stain as a barrier to the gospel he will preach.

Key contrast Both hear a real restoration of a denier to the care of Christ’s lambs. Arminians typically stress that Peter’s “I love you” is genuine restored love—a responsible yes after failure, now turned toward feeding. Reformed readers typically stress that the omniscient Lord is the one who restores and appoints: he blots out the disgrace and reinvests the office; the question catechizes and commissions, it does not inquire from ignorance.

John 21:16

16He said to him a second time, “Simon, son of John, do you love me?” He said to him, “Yes, Lord; you know that I love you.” He said to him, “Tend my sheep.”

Arminian

A second asking, without “more than these.” Peter answers the same honest yes. Tend my sheep widens the charge from lambs to the flock. Love that has spoken once is asked to speak again—so the restoration is as public and as repeated as the fall.

Arminian pastoral note: repetition here is mercy, not distrust for its own sake. Peter is given another opportunity to mean what he says. Shepherding is the shape restored love takes; the sheep remain Christ’s (“my”), and Peter is truly entrusted to tend them.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ does not give Peter the office of feeding all sorts of persons, but his sheep. The flock is defined by the Shepherd’s voice. The second charge is still appointment, not Peter’s self-promotion.

Reformed reading: tend my sheep is the Lord’s own flock placed under a restored undershepherd. The repetition binds the denier to the office as firmly as the denials had unbound him. Christ remains the only true Shepherd; he shares the work of feeding by the ministry of the word.

John 21:17

17He said to him the third time, “Simon, son of John, do you love me?” Peter was grieved because he said to him the third time, “Do you love me?” and he said to him, “Lord, you know everything; you know that I love you.” Jesus said to him, “Feed my sheep.”

Arminian

The third time matches the three denials; Peter is grieved, as if he were not believed (Wesley). Grief is a real passion in a real man. He will not boast; he appeals to the Lord’s knowledge of a love he now actually has: you know that I love you. Then: Feed my sheep.

Classical Arminians put the hinge on restored affection that is truly Peter’s. “You know everything” is not fatalism; it is a sinner telling the truth and being heard. The Lord who searches hearts also receives a genuine confession and sends a genuine follower to feed. Restoration is not a label pasted on a puppet; it is love recovered and office entrusted to a man who can still obey—or, as he once did, fail.

Reformed

Calvin: Peter did not yet see why the question was repeated, and felt indirectly accused; the repetition was not superfluous. Those who will govern the church must not examine their zeal slightly. “Lord, you know everything” is the right court of appeal: exhaustive knowledge belongs to Christ.

Reformed center: the knowing Lord restores and appoints. He does not discover Peter’s love as new information; he knows it, grieves Peter into depth, and completes the threefold appointment so the three denials form no barrier to apostleship. Feed my sheep is Christ’s act. The flock will be fed because the omniscient Shepherd has reinvested this man, not because Peter has repaired himself.

Key contrast Do not flatten the verse. Shared ground: three questions, three charges, real grief, real love, real office. Arminians typically take the grief and the yes as genuine restored love—Peter is truly brought back and truly called to feed and follow. Reformed readers typically take “you know everything” as the accent: the omniscient Lord restores and appoints; he knows the heart, blots out the denial, and places his own sheep in this man’s care.

John 21:18

18Truly, truly, I say to you, when you were young, you used to dress yourself and walk wherever you wanted, but when you are old, you will stretch out your hands, and another will dress you and carry you where you do not want to go.”

Arminian

Love will be costly. Youth’s self-dressing and free walking will give way to stretched hands and a carrying he will not want—according to nature (Wesley). The prediction treats Peter as a man who will still have preferences, and who will still be taken where following requires.

Arminian weight: this is not the cancellation of Peter’s will; it is the future of a restored will under a hard providence. He will glorify God by a path nature dreads. Grace does not make martyrdom easy; it makes a once-denying man able to go.

Reformed

Calvin: after exhorting Peter to feed, Christ arms him for the coming warfare—not only diligence but courage and a cross. He bids him be prepared for death. The Lord who appoints the flock also appoints the end.

Reformed accent: “truly, truly” is decree-shaped mercy. Peter’s old age will invert ease; another will gird him. Sovereignty here is not cruelty: Calvin notes the Lord spares weakness until strength matures. The knowing Lord restores, then assigns even the manner of the road.

Key contrast Both hear a real future of suffering for a restored apostle. Arminians typically keep Peter’s “would not” as a genuine human will that grace will nonetheless lead through. Reformed readers typically keep the prediction as the Lord’s appointment of Peter’s end—the same Christ who restores also determines the cross he will bear.

John 21:19

19(This he said to show by what kind of death he was to glorify God.) And after saying this he said to him, “Follow me.”

Arminian

John interprets: this death will glorify God. Wesley: saints glorify God not only by acting but chiefly by suffering. Then the first Follow me—the same call as the lakeshore years before, now spoken to a man who has fallen and been asked three times about love.

Classical Arminians hear a genuine restored follow. The command is not a metaphor for a status already locked; it is the path love must walk. Peter is truly summoned, and he must truly come after Jesus, including to a death that honors God. Restoration without following would be a slogan; Jesus will not leave it there.

Reformed

Calvin: the design of the prediction is that Peter may be prepared to endure; “since you must endure death by my example, follow your leader.” Christ offers himself as leader to the cross he assigns.

Reformed hearing: Follow me is the knowing Lord’s appointment of Peter’s remaining life. The one who blotted out the denial now commands the road, including the death that will glorify God. Peter’s following is real obedience under a real decree—not Peter inventing a sequel, but Christ leading a restored apostle.

Key contrast Both take Follow me as the restoration’s goal, not a decoration. Arminians typically hear a restored lover truly called to a path he must now walk by grace—genuine love, genuine following. Reformed readers typically hear the same command as the Lord’s sovereign appointment: the Christ who knows, restores, and assigns the death now says, Follow me.

John 21:20

20Peter turned and saw the disciple whom Jesus loved following them, the one who also had leaned back against him during the supper and had said, “Lord, who is it that is going to betray you?”

Arminian

Peter turns; John is already following, the same disciple of the supper question. Wesley lingered on John’s silence about his own zeal: the action speaks; he is not solicitous that men admire it.

Arminian note: following can be quiet and still be real. John does not compete for the threefold charge; he is simply there. Love has more than Peter’s register, and the narrative honors both.

Reformed

The beloved disciple is identified by the supper, so the reader knows which man Peter sees. He is already following when Peter looks.

Reformed note: two callings in one frame. John’s following is not Peter’s martyrdom; it is still following. The Lord who restored Peter also keeps this witness in his own place.

John 21:21

21When Peter saw him, he said to Jesus, “Lord, what about this man?”

Arminian

A natural question after a death-prediction: if I must go that way, what about him? Affection and comparison arrive together. The question is human, not wicked in itself—and it is still the wrong first move after Follow me.

Classical Arminians admit the pull of measuring one’s call by another’s. Peter’s restored love has not yet learned to leave John’s portion with Jesus. The next verse will not shame curiosity as much as relocate obedience.

Reformed

Calvin: in Peter we see curiosity that becomes hurtful when looking at others draws us aside from duty. We examine other people’s lives to find idle excuses.

Reformed diagnosis: “what about this man?” is the reflex of a heart still managing outcomes. The Lord who appoints Peter’s death has not made Peter the registrar of John’s. The question sets up a sovereign answer.

John 21:22

22Jesus said to him, “If it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you? You follow me!”

Arminian

The second Follow me is sharper: You follow me. Wesley: what is that to thee—who art to follow me long before. Peter’s restored life is not a license to administer John’s. The command remains personal, addressable, and genuine—the same Lord who asked “do you love me?” now requires the love to walk without comparison.

Arminian pastoral force: restoration does not dissolve responsibility into fate. “If it is my will” tells the truth about Jesus’ freedom over John’s years; “what is that to you?” refuses Peter the role of umpire; “You follow me” lands on a man who can still obey. Genuine restored love looks at Jesus, not at the other disciple’s lot.

Reformed

Calvin: the whole sentence is Christ’s hand on Peter to keep him within his calling. “It is no concern of yours… leave that to my disposal; think only about yourself, and prepare to follow where you are called.” “If I will” places John’s remaining under Christ’s will.

Reformed accent: the knowing Lord disposes of both lives. He who restored and appointed Peter now asserts will over the beloved disciple’s duration—“until I come”—and repeats the charge. Follow me here is not advice beside sovereignty; it is sovereignty addressing the man he has reinvested. Curiosity about another’s portion is cut short by the Lord’s will.

Key contrast Both refuse comparison-discipleship after restoration. Arminians typically keep the force of a real, personal You follow me—restored love must walk its own path by grace, without managing John’s. Reformed readers typically keep “if it is my will” as Christ’s sovereign disposal of each disciple’s lot, including who remains and who dies; Peter’s following is the appointment of the Lord who already knows and commands.

John 21:23

23So the saying spread abroad among the brothers that this disciple was not to die; yet Jesus did not say to him that he was not to die, but, “If it is my will that he remain until I come, what is that to you?”

Arminian

The church mishears a conditional for a promise of immortality. John corrects the rumor with the actual words. Hearers remain responsible for what Jesus said—not for what they wished he said.

Classical Arminians take the correction as a warning against speculation that excuses us from following. The brothers can err; the text can still be heard rightly. Remain-until-I-come was never a guarantee they were entitled to cash.

Reformed

Calvin: the disciples imagine more than they were told; wanton understanding rushes into foolishness. Christ had not pronounced anything certain about John’s death, only that he had full power to decide about his life and death.

Reformed note: even apostles misread. The safeguard is the Lord’s actual sentence, not a tradition about an undying witness. Providence governs John’s years; rumor does not.

John 21:24

24This is the disciple who is bearing witness about these things, and who has written these things, and we know that his testimony is true.

Arminian

The beloved disciple steps into the first person of witness: he testifies, he has written, and a we knows the testimony is true. The Gospel ends as evidence meant to be believed.

Arminian close: true testimony is given so readers may trust the Son. The we is the church receiving a sincere, sufficient witness—not a private code for a few. What has been written is for whoever will hear.

Reformed

Calvin: having spoken of himself in the third person, John now declares it is himself, that greater weight may attach to an eyewitness. We know his testimony is true.

Reformed accent: God has appointed this witness and this book. Truth here is not majority vote; it is the reliability of the disciple Christ willed to remain as witness—whatever the rumor about his death.

John 21:25

25Now there are also many other things that Jesus did. Were every one of them to be written, I suppose that the world itself could not contain the books that would be written.

Arminian

Wesley: “I suppose” softens the hyperbole and shows John himself wrote this verse. The world cannot hold a complete Jesus; the Gospel nonetheless holds enough to know him, love him, and follow him.

Arminian ending: the uncontainable Christ is still the Christ of a sincere call—Feed, Follow me. What is written is sufficient for faith and obedience; what is unwritten does not shrink the invitation.

Reformed

Calvin: the Evangelists’ summary is sufficient both for regulating faith and for obtaining salvation; their pens were guided so as not to oppress us with unlimited narrative, yet to make known all God knew to be necessary.

Reformed close: the world’s too-small shelves are a confession of Christ’s majesty, not a complaint about a thin Bible. The Lord who restored Peter and appointed the witness has also appointed this book’s bounds. What he has given is enough.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Peter’s restoration, the charge to feed, and Follow me—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 21:15–25.

Arminian Notable voices

“Lovest thou me?—Thrice our Lord asks him, who had denied him thrice: more than these—Thy fellow disciples do?—Peter thought so once, but he now answers only—I love thee, without adding more than these. Thou knowest—He had now learnt by sad experience that Jesus knew his heart. My lambs—The weakest and tenderest of the flock. Follow me—Showing hereby likewise what death he should die. If I will that he tarry… what is that to thee?—Who art to follow me long before.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 21:15–22)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Evangelist now relates in what manner Peter was restored to that rank of honor from which he had fallen… the liberty, as well as the authority, of teaching is restored to him, both of which he had lost through his own fault. And that the disgrace of his apostasy might not stand in his way, Christ blots out and destroys the remembrance of it. The reason why Christ thrice appoints him to be a pastor is, that the three denials… may be set aside, and thus may form no barrier to his apostleship.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 21:15–17)
