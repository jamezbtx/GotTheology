Passage

John 12

Verses 20–36 · ESV · Voices

Greeks at the feast ask to see Jesus. He answers that the hour of the Son of Man’s glorification has come: a grain of wheat must die to bear fruit; loving this life loses it. His soul is troubled, yet He came for this hour. The Father’s voice answers; Jesus speaks of judgment, the ruler cast out, and Himself lifted up drawing all people. The crowd stumbles at a dying Christ; He pleads that they walk and believe while they have the Light, then hides Himself.

John 12:20

20Now among those who went up to worship at the feast were some Greeks.

Arminian

Wesley: “Certain Greeks—A prelude of the Gentile Church.” They came up to worship the God of Israel; circumcision is not stated. The Pharisees’ “world” is already at the gate.

Arminian hope: the nations are not an afterthought. People who came to worship are about to ask for Jesus. The feast’s God is the Father of the Son they wish to see; the widening is mercy, not a threat to Israel’s Scriptures.

Reformed

Calvin doubts they were uncircumcised pagans, given that they came to worship; distant Jews or God-fearers from the Greek world still count as “strangers” in John’s scene. The King is received beyond Judea’s villages.

Reformed accent: report of Christ has crossed the sea. Their costly journey to the temple indicts cold worship in easier places. The hour Jesus names next will match this approach of the nations.

John 12:21

21So these came to Philip, who was from Bethsaida in Galilee, and asked him, “Sir, we wish to see Jesus.”

Arminian

A modest request through a Greek-named Galilean: we wish to see Jesus. They do not barge; they ask. Seeing here is more than tourism—it is access to the One the palms named King.

Classical Arminians: the wish is a real seeking that deserves a real encounter. Philip is a means, not a mediator in heaven; a present disciple can walk them toward a present Lord. “We wish to see” remains the church’s honest prayer, and Jesus will answer it by speaking of a death they did not request.

Reformed

Calvin: reverence begets modesty; they go to Philip, who is present—not to absent saints. The Papist leap to invocation of the dead has no foothold here.

Reformed note: God uses a Bethsaida man as the door. The request is granted not by a private interview John records, but by a public word about the hour. Seeing Jesus will mean seeing Him glorified through death.

John 12:22

22Philip went and told Andrew; Andrew and Philip went and told Jesus.

Arminian

Philip does not freelance; he takes Andrew, and together they tell Jesus. The chain is human and humble: seeker, disciple, another disciple, the Lord.

Arminian pastoral pattern: bring people to Jesus rather than deciding for Him. The Greeks’ wish is neither dismissed nor managed as a program. Disciples are responsible to speak; Jesus remains free to answer with the hour.

Reformed

Two witnesses go in. The narrative slows so that the request is actually laid before Christ.

Reformed reading: even access to Jesus in the flesh runs along appointed means. What they tell Him becomes the cue for the hour-saying. Providence uses ordinary consultation; the next sentence is not Philip’s strategy but the Son’s.

John 12:23

23And Jesus answered them, “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified.

Arminian

Wesley: glorified with the Father and in the sight of every creature—but He must suffer first. Greeks wanting to see Him are answered with an hour, not a handshake. Glory is now dated.

Arminian hearing: the hour “has come” is announcement, not fatalism that cancels preaching. The Son of Man will be glorified in a death and rising meant to be seen. Those who wish to see Him are being told how seeing will actually happen.

Reformed

Calvin prefers this hour as the publication of the gospel through the world, with the ignominy of death no barrier to that glory. Knowledge of Him will spread; courage need not fail.

Reformed accent: the Greeks are a signal that the hour is no longer “not yet.” Glorification includes the cross. The Father’s timetable, not the crowd’s palms or the seekers’ itinerary, names the moment.

John 12:24

24Truly, truly, I say to you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit.

Arminian

Wesley: Lazarus’s raising gave a natural occasion; among seeds, wheat is the fitting picture of dying in the earth. Alone or much fruit—the hinge is death. Life that refuses the ground stays solitary.

Classical Arminians: the saying is first about Jesus, then about those who follow. His death is the appointed means of a large harvest, including Greeks. The call to die with Him is a sincere summons, not a riddle for a secret few. Remaining alone is the cost of self-preservation; fruit is the cost of going into the ground.

Reformed

Calvin: sowing looks like destruction and is the cause of increase. Begin with the Head: the curse-appearance of the cross hides glory until resurrection fruit is seen; then the members, whose life is a kind of continual death.

Reformed consolation: affliction, hunger, reproach, the feel of being overwhelmed, are a sowing that will yield. The grain is not a technique we apply to God; it is Christ’s path, then ours in Him.

John 12:25

25Whoever loves his life loses it, and whoever hates his life in this world will keep it for eternal life.

Arminian

Wesley: loving life more than the will of God loses it eternally; hating it in comparison of that will preserves it. “Whoever” keeps the door as wide as the demand is deep.

Arminian pastoral edge: this is address, not a sorting hat. People are called to prefer God’s will to self-protection. Grace enables the hatred of life-in-this-world that keeps a person for eternity; refusing that preference is a real loss, not a missed decree they never heard.

Reformed

Calvin: immoderate attachment cannot leave the world except by constraint; believers lay life down when it retards them from Christ. To love this life as pilgrims is lawful; beyond that limit a person consigns it to ruin.

Reformed accent: “in this world” belongs on both sides of the contrast. The more anxious we are for safety here, the farther from the true life. Heavenly contemplation, not despair or ambition, is the hatred Christ means.

John 12:26

26If anyone serves me, he must follow me; and where I am, there will my servant be also. If anyone serves me, the Father will honor him.

Arminian

Service is following—into the grain’s grave, toward where He is. The Father’s honor is the servant’s wage, not the world’s. “Anyone” again: the path is particular, the invitation is not coy.

Classical Arminians: follow is a genuine requirement with a genuine promise. You cannot serve at a distance He did not walk. The honor the Father gives is real encouragement to costly obedience, not flattery for those already locked in.

Reformed

Calvin: He admits no disciples except those who take the path He points—He leads to death, and the servant must not have a separate condition from the Lord. The Father will not leave such companions without reward.

Reformed note: “Where I am” is first the road of the cross, then the presence that follows. Honor from the Father answers honor refused from the crowd. Service is defined by following, not by self-chosen sacrifice.

John 12:27

27“Now is my soul troubled. And what shall I say? ‘Father, save me from this hour’? But for this purpose I have come to this hour.

Arminian

Wesley: various foretastes of the passion; not “what shall I choose?”—His heart is fixed—but He labors for utterance. Save-me and for-this-cause flash together; human language barely holds it.

Arminian comfort: the obedient Son is not an unfeeling instrument. Trouble is real; the purpose is also real. He models bringing dread to the Father and then consenting to the hour He came for. We may pray honestly without making our fear the last word.

Reformed

Calvin: death was not sport; He felt the judgment our guilt required. Feelings in Him were real, voluntary, and sinless, then restrained to the Father’s will. He corrects the wish wrung out by sorrow.

Reformed weight: five steps—complaint, the question of what to do, the plea, the recall, rest in the Father’s glory. If His holy affections needed this obedience, ours—entangled with flesh—need it more. The hour is the eternal purpose He will not abandon.

John 12:28

28Father, glorify your name.” Then a voice came from heaven: “I have glorified it, and I will glorify it again.”

Arminian

Wesley: “Whatever I suffer. Now the trouble was over.” Glorify Your name—past and future. Heaven answers audibly: already done in His coming into the hour; yet to be done in His passing through it.

Arminian hearing: the Son’s request is granted in public. The Father is not silent toward a troubled Redeemer or a listening crowd. Glory is the aim of the hour, and the voice makes that aim knowable. People can receive or mishear what God has actually said.

Reformed

Calvin: He prefers the Father’s glory to His own life; that is the rule of desire. God never leaves His work unfinished—He will finish what He began, and already the death has ornaments of glory to prevent the offense of the cross.

Reformed accent: the voice is for the people (v. 30) and still truly the Father’s. Glorified and will glorify: Gethsemane-ahead and the public path are one work. The Name, not our ease, is the center.

John 12:29

29The crowd that stood there and heard it said that it had thundered. Others said, “An angel has spoken to him.”

Arminian

Wesley: they heard a sound, not the distinct words—glory can still leave something obscure to exercise faith. Thunder and angel are both reductions of “I have glorified it.”

Classical Arminians: mixed hearing is a moral fact, not a missing volume knob. Some downgrade a Father’s sentence to weather or a messenger. Light can be present and still mis-labeled. The next verse will say the voice was for their sake—so mishearing is culpable, not inevitable fate.

Reformed

Calvin: monstrous dullness. Some hear only a confused noise; others steal majesty by saying “angel.” So daily: God speaks in the gospel with power that should shake heaven and earth, and many treat it as a man’s talk or as thunder.

Reformed realism: the Evangelist describes what the world commonly does. A portion heard better; the greater part hears God and does not hear Him. Inward deafness, not a weak miracle, is the problem.

John 12:30

30Jesus answered, “This voice has come for your sake, not mine.

Arminian

He did not need the sound; they did. The Father’s speech is mercy to a crowd that will soon argue about a lifted Son of Man. “For your sake” makes the voice an address, not a private comfort leaked by accident.

Arminian stress: God has spoken for them. That removes the excuse of not being included. What they do with thunder-or-angel is now on them. Grace can be public and still refused.

Reformed

Calvin: as Christ took flesh for us, the blessings He received were for us. The voice is also reproof: they are deaf like stones if God speaks for their sake and they will not lend their ears.

Reformed note: He had no need of an outward miracle for Himself. Ingratitude has no shelter. A sign meant for the crowd will, in the next verses, meet hearts already practicing unbelief.

John 12:31

31Now is the judgment of this world; now will the ruler of this world be cast out.

Arminian

Wesley: this moment—judgment given whose the world shall be; Satan, possessor by sin and death, judged, condemned, cast out of Christ’s bounds. “Now” is the cross-hour, not a distant court date only.

Arminian gospel: the world’s crisis is present, and the tyrant’s eviction is good news for those who will follow the drawings of the lifted Son. Judgment here is setting the world to rights, not a denial that people must still believe. Satan cannot keep those who yield to Christ.

Reformed

Calvin: judgment as restoration of order—mishpat—because outside Christ the world is confusion. Satan’s casting out is the death’s daily effect, not a short calendar event; mortification precedes renovation.

Reformed accent: the Prince is disarmed at the cross, though he still wages war. The world is brought back from revolt under God’s government. This “now” is the appointed victory the troubled soul has embraced.

John 12:32

32And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself.”

Arminian

Lifted up is the cross’s manner, as v. 33 will say. Wesley: He will draw all—Gentiles as well as Jews—and those who follow His drawings Satan shall not keep. The Greeks at the door are already a firstfruits of “all.”

Classical Arminians take the drawing as real and wide: the crucified Son pulls, not merely offers information. Drawings can be followed or resisted; they are not a feint toward a tiny circle. “All people” is the scope of the lifted Christ’s saving intent, and fruit comes where that drawing is not refused.

Reformed

Calvin: the cross is a chariot raising people from earth to the Father; death is not a division from us but a means of drawing us up. “All” he refers to the children of God, the flock—yet with Chrysostom he hears Jew and Gentile gathered into one church.

Reformed accent: lifting up is effectual gathering, not a mere wish. The same verb of drawing that John 6 tied to the Father’s gift appears here as the Son’s cross-work. All without distinction of nation, not all without exception of person, is how this camp typically reads the word.

Key contrast Do not flatten “draw all people.” Shared ground: the cross is the manner of death; the crucified Son really draws; Gentiles are in view, not Jews only. Arminians typically hear a sincere, resistible drawing of all people, which Satan cannot stop in those who follow. Reformed readers typically hear an effectual gathering of the flock from all nations—the children of God—without turning the verse into a denial that the invitation is publicly spoken.

John 12:33

33He said this to show by what kind of death he was going to die.

Arminian

John interprets: lifted up means crucifixion, not a vague ascent. The metaphor is nailed to a Roman pole. No one should spiritualize away the kind of death.

Arminian note: the clarification is kindness. Hearers are not left to guess. The drawing of v. 32 runs through a real execution. Faith will have a particular Christ to look at—the One lifted on wood.

Reformed

The Evangelist will not let “lifted up” float free of the cross. Calvin already took the phrase as the manner of death and the chariot of gathering.

Reformed reading: God appoints not only that the Son die, but how. The kind of death is itself revelation. What looks like being carried away from the earth is the means of drawing earth upward.

John 12:34

34So the crowd answered him, “We have heard from the Law that the Christ remains forever. How can you say that the Son of Man must be lifted up? Who is this Son of Man?”

Arminian

Wesley: how reconcile? Easily—He first dies, then abides forever. They clutch one strand of the Law (the Christ remains) and treat the cross as disproof. “Who is this Son of Man?” is not innocent Bible study; it is a dodge.

Classical Arminians: they had light in the Law and in His signs, and they use a half-truth to evade a dying Messiah. Remaining forever and being lifted up are both in Scripture. The question is responsible unbelief dressed as exegesis.

Reformed

Calvin: malice blinds them in the clearest light. They carp that a dying man cannot be the Christ, ignoring that the Law also speaks of His death and of a kingdom that then flourishes. Carnal views of splendor make them reject Him.

Reformed edge: “Who is this Son of Man?” is reproach, as if the objection had silenced Him. Haughty ignorance treats His own words as a confession that He is not Messiah. The Law they cite is not the problem; their use of it is.

John 12:35

35So Jesus said to them, “The light is among you for a little while longer. Walk while you have the light, lest darkness overtake you. The one who walks in the darkness does not know where he is going.

Arminian

Wesley: He does not answer the riddle directly; He exhorts them to use what they have. The Light is Himself and His doctrine, and the window is short. Darkness overtakes those who will not walk while they can.

Arminian urgency: “while you have” is a real season of opportunity. Walking is a commanded response to present light. This is not a taunt aimed at people God never intended to illumine; it is pastoral warning that light refused becomes night, and night is lostness, not adventure.

Reformed

Calvin: a gentle admonition that is also sharp—they shut their eyes, and the light will be taken. Walking while they have it is the point of the gospel’s brightness; contempt of light is followed by darkness.

Reformed warning: He enlightens that we may follow the way of salvation. Those who do not use the grace quench, as far as they can, the light kindled. Apart from His shining, the mind’s sagacity is not a guide—only a wander.

John 12:36

36While you have the light, believe in the light, that you may become sons of light.” When Jesus had said these things, he departed and hid himself from them.

Arminian

Believe, not merely admire: the aim is to become children of light—Wesley: children of God, wise, holy, happy. Then He hides. The public appeal ends in withdrawal; the season is as short as He said.

Classical Arminians: the imperative is sincere. While they have the Light, they are truly called to trust Him so that they may become what they are not yet. Hiding is judgment on delay, not proof that the call was never meant. Today’s light is to be believed today.

Reformed

Calvin: retain the light by faith, as heirs who enjoy it to the end. He withdraws from adversaries burning with envy, not from every eager stranger.

Reformed close: “sons of light” is the Spirit’s family name, not a self-made title. Believing the Light is the means; hiding is the Judge’s freedom when a crowd prefers riddles to faith. The next paragraph will measure unbelief against many signs.

Key contrast Both hear a real command to believe while the Light is present. Arminians typically take the window as a genuine, resistible opportunity whose refusal brings darkness. Reformed readers typically keep the urgency of the hour and still hold that becoming children of light is God’s work in those who believe—the hiding that follows is judicial, not a trick.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the grain of wheat and the lifted Son who draws—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 12:20–36.

Arminian Notable voices

“Lifted up from the earth—This is a Hebraism which signifies dying. Death in general is all that is usually imported. But our Lord made use of this phrase, rather than others that were equivalent, because it so well suited the particular manner of his death. I will draw all men—Gentiles as well as Jews. And those who follow my drawings, Satan shall not be able to keep.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 12:32)

Reformed Notable voices

“Christ, being lifted up on the cross, shall gather all men to himself, in order that he may raise them from earth to heaven. The cross will be, as it were, a chariot, by which he shall raise all men, along with himself, to his Father. The word all, which he employs, must be understood to refer to the children of God, who belong to his flock. Yet I agree with Chrysostom, who says that Christ used the universal term, all, because the Church was to be gathered equally from among Gentiles and Jews.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 12:32)
