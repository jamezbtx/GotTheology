Passage

John 12

Verses 1–8 · ESV · Voices

Six days before Passover, Jesus returns to Bethany, where Lazarus—raised—reclines at table. Mary pours costly nard on His feet and wipes them with her hair; the house fills with fragrance. Judas objects in the name of the poor. Jesus defends her: the act is for His burial, and they will not always have Him.

John 12:1

1Six days before the Passover, Jesus therefore came to Bethany, where Lazarus was, whom Jesus had raised from the dead.

Arminian

The clock is Passover week, and the place is Bethany: Lazarus, lately a corpse, is named as a living man. “Therefore” ties this visit to the raising and the plot already forming. Jesus does not hide from the feast; He comes toward it.

Arminian pastoral note: the Lord who raised Lazarus now walks toward His own death in the open. The same mercy that called a friend out of the tomb still meets this household. Coming is not retreat; it is love keeping an appointment.

Reformed

Bethany is staged six days out: the raised man is still in the village, and the Passover is near. Calvin notes that Jesus comes ready to be sacrificed at the appointed time, not ignorant of what waits in Jerusalem.

Reformed framing: providence, not drift, sets the week. The One who called Lazarus forth now presents Himself for the hour the Father has fixed. Bethany is the last quiet room before the public path to the cross.

John 12:2

2So they gave a dinner for him there. Martha served, and Lazarus was one of those reclining with him at table.

Arminian

A meal, not a debate: Martha serves; Lazarus reclines as a guest among the living. Ordinary table-fellowship holds an extraordinary guest and an extraordinary testimony—death reversed, sitting down to eat.

Classical Arminians hear hospitality as a real answer to grace already given. Martha’s service is not lesser than Mary’s nard; both are love in the grammar each sister knows. The raised man at table is himself a sermon anyone at the dinner can receive or refuse.

Reformed

John lingers on the table: Martha serving, Lazarus reclining with Him. Calvin places the supper at Simon the leper’s house, with Lazarus invited alongside Christ—the raised man a living exhibit of divine power.

Reformed accent: the banquet is not filler. God intends witnesses for the sign before the burial. Service and reclining are appointed nearness; the next verse will show that nearness answering in costly devotion.

John 12:3

3Mary therefore took a pound of expensive ointment made from pure nard, and anointed the feet of Jesus and wiped his feet with her hair. The house was filled with the fragrance of the perfume.

Arminian

“Therefore”: Mary’s act answers who is at the table. A pound of nard, feet and hair, a house filled—nothing stingy, nothing hidden. Love spends what calculation would keep.

Arminian reading: this is a free, costly yes to the Lord she has already heard and seen. Grace does not make her a puppet; it makes room for a devotion Judas will call waste. The fragrance is public: the household cannot pretend nothing happened.

Reformed

Mary does not anoint sparingly. Calvin hears the whole body, down to the feet, and the odor filling the house as the Evangelist’s amplification of lavish honor.

Reformed note: the Spirit can move a saint to an extraordinary act without turning it into a perpetual rule of pomp. What fills the house is not a new liturgy of luxury; it is a burial-sign poured in love, which Christ Himself will interpret.

John 12:4

4But Judas Iscariot, one of his disciples (he who was about to betray him), said,

Arminian

John names the speaker and the coming betrayal before the pious sentence lands. “One of his disciples” is the wound: proximity is not the same as love.

Classical Arminians take the warning at face value. Sitting at this table, handling the common purse, even calling Jesus Lord does not lock a heart. Judas is about to betray by his own grasping; the narrative will not let us blame Mary’s nard for his fall.

Reformed

The murmuring, Calvin thinks, likely began with Judas and spread; Scripture sometimes names the many for the one. John will not let the reader meet the objection as neutral accounting.

Reformed edge: “about to betray” is not a surprise to Christ. The steward of the bag is already on a path the Lord sees. The next verses will show both the man’s guilt and the deep gulf of God’s judgments.

John 12:5

5“Why was this ointment not sold for three hundred denarii and given to the poor?”

Arminian

Three hundred denarii—nearly a year’s wage—makes the charge sound moral. Care for the poor is a real command; here it is a mask. The question is not curiosity; it is indictment of a woman’s worship.

Arminian pastoral weight: true charity and true devotion are not enemies. Judas uses a good cause to scold a better moment. We still do this when we baptize envy as stewardship and call another’s love of Christ a budget error.

Reformed

Judas values the pound correctly as costly ointment, then weaponizes the poor. Calvin notes how plausible the complaint is: the greater the sum, the easier to call Mary’s act injury to the needy.

Reformed hearing: hypocrisy loves a righteous sentence. The Lord will answer the economics with burial, not with a spreadsheet. The poor remain; this hour with Him does not.

John 12:6

6He said this, not because he cared about the poor, but because he was a thief, and having charge of the moneybag he used to help himself to what was put into it.

Arminian

John strips the mask: not care, but theft. The moneybag was a trust; Judas treated it as a private till. The objection to Mary is grief over lost skim, not lost alms.

Classical Arminians insist this is culpable, repeated sin—not a role he was forced to play against his will. Grace had placed him near Christ and the poor; he used both for himself. The warning is pastoral: office and proximity do not save a grasping heart, and he remains accountable for every handful taken.

Reformed

Calvin: the rest condemned Mary thoughtlessly; Judas used the poor as cover, and the lost chance to steal drove him toward betrayal. It is a “frightful beast,” this desire to possess.

Reformed framing: John tells the truth about the man, and Calvin will not pretend Christ was naïve in choosing a thief as steward. The judgments of God are a deep gulf; this is not a parish hiring policy. Judas’s guilt is real, and Christ’s knowledge of it is real. Both stand.

Key contrast Shared ground: Judas is a thief; his “poor” is cover; the sin is his. Arminians typically stress willful, resistible greed in a disciple who could have done otherwise. Reformed readers typically keep that guilt and add that the eternal Wisdom of God, knowing the man, still appointed him—an act Calvin called an opportunity for secret predestination, never a rule that churches should put thieves over sacred things.

John 12:7

7Jesus said, “Leave her alone, so that she may keep it for the day of my burial.

Arminian

“Leave her alone”: the Lord takes Mary’s side against a religious-sounding scold. The nard is not waste; it is kept for burial—the death already in view. She has read the week better than the treasurer.

Arminian hearing: Jesus honors a love that spends itself on Him before the cross. The command to leave her is also a command to stop harassing a sincere worshiper. Burial-language is not morbid; it is the truth this Passover will enact, and she is invited to love Him toward it.

Reformed

Calvin: they act unjustly who disturb a neighbor without cause. Christ defends the anointing as for His burial—an extraordinary service, not a standing law of costly display in the church.

Reformed accent: Mary is moved under the Spirit’s direction to a last duty she had not mapped in advance. Christ translates their irritation into resurrection hope: the fragrance belongs to a grave that will not keep Him. Judge such acts by His verdict, not by Judas’s ledger.

John 12:8

8For the poor you always have with you, but you do not always have me.”

Arminian

The poor remain a standing claim; this bodily nearness of Jesus is passing. He does not cancel alms; He ranks the hour. Missing Him cannot be repaired later by a larger gift to the bag.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere, time-bound invitation: love the Lord who is here, and keep loving the poor who will still be here. The verse is not permission to neglect the needy. It is permission to pour out devotion while the Bridegroom is in the house.

Reformed

Calvin draws a line between Mary’s unique act and the church’s daily service: alms remain acceptable sacrifice; pomp offered as if Christ still needed spikenard is imitation, not obedience.

Reformed close: “Me you have not always” refers to that kind of bodily presence to which burial-anointing belonged. He remains by the Spirit; the poor remain as the ordinary altar of love. Judas’s piety is exposed; Mary’s hour is kept.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Mary’s costly anointing and the burial already near—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside John 12:1–8.

Arminian Notable voices

“It seems Martha was a person of some figure, from the great respect which was paid to her and her sister… as well as from the costly ointment mentioned in the next verse. Then Mary, taking a pound of ointment—There were two persons who poured ointment on Christ. One toward the beginning of his ministry, at or near Nain; the other six days before his last passover, at Bethany. Against the day of my burial—Which now draws nigh.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on John 12:2–3, 7)

Reformed Notable voices

“Mary unquestionably was suddenly moved to do, under the direction of the Spirit of God, what she had not previously intended. But Christ applies to the hope of his resurrection what they so greatly disapproved. The anointing, which Judas finds fault with, is defended on this ground, that it will serve for his burial. Those persons, therefore, are absurd interpreters, who infer from Christ’s reply that costly and magnificent worship is pleasing to God; for he rather excuses Mary on the ground of her having rendered to him an extraordinary service.”
John Calvin · Commentary on John (on John 12:7)
