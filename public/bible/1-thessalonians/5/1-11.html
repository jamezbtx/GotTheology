Passage

1 Thessalonians 5

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Having comforted the church about believers who have died (4:13–18), Paul turns to the timing of the day of the Lord. He does not offer a calendar. He reminds them that the day will come suddenly on those who feel secure, but that believers belong to the day and should live awake, sober, and armed with faith, love, and hope. Arminian and Reformed readers share the main thrust: hope that produces watchful living. They differ mainly on how God’s appointment of believers to salvation (v. 9) relates to the call to stay alert.

1 Thessalonians 5:1

1But concerning the times and the seasons, brothers, you have no need that anything be written to you.

Arminian

Paul turns from the comfort about believers who have died (4:13–18) to the question of timing. Arminian readers note his pastoral restraint: the Thessalonians already have enough teaching, and Paul declines to feed speculation about dates. The point is readiness, not a calendar.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Paul redirecting curiosity toward faithfulness. The times and seasons belong to God’s counsel, and the church does not need a timetable in order to live well. What believers need is already given in the apostolic teaching.

1 Thessalonians 5:2

2For you yourselves know well that the day of the Lord comes like a thief in the night.

Arminian

The day of the Lord comes like a thief in the night, suddenly and without announcement. Arminian readers stress the moral force of the image: because the timing is hidden, every hour is an hour to stay faithful. Paul echoes Jesus’ own teaching on watchfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the day of the Lord is an Old Testament theme of God’s decisive judgment and salvation, now tied to Christ’s coming. The thief image speaks of surprise for the world, not of uncertainty about whether God will act. Believers on both sides hold a range of views on the order of end-time events, and this verse does not settle those debates.

1 Thessalonians 5:3

3For when they are saying, “Peace and safety,” then sudden destruction will come on them, like birth pains on a pregnant woman. Then they will in no way escape.

Arminian

Those who say “Peace and safety” will meet sudden destruction, like labor pains that cannot be stopped once they begin. Arminian readers hear a warning against false security that rests on human calm rather than on God. The destruction is real, and those who rely on that false peace will not escape it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same warning and add that the birth-pain image signals certainty as well as suddenness. Judgment does not depend on human readiness or awareness. Both traditions read this as a sober call that should drive people to Christ rather than to speculation.

1 Thessalonians 5:4

4But you, brothers, aren’t in darkness, that the day should overtake you like a thief.

Arminian

Paul draws the contrast: the brothers are not in darkness, so the day need not catch them like a thief. Arminian readers note that this is an encouragement and an implied exhortation together. Believers live in the light and are to keep living there, so that the day finds them ready.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the indicative first: believers are not in darkness because God has brought them out of it. The surprise that falls on the world will not fall on them in the same way. Their readiness rests on what God has done, and the exhortations that follow flow from it.

1 Thessalonians 5:5

5You are all children of light and children of the day. We don’t belong to the night, nor to darkness,

Arminian

“Children of light and children of the day” describes who believers now are. Arminian readers see identity as the ground for the call to watchful living in the next verse. Belonging to the day is a real change of realm that must be lived out and not abandoned.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a description of a new nature given by God. Those who belong to the day have been made his children, not merely invited to act like them. Both traditions agree that identity comes before duty here.

1 Thessalonians 5:6

6so then let’s not sleep, as the rest do, but let’s watch and be sober.

Arminian

“Let’s not sleep, as the rest do, but let’s watch and be sober.” Arminian readers stress that the exhortation is real and must be heeded. Spiritual drowsiness is a danger believers can fall into, and Paul calls them to alert, clear-headed faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree the command is genuine and binding. They see watchfulness as the fitting response of those God has awakened, and as one of the means by which he keeps his people. Sleep here means moral and spiritual indifference, not physical rest.

1 Thessalonians 5:7

7For those who sleep, sleep in the night; and those who are drunk are drunk in the night.

Arminian

Sleep and drunkenness belong to the night. Arminian readers see Paul using everyday observation: night is when people stop paying attention and lose self-control. Believers are not to adopt the habits of the night while claiming to belong to the day.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the verse similarly, as a picture of a life that does not know what hour it is. The contrast sharpens the identity Paul has just described. The world’s patterns are natural to the night; they are out of place for children of the day.

1 Thessalonians 5:8

8But since we belong to the day, let’s be sober, putting on the breastplate of faith and love, and for a helmet, the hope of salvation.

Arminian

Paul calls believers to sobriety and arms them with the breastplate of faith and love and the helmet of the hope of salvation. Arminian readers note the familiar triad of faith, love, and hope (compare 1:3) and see these as virtues believers actively put on. Readiness is not passive.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the armor as God’s provision, echoing Isaiah 59:17, where the Lord himself wears righteousness and salvation. Believers put on what God supplies. The hope of salvation is a helmet because it guards the mind with confident expectation.

1 Thessalonians 5:9

9For God didn’t appoint us to wrath, but to the obtaining of salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ,

Arminian

God did not appoint believers to wrath but to obtain salvation through Christ. Arminian readers often read “us” as the believing community, whom God has purposed for salvation in Christ. The verse grounds assurance in God’s gracious intention while still assuming the call to watchfulness just given.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see here God’s appointing purpose for his people, which they connect with election (compare 1:4). Believers can face the day without fear because God’s purpose for them is salvation, not wrath. The appointment is the basis for the comfort Paul gives, not a reason to be careless.

Key contrast Both traditions take this verse as solid ground for assurance. Many Reformed readers connect God’s appointment with individual election; many Arminian readers see God’s purpose for the believing community in Christ, alongside Paul’s call to stay watchful.

1 Thessalonians 5:10

10who died for us, that, whether we wake or sleep, we should live together with him.

Arminian

Christ died for us so that, whether we wake or sleep, we should live together with him. Arminian readers see “wake or sleep” here as life or death, tying back to the concern of 4:13–18. Christ’s death secures fellowship with him for believers whether they are alive at his coming or have died.

Reformed

Reformed readers read “wake or sleep” the same way and see Christ’s death as the ground of a shared future that death cannot break. Both traditions find deep comfort here: union with Christ holds through death and into the coming age.

1 Thessalonians 5:11

11Therefore exhort one another, and build each other up, even as you also do.

Arminian

“Therefore exhort one another, and build each other up.” Arminian readers note that end-time teaching is meant to strengthen the community. Paul affirms that they are already doing this and urges them to continue. Mutual encouragement is one of the ways believers persevere.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree and see mutual exhortation as one of the ordinary means God uses to keep his people. The hope just described is not a private comfort but a shared one. Both traditions stress the communal shape of Christian endurance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
