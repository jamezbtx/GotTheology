Passage

1 Thessalonians 5

Verses 23–28 · WEB

Paul closes with a prayer that the God of peace would sanctify the believers completely and keep them blameless until Christ’s coming, and with a promise that the one who calls them is faithful. Then come brief requests, greetings, a charge to read the letter to everyone, and a final blessing of grace. Arminian and Reformed readers both rejoice in God’s faithfulness here. They differ mainly on what complete sanctification looks like in this life and on how God’s keeping relates to believers’ continuing in faith.

1 Thessalonians 5:23

23May the God of peace himself sanctify you completely. May your whole spirit, soul, and body be preserved blameless at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Paul prays that the God of peace would sanctify them completely and keep their whole spirit, soul, and body blameless at Christ’s coming. Arminian readers, especially in the Wesleyan tradition, have drawn on this verse in teaching about entire sanctification, understood as a work of grace believers can experience in this life. Other Arminians read it more as a prayer for growth toward final blamelessness.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the prayer as describing God’s work of sanctification, which is real now and completed at Christ’s coming. They generally do not see it as teaching sinless perfection in this life. “Spirit, soul, and body” is read by many in both traditions as a way of saying the whole person, while some read it as three distinct parts; the verse does not require settling that question.

Key contrast Some Wesleyan Arminians read this prayer as pointing to entire sanctification in this life. Reformed readers generally see sanctification as real now but complete only at Christ’s coming.

1 Thessalonians 5:24

24He who calls you is faithful, who will also do it.

Arminian

“He who calls you is faithful, who will also do it.” Arminian readers find real assurance here: God is faithful to complete the work of sanctification in those who continue in him. Many Arminians hold that this promise does not cancel the warnings elsewhere in Paul against falling away.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this as one of the clearest statements of God’s faithfulness to finish what he began. The one who calls is the one who will sanctify and preserve. Many Reformed readers see the verse as support for the perseverance of the saints: God himself will keep his people.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm God’s faithfulness. Reformed readers commonly see a promise that God will keep every believer to the end; many Arminian readers see God’s faithful work as assuring those who continue in faith, without cancelling Paul’s warnings elsewhere.

1 Thessalonians 5:25

25Brothers, pray for us.

Arminian

“Brothers, pray for us.” Arminian readers note the humility of the apostle asking for prayer. Prayer is a real partnership in the work of the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree and see the request as evidence that God works through his people’s prayers. Even apostles depend on the intercession of the church. There is no difference between the traditions here.

1 Thessalonians 5:26

26Greet all the brothers with a holy kiss.

Arminian

“Greet all the brothers with a holy kiss.” Arminian readers note that this was a common greeting of family affection in that culture, made “holy” by its setting among believers. Its point is warm, pure fellowship.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way. Many in both traditions apply the principle through culturally appropriate forms of greeting. The emphasis is on “all the brothers,” so no one is left out.

1 Thessalonians 5:27

27I solemnly command you by the Lord that this letter be read to all the holy brothers.

Arminian

Paul solemnly commands that the letter be read to all the brothers. Arminian readers see his concern that the whole church, not just a few, hear the teaching. The letter is meant for everyone.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the weight of the charge, “by the Lord,” and see an early sign of the apostolic letters being read publicly in the church as authoritative. Both traditions value the public reading of Scripture.

1 Thessalonians 5:28

28The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you. Amen.

Arminian

“The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you.” Arminian readers note that the letter that opened with grace (1:1) closes with it. Grace is the atmosphere in which the whole Christian life is lived.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree and see the closing blessing as summarizing the whole letter. From election and calling to sanctification and the hope of Christ’s coming, all of it is grace. Both traditions close in shared confession.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
