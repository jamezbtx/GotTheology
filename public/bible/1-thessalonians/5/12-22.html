Passage

1 Thessalonians 5

Verses 12–22 · WEB

Paul gathers short commands for church life: honor those who labor and lead, keep peace, care for different needs wisely, refuse retaliation, rejoice, pray, give thanks, and receive the Spirit’s work with discernment. Most of this section is shared ground between Arminian and Reformed readers. Where views differ, such as on whether prophecy continues today, the differences run within both traditions rather than between them.

1 Thessalonians 5:12

12But we beg you, brothers, to know those who labor among you, and are over you in the Lord and admonish you,

Arminian

Paul asks the church to know, to recognize and value, those who labor among them, lead them in the Lord, and admonish them. Arminian readers note that leadership is described by its work, not by title. Respect is owed because of faithful labor in the Lord.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an early pattern of recognized local leadership in the church. Those who are “over you in the Lord” serve under Christ’s authority, not their own. The verse supports honoring church leaders without placing them beyond accountability.

1 Thessalonians 5:13

13and to respect and honor them in love for their work’s sake. Be at peace among yourselves.

Arminian

The church is to respect and honor its leaders in love because of their work, and to be at peace among themselves. Arminian readers note that respect and peace go together: honoring leaders well helps prevent division.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way and stress that honor is given “in love” and “for their work’s sake.” A healthy church holds esteem for leaders and peace among members together. There is no real disagreement between the traditions here.

1 Thessalonians 5:14

14We exhort you, brothers: Admonish the disorderly; encourage the faint-hearted; support the weak; be patient toward all.

Arminian

Paul gives the whole church, not leaders alone, a set of pastoral duties: admonish the disorderly, encourage the faint-hearted, support the weak, be patient toward all. Arminian readers note the wisdom of matching care to need. Different people need different kinds of help.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same pastoral realism. The “disorderly” may include those who had stopped working (compare 4:11–12). Correction, comfort, and help all belong to the body’s life together, and patience covers all of it.

1 Thessalonians 5:15

15See that no one returns evil for evil to anyone, but always follow after that which is good for one another and for all.

Arminian

No one is to repay evil for evil; believers should always pursue good for one another and for all. Arminian readers see this as the way of Jesus, extending even beyond the church. Retaliation is set aside, and active good is chosen instead.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree and see the command as fruit of the grace believers have received. Because God has not dealt with them as their sins deserve, they do not repay others in kind. Both traditions read the reach to “all” as including outsiders and opponents.

1 Thessalonians 5:16

16Always rejoice.

Arminian

“Always rejoice.” Arminian readers note that this joy is commanded, so it rests in the Lord rather than in circumstances. The Thessalonians had received the word “in much affliction, with joy of the Holy Spirit” (1:6).

Reformed

Reformed readers see joy grounded in God’s unchanging purposes and the hope just described. Rejoicing always does not deny grief (compare 4:13); it holds hope within grief. Both traditions agree here.

1 Thessalonians 5:17

17Pray without ceasing.

Arminian

“Pray without ceasing.” Arminian readers take this as a call to a constant posture of dependence and conversation with God, not unbroken words. Prayer is a real means through which believers walk with God and receive help.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way and see prayer as one of God’s ordained means of grace. God’s sovereignty does not make prayer unnecessary; it gives prayer its confidence. Both traditions affirm persistent prayer.

1 Thessalonians 5:18

18In everything give thanks, for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus toward you.

Arminian

“In everything give thanks, for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus toward you.” Arminian readers note that Paul says “in everything,” not “for everything.” Thanksgiving is possible in any circumstance because God is present and good in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers also stress “in everything” and see God’s providence behind every circumstance as a ground for thanks. Gratitude is God’s will for those who are in Christ. Rejoicing, prayer, and thanksgiving together describe ordinary Christian life.

1 Thessalonians 5:19

19Don’t quench the Spirit.

Arminian

“Don’t quench the Spirit.” Arminian readers often see here a real possibility that believers can resist or hinder the Spirit’s work in the community. The image is of putting out a fire. Paul treats the community’s response to the Spirit as genuinely significant.

Reformed

Reformed readers also read this as a real warning: believers can grieve or hinder the Spirit’s work among them. They see the warning as one of the means God uses to keep his people responsive. Both traditions agree the context concerns the Spirit’s activity in congregational life (vv. 20–21).

1 Thessalonians 5:20

20Don’t despise prophecies.

Arminian

“Don’t despise prophecies.” Arminian readers note that the Thessalonians may have been tempted to dismiss prophetic speech, perhaps after misuse. Paul does not let abuse cancel proper use. Views on whether prophecy continues today differ within both traditions.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a warning against contempt for prophetic speech in the church. Some Reformed readers hold that new revelatory prophecy ceased with the apostolic era; others do not. Either way, the verse calls the church to receive God’s word humbly rather than to scoff at it.

1 Thessalonians 5:21

21Test all things, and hold firmly that which is good.

Arminian

“Test all things, and hold firmly that which is good.” Arminian readers see the balance: neither despise prophecy nor accept everything uncritically. Believers are responsible to discern and keep what is good.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same balance and stress that testing is done by the apostolic teaching and Scripture. Discernment is a community task. Both traditions read verses 19–22 as a unit about receiving the Spirit’s work with wisdom.

1 Thessalonians 5:22

22Abstain from every form of evil.

Arminian

“Abstain from every form of evil.” Arminian readers note that, coming right after “hold firmly that which is good,” this completes the pair: keep the good, refuse the evil. The command is broad and practical.

Reformed

Reformed readers read it the same way. Some translations render the phrase as “every kind of evil,” others as “every appearance of evil”; the context of testing (v. 21) favors rejecting every kind of evil that is discerned. Both traditions agree on the call to holiness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
