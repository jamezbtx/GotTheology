Passage

1 Thessalonians 4

Verses 9–12 · WEB

Paul commends the Thessalonians’ love for believers throughout Macedonia and urges them to grow further. Quiet living, responsible work, and proper conduct toward outsiders make that love visible in ordinary life. This passage is largely shared ground for Arminian and Reformed readers: God-taught love should produce dependable service rather than intrusion or avoidable dependence.

1 Thessalonians 4:9

9But concerning brotherly love, you have no need that one write to you. For you yourselves are taught by God to love one another,

Arminian

God has already taught the Thessalonians to love one another. Arminian readers see divine initiative bearing fruit in learned, practiced love. Paul’s acknowledgment encourages them without implying that they have reached a point where further exhortation is unnecessary.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see brotherly love as evidence of God’s inward work. The Spirit’s teaching and apostolic instruction are not rivals, since Paul immediately urges further growth. Both traditions understand authentic knowledge of God to reach outward in love.

1 Thessalonians 4:10

10for indeed you do it toward all the brothers who are in all Macedonia. But we exhort you, brothers, that you abound more and more;

Arminian

Their love extends throughout Macedonia, yet Paul still calls for increase. Arminian readers see faithful practice as a beginning to deepen, not a limit already reached. Grace enables generosity that can grow beyond familiar circles and established habits.

Reformed

Reformed readers see commendation and exhortation working together: God’s present fruit gives reason to seek more. Existing love does not make further diligence unnecessary. Both traditions affirm that Christian growth includes concrete care for believers beyond the local gathering.

1 Thessalonians 4:11

11and that you make it your ambition to lead a quiet life, and to do your own business, and to work with your own hands, even as we instructed you,

Arminian

Quiet living, attention to one’s own affairs, and work belong to Christian love. Arminian readers see responsibility expressed in ordinary daily habits rather than constant interference with others. The command dignifies labor without requiring indifference to a neighbor’s genuine need.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see faithful vocation and restrained conduct as expressions of sanctification. Paul directs ambition toward dependable living rather than disruptive self-importance. Working with one’s hands is honorable service, not a lower form of life than conspicuous religious activity.

1 Thessalonians 4:12

12that you may walk properly toward those who are outside, and may have need of nothing.

Arminian

Responsible living should commend the church to outsiders and reduce avoidable dependence. Arminian readers see practical conduct affecting the credibility of witness. This instruction should not become a condemnation of those unable to work or a refusal of needed mutual aid.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ordinary diligence serving both neighbor and public witness. Dependence caused by neglect is different from need caused by weakness or hardship. Both traditions recognize that self-support where possible and generous care for those in need can belong together.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
