Passage

1 Thessalonians 4

Verses 1–8 · WEB

Paul turns from thanksgiving and prayer to instruction about a life pleasing to God. Sanctification includes sexual integrity, honorable treatment of others, and refusal to exploit a fellow believer. These commands come with the authority of the Lord and the gift of the Holy Spirit. Both traditions affirm that grace calls and equips believers for holiness, not permission to sin.

1 Thessalonians 4:1

1Finally then, brothers, we beg and exhort you in the Lord Jesus, that as you received from us how you ought to walk and to please God, that you abound more and more.

Arminian

Paul urges further growth in the life that pleases God. Arminian readers see an ongoing response to received instruction and enabling grace. Christian maturity is not satisfied with a past decision or minimal obedience; believers are called to keep growing in faithful practice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace leading to an increasingly obedient life rather than removing moral obligation. The exhortation comes in the Lord Jesus, not as a rival to his saving work. Both traditions distinguish growth in pleasing God from earning acceptance by achievement.

1 Thessalonians 4:2

2For you know what instructions we gave you through the Lord Jesus.

Arminian

The instructions carry the authority of the Lord Jesus rather than mere personal preference. Arminian readers see obedience to Christ as the proper response to his gracious lordship. Familiarity with the teaching does not remove the need to put it into practice.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see apostolic instruction as binding because it is given through the Lord Jesus. Christian freedom is not independence from his authority. The practical commands that follow belong to discipleship, not simply to optional advice for especially serious believers.

1 Thessalonians 4:3

3For this is the will of God: your sanctification, that you abstain from sexual immorality,

Arminian

God’s stated will is their sanctification, here specified as abstaining from sexual immorality. Arminian readers see a clear moral calling accompanied by grace to obey, yet not an assertion that everyone necessarily complies. Holiness includes concrete choices rather than spiritual language detached from conduct.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish God’s revealed moral will from a claim that everything he commands is already being obeyed. Sanctification is a necessary fruit of union with Christ, not the ground of justification. The command gives practical content to holiness rather than treating it as mere religious status.

1 Thessalonians 4:4

4that each one of you know how to control his own body in sanctification and honor,

Arminian

In the WEB rendering, believers must learn to control their bodies with holiness and honor. Arminian readers see responsible self-discipline enabled by grace, not contempt for embodied life. Sexual integrity honors both one’s own body and the people affected by one’s actions.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the body as subject to Christ’s sanctifying rule. Self-control is a real duty sustained by the Spirit, not proof that believers can become holy apart from God. The emphasis is honorable conduct rather than surrender to appetite.

1 Thessalonians 4:5

5not in the passion of lust, even as the Gentiles who don’t know God,

Arminian

Paul contrasts holy conduct with desire ungoverned by knowledge of God. Arminian readers see knowing God as a relationship that should reshape desire and behavior. The contrast is not permission to despise outsiders or an assertion that Christians no longer experience temptation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see knowledge of God bearing moral fruit in the restraint and reordering of desire. Belonging to Christ does not make every impulse innocent. Both traditions recognize that believers must resist lust rather than baptize it with religious language.

1 Thessalonians 4:6

6that no one should take advantage of and wrong a brother or sister in this matter; because the Lord is an avenger in all these things, as also we forewarned you and testified.

Arminian

Sexual wrongdoing can exploit and injure another believer; the Lord takes that harm seriously. Arminian readers see accountability reinforcing the call to faithful conduct. Grace does not protect abuse from judgment, and spiritual claims cannot excuse taking advantage of others.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Lord defending those wronged rather than treating sexual conduct as merely private. Warnings about judgment belong within the church’s moral instruction. Assurance in Christ never authorizes exploitation or permits leaders to minimize the harm done to another person.

1 Thessalonians 4:7

7For God called us not for uncleanness, but in sanctification.

Arminian

God’s call leads away from uncleanness into holiness. Arminian readers see the purpose of grace as a transformed life, calling for a continuing faithful response. Being called is not a reason to tolerate the very conduct from which God summons his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect God’s saving call with the sanctified life it produces. Holiness is not an optional addition for a smaller class of believers. Both traditions reject an appeal to election or grace that leaves persistent uncleanness unchallenged.

1 Thessalonians 4:8

8Therefore he who rejects this doesn’t reject man, but God, who has also given his Holy Spirit to you.

Arminian

Rejecting this teaching means rejecting the God who has given the Holy Spirit. Arminian readers see both a serious warning against resisting grace and assurance that holiness need not be attempted without divine help. The Spirit’s gift summons believers to cooperate with God’s sanctifying work.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gift of the Spirit grounding both the duty and the possibility of holiness. Sanctification is God’s work within believers, not merely external rules, yet they remain responsible to obey. The Spirit’s preserving presence does not imply sinless maturity or make deliberate resistance harmless.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
