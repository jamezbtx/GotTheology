Passage

1 Thessalonians 4

Verses 13–18 · WEB

Paul addresses grief over believers who have died before Christ’s return. Jesus’ death and resurrection assure the church that its dead will not be excluded when he comes: dead and living believers will be gathered together to remain with him. Amillennial, premillennial including pretribulational, and postmillennial interpretations are not determined simply by the Arminian/Reformed distinction. The passage’s pastoral aim is shared hope and mutual comfort, not a complete end-times timetable.

1 Thessalonians 4:13

13But we don’t want you to be ignorant, brothers, concerning those who have fallen asleep, so that you don’t grieve like the rest, who have no hope.

Arminian

Paul does not forbid grief but distinguishes it from hopeless mourning. Arminian readers see Christian hope extending to believers who die before the Lord returns. Describing death as sleep points toward awakening and resurrection; it should not be pressed here into a complete account of the intermediate state.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see room for real sorrow within the assurance of resurrection. The dead in Christ have not missed the promised future. Paul’s aim is consolation for bereaved believers, not dismissal of their pain or a complete description of existence between death and resurrection.

1 Thessalonians 4:14

14For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep in Jesus.

Arminian

The certainty of Jesus’ death and resurrection grounds hope for those who die in him. Arminian readers see their future resting on Christ rather than on surviving until his return. The passage comforts the church with God’s faithful action, not a separate path of salvation for the dead.

Reformed

Reformed readers see union with the risen Christ securing the future of believers who have died. Their death does not sever them from his victory. Both traditions locate resurrection hope in the gospel’s central events rather than in speculation about the precise timetable.

1 Thessalonians 4:15

15For this we tell you by the word of the Lord, that we who are alive, who are left until the coming of the Lord, will in no way precede those who have fallen asleep.

Arminian

Those alive at Christ’s coming have no advantage over believers who have died. Arminian readers see Paul removing fear of exclusion, not setting a date. His inclusion of the living community in the description expresses shared expectation without establishing that he knew he would personally survive until the return.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the equal participation of dead and living believers in Christ’s coming. Paul speaks with the Lord’s authority to reassure the church. The statement does not identify a millennial scheme, and expectancy should not be converted into a timetable Paul supplies nowhere here.

1 Thessalonians 4:16

16For the Lord himself will descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel and with God’s trumpet. The dead in Christ will rise first,

Arminian

The Lord himself descends, and the dead in Christ rise before the living are gathered. Arminian readers see personal resurrection and Christ’s initiative as the center of hope. The shout, archangel’s voice, and trumpet convey the majesty of his arrival rather than permission for confident date-setting.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see bodily resurrection and the Lord’s own coming, not merely an inward spiritual experience. Rising first addresses the relation between deceased and living believers in this paragraph. It should not by itself be made to settle every question about other resurrections or the millennium.

1 Thessalonians 4:17

17then we who are alive, who are left, will be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air. So we will be with the Lord forever.

Arminian

Living and raised believers are gathered together to meet Christ and remain with him. Arminian readers share this hope while differing over its wider chronology. Premillennial readings, including pretribulational approaches, and other millennial readings cannot simply be identified with the Arminian/Reformed divide. The verse’s explicit promise is enduring fellowship with the Lord.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise center the promise on reunion with Christ and with his people. Amillennial, postmillennial, and premillennial interpretations relate this gathering differently to other end-time events; these questions are not settled merely by adopting a Reformed or Arminian account of salvation. Paul does not state the whole subsequent sequence here.

1 Thessalonians 4:18

18Therefore comfort one another with these words.

Arminian

The teaching is given for mutual comfort, not competitive speculation. Arminian readers see a responsibility to bring resurrection hope into the grief of the community. Believers can encourage one another with Christ’s victory and promised presence without agreeing on every detail of end-times chronology.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see pastoral consolation as the test of how this paragraph is used. The church should repeat its clear promises rather than let disputed sequences overwhelm them. Both traditions share the assurance that death will not exclude Christ’s people from life with him.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
