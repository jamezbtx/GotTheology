Passage

1 Thessalonians 1

Verses 6–10 · WEB

Paul continues his thanksgiving by tracing what happened when the gospel reached Thessalonica. The believers received the word in heavy affliction yet with Spirit-given joy, imitated the apostles and the Lord, and became an example across Macedonia and Achaia. Their conversion was so public that others reported it: they turned from idols to serve the living and true God and to wait for his Son from heaven, Jesus, who rescues from the coming wrath. This compact summary of conversion and hope is shared ground for both traditions.

1 Thessalonians 1:6

6You became imitators of us and of the Lord, having received the word in much affliction, with joy of the Holy Spirit,

Arminian

The Thessalonians received the word in “much affliction” yet with joy from the Holy Spirit. Arminian readers stress that “received” describes a real, personal response, empowered by the Spirit, in which they chose to embrace the message despite the cost.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that joy in suffering is something only the Spirit can produce, and they connect this verse to the Spirit’s power in verse 5. Both traditions agree that true reception of the word often comes with trouble, and that imitating Christ includes sharing his path of suffering.

1 Thessalonians 1:7

7so that you became an example to all who believe in Macedonia and in Achaia.

Arminian

Within a short time this young church became a model for believers throughout Greece. Arminian readers note how faithful obedience in one congregation encourages faith in others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s grace multiplying through the visible faithfulness of his people. Both traditions read this as a reminder that a church’s witness is carried by its life as much as by its preaching.

1 Thessalonians 1:8

8For from you the word of the Lord has been declared, not only in Macedonia and Achaia, but also in every place your faith toward God has gone out, so that we need not say anything.

Arminian

The word of the Lord “sounded out” from Thessalonica, a busy port on a major Roman road, so news of their faith spread widely. Arminian readers stress that ordinary believers became active participants in spreading the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that it is “the word of the Lord” that goes out; its power is God’s, and believers are the means he uses. Both traditions agree that the gospel naturally spreads through changed lives.

1 Thessalonians 1:9

9For they themselves report concerning us what kind of a reception we had from you, and how you turned to God from idols to serve a living and true God,

Arminian

Their conversion is summed up as turning to God from idols to serve the living and true God. This shows that the church was largely made up of Gentiles who had worshiped other gods. Arminian readers stress that turning is a real human act of repentance, enabled by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the Thessalonians truly turned, and they add that the turning results from God’s prior work through the word and Spirit (vv. 4–5). Both traditions describe conversion in the same terms: turning away from false gods and toward the service of the true God.

1 Thessalonians 1:10

10and to wait for his Son from heaven, whom he raised from the dead: Jesus, who delivers us from the wrath to come.

Arminian

Believers now wait for Jesus, God’s Son, raised from the dead, who will come from heaven and who rescues from the coming wrath. Arminian readers stress that this rescue is received by faith and gives believers a hope that shapes how they live in the present.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that Jesus himself rescues, and they often link this to his saving work for his people. Both traditions read “the wrath to come” as God’s real future judgment, and both find here a summary of the gospel: resurrection, return, and rescue. The letter will develop this hope in chapters 4–5.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
