Passage

1 Thessalonians 1

Verses 1–5 · WEB

Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy write to a young church in Thessalonica, founded only months earlier and quickly pressed by opposition (Acts 17:1–10). Instead of a correction, the letter opens with warm thanksgiving: the believers’ faith works, their love labors, and their hope endures. Paul is confident that they are chosen by God, because the gospel came to them with power, the Holy Spirit, and deep conviction. Both traditions treat this as a rich text on grace and assurance; the main difference is how they understand election and how Paul could know it.

1 Thessalonians 1:1

1Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy, to the assembly of the Thessalonians in God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ: Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Paul names his co-workers Silvanus and Timothy as co-senders, a reminder that the mission was a team effort. The church exists “in God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ,” placing the Father and the Son side by side as the source of its life. Arminian readers stress that grace and peace are offered freely and received by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note how naturally Paul sets Jesus beside the Father as the one source of grace and peace, an early witness to Christ’s full deity. The church’s identity is “in” God before it is anything else. Both traditions read the greeting as more than a formality: it is a real blessing.

1 Thessalonians 1:2

2We always give thanks to God for all of you, mentioning you in our prayers,

Arminian

Paul’s first word is thanks, and it covers “all of you.” Prayer for the churches was a steady habit for him, not an occasional duty. Arminian readers often note that Paul’s thanksgiving encourages believers who are still young and vulnerable in their faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul thanking God, not the Thessalonians, for their faith, a sign that he regards it as God’s work. Both traditions draw the same pastoral lesson: those who lead should pray constantly for the people they serve.

1 Thessalonians 1:3

3remembering without ceasing your work of faith and labor of love and perseverance of hope in our Lord Jesus Christ, before our God and Father.

Arminian

Faith, love, and hope appear together, each one active: faith that works, love that labors, hope that perseveres. Their hope is fixed on the Lord Jesus. Arminian readers stress that genuine faith shows itself in real, willing obedience, and that grace enables this response without overriding it.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that true faith is never idle; works flow from faith as its fruit and do not ground a person’s standing with God. The triad shows grace at work in the whole life. This is broad shared ground: neither tradition separates saving faith from a changed life.

1 Thessalonians 1:4

4We know, brothers loved by God, that you are chosen,

Arminian

Paul calls them “brothers loved by God” and says he knows they are chosen. Arminian readers typically understand election as God’s choice of a people in Christ, with individuals included through faith, grounded in God’s foreknowledge. Paul’s confidence rests on visible evidence of genuine faith in them.

Reformed

Reformed readers typically understand election here as God’s gracious, unconditional choice of particular persons, rooted in his love (“loved by God”). Paul does not see God’s secret decree directly; he infers their election from its fruit, the effects described in verses 3 and 5. Both traditions agree that election is a comfort and not a cause for speculation.

Key contrast Arminians commonly ground election in God’s foreknowledge of faith and see it as choosing a people in Christ whom individuals join by believing. Reformed theology commonly holds that God chose particular persons unconditionally, and that faith is the fruit, not the ground, of that choice.

1 Thessalonians 1:5

5and that our Good News came to you not in word only, but also in power, and in the Holy Spirit and with much assurance. You know what kind of men we showed ourselves to be among you for your sake.

Arminian

The gospel came not merely as words but with power, the Holy Spirit, and “much assurance,” which can refer to the preachers’ conviction, the hearers’, or both. Arminian readers see the Spirit’s powerful work persuading and enabling the hearers, who freely and joyfully responded. Paul also points to his team’s conduct as part of their witness.

Reformed

Reformed readers often read the power of the Spirit here as the effectual work by which God makes the outward word fruitful in the elect, explaining why some believe. Both traditions agree that preaching depends on the Spirit, and that the messengers’ lives must match their message. The exact sense of “assurance” remains debated among interpreters on both sides.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
