Passage

1 Thessalonians 2

Verses 17–20 · WEB

Paul ends the chapter by opening his heart. Torn away from the Thessalonians after only a short time, he felt like an orphan and tried repeatedly to return, but Satan blocked the way. He calls them his hope, joy, and crown of boasting before the Lord Jesus at his coming. Both traditions read these verses as a tender picture of pastoral love, set in the light of Christ’s return.

1 Thessalonians 2:17

17But we, brothers, being bereaved of you for a short season in presence, not in heart, tried even harder to see your face with great desire,

Arminian

Paul says he was “bereaved” of them, a word that suggests being orphaned, though they were apart only in person, not in heart. Arminian readers note the deep personal bond between a pastor and the people he loves.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree, seeing a pastor’s affection as a reflection of God’s own love for his people. This verse is shared ground for both traditions.

1 Thessalonians 2:18

18because we wanted to come to you—indeed, I, Paul, once and again—but Satan hindered us.

Arminian

Paul tried more than once to return, but Satan hindered him. Arminian readers stress that spiritual opposition is real and can thwart even sincere plans for a time.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Satan truly opposes God’s servants, while holding that he acts only within the limits God allows; the same events could be seen elsewhere as God’s guidance (Acts 16:6–7). Both traditions take the devil’s opposition seriously without making him equal to God.

1 Thessalonians 2:19

19For what is our hope, or joy, or crown of rejoicing? Isn’t it even you, before our Lord Jesus at his coming?

Arminian

Paul asks what his hope, joy, and crown will be at the Lord’s coming, and answers, “Isn’t it even you?” Arminian readers stress that the faithful perseverance of his converts will be his reward, encouraging them to continue.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church as the fruit of God’s grace through Paul’s labor, a fruit he expects to see on the last day. Both traditions agree that Paul’s “crown” is not self-praise but joy in what God has done.

1 Thessalonians 2:20

20For you are our glory and our joy.

Arminian

Paul repeats: they are his glory and his joy. Arminian readers note the pastoral warmth that strengthens believers to endure.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same warmth and hear its eternal horizon. Both traditions close the chapter on common ground: the shepherd’s joy is his people, standing faithful at Christ’s coming.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
