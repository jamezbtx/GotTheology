Passage

1 Thessalonians 2

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Paul now defends the character of his ministry in Thessalonica, probably because opponents were attacking his motives after his hurried departure (Acts 17:5–10). He reminds the believers that he came after suffering at Philippi, spoke boldly, refused flattery and greed, and worked night and day to avoid being a burden. He was gentle like a nursing mother and urged them like a father to live worthily of God, who calls them into his kingdom and glory. Both traditions draw from this passage a shared picture of faithful, self-giving ministry.

1 Thessalonians 2:1

1For you yourselves know, brothers, our visit to you wasn’t in vain,

Arminian

Paul appeals to what they themselves know: his visit was not empty or without result. Arminian readers note that Paul often points to shared, observable experience as evidence for his message.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the visit’s fruitfulness as proof of God’s work through it, echoing 1:5. Both traditions agree that the lasting effect of the gospel among them vindicated Paul’s ministry.

1 Thessalonians 2:2

2but having suffered before and been shamefully treated, as you know, at Philippi, we grew bold in our God to tell you the Good News of God in much conflict.

Arminian

After being beaten and imprisoned at Philippi (Acts 16), Paul and Silas still preached boldly in Thessalonica. Their courage came “in our God,” not from themselves. Arminian readers stress the human faithfulness that grace strengthens.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God supplied the boldness. Both traditions see here a model for ministry under pressure: courage drawn from God in the midst of conflict.

1 Thessalonians 2:3

3For our exhortation is not of error, nor of uncleanness, nor in deception.

Arminian

Paul denies three charges: that his message came from error, from impure motives, or from trickery. Arminian readers note that he is defending the integrity of both his message and his methods.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that faithful preaching must be true in content and pure in motive. This is shared ground: there is no theological divide on this verse.

1 Thessalonians 2:4

4But even as we have been approved by God to be entrusted with the Good News, so we speak—not as pleasing men, but God, who tests our hearts.

Arminian

Paul was approved by God and entrusted with the gospel, so he speaks to please God, who tests hearts, not to please people. Arminian readers stress the steward’s accountability: Paul was tested and found faithful, and he must remain so.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that God both entrusts the gospel and searches the heart, so preachers answer to him alone. Both traditions use this verse to warn against shaping the message to gain human approval.

1 Thessalonians 2:5

5For neither were we at any time found using words of flattery, as you know, nor a cloak of covetousness (God is witness),

Arminian

Paul never used flattery or a pretext for greed, calling God as his witness. Arminian readers note that a preacher’s motives are open to God’s scrutiny and should be open to the church’s as well.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree, seeing the appeal to God as witness as a solemn oath of integrity. Both traditions treat honesty about money and motives as essential to faithful ministry.

1 Thessalonians 2:6

6nor seeking glory from men (neither from you nor from others), when we might have claimed authority as apostles of Christ.

Arminian

As apostles of Christ, Paul’s team could have claimed authority or support, but they did not seek glory from people. Arminian readers see a deliberate choice to give up rights for the sake of the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christlike self-denial here, a pattern Paul describes in 1 Corinthians 9. There is little difference between the traditions on this verse.

1 Thessalonians 2:7

7But we were gentle among you, like a nursing mother cherishes her own children.

Arminian

Paul uses a tender image: he was gentle, like a nursing mother caring for her own children. Some manuscripts read “infants” instead of “gentle,” a known textual question; the WEB follows “gentle.” Arminian readers stress the warmth and personal care of genuine pastoral ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers also highlight the gentleness of Christ’s servants, which reflects the gentleness of God toward his people. Both traditions treat the textual variant as a real but minor question that does not change the passage’s meaning.

1 Thessalonians 2:8

8Even so, affectionately longing for you, we were well pleased to impart to you not the Good News of God only, but also our own souls, because you had become very dear to us.

Arminian

Paul shared not only the gospel but his very life with them, because they had become dear to him. Arminian readers note that love for people is part of faithful proclamation, not a substitute for it.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that truth and love belong together in ministry. This verse is shared pastoral ground for both traditions.

1 Thessalonians 2:9

9For you remember, brothers, our labor and travail; for working night and day, that we might not burden any of you, we preached to you the Good News of God.

Arminian

Paul and his team worked “night and day,” probably at tentmaking (Acts 18:3), so they would not burden anyone while they preached. Arminian readers see sacrificial labor that removed obstacles to the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a pattern of costly service. Both traditions note that Paul did not forbid churches from supporting ministers; he simply chose not to use that right here.

1 Thessalonians 2:10

10You are witnesses with God how holy, righteously, and blamelessly we behaved ourselves toward you who believe.

Arminian

Paul calls the believers and God as witnesses to his holy, righteous, and blameless conduct. Arminian readers stress that holiness of life is both possible and expected by grace, and that it lends credibility to the message.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that godly conduct confirms the message, while noting that “blameless” refers to a life above reproach, not sinless perfection. Both traditions hold that a minister’s life must match his preaching.

1 Thessalonians 2:11

11As you know, we exhorted, comforted, and implored every one of you, as a father does his own children,

Arminian

Now Paul turns to a father image: he exhorted, comforted, and urged each one personally, as a father does his children. Arminian readers note the individual, persuasive nature of pastoral care, which appeals to the will.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that pastors must speak to people personally, using the exhortations God uses to bring his people to maturity. Mother and father images together give a full picture of pastoral care.

1 Thessalonians 2:12

12to the end that you should walk worthily of God, who calls you into his own Kingdom and glory.

Arminian

The goal of Paul’s exhortation is a life worthy of God, “who calls you into his own Kingdom and glory.” The present tense suggests an ongoing call. Arminian readers typically understand this call as a gracious, sincere summons to which believers keep responding in faith and obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers often read the call as God’s effectual call, which brings his people into the kingdom and keeps drawing them toward glory. Both traditions agree that a worthy life is the response to grace, not the means of earning it.

Key contrast Arminians typically read God’s call as a sincere, grace-enabled summons that people may resist; Reformed theology typically reads it in Paul as the effectual call that unfailingly brings the called into the kingdom.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
