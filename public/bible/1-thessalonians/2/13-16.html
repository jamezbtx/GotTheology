Passage

1 Thessalonians 2

Verses 13–16 · WEB

Paul thanks God again that the Thessalonians received his preaching as what it truly is, God’s word, which is now at work in those who believe. Their resulting suffering at the hands of their own countrymen matched what the churches in Judea suffered from some of their fellow Jews. Verses 15–16 are among Paul’s sharpest words, and interpreters handle them with care: they address the opponents who rejected Jesus and hindered the mission, not every Jewish person, and they must be read alongside Romans 9–11. A minority of scholars have argued these verses were a later addition, but all known manuscripts contain them.

1 Thessalonians 2:13

13For this cause we also thank God without ceasing that when you received from us the word of the message of God, you accepted it not as the word of men, but as it is in truth, God’s word, which also works in you who believe.

Arminian

The Thessalonians received the message not as human opinion but as the word of God, which keeps working in those who believe. Arminian readers stress that “accepted” describes a genuine human response, as the hearers welcomed the word by faith under the Spirit’s gracious influence.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the word itself is active and effective, and they connect its power to God’s working within believers. Both traditions affirm the full authority of the apostolic word as God’s word.

Key contrast Both affirm that the word is God’s and that it is truly received. Arminians tend to stress the hearers’ grace-enabled reception; Reformed readers tend to stress the word’s effective work in those God has chosen.

1 Thessalonians 2:14

14For you, brothers, became imitators of the assemblies of God which are in Judea in Christ Jesus; for you also suffered the same things from your own countrymen, even as they did from the Jews

Arminian

These Gentile believers became imitators of the churches in Judea, because they too suffered from their own countrymen. Arminian readers note that suffering for the gospel links believers in different places and cultures.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a shared pattern of suffering among God’s people. Both traditions note that “your own countrymen” likely included local Gentiles stirred up against the church (compare Acts 17:5–9), which suggests Paul is describing opposition from the local community, not making a statement about any ethnic group.

1 Thessalonians 2:15

15who killed both the Lord Jesus and their own prophets, and drove us out, and don’t please God, and are contrary to all men,

Arminian

Paul describes the Judean opponents who killed the Lord Jesus and the prophets and drove out the apostles. Both traditions read this as a description of specific persecutors in light of Jesus’ own words (Matthew 23:29–37), not as a charge against all Jewish people. Paul himself was Jewish and longed for his people’s salvation (Romans 9:1–5).

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Paul is speaking of those who actively opposed the gospel, and they reject any use of this verse to justify contempt for Jewish people. Both traditions read it alongside Romans 11, where Paul affirms God’s continuing purposes for Israel.

1 Thessalonians 2:16

16forbidding us to speak to the Gentiles that they may be saved, to fill up their sins always. But wrath has come on them to the uttermost.

Arminian

The opponents hindered the apostles from preaching to the Gentiles that they might be saved, “filling up” their sins. “Wrath has come on them to the uttermost” is debated: it may refer to a recent event, to the judgment coming in AD 70, or to God’s certain final verdict. Arminian readers often stress that the opponents’ guilt grew through their own continued rejection of the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers often read “to fill up their sins” in light of God’s overruling judgment, as in Genesis 15:16, while still holding the opponents fully responsible. Both traditions agree that wrath here is not God’s final word on Israel as a people, in view of Romans 11. The time reference of the wrath remains uncertain.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
