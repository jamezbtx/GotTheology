Passage

1 Thessalonians 3

Verses 1–5 · WEB

Separated from the Thessalonian believers and anxious about their endurance under affliction, Paul sends Timothy to strengthen their faith. Suffering had been anticipated, but temptation still poses a serious pastoral concern. Both traditions affirm the need for encouragement and vigilance, while explaining differently how the danger of falling away relates to God’s preserving grace.

1 Thessalonians 3:1

1Therefore when we couldn’t stand it any longer, we thought it good to be left behind at Athens alone,

Arminian

Paul accepts being left alone rather than leaving the Thessalonians without help. Arminian readers see love taking practical action to support vulnerable faith. Concern for perseverance is not merely an abstract debate; it can require personal sacrifice and renewed contact.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see pastoral affection expressed through costly action. Confidence in God’s care does not make Paul indifferent to the church’s circumstances. Sending help is part of faithful ministry, not a denial that God sustains his people.

1 Thessalonians 3:2

2and sent Timothy, our brother and God’s servant in the Good News of Christ, to establish you and to comfort you concerning your faith,

Arminian

Timothy is sent to establish and encourage believers concerning their faith. Arminian readers see God working through fellow servants to strengthen a response of trust that needs nourishment. Grace does not eliminate the need for instruction, encouragement, and mutual care.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Timothy’s ministry as one of the means through which God preserves believers. God’s sovereignty gives significance to teaching and encouragement rather than replacing them. Both traditions recognize strengthening the church as service to Christ’s gospel.

1 Thessalonians 3:3

3that no one would be moved by these afflictions. For you know that we are appointed to this task.

Arminian

Affliction should not shake the church loose from its faith. Arminian readers see preparation for suffering as essential to faithful endurance. Being appointed to encounter suffering does not mean being appointed to abandon Christ, nor does hardship prove that God has withdrawn his love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see suffering as falling within God’s providence rather than defeating it. The church should not mistake affliction for evidence that the gospel has failed. Paul’s purpose is to steady believers, not to make them passive toward danger or suffering.

1 Thessalonians 3:4

4For most certainly, when we were with you, we told you beforehand that we are to suffer affliction, even as it happened, and you know.

Arminian

Paul had warned that affliction would come, and events confirmed his warning. Arminian readers see realistic teaching protecting believers from disillusionment. The gospel does not promise immunity from hardship; remembering this helps them continue trusting God when suffering arrives.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see prior instruction as preparation for perseverance. Suffering is not an unexpected exception to Christian life. Paul reminds the church of what it already knows so that experience is interpreted through faithful teaching rather than fear.

1 Thessalonians 3:5

5For this cause I also, when I couldn’t stand it any longer, sent that I might know your faith, for fear that by any means the tempter had tempted you, and our labor would have been in vain.

Arminian

Paul fears that temptation might undermine their faith and render his labor fruitless. Arminian readers see a real danger that believers may abandon faith under pressure, not merely appear to struggle. His response is to seek news and send strengthening help rather than surrender to anxiety.

Reformed

Reformed readers take the danger and Paul’s concern seriously without concluding that the regenerate can finally perish. Paul does not possess infallible knowledge of each person’s heart; continuing faith provides evidence of God’s work. His warning and Timothy’s ministry are means through which God preserves believers.

Key contrast Both affirm real temptation and the necessity of pastoral care. Arminian readers see the possibility of genuine apostasy; Reformed readers distinguish final loss of a profession from loss of regeneration and see care as a means of perseverance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
