Passage

Proverbs 10

Verses 1–16 · WEB

With the heading “The proverbs of Solomon,” the book shifts from the long discourses of chapters 1–9 to the first sentence collection (10:1–22:16). Most sayings are two-line antitheses that set the righteous against the wicked, the diligent against the lazy, and wise speech against foolish talk. They are general observations about how life in God’s world usually goes, not unconditional promises, and both traditions read them that way.

Proverbs 10:1

1The proverbs of Solomon. A wise son makes a glad father; but a foolish son brings grief to his mother.

Arminian

The heading “The proverbs of Solomon” opens a new collection. The first saying grounds wisdom in family life: a son’s choices bring joy or grief to his parents.

Arminian readers note how the discourses’ call to choose wisdom now becomes concrete, everyday conduct. The son’s choices are genuinely his own and genuinely affect others.

Reformed

From here the form changes: short, self-contained couplets, usually antithetic (“but...”). Each proverb should be read on its own terms and alongside its neighbors, not stretched into a full doctrine.

Reformed readers likewise see wisdom as lived out in the covenant household (cf. Deuteronomy 6:6–7). Father and mother are named in parallel, showing that both parents are invested in the child’s path.

Proverbs 10:2

2Treasures of wickedness profit nothing, but righteousness delivers from death.

Arminian

Wealth gained by wickedness looks profitable but ultimately is not. Righteousness “delivers from death,” a line repeated in 11:4.

Arminian readers take this as a general truth about how God’s moral order works, and as a call to integrity over gain.

Reformed

“Death” here most naturally means premature or disastrous death, the kind brought on by wicked schemes. Many readers in both traditions also hear an ultimate horizon, since final judgment brings this principle to completion.

Reformed readers stress that the righteousness of Proverbs is covenant faithfulness flowing from the fear of the LORD, not a merit that earns salvation.

Proverbs 10:3

3The LORD will not allow the soul of the righteous to go hungry, but he thrusts away the desire of the wicked.

Arminian

The LORD himself is named as the one who sustains the righteous and frustrates the wicked’s cravings. This is a proverb, not an unconditional promise; Scripture also records faithful people who go hungry (Hebrews 11:37; 2 Corinthians 11:27).

Arminian readers see the verse as encouragement to trust God’s care while working faithfully, without turning it into a guarantee about any individual’s circumstances.

Reformed

Like Psalm 37:25, the verse describes God’s characteristic faithfulness to his people. Both traditions agree that it states a general pattern under God’s providence, not a promise that the righteous will never lack food.

Reformed readers emphasize providence: the LORD actively governs both provision and frustration. They also read the verse in light of Job and Lamentations, where the pattern is tested and God remains good.

Proverbs 10:4

4He becomes poor who works with a lazy hand, but the hand of the diligent brings wealth.

Arminian

Diligence and laziness have typical results. The “lazy hand” tends toward poverty; the diligent hand toward wealth.

Arminian readers see human effort and responsibility affirmed. God has made a world where work matters, and this is no conflict with dependence on him (compare 10:22).

Reformed

This is one of many proverbs on work (cf. 6:6–11). It describes a general pattern and should not be used to blame every poor person for laziness; Proverbs elsewhere recognizes injustice as a cause of poverty (13:23).

Reformed readers hold diligence and divine blessing together without tension, since God ordinarily works through means. Both traditions agree on the practical lesson.

Proverbs 10:5

5He who gathers in summer is a wise son, but he who sleeps during the harvest is a son who causes shame.

Arminian

Agricultural timing: the wise son gathers when the harvest is ready; the shameful son sleeps through it. The verse continues the family theme of 10:1.

Arminian readers apply it to seizing opportunity, including spiritual opportunity, while it is available.

Reformed

Wisdom includes knowing the right time to act (cf. Ecclesiastes 3:1–2). Missing the harvest brings shame on the household, not only on the son.

Reformed readers note that diligence is a fruit of wisdom, not merely a personality trait. Many in both traditions also hear an echo of Jesus’ call to work while the harvest is plentiful (Matthew 9:37–38), as an application rather than the original sense.

Proverbs 10:6

6Blessings are on the head of the righteous, but violence covers the mouth of the wicked.

Arminian

Blessing rests on the righteous like a crown. The second line is repeated in 10:11, and the Hebrew can also be read as “the mouth of the wicked conceals violence,” which suggests hidden malice behind words.

Arminian readers note how character and speech go together: what people say reveals and shapes what they are.

Reformed

The contrast between “head” and “mouth” may be deliberate: blessing comes onto the righteous, while violence comes out of (or is concealed by) the wicked’s speech.

Reformed readers connect blessing to God’s covenant favor rather than to human deserving. Both traditions read the verse as a general observation about where each way of life tends to lead.

Proverbs 10:7

7The memory of the righteous is blessed, but the name of the wicked will rot.

Arminian

A good memory outlives the righteous, while the wicked’s name decays. Reputation is a real, if lesser, good.

Arminian readers see a reason to choose faithfulness: how we live leaves a lasting trace for those who come after us.

Reformed

Psalm 112:6 says the righteous will be remembered forever. Proverbs here speaks of the community’s memory, which tends to honor the righteous and forget or despise the wicked.

Reformed readers add that God’s remembrance is what finally matters (cf. Malachi 3:16). Both traditions agree that the verse speaks of a general pattern, not every case of earthly reputation.

Proverbs 10:8

8The wise in heart accept commandments, but a chattering fool will fall.

Arminian

The wise in heart receive instruction; the “chattering fool” (literally a fool of lips) talks his way to ruin.

Arminian readers note the link between teachability and stability. Listening before speaking is a choice that protects a person from falling.

Reformed

Receiving commandments is set against compulsive talking. Wisdom listens; folly cannot stop speaking. The same second line appears again in 10:10.

Reformed readers see a heart shaped by God’s word as the root of wise conduct. Both traditions draw the same practical lesson (cf. James 1:19).

Proverbs 10:9

9He who walks blamelessly walks surely, but he who perverts his ways will be found out.

Arminian

The person of integrity walks securely, with nothing to hide. The one who twists his ways will eventually be exposed.

Arminian readers see a call to straightforward living and a warning that crooked paths are never truly safe.

Reformed

“Surely” means securely or safely. Integrity brings peace because there is no fear of discovery.

Reformed readers note that “found out” may include both human discovery and God’s judgment (cf. Numbers 32:23). Both traditions read the verse as a general observation about integrity and exposure.

Proverbs 10:10

10One who winks with the eye causes sorrow, but a chattering fool will fall.

Arminian

Winking with the eye suggests a hint of malice or deceit (compare 6:13). Such gestures cause sorrow to others.

Arminian readers note that the second line repeats 10:8b exactly in the Hebrew. Some translations follow the ancient Greek here (roughly, “but one who rebukes boldly makes peace”); the WEB keeps the Hebrew, and these notes follow the WEB.

Reformed

The pairing suggests two forms of harmful communication: the sly wink and the endless talker. Both damage relationships.

Reformed readers similarly treat the repeated line as part of the received Hebrew text. Both traditions see the verse as a warning that deceitful gestures and careless talk do real harm.

Proverbs 10:11

11The mouth of the righteous is a spring of life, but violence covers the mouth of the wicked.

Arminian

The righteous person’s speech is a “spring of life,” refreshing and sustaining others (compare 13:14; 18:4). The second line repeats 10:6b.

Arminian readers see speech as a primary way people either give life or do harm, and as an area of real moral responsibility.

Reformed

The image of a fountain implies a source within; speech flows from the heart (cf. 4:23; Matthew 12:34).

Reformed readers stress that a heart renewed by God produces life-giving words. Both traditions agree that the verse sets out a pattern of character and speech, not a mechanical rule.

Proverbs 10:12

12Hatred stirs up strife, but love covers all wrongs.

Arminian

Hatred keeps conflict going, while love covers offenses. 1 Peter 4:8 echoes this verse (“love covers a multitude of sins”), and James 5:20 uses similar language.

Arminian readers see love as an active choice to forgive and not to retaliate, one empowered by grace and essential to Christian community.

Reformed

To “cover” here means to refuse to expose or prolong an offense, not to hide wrongdoing that must be addressed. Love declines to stir up strife.

Reformed readers note that this human covering reflects God’s own forgiving grace (cf. Psalm 32:1). Both traditions read the verse the same way pastorally.

Proverbs 10:13

13Wisdom is found on the lips of him who has discernment, but a rod is for the back of him who is void of understanding.

Arminian

Wisdom shows itself in the speech of the discerning. The one without understanding learns, if at all, through painful discipline (“a rod”).

Arminian readers see consequences as part of how God may still teach those who refuse instruction.

Reformed

The contrast is between wisdom found on the lips and correction applied to the back. The fool’s lack of sense invites discipline.

Reformed readers see discipline as a means God uses for correction (cf. Hebrews 12:5–11). Both traditions read the “rod” here in its ancient setting and draw a shared lesson: the teachable avoid much pain.

Proverbs 10:14

14Wise men lay up knowledge, but the mouth of the foolish is near ruin.

Arminian

Wise people store up knowledge for later use; the fool’s mouth is “near ruin,” always one careless word away from disaster.

Arminian readers stress the responsibility to cultivate wisdom deliberately over time.

Reformed

The verse contrasts a treasury with an open mouth. The wise accumulate; the fool spills.

Reformed readers connect storing up knowledge with meditating on God’s word (cf. Psalm 119:11). Both traditions find the same practical counsel.

Proverbs 10:15

15The rich man’s wealth is his strong city. The destruction of the poor is their poverty.

Arminian

This is an observation, not a recommendation. Wealth gives the rich a kind of security, while poverty leaves the poor exposed to ruin.

Arminian readers note that Proverbs describes how things work in a fallen world without endorsing trust in riches; 18:10–11 contrasts the rich man’s imagined wall with the true refuge in the LORD’s name.

Reformed

Proverbs is realistic about money’s practical effects while warning against relying on it (11:4, 28).

Reformed readers likewise see social observation here, not moral approval. Both traditions read the verse as a call to compassion for the poor (cf. 14:31) and to find security in God rather than wealth.

Proverbs 10:16

16The labor of the righteous leads to life. The increase of the wicked leads to sin.

Arminian

What the righteous earn tends toward life; what the wicked gain feeds further sin. The same resources lead in different directions depending on the heart.

Arminian readers see character determining how blessings are used, and a call to steward gains toward life.

Reformed

The verse contrasts not just two outcomes but two trajectories. Prosperity in wicked hands becomes fuel for sin.

Reformed readers note the theme of wages and fruit that Paul develops (cf. Romans 6:23). Both traditions read the verse as a general observation about where each way leads.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
