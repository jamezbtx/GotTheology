Passage

Proverbs 10

Verses 17–32 · WEB

The second half of chapter 10 continues the Solomonic sentence collection. Speech dominates (correction, slander, restraint, the tongue and lips), alongside the contrasting ends of the righteous and the wicked. As elsewhere in the collection, the sayings describe typical outcomes under God’s rule rather than guarantees for every case.

Proverbs 10:17

17He is in the way of life who heeds correction, but he who forsakes reproof leads others astray.

Arminian

Heeding correction keeps a person on the path of life; rejecting reproof harms others too. The Hebrew can be read either as “leads others astray” (as in the WEB) or as “goes astray.”

Arminian readers note that our response to correction affects both our own path and the people who follow us.

Reformed

Teachability again marks the way of life (compare 9:8–9). Refusing reproof is not a private matter; it spreads.

Reformed readers agree and see receiving correction as a fruit of humility worked by grace. Both traditions apply the verse to leaders and parents in particular.

Proverbs 10:18

18He who hides hatred has lying lips. He who utters a slander is a fool.

Arminian

Two sins of speech: concealed hatred that hides behind lies, and open slander. Either way, the speaker is shown to be a fool.

Arminian readers note that hidden hatred is a choice of the heart that eventually corrupts the lips.

Reformed

Hatred tends either to disguise itself in flattery or to burst out in slander. Neither is wise.

Reformed readers trace both forms of sin to the heart (cf. Matthew 15:18–19). Both traditions draw the same pastoral lesson about honest, gracious speech.

Proverbs 10:19

19In the multitude of words there is no lack of disobedience, but he who restrains his lips does wisely.

Arminian

The more one talks, the more likely one is to sin. Restraint in speech is wisdom.

Arminian readers see self-control as a practical discipline that believers grow in by grace and practice (cf. James 1:19; 3:2).

Reformed

This is a general observation, not a ban on speaking at length; but excess words multiply the opportunities for sin.

Reformed readers connect the verse to James 3 on taming the tongue. Both traditions agree on its practical wisdom.

Proverbs 10:20

20The tongue of the righteous is like choice silver. The heart of the wicked is of little worth.

Arminian

The righteous person’s words are valuable like choice silver, while the wicked heart, the source of speech, is worth little.

Arminian readers note how character gives speech its value: a righteous life makes words worth hearing.

Reformed

The two lines contrast tongue and heart, assuming that speech reveals what is within.

Reformed readers stress that the worth of righteous speech derives from a heart renewed by God. Both traditions agree that the verse ties speech closely to character.

Proverbs 10:21

21The lips of the righteous feed many, but the foolish die for lack of understanding.

Arminian

Righteous speech “feeds many,” nourishing others with guidance and encouragement. Fools, by contrast, starve themselves through lack of understanding.

Arminian readers see an invitation to use words for others’ good, and a warning that refusing wisdom harms oneself most.

Reformed

The image recalls a shepherd feeding a flock. Wise words are a form of care.

Reformed readers connect this to the ministry of the word in the church (cf. Jeremiah 3:15). Both traditions read the verse as a general observation about the power of speech.

Proverbs 10:22

22The LORD’s blessing brings wealth, and he adds no trouble to it.

Arminian

Wealth that comes from the LORD’s blessing is free of the sorrow attached to ill-gotten gain. The second line can also be read “and toil adds nothing to it”; the Hebrew allows both.

Arminian readers hold this together with 10:4: human work is real, but blessing finally comes from God.

Reformed

The verse names the LORD as the source of real prosperity. On either reading of the second line, his blessing is primary.

Reformed readers stress God’s sovereignty in provision. Both traditions agree that this is a general truth and not a promise of wealth to every believer; Proverbs also prizes a little with the fear of the LORD over great treasure with trouble (15:16).

Proverbs 10:23

23It is a fool’s pleasure to do wickedness, but wisdom is a man of understanding’s pleasure.

Arminian

A fool enjoys wickedness the way one might enjoy a game; a person of understanding takes pleasure in wisdom.

Arminian readers note that what we delight in shapes who we become, and they see the verse as a call to cultivate good desires.

Reformed

Desires reveal character. The fool’s delight in evil shows a heart turned the wrong way.

Reformed readers see renewed desires as a work of God’s grace (cf. Psalm 37:4). Both traditions agree that wisdom includes learning to love what is good.

Proverbs 10:24

24What the wicked fear will overtake them, but the desire of the righteous will be granted.

Arminian

The wicked’s fears catch up with them; the righteous receive what they desire. Like the rest of the collection, this states a general pattern, not a guarantee that every wish will be met.

Arminian readers read “the desire of the righteous” as desires shaped by righteousness, which align with God’s purposes and so are fulfilled in his time.

Reformed

The contrast is between dread realized and hope fulfilled. Final judgment brings both to completion.

Reformed readers similarly see the righteous person’s desire as ultimately fulfilled in God’s good purposes (cf. Psalm 145:19). Both traditions read the verse as shared ground: the righteous are not promised every earthly wish, but their deepest hope will not fail.

Proverbs 10:25

25When the whirlwind passes, the wicked is no more; but the righteous stand firm forever.

Arminian

A whirlwind sweeps the wicked away; the righteous stand firm. Jesus’ parable of the two builders uses a similar image (Matthew 7:24–27).

Arminian readers see stability as the fruit of a life built on righteousness. The righteous who remain in God’s way are not swept away.

Reformed

“Forever” expresses the lasting foundation of the righteous, in contrast to the sudden end of the wicked.

Reformed readers see the security of the righteous resting on God himself. Both traditions decline to use a single proverb as a proof text for their wider views on perseverance; the verse states a general truth about the ends of two ways of life.

Proverbs 10:26

26As vinegar to the teeth, and as smoke to the eyes, so is the sluggard to those who send him.

Arminian

Vivid, everyday imagery: vinegar sets the teeth on edge and smoke stings the eyes. A lazy messenger is just as irritating.

Arminian readers see faithfulness in small tasks as part of a godly life and a real responsibility to others.

Reformed

The proverb uses humor to make its point about reliability. Whoever sends a sluggard suffers for it.

Reformed readers connect this to faithful service in every vocation (cf. Colossians 3:23). Both traditions read the verse the same way.

Proverbs 10:27

27The fear of the LORD prolongs days, but the years of the wicked shall be shortened.

Arminian

The fear of the LORD tends to lengthen life; wickedness tends to shorten it. This is a general observation, not an unconditional promise; godly people sometimes die young and wicked people sometimes live long (Ecclesiastes 7:15).

Arminian readers see a real link between how one lives and how life tends to go, under God’s ordering of the world.

Reformed

The verse restates 9:11 in sentence form. It describes the typical pattern of a world under God’s rule.

Reformed readers likewise read it as general, not absolute, and see its full realization in the life to come. Both traditions agree on that interpretive principle.

Proverbs 10:28

28The prospect of the righteous is joy, but the hope of the wicked will perish.

Arminian

The righteous look forward to joy, while the hopes of the wicked perish. What a person hopes for reveals where they have placed their trust.

Arminian readers see a call to set one’s hope on God, who does not disappoint those who trust him.

Reformed

“Prospect” refers to expectation or hope. The righteous have a future; the wicked’s hope collapses.

Reformed readers connect the righteous person’s hope to God’s faithfulness (cf. Romans 5:5). Both traditions read the verse as a general contrast between two kinds of hope.

Proverbs 10:29

29The way of the LORD is a stronghold to the upright, but it is a destruction to the workers of iniquity.

Arminian

“The way of the LORD” here may refer to God’s way of acting or to the path he prescribes. Either way, it protects the upright and spells ruin for evildoers.

Arminian readers note that the same way has opposite effects depending on how people relate to it.

Reformed

The LORD’s way is a refuge to those who walk uprightly and a destruction to those who work iniquity.

Reformed readers see God’s justice and protection as two sides of his character. Both traditions agree on the plain sense; the verse does not resolve their wider differences.

Proverbs 10:30

30The righteous will never be removed, but the wicked will not dwell in the land.

Arminian

The righteous will not be uprooted, while the wicked lose their place in the land. The language echoes the covenant promise of the land (cf. Psalm 37:9, 11, 29).

Arminian readers see a general promise of stability for those who remain in righteousness.

Reformed

In Israel’s setting, dwelling in the land meant covenant blessing. Jesus extends the theme to inheriting the earth (Matthew 5:5).

Reformed readers see the security of God’s people grounded in his covenant faithfulness. Both traditions read the verse as a general statement and not as a proof text for a particular doctrine of perseverance.

Proverbs 10:31

31The mouth of the righteous produces wisdom, but the perverse tongue will be cut off.

Arminian

Righteous speech bears the fruit of wisdom; perverse speech will be cut off.

Arminian readers note that our words are accountable to God and a real part of moral life.

Reformed

The image of a mouth that “produces” wisdom is of a tree bearing fruit. A perverse tongue is like a branch cut off.

Reformed readers trace good speech to a heart renewed by God (cf. Luke 6:45). Both traditions share the practical lesson.

Proverbs 10:32

32The lips of the righteous know what is acceptable, but the mouth of the wicked is perverse.

Arminian

The righteous know what is fitting to say; the wicked speak perversely. The chapter closes as it has run, with speech as a test of character.

Arminian readers see wisdom in speech as something learned and practiced, by grace, over a lifetime.

Reformed

“What is acceptable” can mean what pleases others or what pleases God. The righteous know how to speak well.

Reformed readers see such discernment as a gift of wisdom from God (cf. Colossians 4:6). Both traditions agree that the verse calls believers to gracious, truthful speech.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
