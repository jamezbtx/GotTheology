Passage

Proverbs 14

Verses 25–35 · WEB

The chapter closes with sayings on truthful witness, the fear of the LORD as fortress and fountain, the king and his people, patience, envy, care for the poor, and national righteousness. Verse 31 grounds kindness to the needy in honoring the Maker of all people. A few lines involve uncertain Hebrew or ancient versions that differ, notably verses 32 and 33, and the notes mark these. Both traditions see this section as linking personal character, social justice, and public life to reverence for God.

Proverbs 14:25

25A truthful witness saves souls, but a false witness is deceitful.

Arminian

A truthful witness saves lives, while a false witness deceives. In court, honest testimony can mean life or death.

Arminian readers note the weight of every witness’s responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers note again the ninth commandment and God’s concern for justice.

Both traditions agree that truthfulness can protect the innocent and that lies endanger them.

Proverbs 14:26

26In the fear of the LORD is a secure fortress, and he will be a refuge for his children.

Arminian

In the fear of the LORD is a secure fortress, and he will be a refuge for his children. Reverence for God brings security that extends to the family.

Arminian readers note that the fear of the LORD is a lived relationship of trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God himself is the refuge, not the one who fears him.

Both traditions agree that the fear of the LORD is a source of confidence, not anxiety, for God’s people.

Proverbs 14:27

27The fear of the LORD is a fountain of life, turning people from the snares of death.

Arminian

The fear of the LORD is a fountain of life, turning people from the snares of death. The line echoes 13:14, where the teaching of the wise plays the same role.

Arminian readers note that reverence for God steers people away from destructive paths.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the fear of the LORD is both a gift and a guide.

Both traditions agree that true wisdom and true life are found in relationship with God.

Proverbs 14:28

28In the multitude of people is the king’s glory, but in the lack of people is the destruction of the prince.

Arminian

A king’s glory lies in a large population, and a prince is ruined by the lack of people. Rulers depend on those they rule.

Arminian readers note that leaders should value and serve their people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the proverb reminds rulers that their strength is not their own.

Both traditions read the verse as practical wisdom about leadership.

Proverbs 14:29

29He who is slow to anger has great understanding, but he who has a quick temper displays folly.

Arminian

The slow to anger have great understanding, while the quick-tempered display folly. Patience reflects wisdom.

Arminian readers note that patience can be cultivated.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God himself is described as slow to anger (Exodus 34:6).

Both traditions agree that patience is a mark of wisdom and of godly character.

Proverbs 14:30

30The life of the body is a heart at peace, but envy rots the bones.

Arminian

A heart at peace gives life to the body, while envy rots the bones. Inner life affects physical well-being.

Arminian readers note the importance of guarding one’s heart (compare 4:23).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that envy is a sin that harms the one who harbors it.

Both traditions read the verse as practical wisdom about the connection between heart and body.

Proverbs 14:31

31He who oppresses the poor shows contempt for his Maker, but he who is kind to the needy honors him.

Arminian

Oppressing the poor shows contempt for their Maker, while kindness to the needy honors him. The poor bear the dignity of God’s creation (compare 17:5; 22:2).

Arminian readers note that how we treat the poor reflects how we regard God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that every person is made by God, which grounds social ethics in creation.

Both traditions agree strongly here: care for the poor is a matter of honoring God.

Proverbs 14:32

32The wicked is brought down in his calamity, but in death, the righteous has a refuge.

Arminian

The wicked are brought down in calamity, while the righteous have refuge in death. The Greek Septuagint reads differently, probably reflecting a Hebrew text with “in his integrity,” which differs from “in his death” by the order of two letters.

Arminian readers note that the WEB follows the standard Hebrew text.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the reading “in death” would be a notable hint of hope beyond the grave, while recognizing the textual question.

Both traditions hold the verse with appropriate caution on this point.

Proverbs 14:33

33Wisdom rests in the heart of one who has understanding, and is even made known in the inward part of fools.

Arminian

Wisdom rests in the heart of the discerning and is made known even among fools. The Greek translation adds “not,” reading that wisdom is not known among fools.

Arminian readers note the textual difference and hold the meaning cautiously.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the line’s sense is uncertain: it may mean wisdom becomes evident even to fools, or that fools merely make noise.

Both traditions avoid building doctrine on this verse.

Proverbs 14:34

34Righteousness exalts a nation, but sin is a disgrace to any people.

Arminian

Righteousness exalts a nation, while sin is a disgrace to any people. The proverb applies moral order to public life.

Arminian readers note that national life is shaped by collective choices.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God governs nations as well as individuals.

Both traditions read the verse as a general principle, not a promise that any nation will always prosper.

Proverbs 14:35

35The king’s favor is toward a servant who deals wisely, but his wrath is toward one who causes shame.

Arminian

The king favors a servant who acts wisely, while his anger falls on one who causes shame. Wise service is valued.

Arminian readers note that faithful work earns trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that earthly rulers reflect, imperfectly, the greater King who rewards faithful service.

Both traditions read the verse as practical wisdom about work and authority.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
