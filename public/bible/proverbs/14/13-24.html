Passage

Proverbs 14

Verses 13–24 · WEB

This group of sayings covers hidden sorrow, repayment for one’s ways, the gullible and the prudent, anger, the treatment of the poor, and the value of hard work. Several verses observe social realities without endorsing them, such as the rich having many friends in 14:20, and the next verse supplies the moral counterweight. Both traditions read the section as training in discernment: noticing how things usually go, and choosing the path of wisdom and mercy that honors the LORD.

Proverbs 14:13

13Even in laughter the heart may be sorrowful, and mirth may end in heaviness.

Arminian

Even in laughter the heart may ache, and joy can end in grief. Outward cheer does not always reflect inner reality.

Arminian readers note the honesty of Scripture about mixed emotions.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that earthly joy is fragile and that lasting joy is found in God.

Both traditions take pastoral counsel here: look beneath the surface and care for hidden sorrow.

Proverbs 14:14

14The unfaithful will be repaid for his own ways; likewise a good man will be rewarded for his ways.

Arminian

The unfaithful will be repaid for their ways, and the good rewarded for theirs. People reap what their ways produce.

Arminian readers note that God’s justice recognizes real moral choices.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that reward in Proverbs is a general pattern under God’s providence, and that ultimate acceptance with God rests on his grace.

Both traditions agree that conduct matters and that God sees it.

Proverbs 14:15

15A simple man believes everything, but the prudent man carefully considers his ways.

Arminian

The simple believe everything, while the prudent consider their steps. Gullibility is not the same as faith.

Arminian readers stress the value of thoughtful discernment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that true faith rests on God’s trustworthy word, not on every claim.

Both traditions agree that wisdom includes testing what one hears.

Proverbs 14:16

16A wise man fears and shuns evil, but the fool is hot headed and reckless.

Arminian

The wise fear and turn from evil, while the fool is hotheaded and reckless. Caution guided by reverence marks wisdom.

Arminian readers note that turning from evil is a deliberate choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that a healthy fear of consequences belongs to the fear of the LORD.

Both traditions read the verse as a contrast between careful wisdom and impulsive folly.

Proverbs 14:17

17He who is quick to become angry will commit folly, and a crafty man is hated.

Arminian

The quick-tempered act foolishly, and a crafty man is hated. Both impulsiveness and scheming harm relationships.

Arminian readers note that anger and cunning are both choices with consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the verse exposes two different expressions of the same disordered heart.

Both traditions agree that patience and openness are wiser paths.

Proverbs 14:18

18The simple inherit folly, but the prudent are crowned with knowledge.

Arminian

The simple inherit folly, while the prudent are crowned with knowledge. Folly is passed on; wisdom is a crown.

Arminian readers note that the simple can still choose wisdom (compare 1:22–23; 8:5).

Reformed

Reformed readers note that folly is the natural inheritance of those who do not seek wisdom, and that knowledge is a gift to be received.

Both traditions agree that the simple are not fixed in their state and are invited to learn.

Proverbs 14:19

19The evil bow down before the good, and the wicked at the gates of the righteous.

Arminian

The evil bow before the good, and the wicked at the gates of the righteous. In the end, goodness is vindicated.

Arminian readers hear encouragement for those who persevere in doing good.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this vindication is not always visible in this life and finds its completion at God’s judgment.

Both traditions read the verse as a general expectation under God’s justice.

Proverbs 14:20

20The poor person is shunned even by his own neighbor, but the rich person has many friends.

Arminian

The poor are shunned even by neighbors, while the rich have many friends. This observes a social reality rather than approving it.

Arminian readers note the sad honesty of the proverb.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the next verse supplies the moral counterweight.

Both traditions agree that Proverbs often describes how people behave before showing how God would have them behave.

Proverbs 14:21

21He who despises his neighbor sins, but he who has pity on the poor is blessed.

Arminian

Despising one’s neighbor is sin, while pity on the poor is blessed. This answers the observation of verse 20.

Arminian readers note that mercy toward the poor is a real choice that God blesses.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that kindness to the needy reflects God’s own care for them (compare 14:31).

Both traditions agree that care for the poor is central to biblical righteousness.

Proverbs 14:22

22Don’t they go astray who plot evil? But love and faithfulness belong to those who plan good.

Arminian

Those who plot evil go astray, while love and faithfulness belong to those who plan good. Planning reveals the heart.

Arminian readers note that intentions are morally significant.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that “love and faithfulness” echo God’s covenant character.

Both traditions agree that wisdom includes planning good for others.

Proverbs 14:23

23In all hard work there is profit, but the talk of the lips leads only to poverty.

Arminian

Hard work brings profit, while mere talk leads to poverty. Action matters more than words.

Arminian readers stress the value of diligent labor.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that work is part of God’s good design for human life.

Both traditions read the verse as a general observation about effort and idle talk.

Proverbs 14:24

24The crown of the wise is their riches, but the folly of fools crowns them with folly.

Arminian

The crown of the wise is their riches, while the folly of fools is only folly. Wisdom brings honor; folly multiplies itself.

Arminian readers note that wise living tends to bring its own reward.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that “riches” here may include more than money, though the line is compact.

Both traditions read the verse as a general contrast, holding the exact sense of the first line with some caution.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
