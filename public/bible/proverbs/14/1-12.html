Passage

Proverbs 14

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Chapter 14 opens with sayings on the wise and foolish woman, the fear of the LORD, speech, honest witness, and the limits of human self-assessment. The verses remain largely independent proverbs, though some form loose clusters around speech and the house. Verse 12, which recurs at 16:25, warns that a path can seem right and still end in death. Both traditions read these lines as practical wisdom rooted in reverence for God, and both agree that the proverbs describe typical outcomes, not mechanical rules.

Proverbs 14:1

1Every wise woman builds her house, but the foolish one tears it down with her own hands.

Arminian

A wise woman builds her house, while a foolish one tears it down with her own hands. The house stands for household and family life (compare 9:1; 24:3).

Arminian readers note how daily choices build up or wear down a home.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that wisdom is pictured as constructive and folly as self-destructive.

Both traditions read the verse as practical wisdom for all, not as a statement limited to women.

Proverbs 14:2

2He who walks in his uprightness fears the LORD, but he who is perverse in his ways despises him.

Arminian

The upright fear the LORD, while the perverse despise him. Conduct reveals one’s attitude toward God.

Arminian readers note that walking uprightly is the visible form of reverence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the verse ties ethics directly to the knowledge of God.

Both traditions agree that the fear of the LORD is the foundation of wisdom (1:7) and that it shows up in how a person lives.

Proverbs 14:3

3The fool’s talk brings a rod to his back, but the lips of the wise protect them.

Arminian

A fool’s talk brings a rod on his back, while the lips of the wise protect them. The Hebrew of the first line can also be read as a rod of pride.

Arminian readers note that careless words invite trouble.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that wise speech is a means God uses to preserve those who speak it.

Both traditions read the verse as a general warning about speech, holding the exact image with some caution.

Proverbs 14:4

4Where no oxen are, the crib is clean, but much increase is by the strength of the ox.

Arminian

Where there are no oxen the crib is clean, but much increase comes by the ox’s strength. Productive work brings mess along with gain.

Arminian readers note that avoiding effort and risk may keep things tidy but bears little fruit.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s gifts often come through ordinary means that require care and labor.

Both traditions draw a simple practical lesson about the cost and value of fruitful work.

Proverbs 14:5

5A truthful witness will not lie, but a false witness pours out lies.

Arminian

A truthful witness does not lie, while a false witness pours out lies. Honest testimony was vital in Israel’s courts.

Arminian readers note the personal responsibility of every witness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this echoes the ninth commandment (Exodus 20:16).

Both traditions agree that truthfulness in testimony protects justice and reflects God’s character.

Proverbs 14:6

6A scoffer seeks wisdom, and doesn’t find it, but knowledge comes easily to a discerning person.

Arminian

The scoffer seeks wisdom and cannot find it, while knowledge comes easily to the discerning. Attitude shapes learning.

Arminian readers note that a mocking spirit blocks understanding.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that wisdom is received by the humble, not seized by the proud.

Both traditions agree that a teachable heart matters more than raw ability in gaining wisdom.

Proverbs 14:7

7Stay away from a foolish man, for you won’t find knowledge on his lips.

Arminian

Stay away from a foolish man, because you will not find knowledge on his lips. Companions shape us (compare 13:20).

Arminian readers note the practical wisdom of choosing one’s company.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the command does not forbid compassion toward fools, but warns against relying on them for counsel.

Both traditions read the verse as guidance about influence and learning.

Proverbs 14:8

8The wisdom of the prudent is to think about his way, but the folly of fools is deceit.

Arminian

The wise think about their way, while fools deceive. Reflection guards against self-deception.

Arminian readers stress the value of considering one’s path before acting.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that folly often deceives the fool himself as well as others.

Both traditions agree that wisdom includes honest self-examination.

Proverbs 14:9

9Fools mock at making atonement for sins, but among the upright there is good will.

Arminian

Fools mock at making atonement for sins, while among the upright there is goodwill. The Hebrew is difficult; it may refer to mocking guilt itself, or the guilt offering.

Arminian readers note that the WEB rendering is one plausible reading.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the uncertainty and avoid building doctrine on this line.

Both traditions agree on the general contrast: fools make light of sin, while the upright seek restored relationship.

Proverbs 14:10

10The heart knows its own bitterness and joy; he will not share these with a stranger.

Arminian

The heart knows its own bitterness and joy, and a stranger cannot fully share them. Some inner experience is private.

Arminian readers note the dignity and depth of each person’s inner life.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that only God fully knows the heart (compare 1 Samuel 16:7; Psalm 139).

Both traditions take pastoral encouragement here: no one fully understands another’s grief, but God does.

Proverbs 14:11

11The house of the wicked will be overthrown, but the tent of the upright will flourish.

Arminian

The house of the wicked will be overthrown, while the tent of the upright will flourish. The images may surprise: a house falls while a tent stands.

Arminian readers note that outward strength does not guarantee permanence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s favor, not the strength of one’s dwelling, determines what endures.

Both traditions read the verse as a general pattern under God’s rule.

Proverbs 14:12

12There is a way which seems right to a man, but in the end it leads to death.

Arminian

There is a way that seems right to a man, but it ends in death. The same saying appears again at 16:25.

Arminian readers note that sincerity is not enough; a person needs God’s wisdom to judge rightly, and grace enables people to turn from false paths.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that human judgment is limited and affected by sin, so that people can be deeply mistaken about their own way.

Both traditions agree that the verse warns against trusting one’s own sense of what is right apart from God (compare 3:5–7).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
