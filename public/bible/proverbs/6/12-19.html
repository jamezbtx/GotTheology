Passage

Proverbs 6

Verses 12–19 · WEB

The third instruction portrays the worthless person, whose crooked speech, sly gestures, and scheming heart spread discord, and whose ruin comes suddenly. A numerical saying follows: six things the LORD hates, seven that are an abomination to him. The list runs from the eyes to the feet and ends where the portrait began, with the one who sows discord. Both sections describe character before God.

Proverbs 6:12

12A worthless person, a man of iniquity, is he who walks with a perverse mouth,

Arminian

“A worthless person” renders a Hebrew phrase for a person of no moral worth, one given to wickedness. His defining feature is a “perverse mouth”: speech that twists truth.

Arminian readers see a portrait of settled character formed by repeated choices. Wisdom warns the son so he can recognize such a person and refuse to become one.

Reformed

The portrait begins with the mouth, as Proverbs often does, because speech reveals the heart.

Reformed readers, in agreement, read the description as wisdom’s warning about a kind of person to avoid. Both traditions treat it as moral description, not a doctrinal argument.

Proverbs 6:13

13who winks with his eyes, who signals with his feet, who motions with his fingers,

Arminian

Winking, signaling with the feet, and motioning with the fingers describe secret signals: communication meant to deceive or to conspire behind someone’s back.

Arminian readers note that the whole body can become an instrument of deceit. Integrity means honesty in gesture as well as word.

Reformed

The worthless person speaks one thing aloud and signals another. His body serves his scheming.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see how sin employs every member (compare Romans 6:13). Both traditions call for openness and honesty before God and neighbor.

Proverbs 6:14

14in whose heart is perverseness, who devises evil continually, who always sows discord.

Arminian

Here the root is named: perverseness in the heart, a mind that devises evil continually, and a habit of sowing discord. Outward signals flow from inward intent.

Arminian readers stress that the heart can be renewed by grace, but the passage warns about what a person becomes when evil is chosen and cultivated.

Reformed

The heart is the source of the crooked words and gestures. Proverbs consistently locates conduct in the heart (4:23).

Reformed readers, sharing this focus, emphasize the depth of the heart’s corruption apart from grace. Both traditions agree the sower of discord is morally responsible and that such a character is condemned.

Proverbs 6:15

15Therefore his calamity will come suddenly. He will be broken suddenly, and that without remedy.

Arminian

Calamity comes suddenly and “without remedy.” The schemer who worked in secret is broken without warning.

Arminian readers see the just outcome of a chosen path. The warning is meant to turn the hearer away before that end comes.

Reformed

The sudden ruin answers the secret plotting. Proverbs often describes judgment arriving unexpectedly.

Reformed readers, in agreement, see God’s righteous governance in the fall of the wicked. Both traditions read this as a general pattern of wisdom, not a timetable for every case.

Proverbs 6:16

16There are six things which the LORD hates; yes, seven which are an abomination to him:

Arminian

The numerical pattern “six… yes, seven” is a Hebrew poetic device (compare 30:15–31; Amos 1–2) that builds to a full list with emphasis on the last item. “Abomination” describes what is morally repugnant to God.

Arminian readers take the LORD’s “hatred” as his holy opposition to wickedness and his settled judgment against it. God’s love for sinners and his call to repentance stand alongside this, not against it.

Reformed

The LORD himself is the one offended. These are not just social faults; they are sins against God.

Reformed readers likewise understand God’s hatred as his holy, just opposition to evil and to those who persist in it (compare Psalm 5:5). They also affirm God’s patience and offer of mercy. Both traditions read the verse as a statement of God’s moral character, not as a fork.

Proverbs 6:17

17arrogant eyes, a lying tongue, hands that shed innocent blood,

Arminian

“Arrogant eyes,” or literally haughty eyes, come first: pride is the root of many sins. A lying tongue and violence against the innocent follow.

Arminian readers note the range of the list, from inner attitude (pride) to speech (lies) to deeds (violence). Holiness touches every part of life.

Reformed

Pride, falsehood, and bloodshed are named together. Shedding innocent blood recalls the commandment “You shall not murder.”

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, often note that pride heads the list, as it heads many lists of sin in Christian teaching. Both traditions agree these are hateful to God and harmful to neighbor.

Proverbs 6:18

18a heart that devises wicked schemes, feet that are swift in running to mischief,

Arminian

A scheming heart and feet that run to mischief show sin moving from plan to action. The body imagery continues: eyes, tongue, hands, heart, feet.

Arminian readers see how evil grows when intent is welcomed rather than resisted; eagerness to do wrong marks a hardened character.

Reformed

The heart plans and the feet hurry. The sinner is eager, not reluctant.

Reformed readers, in agreement, see the heart as the seat of the corruption that drives action. The list together portrays a whole person turned toward evil, which wisdom calls the son to flee.

Proverbs 6:19

19a false witness who utters lies, and he who sows discord among brothers.

Arminian

The last two items concern community: the false witness, whose lies can destroy an innocent person in court (compare the ninth commandment, Exodus 20:16), and the one who sows discord among brothers. The seventh item, given emphasis, echoes verse 14.

Arminian readers note that sins which break fellowship are especially grievous to God, who calls his people to peace.

Reformed

The climax is division. Lies in court and stirring up strife tear apart the people God has joined together.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the importance God places on truth and unity among his people (compare Psalm 133:1). Both traditions hear a call to be peacemakers.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
