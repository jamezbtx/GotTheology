Proverbs 6

Verses 1–5 · WEB

Proverbs 6 opens with four short instructions set between the long father-to-son discourses of chapters 5 and 7. The first concerns surety: a son who has pledged himself to cover another person’s debt. The father does not forbid kindness or lending; he warns against binding yourself to obligations you cannot control and urges the son to get free of a rash pledge quickly and humbly.

Proverbs 6:1

1My son, if you have become collateral for your neighbor, if you have struck your hands in pledge for a stranger,

Arminian

“Collateral” here means standing surety: guaranteeing someone else’s debt with your own property or person. Striking hands was the public gesture that sealed the pledge. Proverbs repeats the warning elsewhere (11:15; 17:18; 22:26–27).

Arminian readers, with all Christian readers, take this as practical prudence, not a ban on generosity. Scripture elsewhere commends giving and lending to the poor (19:17; Psalm 37:21). The issue is a pledge that puts your household at the mercy of another person’s choices.

Reformed

The scene is a commercial or legal guarantee, whether for a neighbor or a stranger. The father speaks as a wise counselor about a common snare of economic life in Israel.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see wisdom here as faithful stewardship. Generosity remains a duty, but it should be freely given within one’s means rather than staked through open-ended promises that may wrong one’s own family.

Proverbs 6:2

2you are trapped by the words of your mouth; you are ensnared with the words of your mouth.

Arminian

The son has been caught by his own words. Proverbs takes speech seriously: a spoken promise binds, and careless words become a trap.

Arminian readers see a call to own the consequences of one’s commitments. Because one’s word matters, the remedy is not to deny the promise but to seek release honestly.

Reformed

The repeated line (“trapped… ensnared”) stresses that the danger began with the son’s own mouth. No one forced him.

Reformed readers hear an echo of the wider biblical concern for truthful, weighty speech (compare Matthew 5:37). Both traditions read the verse as moral realism about promises, not as a doctrinal statement.

Proverbs 6:3

3Do this now, my son, and deliver yourself, since you have come into the hand of your neighbor. Go, humble yourself. Press your plea with your neighbor.

Arminian

Having come “into the hand of your neighbor,” the son is now under another person’s power. The father’s counsel is urgent and humbling: go, ask, and keep asking to be released.

Arminian readers note the active responsibility placed on the son. Wisdom includes swallowing pride to repair a bad situation rather than waiting for it to collapse.

Reformed

Humility is the path out. The son must set aside his reputation and press his plea until he is freed.

Reformed readers, in agreement, see the practical virtue of humility at work. Seeking release honestly is not dishonorable; it is wiser than letting a reckless pledge ruin both parties.

Proverbs 6:4

4Give no sleep to your eyes, nor slumber to your eyelids.

Arminian

Sleep is set aside until the matter is settled. The point is urgency, not a rule about rest.

Arminian readers note that delay in dealing with a known danger is itself folly. The same image of sleep returns in the next unit, where it describes the sluggard.

Reformed

The father’s hyperbole says: do not let this rest. A debt guarantee can grow worse with time.

Reformed readers, like Arminians, take the verse as wise counsel about prompt action. Many also note a fitting parallel with Jesus’ counsel to settle with an adversary quickly (Matthew 5:25).

Proverbs 6:5

5Free yourself, like a gazelle from the hand of the hunter, like a bird from the snare of the fowler.

Arminian

The gazelle and the bird picture an animal escaping a hunter’s grasp. The son should struggle free with the same alertness and energy.

Arminian readers see a vivid summary: prudence acts decisively to escape entanglements that threaten one’s freedom and household.

Reformed

The hunting images present the pledge as a real snare, not a minor inconvenience.

Reformed readers share the plain reading: get free while you still can. Both traditions treat the unit as wisdom for ordinary life under God, not as a forked doctrinal text.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
