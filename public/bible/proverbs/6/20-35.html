Passage

Proverbs 6

Verses 20–35 · WEB

The final instruction returns to the father’s voice. Parents’ teaching is to be bound on the heart, guiding, guarding, and speaking to the son, like a lamp on his path. Its purpose here is to keep him from adultery. The father argues soberly: desire must be resisted in the heart; adultery is like carrying fire; and unlike a hungry thief, the adulterer faces a jealous husband who will accept no payment and a disgrace that does not go away.

Proverbs 6:20

20My son, keep your father’s commandment, and don’t forsake your mother’s teaching.

Arminian

Father and mother both teach. Proverbs presents the household as the first school of wisdom, and the son is to keep and not forsake what he has learned.

Arminian readers see faithful upbringing as a means of grace that the son must still choose to embrace.

Reformed

The command honors both parents and recalls the fifth commandment.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, often stress the covenant household as a God-given setting for passing on faith. Both traditions agree that parental instruction, rooted in God’s law, is to be received and kept.

Proverbs 6:21

21Bind them continually on your heart. Tie them around your neck.

Arminian

Binding the words on the heart and around the neck recalls Deuteronomy 6:6–8. The teaching is meant to be inward and constantly present.

Arminian readers note the son’s active part: he must bind, keep, and remember.

Reformed

The images are of closeness and permanence. God’s word through the parents should shape the son’s inner life.

Reformed readers, in agreement, see the word written on the heart as a key theme that later finds fuller expression in the new covenant (Jeremiah 31:33).

Proverbs 6:22

22When you walk, it will lead you. When you sleep, it will watch over you. When you awake, it will talk with you.

Arminian

Walking, sleeping, and waking cover the whole day. The teaching leads, watches over, and speaks.

Arminian readers see wisdom as a constant companion for the one who keeps it, guiding real choices at each stage of the day.

Reformed

The teaching is almost personified as a guide, guardian, and counselor.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the word of God applied by the Spirit as the believer’s steady guide. Both traditions read the verse as encouragement to keep God’s word near at all times.

Proverbs 6:23

23For the commandment is a lamp, and the law is light. Reproofs of instruction are the way of life,

Arminian

The commandment is a lamp and the law is light, much like Psalm 119:105. Even reproof, correction that may sting, belongs to the way of life.

Arminian readers note that light must be walked in. Correction received humbly keeps a person on the path.

Reformed

Instruction lights the way, and reproof is a gift that leads to life.

Reformed readers, in agreement, see God’s law as a guide for grateful obedience in the life of believers. Both traditions agree that receiving correction is a mark of wisdom.

Proverbs 6:24

24to keep you from the immoral woman, from the flattery of the wayward wife’s tongue.

Arminian

The purpose is stated: to keep the son from the immoral woman and the flattering speech of another man’s wife. As in chapters 5 and 7, the danger begins with words.

Arminian readers see instruction as protective grace. It strengthens the son to resist a real temptation he could give in to.

Reformed

The teaching guards against a specific danger: adultery, approached through flattery.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the law as a guide and safeguard for God’s people. The warning is addressed to a young man, but both traditions apply its principle to men and women alike.

Proverbs 6:25

25Don’t lust after her beauty in your heart, neither let her captivate you with her eyelids.

Arminian

The battle begins in the heart: do not desire her beauty or be drawn in by her glances. Jesus makes the same point when he says lust in the heart is already adultery (Matthew 5:28).

Arminian readers stress guarding the heart by grace before temptation becomes action.

Reformed

Desire is addressed before deed. Beauty itself is not condemned; coveting another man’s wife is.

Reformed readers, in agreement, note that the seventh commandment, as Reformed catechetical teaching explains it, requires purity of heart, speech, and conduct. Both traditions call for watchfulness over the eyes and desires.

Proverbs 6:26

26For a prostitute reduces you to a piece of bread. The adulteress hunts for your precious life.

Arminian

The verse contrasts the cost of sexual sin. Its first line is hard to translate; many read it as saying that a prostitute can leave a man with only a loaf of bread, while the adulteress hunts his very life.

Arminian readers take the point as escalating cost: sexual sin always costs dearly, and adultery threatens life itself.

Reformed

The wording is difficult, but the second line is clear: the adulteress “hunts for your precious life.”

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, note that the verse does not excuse one sin by comparison with another. Its aim is to show how far the danger reaches.

Proverbs 6:27

27Can a man scoop fire into his lap, and his clothes not be burned?

Arminian

Fire carried in the lap will burn the clothes. The image is simple and sober: some actions carry built-in consequences.

Arminian readers see an appeal to common sense: no one can expect to play with fire unharmed.

Reformed

The rhetorical question expects the answer no. Sin carries its own consequences.

Reformed readers, in agreement, see God’s moral order at work in creation. Both traditions read the image as a warning, not as a description of the act.

Proverbs 6:28

28Or can one walk on hot coals, and his feet not be scorched?

Arminian

Walking on hot coals and expecting not to be scorched is the second picture of foolish confidence.

Arminian readers note that the wise person avoids the path of temptation instead of testing how close he can come.

Reformed

The two images, fire and coals, reinforce each other. Sin cannot be handled safely.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the wisdom of fleeing temptation (compare 1 Corinthians 6:18; 2 Timothy 2:22).

Proverbs 6:29

29So is he who goes in to his neighbor’s wife. Whoever touches her will not be unpunished.

Arminian

The images are applied: going in to a neighbor’s wife brings certain consequences. “Will not be unpunished” means he will not escape guilt.

Arminian readers see personal accountability before God and the community for a deliberate wrong against a neighbor.

Reformed

Adultery wrongs a neighbor and violates the seventh commandment. The one who does it will not go unpunished.

Reformed readers, in agreement, emphasize that adultery is a sin against God, the spouse, and the neighbor whose marriage is violated. Both traditions also affirm that God forgives the repentant.

Proverbs 6:30

30Men don’t despise a thief if he steals to satisfy himself when he is hungry,

Arminian

The father begins a comparison. People may understand, though not excuse, a thief who steals because he is hungry.

Arminian readers note that the passage weighs circumstances. Need may soften how people view theft, even though it remains wrong.

Reformed

The thief’s motive draws some sympathy because he is meeting a real need.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the comparison setting up a contrast: there is no comparable excuse for adultery.

Proverbs 6:31

31but if he is found, he shall restore seven times. He shall give all the wealth of his house.

Arminian

Even the hungry thief must repay, “seven times,” which probably signals full restitution; the law itself specified various amounts (Exodus 22:1–4). It may cost him everything he owns.

Arminian readers note that justice still requires restitution even when sympathy exists.

Reformed

Theft can be repaid. The thief may lose his wealth, but the debt can be settled.

Reformed readers, in agreement, see the point of the contrast: what is stolen can be restored, but what adultery takes cannot simply be paid back.

Proverbs 6:32

32He who commits adultery with a woman is void of understanding. He who does it destroys his own soul.

Arminian

The adulterer “is void of understanding” and “destroys his own soul,” meaning his own life. The sin is folly as well as wrong.

Arminian readers see self-destruction as the consequence of a freely chosen path, and they urge the reader to turn back before that end.

Reformed

Adultery is self-ruin. The one who commits it lacks sense and harms himself most of all.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the wages of sin and the deceitfulness of desire. Both traditions agree that the verse warns, and that forgiveness remains available through repentance.

Proverbs 6:33

33He will get wounds and dishonor. His reproach will not be wiped away.

Arminian

Wounds, dishonor, and lasting reproach follow. In Israel adultery brought public shame, and the disgrace would not easily fade.

Arminian readers note that forgiveness from God does not always remove the earthly consequences of sin, which is why prevention matters.

Reformed

The reproach “will not be wiped away.” The damage to name and relationships endures.

Reformed readers, in agreement, distinguish forgiveness before God from consequences in this life. The verse is sober realism, not a denial of grace.

Proverbs 6:34

34For jealousy arouses the fury of the husband. He won’t spare in the day of vengeance.

Arminian

The husband’s jealousy becomes fury, and he will not spare. This is the anger of a man whose marriage has been violated.

Arminian readers read the verse as a sober description of how deep the wrong goes, not as an endorsement of personal revenge.

Reformed

Jealousy here is the outrage of a wronged husband. The “day of vengeance” may refer to the time when he seeks justice, possibly through the courts, since Israel’s law treated adultery as a capital offense (Leviticus 20:10).

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, note that the verse describes the reality the adulterer will face. It does not authorize private vengeance (compare Romans 12:19).

Proverbs 6:35

35He won’t regard any ransom, neither will he rest content, though you give many gifts.

Arminian

No ransom or gift will satisfy the husband. Unlike theft, this wrong cannot be bought off.

Arminian readers see the final warning: some damage cannot be repaired with money. Wisdom chooses faithfulness from the start.

Reformed

The wronged husband will not accept compensation in place of justice. The comparison with the thief is complete.

Reformed readers, in agreement, see the father’s argument closing on the lasting cost of adultery. Both traditions read the chapter as a call to faithfulness in marriage, grounded in reverence for God.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
