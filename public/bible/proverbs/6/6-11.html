Proverbs 6

Verses 6–11 · WEB

The second instruction sends the sluggard to the ant. With no overseer, the ant prepares in summer for later need. The father then mocks the sluggard’s endless “little” sleep and warns that poverty arrives suddenly. The lesson is diligence and foresight, not a promise that hard work guarantees wealth.

Proverbs 6:6

6Go to the ant, you sluggard. Consider her ways, and be wise;

Arminian

“Sluggard” translates a word Proverbs uses for the habitually lazy person. He is sent to a tiny creature for instruction, which is itself a gentle rebuke. Proverbs 30:25 also praises the ant’s foresight.

Arminian readers note the call to “consider… and be wise”: wisdom is learned by attentive observation of God’s ordered creation and put into practice by choice.

Reformed

Creation itself teaches. The ant is a living lesson in diligence placed in the world by its Maker.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, often speak of God’s wisdom displayed in ordinary creatures. Both traditions take the verse as moral instruction drawn from nature, not as a statement about salvation.

Proverbs 6:7

7which having no chief, overseer, or ruler,

Arminian

The ant works with no “chief, overseer, or ruler” standing over it. Its diligence is self-motivated.

Arminian readers see a model of responsibility that does not depend on outside pressure. A wise person works faithfully even when no one is watching.

Reformed

The contrast is pointed: the ant needs no supervisor, yet the sluggard ignores even repeated warnings.

Reformed readers, in agreement, see work done faithfully as service before God rather than for human eyes (compare Colossians 3:23).

Proverbs 6:8

8provides her bread in the summer, and gathers her food in the harvest.

Arminian

Summer and harvest are the seasons for gathering. The ant prepares in good times for the lean season ahead.

Arminian readers note that foresight is part of diligence: using present opportunity well instead of assuming the future will take care of itself.

Reformed

The ant stores food when it is available. Wisdom works with the rhythms God has built into the year.

Reformed readers likewise see ordinary planning and provision as faithful stewardship, not a lack of trust in God. Diligence and dependence on God belong together.

Proverbs 6:9

9How long will you sleep, sluggard? When will you arise out of your sleep?

Arminian

The father turns to direct questions: how long will you sleep? The tone is impatient and a little mocking, meant to wake the hearer up.

Arminian readers see an appeal to the will: the sluggard is addressed as someone who can and should get up.

Reformed

The questions expose the sluggard’s endless delay. He always intends to rise, but later.

Reformed readers share the plain sense: idleness is a moral fault, not merely bad luck. Both traditions read the verse as an urgent call to responsibility.

Proverbs 6:10

10A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to sleep—

Arminian

The repeated “a little” captures how laziness works: each small delay seems harmless. Folding the hands is the posture of rest when work is waiting. These lines recur almost word for word in 24:33–34.

Arminian readers note how habits form through many small choices, so faithfulness in small things matters.

Reformed

The sluggard never decides to be ruined; he only asks for a little more rest.

Reformed readers, in agreement, see the subtle drift of sin and neglect, where small indulgences add up. The verse is about character, not a criticism of rightful rest or Sabbath.

Proverbs 6:11

11so your poverty will come as a robber, and your scarcity as an armed man.

Arminian

Poverty comes “as a robber” and “as an armed man”: suddenly and beyond resistance. Neglect has consequences that arrive all at once.

Arminian readers, with all Christian readers, take this as a general pattern of wisdom, not a law that all poverty results from laziness. Scripture elsewhere honors the righteous poor and warns against oppressing them.

Reformed

Like a raider, want strikes the unprepared. The proverb states how life typically goes, not an absolute rule.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, reject prosperity teaching here. Diligence is commended as faithfulness, while wealth and poverty remain under God’s providence and must not be used to judge a person’s standing before him.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
