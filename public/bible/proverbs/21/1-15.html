Passage

Proverbs 21

Verses 1–15 · WEB

These sayings belong to the larger Solomonic collection (10:1–22:16). The unit opens with the king’s heart in the LORD’s hand and the LORD who weighs hearts, then moves through justice over sacrifice, pride, haste, dishonest gain, the cry of the poor, and the bribe that quiets anger. Several sayings set the righteous and the wicked side by side and look to God as the one who sees and judges. Both traditions read these lines as shared wisdom for life under God, observations of how his world usually works rather than unconditional promises; they differ mainly in how they describe God’s rule over the king’s heart in verse 1.

Proverbs 21:1

1The king’s heart is in the LORD’s hand like the watercourses. He turns it wherever he desires.

Arminian

Irrigation channels carry water wherever the farmer directs, and the LORD turns even a king’s heart as he wills. No ruler stands outside God’s rule (cf. 16:9; Ezra 1:1).

Arminian readers see God truly governing rulers, steering, restraining and overruling their purposes for his ends while ordinarily working through their real choices rather than determining each one.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly see here God’s meticulous providence, directing even the free decisions of kings so that his purpose stands (cf. 19:21; Daniel 4:35).

Shared ground: God reigns over the powerful, and kings remain responsible for what they do.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm God’s real rule over rulers and the ruler’s genuine responsibility, and each has internal diversity on how the two fit. Many Reformed readers see a meticulous providence that directs even free choices, while many Arminian readers see sovereign governance that steers and overrules without determining every choice.

Proverbs 21:2

2Every way of a man is right in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the hearts.

Arminian

People justify their own paths, but the LORD weighs the heart (cf. 16:2). Self-assessment is not the final verdict.

Arminian readers hear a call to honest self-examination before the God who sees motives.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the deceitfulness of the human heart and the need for God’s searching judgment.

Shared ground: only God sees the heart truly, so humility is the wise response.

Proverbs 21:3

3To do righteousness and justice is more acceptable to the LORD than sacrifice.

Arminian

Doing what is right and just pleases the LORD more than ritual offerings. The saying echoes the prophets (1 Samuel 15:22; Hosea 6:6; Micah 6:8).

Arminian readers stress that worship must be joined with obedient, merciful living.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that outward religion without a righteous life is empty before God.

Both traditions agree the verse does not reject worship but puts it in its proper place alongside justice.

Proverbs 21:4

4A high look and a proud heart, the lamp of the wicked, is sin.

Arminian

Haughty eyes and a proud heart are sin, however bright they may seem as the lamp that guides the wicked.

Arminian readers see pride as a chosen posture that shapes everything a person pursues.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pride as a root sin that corrupts even a person’s guiding light.

Shared ground: pride is not a small fault but a direction of life opposed to God.

Proverbs 21:5

5The plans of the diligent surely lead to profit; and everyone who is hasty surely rushes to poverty.

Arminian

Careful, diligent planning tends to lead to gain, while hasty shortcuts tend toward loss.

Arminian readers see diligence and patience as responsible stewardship of the opportunities God gives.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wise planning as one of the ordinary means through which God provides.

Both traditions read the saying as a general observation, not a promise that every plan will prosper.

Proverbs 21:6

6Getting treasures by a lying tongue is a fleeting vapor for those who seek death.

Arminian

Wealth gained by lies is a vanishing vapor and moves its seekers toward death (cf. 12:19).

Arminian readers see a warning that dishonest gain trades lasting life for something fleeting.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the futility of riches pursued apart from God’s ways and his judgment on deceit.

Shared ground: honesty is worth more than any profit gained by falsehood.

Proverbs 21:7

7The violence of the wicked will drive them away, because they refuse to do what is right.

Arminian

The violence of the wicked eventually sweeps them away, because they refuse to do what is right.

Arminian readers note the stress on refusal: the wicked are undone by a course they have chosen.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s just order turning violence back on those who practice it.

Both traditions agree that persistent wrongdoing carries its own destructive consequences.

Proverbs 21:8

8The way of the guilty is devious, but the conduct of the innocent is upright.

Arminian

The path of the guilty twists, while the conduct of the innocent runs straight.

Arminian readers see conduct revealing character and character shaped by repeated choices.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the outward life as the visible fruit of the heart’s condition.

Shared ground: integrity shows in plain, straightforward living.

Proverbs 21:9

9It is better to dwell in the corner of the housetop than to share a house with a contentious woman.

Arminian

A cramped corner of a flat roof is better than a spacious house full of quarreling (repeated in 25:24). The point is the value of peace in the home.

Arminian readers see a call to choose and cultivate peace in close relationships.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the household as a place where godliness or strife is daily on display.

Both traditions read the saying as wisdom about contention that applies to husbands and wives alike, not as a slight against women.

Proverbs 21:10

10The soul of the wicked desires evil; his neighbor finds no mercy in his eyes.

Arminian

The wicked person craves evil and has no mercy even for a neighbor.

Arminian readers see how settled desire for evil hardens a person against compassion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the corruption of desire at the root of cruelty.

Shared ground: what a person longs for shows in how they treat those nearby.

Proverbs 21:11

11When the mocker is punished, the simple gains wisdom. When the wise is instructed, he receives knowledge.

Arminian

When a mocker is punished, the naïve observer learns; the wise learn simply by being taught.

Arminian readers see that people respond differently to correction and that learning is a real choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see discipline as a means God uses to instruct both the onlooker and the teachable.

Both traditions value a teachable heart that does not need punishment in order to grow.

Proverbs 21:12

12The Righteous One considers the house of the wicked, and brings the wicked to ruin.

Arminian

The Righteous One observes the house of the wicked and brings it to ruin. The Hebrew can be read of God or of a righteous person; the WEB takes it as God.

Arminian readers see God attentive to evil and just in bringing it to account.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s righteous judgment over the wicked, carried out in his time.

Shared ground: God sees and judges wrongdoing, whatever reading of the first word is preferred.

Proverbs 21:13

13Whoever stops his ears at the cry of the poor, he will also cry out, but shall not be heard.

Arminian

Whoever ignores the cry of the poor will one day cry out and not be answered (cf. James 2:13).

Arminian readers hear a sober warning that refusing mercy closes a person off from mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in God’s concern for the poor a call to mercy that flows from grace received.

Both traditions agree that how we treat the needy matters deeply to God (cf. 14:31).

Proverbs 21:14

14A gift in secret pacifies anger, and a bribe in the cloak, strong wrath.

Arminian

A secret gift can calm anger and a hidden bribe can quiet fury. The proverb describes how things work, not what God approves (compare 17:23).

Arminian readers see a realistic observation that leaves the reader responsible to discern right from wrong.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Scripture elsewhere condemns bribes that pervert justice (Deuteronomy 16:19).

Shared ground: Proverbs sometimes describes human behavior without endorsing it, and both traditions read this verse that way.

Proverbs 21:15

15It is joy to the righteous to do justice; but it is a destruction to the workers of iniquity.

Arminian

Justice brings joy to the righteous but terror to evildoers.

Arminian readers see that a just order is welcomed by those who have embraced what is right.

Reformed

Reformed readers see delight in justice as a mark of a heart renewed by God.

Shared ground: the same just act blesses some and threatens others, depending on how they stand toward it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
