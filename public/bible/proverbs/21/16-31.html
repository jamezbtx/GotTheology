Passage

Proverbs 21

Verses 16–31 · WEB

The second half of chapter 21 continues the Solomonic sentence collection with sayings on wandering from understanding, love of pleasure, the contentious household, the treasure of the wise, guarded speech, the sluggard’s craving, the false witness, and the sacrifice of the wicked. It closes with two sayings that place all human wisdom and preparation under the LORD: no counsel stands against him, and victory belongs to him. Both traditions read these closing verses as shared ground about God’s supremacy and the real value of human diligence. Throughout, the proverbs describe typical outcomes in God’s world, not mechanical guarantees.

Proverbs 21:16

16The man who wanders out of the way of understanding shall rest in the assembly of the departed spirits.

Arminian

Whoever wanders from the way of understanding ends up among the departed spirits, the company of the dead (cf. 2:18; 9:18).

Arminian readers hear a warning that it is possible to stray from wisdom’s path and that straying is deadly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the deadly end of departing from God’s ways.

Both traditions read this as a serious call to stay on the path of understanding, without building a full doctrine of the afterlife on one line.

Proverbs 21:17

17He who loves pleasure will be a poor man. He who loves wine and oil won’t be rich.

Arminian

Love of pleasure, wine and oil tends to impoverish rather than enrich.

Arminian readers see self-control as a responsibility that guards against slow ruin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see disordered loves crowding out wise stewardship.

Shared ground: the gifts are not evil, but loving them above wisdom leads to want.

Proverbs 21:18

18The wicked is a ransom for the righteous, the treacherous for the upright.

Arminian

The wicked become a ransom for the righteous: trouble meant for the upright falls on the treacherous instead (cf. 11:8).

Arminian readers see God’s justice reversing the schemes of the wicked.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providential care for the righteous, with judgment falling where it is due.

Both traditions agree that “ransom” here is a proverbial image of reversal, not a statement about atonement.

Proverbs 21:19

19It is better to dwell in a desert land, than with a contentious and fretful woman.

Arminian

Living in a barren land is better than living with constant quarreling and irritation (compare 21:9).

Arminian readers see a strong push to value peace and to avoid feeding strife.

Reformed

Reformed readers see how deeply ongoing contention damages a household.

Shared ground: the saying addresses contentiousness itself and applies to anyone who makes a home a battlefield.

Proverbs 21:20

20There is precious treasure and oil in the dwelling of the wise, but a foolish man swallows it up.

Arminian

The wise keep reserves of treasure and oil, while the fool consumes everything at once.

Arminian readers see prudent saving as faithful management of what God provides.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wise stewardship as part of living under God’s provision.

Both traditions agree the contrast is between foresight and careless consumption.

Proverbs 21:21

21He who follows after righteousness and kindness finds life, righteousness, and honor.

Arminian

Pursuing righteousness and kindness leads to life, righteousness and honor.

Arminian readers see real pursuit and real reward, with grace enabling the seeker.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fruit of a life shaped by God’s righteousness, received as gift rather than earned.

Shared ground: those who seek righteousness and kindness find that God honors that path.

Proverbs 21:22

22A wise man scales the city of the mighty, and brings down the strength of its confidence.

Arminian

One wise person can scale a strong city and topple the fortress it trusted.

Arminian readers see wisdom as a power that outmatches brute strength.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that confidence in walls and strength is fragile before God-given wisdom.

Both traditions read this as praise for wisdom over force.

Proverbs 21:23

23Whoever guards his mouth and his tongue keeps his soul from troubles.

Arminian

Guarding mouth and tongue keeps a person out of trouble (cf. 13:3; 18:21).

Arminian readers see restraint in speech as a daily, chosen discipline.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the tongue as revealing the heart and needing God’s help to govern.

Shared ground: careful speech protects the speaker and others.

Proverbs 21:24

24The proud and arrogant man—“Scoffer” is his name— he works in the arrogance of pride.

Arminian

The proud and arrogant person earns the name “Scoffer,” acting from overflowing pride.

Arminian readers see how repeated arrogance becomes a settled identity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pride as a ruling disposition that defines a person.

Both traditions agree that scoffing is not mere attitude but a way of life opposed to wisdom.

Proverbs 21:25

25The desire of the sluggard kills him, for his hands refuse to labor.

Arminian

The sluggard’s cravings kill him, because his hands refuse to work (cf. 6:6).

Arminian readers note the word “refuse”: laziness is a choice with real consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers see unfulfilled desire without labor as a slow self-destruction.

Shared ground: wanting is not the same as working, and the gap can be deadly.

Proverbs 21:26

26There are those who covet greedily all day long; but the righteous give and don’t withhold.

Arminian

Some covet all day long, while the righteous keep giving and do not hold back.

Arminian readers see generosity as a chosen practice that breaks the grip of craving.

Reformed

Reformed readers see open-handed giving as the fruit of a heart freed from greed (cf. 2 Corinthians 9:7).

Both traditions contrast endless wanting with generous giving.

Proverbs 21:27

27The sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination— how much more, when he brings it with a wicked mind!

Arminian

The offering of the wicked is detestable, and worse when brought with evil intent (cf. 15:8).

Arminian readers see that worship cannot be used as cover for an unrepentant life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that God looks at the heart of the worshiper, not merely the gift.

Shared ground: God is not impressed by religious acts divorced from righteousness.

Proverbs 21:28

28A false witness will perish. A man who listens speaks to eternity.

Arminian

A false witness will perish, but the one who listens speaks in a way that lasts. The Hebrew of the second line is difficult, and the WEB’s “speaks to eternity” is one way of rendering it.

Arminian readers see a contrast between careless lies and careful, truthful speech.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s judgment on false testimony (cf. 19:5).

Both traditions hold the exact sense of the second line with caution while agreeing on the value of truthful, attentive speech.

Proverbs 21:29

29A wicked man hardens his face; but as for the upright, he establishes his ways.

Arminian

The wicked put on a hard, brazen face, while the upright make their ways secure.

Arminian readers see a hardened face as the outward sign of a heart closed to correction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the upright establishing their ways as the fruit of walking in God’s path.

Shared ground: brazenness and integrity lead in very different directions.

Proverbs 21:30

30There is no wisdom nor understanding nor counsel against the LORD.

Arminian

No wisdom, understanding or counsel can stand against the LORD (cf. 19:21).

Arminian readers see God’s purposes as finally unthwartable, even as he works with and through human counsel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s supreme wisdom over all human plans.

Shared ground: both traditions read this as a confession of God’s supremacy, not a point of division.

Proverbs 21:31

31The horse is prepared for the day of battle; but victory is with the LORD.

Arminian

The horse is made ready for battle, but victory belongs to the LORD (cf. Psalm 20:7; 33:17).

Arminian readers see preparation as a real duty that does not guarantee the outcome.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human means used faithfully while the result rests with God.

Shared ground: prepare diligently and trust God for the victory; both traditions affirm both halves of the verse.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
