Passage

Proverbs 3

Verses 1–12 · WEB

A father urges his son to keep his teaching, hold to kindness and truth, trust the LORD with all his heart, fear the LORD, honor him with his wealth, and receive his discipline. Read as wisdom literature, the promised blessings describe general patterns rather than absolute guarantees. Wholehearted trust and God’s loving correction are shared ground for Arminian and Reformed readers, who both hear here a call to humble dependence on a faithful Father.

Proverbs 3:1

1My son, don’t forget my teaching, but let your heart keep my commandments,

Arminian

A father urges his son to remember his teaching and keep his commands in his heart. Arminian readers see instruction aimed at willing, inward embrace rather than outward compliance alone. Wisdom must be held onto by a real choice, renewed over time, which is why the father keeps urging his son not to forget.

Reformed

Reformed readers see covenant instruction passed from parent to child, with the heart as the place where obedience must take root. The command assumes that what is taught can be forgotten. Both traditions see this as the familiar opening of a father’s appeal, setting the tone for wisdom received and treasured.

Proverbs 3:2

2for they will add to you length of days, years of life, and peace.

Arminian

Keeping wisdom adds length of days, years of life, and peace. Arminian readers read this as a general pattern in wisdom literature: faithful living tends toward flourishing. It is a motivation for the son, not a guarantee that every faithful person will live long or avoid hardship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s ordinary blessing attached to wise living in a world he governs. Proverbs states what generally follows, while other Scripture shows the righteous sometimes suffer. Both traditions agree the verse should not be pressed into an absolute promise or a formula for securing outcomes.

Proverbs 3:3

3Don’t let kindness and truth forsake you. Bind them around your neck. Write them on the tablet of your heart.

Arminian

The son must not let kindness and truth leave him, but bind them on his neck and write them on his heart. Arminian readers see loyal love and faithfulness as virtues to be actively cultivated. The vivid images call for constant, personal attention so these qualities become part of his character.

Reformed

Reformed readers see covenant virtues inscribed on the heart, an image of inward devotion rather than mere external display. Kindness and truth reflect God’s own character. Both traditions see this as a call to internalize faithful love, with shared language that later Scripture also uses for the law written on hearts.

Proverbs 3:4

4So you will find favor, and good understanding in the sight of God and man.

Arminian

Holding to kindness and truth brings favor and good understanding with God and people. Arminian readers see integrity shaping relationships both vertical and horizontal. A life marked by faithful love tends to win respect, while also pleasing God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see favor with God and man as the fruit of a life shaped by covenant virtues. The proverb describes a general outcome, not a mechanical reward. Both traditions see the verse joining piety and social wisdom, which Proverbs consistently refuses to separate.

Proverbs 3:5

5Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and don’t lean on your own understanding.

Arminian

The son is to trust the LORD with all his heart and not lean on his own understanding. Arminian readers see wholehearted trust as a personal response to God’s faithfulness, not a rejection of thinking but of self-reliance. Both traditions treat this as shared ground: God is trustworthy, and human insight is limited.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wholehearted dependence on God as the heart of wisdom, set against reliance on one’s own understanding. Trust is not opposed to careful thought but to self-sufficiency. Arminian and Reformed readers alike treasure this verse as a call to rest in God rather than in human judgment.

Proverbs 3:6

6In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make your paths straight.

Arminian

In all his ways the son should acknowledge God, who will make his paths straight. Arminian readers see trust expressed across every area of life, with God guiding those who look to him. The promise encourages dependence and assures that God directs faithful steps, without suggesting life will be free of difficulty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s guidance over the lives of those who acknowledge him in all things. Straight paths describe a life kept on course rather than an easy one. Both traditions affirm God’s faithful direction here, and this verse is common ground rather than a point of dispute about providence.

Proverbs 3:7

7Don’t be wise in your own eyes. Fear the LORD, and depart from evil.

Arminian

The son must not be wise in his own eyes but fear the LORD and turn from evil. Arminian readers see humility and reverence as the posture that opens a person to real wisdom. Turning from evil is a concrete moral choice that flows from fearing God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-conceit as the enemy of wisdom and the fear of the LORD as its foundation. Reverence for God naturally leads away from evil. Both traditions see this verse continuing the call to humble trust, linking right worship to right conduct.

Proverbs 3:8

8It will be health to your body, and nourishment to your bones.

Arminian

Fearing God and turning from evil brings health to the body and nourishment to the bones. Arminian readers see the whole person affected by wise living, body as well as soul. The saying describes a general benefit, not a promise that the godly never become ill.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the physical and spiritual dimensions of life held together in God’s design. Wise, God-fearing living often brings bodily well-being. Both traditions caution against treating the proverb as a health guarantee, since wisdom literature speaks in general patterns.

Proverbs 3:9

9Honor the LORD with your substance, with the first fruits of all your increase;

Arminian

The son is to honor the LORD with his wealth and the first fruits of all his produce. Arminian readers see generous giving as an act of worship and gratitude. Offering the first and best acknowledges that everything comes from God and belongs to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see stewardship expressed in giving the first fruits, recalling Israel’s worship. Honoring God with possessions recognizes him as the source of all provision. Both traditions see generosity and worship joined, rather than wealth being treated as a private possession.

Proverbs 3:10

10so your barns will be filled with plenty, and your vats will overflow with new wine.

Arminian

Then barns will be filled and vats overflow with new wine. Arminian readers see God’s blessing on faithful generosity within Israel’s agricultural setting. The proverb encourages trust that giving to God does not ultimately impoverish, while not offering a formula for guaranteed riches.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s covenant blessing in the land described in terms of harvest abundance. The verse expresses a general principle of divine provision. Both traditions reject turning it into a prosperity guarantee, reading it within the wisdom genre and Israel’s covenant context.

Proverbs 3:11

11My son, don’t despise the LORD’s discipline, neither be weary of his correction;

Arminian

The son must not despise the LORD’s discipline or grow weary of his correction. Arminian readers see discipline as part of God’s loving relationship with his people, calling for a humble and teachable response. Despising correction refuses the growth God intends through it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s discipline as purposeful and loving, meant to correct and shape his children. Weariness under correction is a real temptation. Arminian and Reformed readers share this ground fully: God disciplines those he loves, and believers are called to receive it without resentment.

Proverbs 3:12

12for whom the LORD loves, he corrects, even as a father reproves the son in whom he delights.

Arminian

The LORD corrects those he loves, as a father reproves a son in whom he delights. Arminian readers see discipline as evidence of God’s love, not his rejection. The father image makes correction personal and relational, aimed at restoration and growth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see discipline as a mark of sonship and divine favor, a theme later Scripture also takes up. God’s correction flows from delight, not anger alone. Both traditions treat this verse as shared ground, seeing God’s fatherly discipline as loving care rather than punishment for its own sake.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
