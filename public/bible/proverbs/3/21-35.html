Passage

Proverbs 3

Verses 21–35 · WEB

Holding to sound wisdom brings security, fearless rest, and confidence in the LORD who keeps the feet of his people. The father then turns to neighbor ethics: prompt generosity, no plotting or needless quarrels, no envy of the violent. The chapter ends with the contrast between God’s favor to the upright and humble and his opposition to the perverse and mocking. Both traditions see shared ground in these practical, God-centered teachings.

Proverbs 3:21

21My son, let them not depart from your eyes. Keep sound wisdom and discretion,

Arminian

The son must keep sound wisdom and discretion before his eyes. Arminian readers see the need for constant attentiveness to wisdom in daily life. Wisdom must be held and practiced, not merely admired.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom and discretion as treasures to be guarded and kept in view. The father’s repeated appeals show their importance. Both traditions see a call to vigilance in holding onto wise counsel.

Proverbs 3:22

22so they will be life to your soul, and grace for your neck.

Arminian

Wisdom and discretion will be life to the soul and grace to the neck. Arminian readers see inner vitality and outward beauty flowing from wise living. The image of an ornament on the neck pictures wisdom as adorning a person’s life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom bringing life and grace, both inwardly and in how a person appears to others. The proverb describes typical fruit of wisdom. Both traditions see wisdom as enriching the whole person.

Proverbs 3:23

23Then you shall walk in your way securely. Your foot won’t stumble.

Arminian

With wisdom the son will walk securely without stumbling. Arminian readers see confidence that comes from living wisely. Wise choices help avoid many pitfalls, though the proverb speaks generally rather than promising freedom from all difficulty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see security in walking according to wisdom, which keeps a person from many dangers. God’s order provides stability. Both traditions read this as a general benefit, consistent with the genre of Proverbs.

Proverbs 3:24

24When you lie down, you will not be afraid. Yes, you will lie down, and your sleep will be sweet.

Arminian

Lying down, the son will not fear, and his sleep will be sweet. Arminian readers see peace of mind as a fruit of living wisely and trusting God. A clear conscience and wise living allow restful sleep.

Reformed

Reformed readers see rest and peace flowing from the security wisdom provides. Sweet sleep pictures freedom from anxiety. Both traditions see this as a general description of the peace that accompanies trust and wisdom.

Proverbs 3:25

25Don’t be afraid of sudden fear, neither of the desolation of the wicked, when it comes;

Arminian

The son need not fear sudden terror or the ruin that comes upon the wicked. Arminian readers see confidence for those who live wisely and trust God. Calamity may come, but fear need not govern them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see assurance that those who trust God need not be overcome by fear of sudden disaster. The ruin of the wicked does not threaten the righteous in the same way. Both traditions see this as encouragement rather than a promise that the righteous never face hardship.

Proverbs 3:26

26for the LORD will be your confidence, and will keep your foot from being taken.

Arminian

The LORD will be the son’s confidence and keep his foot from the snare. Arminian readers see God’s protective care for those who trust him. Confidence rests in God rather than in wisdom alone, holding human prudence and divine care together.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the ultimate ground of security, guarding his people from being trapped. Wisdom is never independent of the LORD. Both traditions share this ground, seeing God’s keeping care as the foundation of the confidence described.

Proverbs 3:27

27Don’t withhold good from those to whom it is due, when it is in the power of your hand to do it.

Arminian

Do not withhold good from those to whom it is due when it is in your power to act. Arminian readers see practical justice and generosity as essential to wise living. Neglecting to help when able is a moral failure.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an obligation to do good, especially when it is owed and within one’s ability. Wisdom shows itself in just and generous conduct. Both traditions see this as shared ethical ground grounded in love of neighbor.

Proverbs 3:28

28Don’t say to your neighbor, “Go, and come again; tomorrow I will give it to you,” when you have it by you.

Arminian

Do not tell a neighbor to come back tomorrow when you have what he needs now. Arminian readers see procrastination in generosity as a form of injustice. Love responds promptly to real need.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom requiring timely action toward a neighbor. Delaying help when it is available is unkind. Both traditions see this as practical wisdom about love and justice in everyday relationships.

Proverbs 3:29

29Don’t devise evil against your neighbor, since he dwells securely by you.

Arminian

Do not plot harm against a neighbor who lives trustingly beside you. Arminian readers see betrayal of trust as especially wrong. Wisdom honors the security neighbors place in one another.

Reformed

Reformed readers see scheming against a trusting neighbor as a violation of covenant community. Neighborly trust must be protected. Both traditions see shared ethical ground in this warning.

Proverbs 3:30

30Don’t strive with a man without cause, if he has done you no harm.

Arminian

Do not quarrel with someone without cause when they have done you no harm. Arminian readers see peace-loving restraint as a mark of wisdom. Needless conflict harms relationships and reflects pride.

Reformed

Reformed readers see contentiousness without reason as folly. Wisdom pursues peace where possible. Both traditions see this as practical guidance for living at peace with others.

Proverbs 3:31

31Don’t envy the man of violence. Choose none of his ways.

Arminian

Do not envy the violent or choose any of their ways. Arminian readers see a warning against admiring those who gain through force. Choosing the path of violence is a moral decision the son must refuse.

Reformed

Reformed readers see envy of the violent as a temptation when such people seem to prosper. Wisdom rejects their ways entirely. Both traditions see shared ground in this call to resist the attraction of wickedness.

Proverbs 3:32

32For the perverse is an abomination to the LORD, but his friendship is with the upright.

Arminian

The perverse are an abomination to the LORD, but he befriends the upright. Arminian readers see God’s moral character shaping how he relates to people. Intimacy with God is linked to uprightness of life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s holiness opposing perversity, while his friendship rests with the upright. The contrast highlights God’s delight in righteousness. Both traditions see shared ground in God’s favor toward those who walk uprightly.

Proverbs 3:33

33The LORD’s curse is in the house of the wicked, but he blesses the habitation of the righteous.

Arminian

The LORD’s curse is on the house of the wicked, but he blesses the home of the righteous. Arminian readers see moral choices affecting households, with God responding to wickedness and righteousness. The proverb describes a general pattern of divine justice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s just governance over households, bringing blessing or curse. The verse reflects covenant categories of blessing and curse. Both traditions read it as a general principle rather than an explanation of every family’s circumstances.

Proverbs 3:34

34Surely he mocks the mockers, but he gives grace to the humble.

Arminian

God mocks the mockers but gives grace to the humble. Arminian readers see humility opening the way to receive God’s grace, while pride sets a person against him. Grace is freely given, and humility is the posture that receives it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God opposing the proud and showing favor to the humble, a theme later Scripture also echoes. Humility itself is understood as fruit of grace. Both traditions see shared ground in God’s opposition to arrogance and his kindness to the lowly.

Proverbs 3:35

35The wise will inherit glory, but shame will be the promotion of fools.

Arminian

The wise will inherit glory, but fools gain shame. Arminian readers see the end results of wisdom and folly as consequences of the paths people choose. The proverb closes the section with a clear contrast to motivate wise choices.

Reformed

Reformed readers see honor and shame as the respective outcomes of wisdom and folly. The final contrast summarizes the chapter’s teaching. Both traditions see this verse encouraging pursuit of wisdom by setting its end beside the end of folly.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
