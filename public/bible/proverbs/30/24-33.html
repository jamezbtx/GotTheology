Proverbs 30

Verses 24–33 · WEB

The last part of Agur’s words sets out two more numerical sayings. Four small creatures are wise despite their weakness, and four things are stately in their bearing. The chapter ends with a warning: if you have exalted yourself or planned evil, stop, because pressing anger produces strife. Both traditions read these verses as practical wisdom drawn from creation. Several Hebrew words naming animals here are uncertain.

Proverbs 30:24

24“There are four things which are little on the earth, but they are exceedingly wise:

Arminian

Four small creatures are “exceedingly wise.” The saying teaches that wisdom is not a matter of size or strength. Arminian readers see creation itself as a teacher, offering lessons for anyone willing to observe and learn (compare 6:6).

Reformed

The weak and small display a wisdom that the strong often lack. Reformed readers, sharing this reading with Arminians, see God’s wisdom built into creation, where the humble creatures instruct proud humans.

Proverbs 30:25

25The ants are not a strong people, yet they provide their food in the summer.

Arminian

The ant is not strong, yet it gathers food in summer, a lesson in foresight that echoes 6:6–8. Arminian readers see diligence and planning as part of wise living, using the opportunities God gives.

Reformed

The ant’s preparation teaches prudent use of the present season. Reformed readers, with Arminians, read this as wisdom about diligence and stewardship under God’s providence.

Proverbs 30:26

26The hyraxes are but a feeble folk, yet make they their houses in the rocks.

Arminian

The “hyraxes” are small rock-dwelling animals (compare Psalm 104:18). Weak as they are, they find safety in the rocks. Arminian readers see wisdom in knowing one’s weakness and seeking a secure refuge.

Reformed

The hyrax’s safety lies not in its strength but in where it lives. Many Christian readers in both traditions have drawn the further application that the weak find safety in God, often called a rock in Scripture (Psalm 18:2). Reformed readers share this reading with Arminians.

Proverbs 30:27

27The locusts have no king, yet they advance in ranks.

Arminian

Locusts have no king, yet they advance in ordered ranks (compare Joel 2:7–8). Arminian readers see the power of order and cooperation even without visible leadership.

Reformed

The locusts’ disciplined movement without a ruler teaches the strength of unity. Reformed readers, in agreement with Arminians, take the lesson as practical wisdom about common purpose and good order.

Proverbs 30:28

28You can catch a lizard with your hands, yet it is in kings’ palaces.

Arminian

The creature in this verse is often identified as a lizard, though the Hebrew word is uncertain and some older translations render it “spider.” It can be caught by hand, yet it is found even in royal palaces. Arminian readers see a picture of how the small and overlooked can reach places that the mighty guard.

Reformed

The creature’s identity is uncertain, but the point is clear: something easily caught still makes its way into kings’ houses. Reformed readers, with Arminians, see wisdom in persistence and in the ability of the lowly to reach high places.

Proverbs 30:29

29“There are three things which are stately in their march, four which are stately in going:

Arminian

A final numerical saying lists four things “stately in their march.” Arminian readers note the shift from small wise creatures to dignified, confident ones, together giving a fuller picture of order and bearing in God’s world.

Reformed

The saying turns to bearing and dignity. Reformed readers, sharing this reading with Arminians, see these examples as images of confident, orderly movement.

Proverbs 30:30

30The lion, which is mightiest among animals, and doesn’t turn away for any;

Arminian

The lion, “mightiest among animals,” turns away from nothing. Its fearless stride pictures settled confidence. Arminian readers see an image that elsewhere in Proverbs describes the righteous, who are “bold as a lion” (28:1).

Reformed

The lion is the first and plainest example of stately bearing. Reformed readers, with Arminians, often connect the image to the boldness of the righteous in 28:1.

Proverbs 30:31

31the greyhound; the male goat; and the king against whom there is no rising up.

Arminian

The “greyhound” renders a Hebrew phrase whose meaning is uncertain; suggestions include a strutting rooster, a warhorse, or a greyhound. The male goat leads the flock, and the final phrase about a king “against whom there is no rising up” is also difficult in the Hebrew. Arminian readers hold these details lightly; the common thread is confident, stately bearing.

Reformed

Several words in this verse are uncertain: the creature rendered “greyhound” and the phrase describing the king have both been translated in various ways. Reformed readers, with Arminians, take the list’s climax to be the king leading his people with authority. Both traditions read the verse as observation, not doctrine.

Proverbs 30:32

32“If you have done foolishly in lifting up yourself, or if you have thought evil, put your hand over your mouth.

Arminian

The section closes with direct counsel: if you have exalted yourself or planned evil, put your hand over your mouth. The gesture means stopping speech before pride or anger does more harm (compare Job 40:4). Arminian readers see a call to restraint and repentance, set in contrast with the lofty eyes of verse 13.

Reformed

Self-exaltation and evil plans call for silence, not more words. The hand over the mouth recalls Job’s response before God (Job 40:4). Reformed readers, in agreement with Arminians, see the chapter ending where it began, with humility.

Proverbs 30:33

33For as the churning of milk produces butter, and the wringing of the nose produces blood, so the forcing of wrath produces strife.”

Arminian

Three images share one verb in the Hebrew, commonly rendered as pressing or churning: pressing milk produces butter, pressing the nose produces blood, and pressing anger produces strife. Many note that the Hebrew words for “nose” and “wrath” are closely related, which makes the wordplay sharper. Arminian readers see a practical warning that provoking anger always brings conflict.

Reformed

The verse warns that stirring up wrath has predictable results, just as churning milk and wringing the nose do. Interpreters commonly note a wordplay in the Hebrew between “nose” and “anger.” Reformed readers, with Arminians, read the chapter’s closing words as wisdom for peacemaking (compare 15:1; James 1:19–20).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
