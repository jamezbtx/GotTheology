Passage

Proverbs 30

Verses 10–23 · WEB

After a single saying about slander, Agur’s collection turns to lists. Four sayings describe a “generation” marked by dishonor, self-righteousness, pride, and cruelty. Numerical sayings follow in the form “three things… four”: things never satisfied, things too wonderful to understand, and things under which the earth trembles. A brief, restrained saying about the adulterous woman shows sin hiding behind denial. Both traditions read these sayings as shared wisdom about pride, greed, and self-deception.

Proverbs 30:10

10“Don’t slander a servant to his master, lest he curse you, and you be held guilty.

Arminian

A single saying warns against slandering a servant to his master. A servant had little standing to defend himself, so false accusation could do great harm, and his curse against an unjust accuser is presented as something that may rightly fall on the slanderer. Arminian readers see the verse as care for the vulnerable and a warning against using words to harm those with less power.

Reformed

The verse protects a servant from false accusation before the one who controls his life. The phrase “be held guilty” suggests that the slanderer, not the servant, will bear the guilt. Reformed readers, with Arminians, read it as wisdom about justice in speech and concern for the weak (compare Deuteronomy 23:15–16).

Proverbs 30:11

11There is a generation that curses their father, and doesn’t bless their mother.

Arminian

Four sayings each begin “There is a generation,” meaning a kind or class of people. The first curses father and doesn’t bless mother, a grave wrong under the law (Exodus 21:17; compare Proverbs 20:20). Arminian readers see the honoring of parents as basic to a wise and godly life.

Reformed

“Generation” here describes a type of person rather than an age. Dishonor toward parents comes first, reflecting the fifth commandment (Exodus 20:12). Reformed readers, sharing this reading with Arminians, see the list as a portrait of pride that refuses God’s ordering of human relationships.

Proverbs 30:12

12There is a generation that is pure in their own eyes, yet are not washed from their filthiness.

Arminian

This kind of person is “pure in their own eyes” yet unwashed from filth. The image exposes self-deception: a person can feel clean while remaining defiled (compare 16:2; 21:2). Arminian readers, with all Christians, hear a call to honest self-examination and to seek the cleansing that God alone provides (Psalm 51:2).

Reformed

Self-righteousness is the second mark of this generation. Reformed readers often connect the verse to Jesus’ parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector (Luke 18:9–14). Both traditions read it the same way: the first step toward cleansing is to stop trusting one’s own estimate of purity.

Proverbs 30:13

13There is a generation, oh how lofty are their eyes! Their eyelids are lifted up.

Arminian

“Lofty eyes” and lifted eyelids picture arrogance, a sin the LORD hates (6:17). Arminian readers note that the progression of this list moves from dishonor to self-righteousness to open pride, each sin feeding the next.

Reformed

Haughty eyes appear elsewhere in Proverbs as hateful to God (6:17; 21:4). Reformed readers, with Arminians, see pride as the root of the other sins listed here, the same pride Agur set aside in verses 2–3.

Proverbs 30:14

14There is a generation whose teeth are like swords, and their jaws like knives, to devour the poor from the earth, and the needy from among men.

Arminian

The last kind has teeth like swords and jaws like knives, devouring the poor and needy. The picture is of predatory greed that treats the vulnerable as food. Arminian readers see God’s concern for the poor throughout Proverbs (14:31; 22:22–23) and a warning that pride ends in cruelty.

Reformed

The violent imagery shows where pride and self-righteousness can lead: exploiting the poor. Reformed readers, in agreement with Arminians, hear the prophets’ similar charge against oppressors (compare Micah 3:1–3). Both traditions read the verse as moral indictment, not doctrinal controversy.

Proverbs 30:15

15“The leech has two daughters: ‘Give, give.’ “There are three things that are never satisfied; four that don’t say, ‘Enough!’:

Arminian

The leech with two daughters crying “Give, give” is a picture of insatiable craving. The Hebrew word for “leech” occurs only here, so its precise sense is not certain, but the point is clear. The verse then introduces a numerical saying, “three things… four,” a pattern found in wisdom and prophetic writing (compare 6:16; Amos 1:3). Arminian readers see a vivid warning about desires that never say “Enough.”

Reformed

The image of the leech and its daughters portrays greed that always demands more. The “three… four” pattern builds to a climax and invites reflection on the examples that follow. Reformed readers, with Arminians, see a picture of restless human desire, which Scripture answers with contentment in God (compare Ecclesiastes 1:8; Hebrews 13:5).

Proverbs 30:16

16Sheol, the barren womb, the earth that is not satisfied with water, and the fire that doesn’t say, ‘Enough!’

Arminian

Four things never say “Enough”: Sheol, the place of the dead; the barren womb; dry earth; and fire. Each consumes or longs without end. Arminian readers note that the barren womb is listed with compassion, not blame: the longing for children is real and deep (compare Genesis 30:1; 1 Samuel 1:10).

Reformed

Death, unfulfilled longing for children, thirsty ground, and fire all illustrate what is never satisfied. Reformed readers, in agreement with Arminians, read the list as observation of the created and fallen world, which teaches that endless craving is a mark of things that cannot finally satisfy.

Proverbs 30:17

17“The eye that mocks at his father, and scorns obedience to his mother, the ravens of the valley shall pick it out, the young eagles shall eat it.

Arminian

The eye that mocks a father and scorns a mother will be picked out by ravens and eaten by young eagles. The image suggests a violent death and an unburied body, a great disgrace in the ancient world. Arminian readers see a sober warning that contempt for parents leads to ruin (compare verse 11; Deuteronomy 21:18–21).

Reformed

The mocking eye is punished in the organ that sinned. Reformed readers, sharing this reading with Arminians, see the fifth commandment’s promise reversed: honoring parents brings life (Exodus 20:12), but scorn of them ends in shame.

Proverbs 30:18

18“There are three things which are too amazing for me, four which I don’t understand:

Arminian

Agur names “three things… four” that are too wonderful for him to understand. The saying returns to the humble wonder of verses 2–4. Arminian readers see wisdom that delights in the mysteries of God’s world rather than claiming to master them.

Reformed

The confession of wonder echoes Agur’s earlier admission of ignorance. Reformed readers, with Arminians, see wisdom as including reverent amazement at what God has made.

Proverbs 30:19

19The way of an eagle in the air, the way of a serpent on a rock, the way of a ship in the middle of the sea, and the way of a man with a maiden.

Arminian

An eagle in the air, a serpent on a rock, a ship at sea, and “a man with a maiden” all move in ways that are hard to trace or explain. Interpreters differ on the common thread: the mystery of movement without a visible track, or the mystery of attraction and the joining of a man and woman. Arminian readers, with others, take the last example as wonder at love between a man and a young woman, a good part of God’s creation.

Reformed

The four “ways” share a quality of mystery: each leaves no lasting trace or follows a path one cannot fully explain. The last, “the way of a man with a maiden,” is commonly read as the wonder of courtship and love. Reformed readers, sharing this reading with Arminians, see the marriage bond and the attraction leading to it as among God’s wonders (compare Song of Songs 8:6–7).

Proverbs 30:20

20“So is the way of an adulterous woman: She eats and wipes her mouth, and says, ‘I have done nothing wrong.’

Arminian

By contrast, the adulterous woman “eats and wipes her mouth” and claims she has done nothing wrong. The image is a restrained, chaste way to describe sin treated as casually as a meal. Like the ship that leaves no trace, she imagines her act leaves no mark. Arminian readers see a warning about how repeated sin can dull the conscience (compare 1 Timothy 4:2).

Reformed

The adulteress treats her sin as lightly as wiping her mouth after eating, and she denies any wrong. Reformed readers, in agreement with Arminians, see the heart’s capacity for self-justification, which links this verse with the self-deceived generation of verse 12. Both traditions read the verse as moral warning about hardened conscience.

Proverbs 30:21

21“For three things the earth trembles, and under four, it can’t bear up:

Arminian

Another numerical saying lists four things under which “the earth trembles.” The exaggeration is deliberate and humorous in tone, yet the point is serious: some reversals of social order bring disorder and harm. Arminian readers take the list as wisdom about how unready people handle new positions.

Reformed

The image of the earth unable to bear up portrays situations that unsettle a community. Reformed readers, with Arminians, read the saying as observation of human folly, not a rule for social rank.

Proverbs 30:22

22For a servant when he is king, a fool when he is filled with food,

Arminian

A servant who becomes king and a fool who is filled with food are two pictures of people given power or plenty they are not prepared to handle (compare 19:10; Ecclesiastes 10:5–7). Arminian readers note that character matters more than position: the problem is unreadiness and folly, not low birth itself.

Reformed

A servant suddenly made king may rule harshly, and a fool who is well fed becomes even more insufferable. Reformed readers, in agreement with Arminians, see the concern as character, not class; Scripture elsewhere honors faithful servants raised to authority, such as Joseph.

Proverbs 30:23

23for an unloved woman when she is married, and a servant who is heir to her mistress.

Arminian

An “unloved woman when she is married” may become harsh or vengeful once she gains a home, and a maidservant who displaces her mistress may become proud (compare Hagar and Sarah, Genesis 16:4). Arminian readers see sympathy as well as warning: the unloved and the displaced are often wounded people, and new status can expose old hurts.

Reformed

The final two examples concern household life. The unloved wife may recall Leah (Genesis 29:31), and the servant who becomes heir may recall Hagar (Genesis 16:4), though the text names no one. Reformed readers, with Arminians, read these as observations about how sudden change can bring out pride or bitterness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
