Proverbs 30

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Proverbs 30 contains “the words of Agur,” a separate collection with an unknown author. Verses 1–9 form one unit: Agur confesses his ignorance, asks who could know the Creator and his son, affirms that every word of God is flawless and must not be added to, and prays for neither poverty nor riches. Both traditions read the unit as a model of humility before God, trust in his word, and contentment. The Hebrew of the heading is difficult, and its names and terms are uncertain.

Proverbs 30:1

1The words of Agur the son of Jakeh, the revelation: the man says to Ithiel, to Ithiel and Ucal:

Arminian

The heading names Agur son of Jakeh, who is otherwise unknown; nothing in Scripture identifies him further. “The revelation” translates a Hebrew word often rendered “oracle” or “burden,” though some take it as a place or people named Massa (compare 31:1). The names “Ithiel and Ucal” are also uncertain: many read them as the men Agur addresses, while others redivide the Hebrew letters as a lament such as “I am weary, O God.” Arminian readers, with all careful readers, hold these questions loosely and receive the words as part of inspired Scripture whatever their human author’s background.

Reformed

Agur son of Jakeh appears only here, and attempts to identify him with Solomon or another known figure are speculative. The Hebrew of the heading is difficult: the word rendered “the revelation” may mean “oracle” or may name Massa, and “Ithiel and Ucal” may be names or, with different word division, a cry of weariness before God. Reformed readers, sharing this caution with Arminians, note that the collection’s canonical place and not the certainty of its heading is what gives these sayings their authority.

Proverbs 30:2

2“Surely I am the most ignorant man, and don’t have a man’s understanding.

Arminian

Agur opens with a striking confession: he is “the most ignorant man” and lacks ordinary human understanding. This is not false modesty but a deliberate posture before God, the opposite of the man who is wise in his own eyes (3:7; 26:12). Arminian readers see here the humility in which true wisdom begins, and which they regard as the right posture for every learner before God.

Reformed

The self-description is deliberately extreme. Set beside the questions of verse 4, it confesses how far human reason falls short of knowing God by its own powers. Reformed readers, with Arminians, hear a theme found across Scripture (compare Job 42:3; Psalm 73:22): the beginning of wisdom includes knowing the limits of one’s own understanding.

Proverbs 30:3

3I have not learned wisdom, neither do I have the knowledge of the Holy One.

Arminian

Agur says he has not learned wisdom or gained “the knowledge of the Holy One.” The plural Hebrew form behind “Holy One” is commonly read as a reference to God, as in 9:10. Arminian readers take the line as an honest admission that knowing God is not a human achievement; it rests on what God makes known, which verses 5–6 go on to affirm.

Reformed

“The knowledge of the Holy One” echoes 9:10, where it stands parallel to the fear of the LORD. Agur confesses that he cannot reach that knowledge by his own effort. Reformed readers, in agreement with Arminians, see the movement from verses 2–4 to verses 5–6 as a move from the limits of human searching to the sufficiency of God’s word.

Proverbs 30:4

4Who has ascended up into heaven, and descended? Who has gathered the wind in his fists? Who has bound the waters in his garment? Who has established all the ends of the earth? What is his name, and what is his son’s name, if you know?

Arminian

Five questions recall God’s work in creation: ascending to heaven, gathering the wind, binding the waters, and setting the ends of the earth (compare Job 38; Isaiah 40:12). The expected answer is that only God does these things. The final question, “What is his name, and what is his son’s name, if you know?”, is read in different ways. Some take “son” to refer to Israel, God’s son (Exodus 4:22), and others take the question as a rhetorical sharpening of the challenge. Many Christian readers in both traditions, from the early church onward, have also heard a pointer toward the Son, often linking the verse with Jesus’ words about the one who ascended and descended (John 3:13). Arminian readers share that christological reading as common Christian ground while recognizing its original force as a confession of God’s transcendence.

Reformed

The questions place Agur, and the reader, before the Creator whose works no human can match (compare Job 38–41; Isaiah 40:12–14). “What is his name?” asks who this God truly is; “what is his son’s name?” intensifies the challenge. Interpreters differ on the son’s identity: Israel as God’s son (Exodus 4:22), a rhetorical flourish, or a hint later filled out in Christ, whom the New Testament presents as the one who descended and ascended (John 3:13; Ephesians 4:9–10). Reformed readers, like Arminians, often read the verse christologically in the light of the whole canon, while holding that its first point is God’s incomprehensible greatness.

Proverbs 30:5

5“Every word of God is flawless. He is a shield to those who take refuge in him.

Arminian

“Every word of God is flawless” closely echoes David’s song (Psalm 18:30; 2 Samuel 22:31). The word translated “flawless” suggests metal refined and tested. God is also “a shield to those who take refuge in him”: his trustworthy word and his protection belong together. Arminian readers, sharing classic Protestant convictions about Scripture, find here a basis for trusting God’s word as true and reliable.

Reformed

After confessing human ignorance, Agur turns to what God has spoken. The image behind “flawless” is commonly taken as that of refined, purified metal, and the line nearly repeats Psalm 18:30. Reformed readers see the verse as grounding trust in Scripture’s truthfulness, and they share with Arminians the conviction that God’s word, received in faith, is a refuge for his people.

Proverbs 30:6

6Don’t you add to his words, lest he reprove you, and you be found a liar.

Arminian

“Don’t you add to his words” recalls Moses’ warning in Deuteronomy 4:2 and 12:32 and anticipates Revelation 22:18–19. Adding to God’s words would make the speaker a liar whom God himself reproves. Arminian and Reformed Protestants alike have drawn from such texts the sufficiency and final authority of Scripture over human traditions; this is shared ground, not a point of division.

Reformed

The warning protects God’s words from human additions that would carry his name while expressing only human opinion (compare Deuteronomy 4:2; Revelation 22:18–19). Reformed readers draw on it, among other texts, when affirming Scripture’s sufficiency and its authority over church tradition. Arminian Protestants have affirmed the same, so the verse expresses common Protestant conviction rather than a contrast between the traditions.

Proverbs 30:7

7“Two things I have asked of you. Don’t deny me before I die.

Arminian

Agur’s confession turns to prayer, often regarded as the only extended petition in Proverbs addressed directly to God. He asks for “two things” before he dies, which suggests a request for a whole life, not a passing moment. Arminian readers note the earnestness of the petition and see in it the dependence on God that marks true wisdom.

Reformed

The prayer is addressed directly to God and framed as a lifelong request. Reformed readers, with Arminians, see wisdom here turning into prayer: the man who confessed his ignorance in verse 2 now asks God for what he cannot secure by himself.

Proverbs 30:8

8Remove far from me falsehood and lies. Give me neither poverty nor riches. Feed me with the food that is needful for me,

Arminian

The first request is moral: remove falsehood and lies. The second concerns circumstances: neither poverty nor riches, only “the food that is needful for me.” Many readers hear an anticipation of the Lord’s Prayer, “Give us today our daily bread” (Matthew 6:11). Arminian readers, with their tradition’s emphasis on holiness of heart and life, see in the prayer a heart that values integrity and contentment above wealth (compare 1 Timothy 6:6–8).

Reformed

Agur asks first for truthfulness and then for a portion suited to him. The phrase “food that is needful” suggests an allotted portion, received from God. Reformed readers see here contentment with God’s providential provision (compare Philippians 4:11–12; Matthew 6:11). Both traditions read the prayer as a model of desire set in proper order, with integrity and nearness to God above wealth.

Proverbs 30:9

9lest I be full, deny you, and say, ‘Who is the LORD?’ or lest I be poor, and steal, and so dishonor the name of my God.

Arminian

The reasons are honest about human weakness. Fullness may lead to forgetting God and saying, “Who is the LORD?” (compare Deuteronomy 8:11–14), and poverty may tempt someone to steal and so dishonor God’s name. Arminian readers see a realistic awareness that both prosperity and need bring real temptations, and a wise request to be spared from either.

Reformed

Both extremes carry spiritual danger: riches can breed proud self-sufficiency, and poverty can press a person toward theft that dishonors God’s name. Reformed readers, in agreement with Arminians, see the prayer’s final concern in God’s honor, not merely personal comfort. Both traditions read it as wise prayer that knows the heart’s weakness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
