Passage

Proverbs 25

Verses 11–20 · WEB

These sayings use vivid comparisons to describe the worth of well-timed words and wise rebuke, faithful and unfaithful people, patience, moderation in pleasures and visits, false witness, and insensitivity to grief. The themes are speech, trust, and restraint in everyday relationships. Both traditions find broad common ground here.

Proverbs 25:11

11A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver.

Arminian

A fitting word is like gold apples in silver settings. Arminian readers note that wisdom involves timing and suitability, not only true content (cf. Prov 15:23).

Words chosen well are beautiful and valuable.

Reformed

Reformed readers see speech as a gift to be used for building others up (Eph 4:29; Col 4:6).

The right word at the right time reflects wisdom from God.

Proverbs 25:12

12As an earring of gold, and an ornament of fine gold, so is a wise reprover to an obedient ear.

Arminian

A wise reprover heard by an obedient ear is like gold jewelry. Arminian readers stress that rebuke is valuable only when it is received with willingness.

Both giving and receiving correction take wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see godly correction as a gift (Ps 141:5; Prov 15:31). A teachable heart treasures it.

The saying honors both the reprover and the one who listens.

Proverbs 25:13

13As the cold of snow in the time of harvest, so is a faithful messenger to those who send him; for he refreshes the soul of his masters.

Arminian

A faithful messenger is as refreshing as snow-cold in harvest heat. Arminian readers note the value of reliability in carrying out a trust.

The image is often taken to mean a cooling drink rather than a snowfall, which verse 26:1 calls out of place; either way, the point is refreshment.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with the value of a faithful envoy (Prov 13:17). Faithfulness in small duties refreshes others.

Christians often apply the saying to faithfulness in carrying God’s message.

Proverbs 25:14

14As clouds and wind without rain, so is he who boasts of gifts deceptively.

Arminian

Boasting of gifts not given is like clouds and wind without rain. Arminian readers see the disappointment caused by empty promises.

The image appears again in Jude 12 for empty teachers.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note Jude 12. Pretended generosity raises hopes it never fulfills.

Words should match deeds.

Proverbs 25:15

15By patience a ruler is persuaded. A soft tongue breaks the bone.

Arminian

Patience persuades a ruler, and a soft tongue breaks bone. Arminian readers note that gentleness can accomplish what force cannot (cf. Prov 15:1).

Persistent, calm speech has real power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same wisdom in calmness before authority (Eccl 10:4). Gentle speech can overcome hard resistance.

The image of breaking bone stresses surprising strength.

Proverbs 25:16

16Have you found honey? Eat as much as is sufficient for you, lest you eat too much, and vomit it.

Arminian

Honey is good, but too much makes you sick. Arminian readers see moderation as wise: good gifts can be misused through excess.

The saying returns in verse 27.

Reformed

Reformed readers see creation’s gifts as good when received with thanksgiving (1 Tim 4:4–5), but self-control is needed.

Excess turns a blessing into harm.

Proverbs 25:17

17Let your foot be seldom in your neighbor’s house, lest he be weary of you, and hate you.

Arminian

Don’t overstay your welcome at a neighbor’s house. Arminian readers note the practical application of the honey principle to friendship.

Even good company can become a burden.

Reformed

Reformed readers see love expressed in respect for others’ space and time.

Wisdom applies to the small courtesies of daily life.

Proverbs 25:18

18A man who gives false testimony against his neighbor is like a club, a sword, or a sharp arrow.

Arminian

False testimony is like a club, a sword, or an arrow. Arminian readers stress the real harm of lying about a neighbor (Exod 20:16).

Words can wound like weapons.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the ninth commandment and the biblical image of the tongue as a weapon (Ps 57:4; 64:3).

False witness is violence by speech.

Proverbs 25:19

19Confidence in someone unfaithful in time of trouble is like a bad tooth or a lame foot.

Arminian

Trusting an unfaithful person in trouble is like a bad tooth or a lame foot. Arminian readers note the pain of relying on someone who fails when needed.

The saying encourages careful judgment about whom to trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with warnings against trusting people rather than the LORD (Ps 118:8; Jer 17:5–7).

Only God is wholly reliable.

Proverbs 25:20

20As one who takes away a garment in cold weather, or vinegar on soda, so is one who sings songs to a heavy heart.

Arminian

Singing songs to a heavy heart is as unhelpful as taking away a coat in the cold. Arminian readers see compassion as attentiveness to how others actually feel (cf. Rom 12:15).

Cheerfulness at the wrong time can wound.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the call to weep with those who weep (Rom 12:15). Job’s friends did best when they sat with him in silence (Job 2:13).

“Vinegar on soda” pictures a reaction that does no good. Comfort must fit the sufferer.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
