Passage

Proverbs 25

Verses 1–10 · WEB

A new heading introduces more proverbs of Solomon that the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out, and the first sayings are about kings, God’s hidden glory, removing the wicked from court, humility before the great, and settling disputes quietly with a neighbor. Both traditions read this as practical wisdom for public and neighborly life under God, with broad common ground and no real soteriological dispute.

Proverbs 25:1

1These also are proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out.

Arminian

The heading says these are more proverbs of Solomon, copied out by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah. Arminian readers take it as a note on how the book came together: Solomonic material was preserved and arranged generations after Solomon (cf. Prov 10:1; 1 Kings 4:32).

The verse does not say how much the copyists selected or arranged, so readers should not claim more than it says. It does show that wisdom was valued and handed on within Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s providence in how Scripture was preserved. Faithful scribes in Hezekiah’s court copied out older material, and God used that ordinary work to give his people this collection.

Hezekiah is remembered as a king who trusted the LORD and restored worship (2 Kings 18:3–7; 2 Chron 29:30), so it fits that his court valued Solomon’s wisdom. The heading itself gives no further details about how the copying was done.

Proverbs 25:2

2It is the glory of God to conceal a thing, but the glory of kings is to search out a matter.

Arminian

God’s glory includes what he chooses to conceal, while a king’s glory is to search matters out. Arminian readers note that hidden things belong to God, yet people are given real responsibility to investigate and judge well (cf. Deut 29:29).

Rulers are honored for diligent inquiry, not for guessing.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s ways are beyond full human tracing (Rom 11:33; Deut 29:29). Some things belong to God alone.

Within what God has given, kings and all people are called to diligent investigation. Mystery and inquiry are both part of the life of faith.

Proverbs 25:3

3As the heavens for height, and the earth for depth, so the hearts of kings are unsearchable.

Arminian

The hearts of kings are as unsearchable as the height of heaven or the depth of the earth. Arminian readers see a sober note about the limits of what subjects can know of those who rule them.

The saying counsels caution in dealing with powerful people.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that what is hidden from subjects is not hidden from God, who directs the king’s heart (Prov 21:1).

The verse humbles human knowledge and points to God’s oversight of rulers.

Proverbs 25:4

4Take away the dross from the silver, and material comes out for the refiner.

Arminian

Removing dross from silver yields material for the refiner. Arminian readers note that this verse sets up the comparison in verse 5: purification makes something useful.

Refining imagery is common in Scripture for removing what is worthless.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that refining imagery is often used of God’s purifying work (Mal 3:2–3). Here it serves a practical point about government.

What is impure must be removed before something good can be made.

Proverbs 25:5

5Take away the wicked from the king’s presence, and his throne will be established in righteousness.

Arminian

Removing the wicked from the king’s presence establishes his throne in righteousness. Arminian readers stress the responsibility of leaders to choose upright counselors.

The company a ruler keeps shapes the justice of his rule.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with other sayings that a throne is established by righteousness (Prov 16:12; 20:8, 26). Just rule reflects God’s order.

The verse applies to any office of authority: corrupt advisers corrupt government.

Proverbs 25:6

6Don’t exalt yourself in the presence of the king, or claim a place among great men;

Arminian

Don’t exalt yourself in the king’s presence or claim a place among the great. Arminian readers see humility as wise conduct in public settings.

Self-promotion is warned against, not ambition to serve well.

Reformed

Reformed readers see humility as a fitting posture before those in authority and before God (James 4:10; 1 Pet 5:6).

Pride seeks a place; humility waits to be given one.

Proverbs 25:7

7for it is better that it be said to you, “Come up here,” than that you should be put lower in the presence of the prince, whom your eyes have seen.

Arminian

It is better to be invited up than put lower in front of the prince. Arminian readers note how closely Jesus’ parable about seats at a feast resembles this saying (Luke 14:7–11).

Humility guards a person from public shame.

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear Luke 14:7–11 and see the gospel pattern in which God exalts the humble.

The last line, “whom your eyes have seen,” is read in different ways, and some translations attach it to verse 8. The main point is clear either way.

Proverbs 25:8

8Don’t be hasty in bringing charges to court. What will you do in the end when your neighbor shames you?

Arminian

Don’t rush to bring charges, since you may be shamed in the end. Arminian readers see practical caution: first impressions can be wrong, and haste can backfire.

The saying encourages patience before accusing a neighbor.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with Jesus’ counsel to reconcile quickly with an opponent (Matt 5:25) and Paul’s concern about believers rushing to court (1 Cor 6:1–7).

Quick accusation often comes from pride rather than justice.

Proverbs 25:9

9Debate your case with your neighbor, and don’t betray the confidence of another,

Arminian

Debate the case with your neighbor directly, and don’t betray another’s confidence. Arminian readers note the principle of going to the person rather than spreading the matter (cf. Matt 18:15).

Resolving conflict should not involve exposing others’ secrets.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Matthew 18:15 in the background. Private, honest dealing protects both truth and trust.

Betraying a confidence is condemned elsewhere in Proverbs (Prov 11:13; 20:19).

Proverbs 25:10

10lest one who hears it put you to shame, and your bad reputation never depart.

Arminian

Betraying confidences brings lasting shame. Arminian readers note that reputation, once lost through gossip, is hard to recover.

The consequences fall back on the one who betrays.

Reformed

Reformed readers see trustworthiness as part of loving one’s neighbor. Disregarding it harms both the neighbor and oneself.

A good name is worth guarding (Prov 22:1).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
