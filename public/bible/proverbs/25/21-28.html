Passage

Proverbs 25

Verses 21–28 · WEB

The section opens with the command to feed and give drink to a hungry or thirsty enemy, which Paul later quotes in Romans 12:20, and continues with sayings about backbiting, a quarrelsome home, good news, a righteous person who gives way, seeking one’s own honor, and lack of self-control. Both traditions find shared ground in enemy-love and self-control; the image of heaping coals of fire has more than one interpretation, and those readings cut across both traditions.

Proverbs 25:21

21If your enemy is hungry, give him food to eat. If he is thirsty, give him water to drink;

Arminian

Feed a hungry enemy and give water to a thirsty one. Arminian readers see this as a clear call to active kindness toward those who oppose us, echoed in Jesus’ command to love enemies (Matt 5:44) and in the Law’s care for an enemy’s animal (Exod 23:4–5).

Enemy-love is not a New Testament novelty; it is already present in Israel’s wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Matthew 5:44 and Romans 12:20 in view, and an example in Elisha’s feeding of the Aramean army (2 Kings 6:22–23).

Kindness to enemies reflects God’s kindness to the undeserving.

Proverbs 25:22

22for you will heap coals of fire on his head, and the LORD will reward you.

Arminian

Heaping coals of fire on the enemy’s head has been read in more than one way. A common reading is that kindness produces burning shame or remorse that may lead the enemy to repentance. Some scholars have suggested a possible link to an Egyptian custom in which a penitent carried coals on the head; the evidence is limited and debated. Others read coals as an image of judgment that falls on an enemy who stays hostile (cf. Ps 140:10).

Many Arminian readers, like many readers generally, favor the remorse-and-repentance reading because Paul follows the quotation with “overcome evil with good” (Rom 12:21). The LORD will reward the one who shows kindness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise weigh the shame-leading-to-repentance reading alongside the judgment reading, which some connect with Romans 12:19, where Paul tells believers to leave vengeance to God.

The interpretation of the coals does not divide along Reformed and Arminian lines. Both agree that the believer’s part is kindness, and the outcome belongs to the LORD, who rewards it.

Proverbs 25:23

23The north wind produces rain; so a backbiting tongue brings an angry face.

Arminian

As surely as wind brings rain, a backbiting tongue brings an angry face. Arminian readers see cause and effect in speech: gossip provokes anger.

The saying warns against secret slander.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the destructive power of hidden speech. Backbiting breaks fellowship.

The meteorological detail is debated, but the point is clear: words have predictable effects.

Proverbs 25:24

24It is better to dwell in the corner of the housetop than to share a house with a contentious woman.

Arminian

It is better to live on a corner of the roof than share a house with a contentious woman. Arminian readers note that the saying appears almost word for word in 21:9 (cf. 21:19; 27:15).

The repetition stresses how much strife damages a home. The principle applies to quarrelsome people generally.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note 21:9. The saying is addressed to young men in the book’s setting, but its concern is peace in the household.

Contention makes a home unlivable, whatever its comforts.

Proverbs 25:25

25Like cold water to a thirsty soul, so is good news from a far country.

Arminian

Good news from far away is like cold water to a thirsty soul. Arminian readers note the refreshing power of good tidings (cf. Gen 45:27).

The saying invites reflection on the joy of hearing from distant loved ones.

Reformed

Reformed readers often hear an echo of the beautiful feet of those who bring good news (Isa 52:7).

Christians naturally apply this to the gospel as good news from God.

Proverbs 25:26

26Like a muddied spring and a polluted well, so is a righteous man who gives way before the wicked.

Arminian

A righteous person who gives way before the wicked is like a muddied spring. Arminian readers note that compromise pollutes what should refresh others.

The righteous are called to stand firm (cf. Exod 23:2).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the damage done when the righteous yield to pressure. Peter’s wavering at Antioch shows how compromise affects others (Gal 2:11–14).

A public stand for righteousness keeps the spring clear.

Proverbs 25:27

27It is not good to eat much honey, nor is it honorable to seek one’s own honor.

Arminian

Too much honey is not good, and seeking one’s own honor is not honorable. Arminian readers link this back to verse 16: excess and self-glory both sour what is good.

The Hebrew of the second line is difficult, and translations vary.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with warnings against seeking glory from people (John 5:44; 7:18; Prov 27:2).

Honor received from God is better than honor grasped for oneself.

Proverbs 25:28

28Like a city that is broken down and without walls is a man whose spirit is without restraint.

Arminian

A person without self-restraint is like a city with broken walls. Arminian readers stress that self-control protects a life, and they connect it with the Spirit’s fruit (Gal 5:23).

Without restraint, a person is open to every attack.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see self-control as fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:23) and as strength (Prov 16:32).

The image of a defenseless city shows the danger of an ungoverned spirit.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
