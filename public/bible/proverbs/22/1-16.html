Passage

Proverbs 22

Verses 1–16 · WEB

These verses close the main Solomonic sentence collection that began at 10:1. They return to familiar themes: a good name over riches, the rich and poor sharing one Maker, prudence, humility and the fear of the LORD, training children, debt, generosity, and the sluggard’s excuses. The collection ends with a warning that oppressing the poor or currying favor with the rich leads to want. Both traditions read these lines as shared wisdom about life under God, and both read verse 6 in the way proverbs are generally read: as a statement of how things usually go, not an unconditional promise.

Proverbs 22:1

1A good name is more desirable than great riches, and loving favor is better than silver and gold.

Arminian

A good reputation and the goodwill of others are worth more than great wealth (cf. 10:7).

Arminian readers see character built through consistent choices as a lasting treasure.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a good name as the fruit of a life shaped by God’s ways, not a possession to be grasped.

Shared ground: integrity outweighs riches.

Proverbs 22:2

2The rich and the poor have this in common: The LORD is the maker of them all.

Arminian

Rich and poor meet on common ground: the LORD made them both (cf. 14:31; 29:13).

Arminian readers see equal dignity and God’s concern for every person regardless of status.

Reformed

Reformed readers see all people as creatures of one Maker, bearing his image (Genesis 1:27).

Both traditions agree that this shared origin rules out contempt for the poor and flattery of the rich.

Proverbs 22:3

3A prudent man sees danger and hides himself; but the simple pass on, and suffer for it.

Arminian

The prudent see danger coming and take cover, while the naïve walk on and pay for it.

Arminian readers see foresight as a responsible use of the wisdom God makes available.

Reformed

Reformed readers see prudence as a gift that guards God’s people through ordinary means.

Shared ground: wisdom pays attention and acts, while carelessness invites harm.

Proverbs 22:4

4The result of humility and the fear of the LORD is wealth, honor, and life.

Arminian

Humility and the fear of the LORD lead to wealth, honor and life (cf. 15:33).

Arminian readers see these as typical blessings of a humble, God-fearing life, not a formula for prosperity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fear of the LORD as the root from which such blessings grow, given by God in his wisdom.

Both traditions read the verse as a general pattern, aware that the righteous sometimes suffer loss.

Proverbs 22:5

5Thorns and snares are in the path of the wicked; whoever guards his soul stays far from them.

Arminian

Thorns and traps line the path of the twisted, and the one who guards his life stays clear of them.

Arminian readers see self-guarding as an active choice to avoid ruinous paths.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s ways as a protection from the snares that entangle the wicked.

Shared ground: keeping one’s distance from evil is itself a form of wisdom.

Proverbs 22:6

6Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it.

Arminian

Train a child in the way he should go, and in old age he will not turn from it. Like other proverbs, it states how things usually go.

Arminian readers commonly read it as a general proverb rather than an unconditional promise, encouraging faithful parenting while recognizing that a grown child makes real choices.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise commonly treat it as a general proverb about God’s ordinary blessing on faithful instruction, not a guarantee (cf. Ephesians 6:4).

Shared ground: in keeping with how wisdom sayings work, both traditions encourage parents to train diligently and comfort grieving parents that the verse is not a verdict on them.

Proverbs 22:7

7The rich rule over the poor. The borrower is servant to the lender.

Arminian

The rich tend to rule the poor, and the borrower becomes servant to the lender.

Arminian readers see a sober warning to weigh carefully before taking on debt.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a realistic description of how wealth and debt shape power in a fallen world.

Both traditions read the verse as observation and caution, not approval of domination.

Proverbs 22:8

8He who sows wickedness reaps trouble, and the rod of his fury will be destroyed.

Arminian

Whoever sows injustice reaps trouble, and the rod of his fury will fail (cf. Galatians 6:7).

Arminian readers see the harvest principle as a call to choose what to sow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s moral order bringing the oppressor’s power to an end.

Shared ground: injustice does not have the last word.

Proverbs 22:9

9He who has a generous eye will be blessed, for he shares his food with the poor.

Arminian

The generous person is blessed because he shares his bread with the poor (cf. 19:17).

Arminian readers see generosity as a chosen habit of seeing others’ needs.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a generous eye as the fruit of God’s grace at work.

Both traditions agree that God delights in those who share with the needy.

Proverbs 22:10

10Drive out the mocker, and strife will go out; yes, quarrels and insults will stop.

Arminian

Drive out the mocker and strife leaves with him, and quarrels and insults end.

Arminian readers see that communities have a responsibility to address persistent troublemakers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the need for loving but firm discipline to protect the peace of a community.

Shared ground: some strife has a source that must be dealt with, not ignored.

Proverbs 22:11

11He who loves purity of heart and speaks gracefully is the king’s friend.

Arminian

Whoever loves a pure heart and speaks graciously wins the king’s friendship.

Arminian readers see sincerity and kind speech as qualities anyone can pursue.

Reformed

Reformed readers see purity of heart as God’s work, showing itself in gracious words.

Both traditions agree that inner integrity and good speech earn trust even among the powerful.

Proverbs 22:12

12The LORD’s eyes watch over knowledge, but he frustrates the words of the unfaithful.

Arminian

The LORD watches over knowledge but overturns the words of the faithless.

Arminian readers see God guarding truth and frustrating deception.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providential care preserving true knowledge and bringing falsehood to nothing.

Shared ground: truth is under God’s protection.

Proverbs 22:13

13The sluggard says, “There is a lion outside! I will be killed in the streets!”

Arminian

The sluggard invents a lion in the street to excuse staying home (cf. 26:13).

Arminian readers see how excuses can become a refusal to take responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers see laziness disguised as caution and the self-deception it requires.

Both traditions read the humor of the verse as a mirror for our own excuses.

Proverbs 22:14

14The mouth of an adulteress is a deep pit. He who is under the LORD’s wrath will fall into it.

Arminian

The seductive words of an adulteress are a deep pit, and one under the LORD’s wrath falls into it.

Arminian readers see a warning that yielding to temptation can become itself a form of judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God giving people over to the sin they pursue as a mark of his displeasure.

Shared ground: sexual unfaithfulness is a trap, and avoiding it is part of fearing the LORD.

Proverbs 22:15

15Folly is bound up in the heart of a child; the rod of discipline drives it far from him.

Arminian

Folly is bound up in a child’s heart, and discipline drives it away (cf. 13:24; 29:15).

Arminian readers see children needing guidance and correction as part of growing in wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in folly’s presence a sign of the human condition and the need for loving discipline.

Both traditions agree that discipline must be loving and aimed at wisdom, and they apply the image of the rod with care in light of Ephesians 6:4.

Proverbs 22:16

16Whoever oppresses the poor for his own increase and whoever gives to the rich, both come to poverty.

Arminian

Oppressing the poor for profit and giving to the rich to gain favor both end in want. This saying closes the main Solomonic collection.

Arminian readers see two paths of self-interest that God will not let prosper.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s justice overturning schemes that exploit the weak and flatter the strong.

Shared ground: both traditions read this closing word as a warning against using people for gain.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
