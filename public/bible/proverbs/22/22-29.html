Proverbs 22

Verses 22–29 · WEB

These verses begin the sayings of the wise introduced in 22:17–21, now usually in the form of commands with reasons attached. They forbid exploiting the poor in court, befriending the hot-tempered, pledging security for debts, and moving ancient boundary stones, and they end by praising skilled work. The LORD is pictured as the advocate of the poor who pleads their case. Both traditions read these instructions as shared wisdom for justice, prudent relationships, and faithful work, with no significant point of division.

Proverbs 22:22

22Don’t exploit the poor because he is poor; and don’t crush the needy in court;

Arminian

Do not exploit the poor because they are weak or crush the needy in court.

Arminian readers see a command that addresses every person’s responsibility toward the vulnerable.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s concern for justice in the courts, where the weak are easily overlooked.

Shared ground: taking advantage of the poor is a serious wrong.

Proverbs 22:23

23for the LORD will plead their case, and plunder the life of those who plunder them.

Arminian

The LORD himself will plead their case and plunder those who plunder them (cf. 23:11).

Arminian readers find comfort that God stands with the poor and holds oppressors accountable.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the defender of the defenseless and righteous judge.

Both traditions agree that God takes the side of the oppressed.

Proverbs 22:24

24Don’t befriend a hot-tempered man. Don’t associate with one who harbors anger,

Arminian

Do not befriend the hot-tempered or keep company with the easily angered.

Arminian readers see friendship as a choice that shapes who we become.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the influence of companions on the heart and the need for wise associations.

Shared ground: anger is contagious, and friendships should be chosen carefully.

Proverbs 22:25

25lest you learn his ways and ensnare your soul.

Arminian

Otherwise you may learn their ways and set a snare for your own life.

Arminian readers see how imitation can gradually lead a person into harmful patterns.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the danger of being shaped by ungodly influences.

Both traditions agree that the company we keep affects our character.

Proverbs 22:26

26Don’t you be one of those who strike hands, of those who are collateral for debts.

Arminian

Do not be among those who shake hands to guarantee others’ debts (cf. 6:1).

Arminian readers see a caution to weigh the responsibility of such commitments seriously.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wise stewardship in avoiding obligations one cannot fulfill.

Shared ground: rash pledges can bring serious harm, and the saying counsels caution rather than forbidding all help to others.

Proverbs 22:27

27If you don’t have means to pay, why should he take away your bed from under you?

Arminian

If you cannot pay, why let your very bed be taken from under you?

Arminian readers see the vivid picture as a call to count the cost before making promises.

Reformed

Reformed readers see realistic wisdom about the consequences of overextending oneself.

Both traditions read the verse as practical caution about debt.

Proverbs 22:28

28Don’t move the ancient boundary stone which your fathers have set up.

Arminian

Do not move the ancient boundary stone set by previous generations (cf. 23:10; Deuteronomy 19:14).

Arminian readers see respect for others’ rightful inheritance as a matter of justice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the boundary as protecting what God allotted to families in the land.

Shared ground: stealing land by shifting markers is an injustice, often against the vulnerable.

Proverbs 22:29

29Do you see a man skilled in his work? He will serve kings. He won’t serve obscure men.

Arminian

The skilled worker will stand before kings, not obscure people.

Arminian readers see diligent excellence as a faithful use of God-given abilities.

Reformed

Reformed readers see skilled work as a calling through which God honors faithfulness.

Shared ground: careful, skilled work is valued and often recognized, though not always.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
