Passage

Proverbs 22

Verses 17–21 · WEB

At 22:17 the style changes from short two-line sayings to a direct appeal: listen to “the words of the wise,” keep them within, and have them ready on your lips. This brief prologue introduces a new collection of longer instructions, marked by a teacher addressing a student and by the stated aim that the reader’s trust may be in the LORD. The mention of “thirty” sayings is debated in its Hebrew wording and in how it relates to what follows. Both traditions see here a shared purpose: wisdom is taught so that people will trust God and speak truthfully to others.

Proverbs 22:17

17Turn your ear, and listen to the words of the wise. Apply your heart to my teaching.

Arminian

A new section begins: the reader is urged to listen to the words of the wise and to set the heart on the teacher’s instruction (cf. 24:23).

Arminian readers see the appeal as an invitation that calls for a willing, attentive response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom passed on through faithful teaching as a means God uses to shape his people.

Shared ground: wisdom must be heard, received and taken to heart.

Proverbs 22:18

18For it is a pleasant thing if you keep them within you, if all of them are ready on your lips.

Arminian

It is pleasant to keep these words within and have them ready on one’s lips.

Arminian readers see memorized wisdom as a resource that helps a person choose well in the moment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s truth stored in the heart and flowing out in speech.

Both traditions value wisdom that is both internalized and ready for use.

Proverbs 22:19

19I teach you today, even you, so that your trust may be in the LORD.

Arminian

The teacher’s aim is that the student’s trust may be in the LORD (cf. 3:5).

Arminian readers see instruction as inviting a personal, active trust in God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see trust in the LORD as the goal of all wisdom, fostered by God through his word.

Shared ground: wisdom is not mere skill but a path to trusting God.

Proverbs 22:20

20Haven’t I written to you thirty excellent things of counsel and knowledge,

Arminian

The teacher asks whether he has not written thirty excellent sayings. The Hebrew word behind “thirty” is uncertain, and translations differ on how to render it.

Arminian readers see the written form of the counsel as a gift that can be returned to and reflected on.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the care taken to put counsel into writing as a sign of its value for God’s people.

Both traditions hold the exact meaning and count with caution while agreeing that the instruction is meant to be thoughtful and trustworthy.

Proverbs 22:21

21To teach you truth, reliable words, to give sound answers to the ones who sent you?

Arminian

The goal is truth and reliable words, so the student can give sound answers to those who sent him.

Arminian readers see wisdom equipping people for responsible service to others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see truth from God shaping faithful, dependable speech.

Shared ground: wisdom makes a person trustworthy in what they report and say.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
