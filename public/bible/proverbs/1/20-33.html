Passage

Proverbs 1

Verses 20–33 · WEB

Wisdom is personified as a woman calling out in the public places of the city, rebuking the simple, mockers, and fools, and promising to pour out her spirit and make her words known to those who turn. Because they have refused her call, she warns that disaster will come and they will seek her too late, while whoever listens will dwell securely. Both traditions hear a real summons and a real warning; they differ mainly in how they relate the call, the response, and God’s enabling grace, and some Christian readers connect personified Wisdom with Christ.

Proverbs 1:20

20Wisdom calls aloud in the street. She utters her voice in the public squares.

Arminian

Wisdom is personified as a woman calling in the street and public squares. Arminian readers stress that her call is public and open, addressed to anyone who will hear (cf. Prov 8:1–4).

Wisdom is not hidden or reserved for a few. She goes where people are.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see a public summons, freely proclaimed to all who pass by. The openness of the call is not in dispute.

Some Christian readers connect personified Wisdom with Christ, who is called “the wisdom of God” (1 Cor 1:24, 30; Col 2:3). Others read the figure first as a poetic personification of God’s wisdom. Both are readings, not settled consensus.

Proverbs 1:21

21She calls at the head of noisy places. At the entrance of the city gates, she utters her words:

Arminian

Wisdom calls at the noisy places and the city gates, where business and justice are done. Arminian readers note that God’s wisdom addresses ordinary public life, not only religious settings.

Her message competes with the city’s noise, and people can attend to it or ignore it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the gates were places of judgment and decision. Wisdom speaks into the center of community life.

God’s truth claims authority over every sphere of life, not only private devotion.

Proverbs 1:22

22“How long, you simple ones, will you love simplicity? How long will mockers delight themselves in mockery, and fools hate knowledge?

Arminian

“How long?” expresses both lament and appeal. Arminian readers note that the simple, mockers, and fools are described by what they love and hate, so their condition involves their desires and choices.

The question implies that change is possible and urgently desired.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an escalating description of the heart’s resistance: love of simplicity, delight in mockery, hatred of knowledge. The problem lies in the affections.

The question exposes persistence in folly and calls the hearers to account.

Proverbs 1:23

23Turn at my reproof. Behold, I will pour out my spirit on you. I will make known my words to you.

Arminian

Wisdom calls for a turning and promises to pour out her spirit and make her words known. The Hebrew word rendered “spirit” can also be taken as “thoughts” or “mind,” in parallel with “words”; the WEB has “spirit.” Arminian readers see a sincere offer of real help to all addressed, tied to a real response (cf. Isa 55:1; Matt 11:28).

The promise of the spirit and words shows grace extended alongside the call. Many Arminian readers relate this to prevenient grace: the call comes with enabling help, and people can still resist it (cf. Acts 7:51).

Reformed

Reformed readers also hear a genuine summons and a gracious promise. Some, reading “spirit” with the WEB, connect the outpouring with God’s work of renewing the heart, as in later promises of the Spirit (Ezek 36:26–27; Joel 2:28). Others take the Hebrew word as “thoughts” or “mind.”

Reformed theology distinguishes the outward call, offered freely to all, from the inward call by which God makes the call effective in those he draws (John 6:44). Those who read the verse as a promise of the Spirit sometimes relate both to this line.

Proverbs 1:24

24Because I have called, and you have refused; I have stretched out my hand, and no one has paid attention;

Arminian

Wisdom has called and stretched out her hand, and the hearers refused. Arminian readers note the language of genuine refusal, echoed in God’s appeal to Israel (Isa 65:2; Rom 10:21) and in Jesus’ lament over Jerusalem (Matt 23:37).

The offer was real, and so was the rejection. Judgment follows from the refusal of an offer they could have accepted.

Reformed

Reformed readers fully affirm that the refusal is real and culpable. The hearers are judged for what they did, not for something forced on them.

Reformed readers explain the refusal by the heart’s bondage to sin (Rom 8:7), which rejects the call unless God renews it. The responsibility remains entirely theirs.

Key contrast Both agree that the call was real, the refusal was real, and the judgment is just. Arminian readers see the refusal as resisting grace sufficient to enable a different response. Reformed readers see it as the natural resistance of the unrenewed heart, which God justly leaves to its choice, while maintaining that it is fully culpable.

Proverbs 1:25

25but you have ignored all my counsel, and wanted none of my reproof;

Arminian

The hearers ignored all of Wisdom’s counsel and wanted none of her reproof. Arminian readers note that the rejection was sustained and deliberate.

Correction was offered repeatedly. Its refusal is what makes the coming calamity a matter of justice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a settled contempt for counsel. Rejecting reproof is a mark of the fool throughout Proverbs (Prov 12:1).

The repeated “all” and “none” stress how complete the rejection is.

Proverbs 1:26

26I also will laugh at your disaster. I will mock when calamity overtakes you,

Arminian

Wisdom’s laughter is a strong poetic image of the reversal of roles: those who mocked are now mocked. Arminian readers caution against reading it as cruelty; it expresses the justice of consequences.

The image parallels God’s laughter at rebellious rulers (Ps 2:4).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the image of God’s holy derision at rebellion (Ps 2:4). It expresses the finality of judgment on those who scorned him, not delight in suffering for its own sake (cf. Ezek 33:11).

The mockers receive the measure they gave.

Proverbs 1:27

27when calamity overtakes you like a storm, when your disaster comes on like a whirlwind, when distress and anguish come on you.

Arminian

Calamity arrives like a storm and whirlwind. Arminian readers note the suddenness: the delay of consequences is not their cancellation.

The repetition builds a picture of overwhelming distress.

Reformed

Reformed readers see judgment coming with force when God’s patience has been spurned. Storm imagery often depicts divine judgment in the prophets (cf. Isa 29:6).

Those who felt secure in their mockery are swept away.

Proverbs 1:28

28Then they will call on me, but I will not answer. They will seek me diligently, but they will not find me,

Arminian

Now they call, but Wisdom does not answer. Arminian readers stress that there is a time to respond (Isa 55:6), and that the window of opportunity can close (cf. Matt 25:10–12).

This does not mean God refuses true repentance. Their seeking is a cry for rescue from calamity, which the next verse links to their earlier hatred of knowledge.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a seeking that is too late and not true turning (cf. Heb 12:17). The cry comes from distress rather than love of wisdom.

God’s patience is real, but it is not to be presumed upon. The warning presses the hearers to respond while the call is sounding.

Proverbs 1:29

29because they hated knowledge, and didn’t choose the fear of the LORD.

Arminian

The reason is given: they hated knowledge and did not choose the fear of the LORD. Arminian readers highlight the language of choice (cf. Deut 30:19; Josh 24:15).

The fear of the LORD is presented as something to be chosen. Not choosing it is the root of the judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the hearers did not choose and are fully responsible. They also hold that choosing the fear of the LORD flows from a heart God renews (cf. Jer 32:40; Phil 2:12–13).

The verse names the human act. Reformed theology places that act within God’s gracious work without reducing its reality.

Proverbs 1:30

30They wanted none of my counsel. They despised all my reproof.

Arminian

The refusal is restated: no counsel wanted, all reproof despised. Arminian readers note how the passage keeps returning to the hearers’ own stance toward Wisdom.

Repetition stresses accountability.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the summary of a hardened attitude. Contempt for reproof leaves a person without remedy (Prov 29:1).

The verse echoes verse 25 and closes the charge.

Proverbs 1:31

31Therefore they will eat of the fruit of their own way, and be filled with their own schemes.

Arminian

They will eat the fruit of their own way. Arminian readers see judgment as the outworking of chosen paths (cf. Gal 6:7).

God’s justice here takes the form of letting people receive what they have chosen.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see judgment fitted to the sin, and note how God may give people over to the desires they insist on (Rom 1:24). Sin becomes its own punishment.

The imagery of eating and being filled shows the consequences becoming full.

Proverbs 1:32

32For the backsliding of the simple will kill them. The careless ease of fools will destroy them.

Arminian

The backsliding or turning away of the simple will kill them, and the careless ease of fools will destroy them. Arminian readers note that “backsliding” implies turning away from a path, a warning that drifting has deadly consequences.

Complacency is as dangerous as open rebellion.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the turning away as the simple refusing Wisdom’s call, and the ease of fools as a false security. Both lead to ruin.

The verse warns against treating spiritual indifference as harmless.

Proverbs 1:33

33But whoever listens to me will dwell securely, and will be at ease, without fear of harm.”

Arminian

The speech ends with promise: whoever listens will dwell securely. Arminian readers note that the invitation stays open to all, and it ends on hope rather than judgment.

Listening brings safety. The way of wisdom is available to anyone who will hear.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the security of those who hear and heed the call (cf. Prov 3:23–24; John 10:27–28). Peace belongs to those who belong to Wisdom.

The passage closes by holding out the blessing to anyone who listens, the same free offer it made at the start.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
