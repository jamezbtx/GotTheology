Passage

Proverbs 1

Verses 8–19 · WEB

A father urges his son to hold on to his parents’ teaching and to refuse the invitation of violent men who promise easy plunder. The speech exposes the gang’s logic: those who lie in wait for others end by ambushing their own lives. Both traditions read this chiefly as moral instruction about companions, greed, and violence, with broad common ground.

Proverbs 1:8

8My son, listen to your father’s instruction, and don’t forsake your mother’s teaching;

Arminian

The first discourse begins with a father’s appeal and honors both father and mother as teachers (cf. Prov 6:20). Arminian readers note that the son is addressed as one who can listen or forsake.

Wisdom is passed on within the family. The home is a primary place where faith and character are formed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant household at work, with parents teaching children God’s ways (Deut 6:6–7). The fifth commandment stands in the background (Exod 20:12).

Parental instruction is one of God’s ordinary means of grace for shaping the next generation.

Proverbs 1:9

9for they will be a garland to grace your head, and chains around your neck.

Arminian

Parental teaching is pictured as a garland and a necklace, signs of honor and beauty. Arminian readers note that wisdom adorns a life; it is attractive, not merely a burden.

The father motivates by showing the good that wisdom brings.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the imagery of grace and honor (cf. Prov 4:9). Obedience to godly instruction is fitting and beautiful.

God’s commands are given for the good of those who keep them.

Proverbs 1:10

10My son, if sinners entice you, don’t consent.

Arminian

The warning is direct: if sinners entice you, don’t consent. Arminian readers stress that temptation is real and that the son is able, and responsible, to refuse.

Consent is the decisive point. Enticement does not force anyone, but yielding is a real choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the son must refuse and is responsible for his consent. They also see the need for God’s keeping power in temptation (Matt 6:13; 1 Cor 10:13).

The command and dependence on grace sit together. The father’s warning is itself one means God uses to guard the young.

Proverbs 1:11

11If they say, “Come with us. Let’s lie in wait for blood. Let’s lurk secretly for the innocent without cause.

Arminian

The gang’s invitation begins with belonging: “Come with us.” Arminian readers note how sin often recruits through companionship before it reveals its cruelty.

The innocent are targeted “without cause.” Their plan is calculated malice, not passion.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the sinners’ speech shows the deceitfulness of sin. It hides violence behind fellowship and promise.

The innocent victims recall the biblical concern for those who are attacked unjustly (cf. Ps 10:8).

Proverbs 1:12

12Let’s swallow them up alive like Sheol, and whole, like those who go down into the pit.

Arminian

The gang pictures themselves as devouring like Sheol, the place of the dead. Arminian readers note the irony: they talk of swallowing others, but the speech already points toward death.

Violence casts itself in the role of the grave.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the boast of those who imagine they hold power over life and death. Such boasting belongs to the rebellious heart.

Sheol and the pit appear again later in the passage as the real end of the way of the wicked.

Proverbs 1:13

13We’ll find all valuable wealth. We’ll fill our houses with plunder.

Arminian

The appeal turns to wealth: valuable goods and houses full of plunder. Arminian readers see the old lure of quick gain without honest work.

Greed drives the invitation, as verse 19 will make explicit.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that covetousness lies beneath the violence (cf. Exod 20:17). Sinful desire for gain leads to sinful means.

The tenth commandment’s concern with the heart is visible here.

Proverbs 1:14

14You shall cast your lot among us. We’ll all have one purse”—

Arminian

“We’ll all have one purse” promises shared risk and shared reward. Arminian readers note the appeal to solidarity and fairness among thieves.

The gang offers a counterfeit community.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a false fellowship bound together by sin. Its unity rests on shared guilt, not on love of God or neighbor.

Casting one’s lot with them means joining their way and sharing its outcome.

Proverbs 1:15

15my son, don’t walk on the path with them. Keep your foot from their path,

Arminian

The father repeats his warning: don’t walk with them; keep your foot from their path. Arminian readers stress the practical wisdom of avoiding the road altogether.

The path image runs through Proverbs. Where one walks shapes who one becomes (cf. Ps 1:1).

Reformed

Reformed readers note the call to separation from the ways of sinners. Godliness involves deliberate distance from evil company (Ps 1:1; 1 Cor 15:33).

The command is concrete: guard your steps.

Proverbs 1:16

16for their feet run to evil. They hurry to shed blood.

Arminian

The reason for the warning: their feet run to evil and hurry to shed blood (cf. Isa 59:7). Arminian readers note the eagerness of sin once it takes hold.

What begins as an invitation becomes a rush toward violence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the similar language in Isaiah 59:7, and Paul’s use of such language in describing human sin (Rom 3:15).

The verse exposes the swiftness of the sinful heart toward evil.

Proverbs 1:17

17For the net is spread in vain in the sight of any bird;

Arminian

A bird that sees the net being spread may avoid it. Arminian readers often read the saying as a warning to the son: you have now seen the trap, so don’t walk into it.

The saying is brief, and its exact application has been read in more than one way.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a contrast between a bird and these men, who walk into their own trap even when it is in plain view.

The proverb is compressed, so interpreters differ on its precise point. Its general force, folly blind to obvious danger, is clear.

Proverbs 1:18

18but these lay in wait for their own blood. They lurk secretly for their own lives.

Arminian

The ambush turns back on the ambushers. Arminian readers see the moral logic of choices: those who plot against others end up harming themselves.

The consequences flow from the path chosen.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s just ordering of the world, in which the wicked fall into the pit they dig (Ps 7:15–16; Prov 26:27).

Sin carries its own penalty within it, under God’s government.

Proverbs 1:19

19So are the ways of everyone who is greedy for gain. It takes away the life of its owners.

Arminian

The father names the root: greed for gain, which takes away the life of those who hold it. Arminian readers note that greed promises life but consumes it.

The discourse ends by naming the root rather than only the outward crime.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with the New Testament warning that the love of money leads to ruin (1 Tim 6:9–10).

The wealth seized by violence ends up destroying its owners. Sin is self-defeating.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
