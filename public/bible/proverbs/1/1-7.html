Passage

Proverbs 1

Verses 1–7 · WEB

The book opens with its title and purpose: Solomon’s proverbs are given so that the simple, the young, and the already wise may gain wisdom, instruction, and sound counsel for a righteous life. The prologue ends with the motto of the whole book, that the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge. This section is broad common ground: both traditions read it as covenant wisdom rooted in reverence for God, not as a debate about salvation.

Proverbs 1:1

1The proverbs of Solomon, the son of David, king of Israel:

Arminian

The heading ties the book to Solomon, whom Scripture remembers for God-given wisdom and many proverbs (1 Kings 4:29–32). Arminian readers note that later headings name other collections and authors as well (Prov 10:1; 25:1; 30:1; 31:1), so the opening heading introduces the book as a whole.

Wisdom is offered here through a king to his people. It is meant to be learned, practiced, and passed on.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Solomon, the son of David, as a covenant king through whom God’s wisdom is given to Israel (1 Kings 3:12). The book comes to God’s people as part of their inheritance, not as general advice from nowhere.

The greater Son of David is later said to be greater than Solomon (Matt 12:42), so Christians read this wisdom with an eye on Christ.

Proverbs 1:2

2to know wisdom and instruction; to discern the words of understanding;

Arminian

The purpose is stated plainly: to know wisdom and instruction and to discern words of understanding. Arminian readers note that wisdom is presented as something a person can genuinely come to know and grow in.

Instruction includes discipline and correction. Learning wisdom is a process that involves willingness to be taught.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that wisdom here is practical skill for living under God, joined with instruction that corrects and shapes. It is not mere information.

The book assumes that God’s people need to be taught. Growth in discernment is part of the ordinary life of faith.

Proverbs 1:3

3to receive instruction in wise dealing, in righteousness, justice, and equity;

Arminian

Wise dealing is joined to righteousness, justice, and equity. Arminian readers stress that biblical wisdom is moral, not simply clever: it shows itself in fair and upright conduct toward others.

This guards against treating faith as private feeling. Wisdom is meant to be seen in how people treat their neighbors.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the vocabulary of God’s own law and character in righteousness, justice, and equity. Wisdom conforms life to the standards God reveals.

The wise life is a righteous life. Proverbs does not separate skill in living from obedience to God.

Proverbs 1:4

4to give prudence to the simple, knowledge and discretion to the young man—

Arminian

The simple and the young are the first audience named. Arminian readers note that the “simple” are not yet settled for good or ill; they are open, impressionable, and can still be shaped.

That makes the book’s warnings urgent and its invitations real. The young person stands at a point of decision.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s care in giving prudence to those who lack it. The simple are not left to discover wisdom alone; it is given to them through instruction.

The verse also reflects covenant nurture, as older generations teach the young within the community of faith (Deut 6:6–7).

Proverbs 1:5

5that the wise man may hear, and increase in learning; that the man of understanding may attain to sound counsel;

Arminian

Even the wise need to keep listening and growing. Arminian readers see wisdom as a path one keeps walking rather than a finished state.

The mark of the wise is teachability. Hearing and increasing go together.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that wisdom is never something the believer outgrows. The wise grow wiser by continuing to hear (Prov 9:9).

Sanctification is gradual. Sound counsel is gained through continued attention to God’s instruction.

Proverbs 1:6

6to understand a proverb and parables, the words and riddles of the wise.

Arminian

Proverbs, parables, and riddles require thought. Arminian readers note that the book expects active engagement: its sayings have to be pondered and applied with discernment.

Wisdom literature trains judgment, not just memory.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the forms named here call for careful interpretation. A proverb is a compressed truth that must be applied fittingly (Prov 26:7, 9).

God’s wisdom comes through words that reward study and meditation.

Proverbs 1:7

7The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, but the foolish despise wisdom and instruction.

Arminian

The fear of the LORD is the starting point and foundation of knowledge (cf. Prov 9:10; Ps 111:10; Job 28:28). Arminian readers understand this fear as reverent, trusting submission to Yahweh, not terror. The fool, by contrast, despises instruction.

The verse sets two responses side by side. People can embrace or despise wisdom, and Proverbs treats that response as genuinely theirs.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this as the motto of the book. True knowledge begins with a right relation to God; knowledge cut off from him is distorted at the root.

The fool’s contempt for wisdom shows a heart turned from God. Reformed readers commonly add that this reverent fear is itself a fruit of God’s grace (cf. Jer 32:40), while still treating the fool as fully responsible.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
