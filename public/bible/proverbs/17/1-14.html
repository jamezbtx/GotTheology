Passage

Proverbs 17

Verses 1–14 · WEB

These sayings belong to the larger Solomonic collection (10:1–22:16). The unit gathers around peace in the household and the community: quiet over strife, the LORD who tests hearts, covering offenses, receiving rebuke, and stopping a quarrel before it bursts out. Both traditions read these proverbs as shared wisdom for life under God, observations of how his world usually works rather than unconditional promises.

Proverbs 17:1

1Better is a dry morsel with quietness, than a house full of feasting with strife.

Arminian

A crust eaten in peace is better than a feast eaten in conflict. Proverbs often weighs goods against each other, and here relationships outrank abundance (cf. 15:17).

Arminian readers see a call to choose peace, a choice the reader is responsible to make.

Reformed

Reformed readers see contentment with God’s provision, a theme Paul echoes (Philippians 4:11–12).

Shared ground: peace is a greater blessing than plenty.

Proverbs 17:2

2A servant who deals wisely will rule over a son who causes shame, and shall have a part in the inheritance among the brothers.

Arminian

A wise servant can outrank a shameful son and even share the inheritance. Character, not birth, determines standing in the end.

Arminians see wisdom and folly as real moral paths with real consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God overturning human expectations, raising the humble and wise (cf. 1 Samuel 2:7–8).

Both agree that faithfulness can matter more than status.

Proverbs 17:3

3The refining pot is for silver, and the furnace for gold, but the LORD tests the hearts.

Arminian

Fire tests metals; the LORD tests hearts. God sees beneath appearances and uses trials to reveal and purify.

Arminians see testing as God proving and strengthening a responsive faith (cf. James 1:2–4).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s searching knowledge and his refining work in his people (cf. 1 Peter 1:6–7).

Shared ground: God knows the heart fully and refines through trial.

Proverbs 17:4

4An evildoer heeds wicked lips. A liar gives ear to a mischievous tongue.

Arminian

Wicked people listen eagerly to wicked talk. What we choose to hear shows who we are.

Arminians stress responsibility for what we heed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the heart’s corruption showing itself in appetite for evil words.

Both agree that listening is a moral act.

Proverbs 17:5

5Whoever mocks the poor reproaches his Maker. He who is glad at calamity shall not be unpunished.

Arminian

Mocking the poor insults the God who made them (cf. 14:31). Rejoicing at someone’s disaster will not go unpunished.

Arminians see God’s care for every person as the ground of human dignity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the image of God in the poor and God’s justice against those who despise them.

Shared ground: how we treat the poor is how we treat their Maker.

Proverbs 17:6

6Children’s children are the crown of old men; the glory of children is their parents.

Arminian

Grandchildren crown the aged, and children take pride in their parents. Generations bless and honor one another.

Arminians and Reformed readers alike see the family as a God-given setting for blessing.

Reformed

Reformed readers often note the covenant pattern of faith passed through generations (cf. Psalm 103:17).

Shared ground: honoring family across generations is wise.

Proverbs 17:7

7Excellent speech isn’t fitting for a fool, much less do lying lips fit a prince.

Arminian

Fine words do not fit a fool, and lies fit a ruler even less. Speech should match character, and leaders are held to a higher standard.

Arminians see integrity expected of those with influence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see leaders accountable to God for truthfulness.

Both agree that lies in high places are especially shameful.

Proverbs 17:8

8A bribe is a precious stone in the eyes of him who gives it; wherever he turns, he prospers.

Arminian

This proverb describes how a bribe looks to the one who gives it: like a magic stone that opens every door. It observes; it does not approve. Verse 23 condemns bribery outright.

Arminians and Reformed readers both read observational proverbs in light of the book’s moral teaching.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Proverbs often reports the world as it seems before showing God’s verdict.

Shared ground: bribery’s apparent success is not God’s approval.

Proverbs 17:9

9He who covers an offense promotes love; but he who repeats a matter separates best friends.

Arminian

Covering an offense, choosing not to broadcast it, fosters love; repeating it breaks friendships (cf. 10:12; 1 Peter 4:8). This is not hiding sin from correction but refusing to spread it.

Arminians see love actively choosing restraint.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gospel shape of forgiveness, as God covers his people’s sin.

Shared ground: love does not gossip.

Proverbs 17:10

10A rebuke enters deeper into one who has understanding than a hundred lashes into a fool.

Arminian

A rebuke sinks into a discerning person more deeply than a hundred lashes into a fool. Teachability is a mark of wisdom.

Arminians see openness to correction as a responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a teachable heart as fruit of God’s work (cf. Psalm 141:5).

Both agree the wise welcome correction.

Proverbs 17:11

11An evil man seeks only rebellion; therefore a cruel messenger shall be sent against him.

Arminian

The rebel seeks only rebellion, and a severe messenger will be sent against him. Rebellion invites judgment.

Arminians see consequences that follow freely chosen rebellion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s justice working through human authorities against rebellion.

Shared ground: rebellion does not end well.

Proverbs 17:12

12Let a bear robbed of her cubs meet a man, rather than a fool in his folly.

Arminian

Better to meet a bear robbed of her cubs than a fool in his folly. Folly is dangerous, not merely silly.

Arminians and Reformed readers alike see the vivid warning against fools.

Reformed

Reformed readers see folly as a moral and spiritual condition, not only a lack of intelligence.

Both agree fools can do great harm.

Proverbs 17:13

13Whoever rewards evil for good, evil shall not depart from his house.

Arminian

Repaying good with evil brings lasting trouble on one’s household (cf. David and Uriah, 2 Samuel 12:10).

Arminians see sin’s consequences spreading through families.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s moral order in which ingratitude meets judgment.

Shared ground: returning evil for good is grievous.

Proverbs 17:14

14The beginning of strife is like breaching a dam, therefore stop contention before quarreling breaks out.

Arminian

Starting a quarrel is like breaching a dam: once the water flows, it is hard to stop. Stop it early.

Arminians see practical responsibility to choose peace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom in restraint, echoed in James 3:5–6.

Shared ground: quarrels are easier to prevent than to end.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
