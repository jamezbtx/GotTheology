Passage

Proverbs 17

Verses 15–28 · WEB

The second half of chapter 17 centers on justice and speech: God hates the perversion of justice, a friend’s loyalty in hard times, folly’s grief to parents, the healing power of a cheerful heart, and the wisdom of restrained words. Both traditions read these as shared wisdom, while 17:15 raises a real theological question when set beside Paul’s teaching that God justifies the ungodly.

Proverbs 17:15

15He who justifies the wicked, and he who condemns the righteous, both of them alike are an abomination to the LORD.

Arminian

Acquitting the guilty and condemning the innocent are both abominable to God. Judges must declare what is true. Arminians affirm that God remains just in justifying sinners because Christ died for sin (Romans 3:25–26). Classical Arminians affirm justification by grace through faith; they differ among themselves on how to describe imputation, some speaking of faith being reckoned as righteousness and others affirming the imputation of Christ’s righteousness.

The proverb guards against corrupt human courts.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this verse sharpens the wonder of Romans 4:5, where God “justifies the ungodly.” God does not violate this principle, because the sinner is justified on the basis of Christ’s righteousness imputed and his penalty borne. God declares righteous only those clothed in another’s righteousness.

The proverb first governs human judges, who must never pervert justice.

Key contrast Both traditions agree the verse condemns corrupt judging and that God remains just in justifying sinners through Christ’s death. Reformed theology typically explains this through the imputation of Christ’s active and passive obedience. Arminians agree on justification by grace through faith but describe imputation in more than one way, some emphasizing faith reckoned as righteousness and the pardon secured by Christ’s death.

Proverbs 17:16

16Why is there money in the hand of a fool to buy wisdom, since he has no understanding?

Arminian

Money cannot buy wisdom for someone with no heart for it. Wisdom requires desire, not just means.

Arminians see the need for a willing heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom as a gift requiring a heart God has made receptive.

Shared ground: wisdom is not purchased.

Proverbs 17:17

17A friend loves at all times; and a brother is born for adversity.

Arminian

A true friend loves at all times; a brother is born for hard times. Loyalty shows itself in adversity.

Arminians and Reformed readers alike see faithful love as a reflection of God’s own love.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see the faithful friend fulfilled in Christ (cf. John 15:13–15).

Shared ground: love that endures adversity is true love.

Proverbs 17:18

18A man void of understanding strikes hands, and becomes collateral in the presence of his neighbor.

Arminian

Putting up security for a neighbor (striking hands) is foolish (cf. 6:1–5; 11:15). Proverbs warns against financial entanglements one cannot control.

Arminians and Reformed readers see practical prudence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see stewardship of resources as part of wisdom.

Shared ground: rash pledges are unwise.

Proverbs 17:19

19He who loves disobedience loves strife. One who builds a high gate seeks destruction.

Arminian

Loving offense is loving strife, and building a high gate, likely a picture of arrogance or showy self-exaltation, invites ruin. The second line’s image is uncertain, but the theme is pride.

Arminians see pride as a chosen path to destruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pride as the root of conflict and downfall.

Both agree pride invites ruin.

Proverbs 17:20

20One who has a perverse heart doesn’t find prosperity, and one who has a deceitful tongue falls into trouble.

Arminian

A crooked heart and a deceitful tongue lead to trouble, not prosperity.

Arminians see consequences that follow moral choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see heart and tongue connected (cf. Matthew 12:34).

Shared ground: what is inside comes out in speech and outcome.

Proverbs 17:21

21He who becomes the father of a fool grieves. The father of a fool has no joy.

Arminian

A fool brings sorrow to his father. Parents cannot guarantee a child’s wisdom.

Arminians see the child’s own responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the grief of parents who pray and teach yet see folly, while trusting God’s sovereignty.

Shared ground: a foolish child grieves parents deeply.

Proverbs 17:22

22A cheerful heart makes good medicine, but a crushed spirit dries up the bones.

Arminian

A cheerful heart is good medicine; a crushed spirit dries up the bones. Inner life affects the body.

Arminians and Reformed readers alike see the unity of body and soul.

Reformed

Reformed readers see joy as a gift of God that sustains believers.

Shared ground: the heart’s condition affects health.

Proverbs 17:23

23A wicked man receives a bribe in secret, to pervert the ways of justice.

Arminian

The wicked take bribes secretly to twist justice. Here Proverbs gives its verdict on what v. 8 described.

Arminians see corruption condemned.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s justice against those who pervert it.

Shared ground: bribery perverts justice.

Proverbs 17:24

24Wisdom is before the face of one who has understanding, but the eyes of a fool wander to the ends of the earth.

Arminian

The wise keep wisdom in view; the fool’s eyes wander to the ends of the earth. Wisdom is near; folly is distracted.

Arminians see focus as a responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom as present to the heart God has opened.

Both agree that attention matters.

Proverbs 17:25

25A foolish son brings grief to his father, and bitterness to her who bore him.

Arminian

A foolish son brings grief to father and mother. The line includes the mother, stressing the shared sorrow.

Arminians see the child’s responsibility for the pain caused.

Reformed

Reformed readers see family sorrow as a fruit of folly.

Shared ground: folly wounds a whole household.

Proverbs 17:26

26Also to punish the righteous is not good, nor to flog officials for their integrity.

Arminian

Punishing the righteous or flogging honest officials is wrong. Justice must protect integrity.

Arminians see God’s concern for fairness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see rulers accountable to God for justice.

Shared ground: punishing integrity is evil.

Proverbs 17:27

27He who spares his words has knowledge. He who is even tempered is a man of understanding.

Arminian

Restrained words show knowledge; a calm spirit shows understanding.

Arminians see self-control as a practiced virtue.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-control as a fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:23).

Shared ground: restraint is wise.

Proverbs 17:28

28Even a fool, when he keeps silent, is counted wise. When he shuts his lips, he is thought to be discerning.

Arminian

Even a fool seems wise when silent. Silence may not make one wise, but it prevents much folly.

Arminians and Reformed readers alike see humor and practical wisdom here.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the wisdom of guarding the tongue (cf. James 1:19).

Shared ground: silence often serves better than speech.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
