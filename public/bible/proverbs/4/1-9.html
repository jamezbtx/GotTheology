Passage

Proverbs 4

Verses 1–9 · WEB

The father calls his sons to listen, recalling how his own father once taught him. The grandfather’s remembered words urge getting wisdom at any cost, loving and embracing her as one who preserves, guards, and crowns. Both traditions see wisdom passed down through generations as a gift to be prized, and they read the promised honor as a general pattern rather than a guarantee.

Proverbs 4:1

1Listen, sons, to a father’s instruction. Pay attention and know understanding;

Arminian

A father calls his sons to listen to instruction and gain understanding. Arminian readers see teaching that invites attentive, willing reception. Wisdom is offered, and the sons must choose to listen and learn rather than drift past it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see parental instruction as a God-given means of passing on wisdom. The plural sons widens the appeal to the household. Both traditions see this opening as an invitation to receive wisdom within the family context.

Proverbs 4:2

2for I give you sound learning. Don’t forsake my law.

Arminian

The father gives sound teaching and urges them not to abandon his instruction. Arminian readers see the danger of forsaking what has been received, which calls for continued faithfulness. The good content of the teaching makes holding onto it worthwhile.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sound instruction that must be retained. The word law here refers to the father’s teaching, consistent with the covenant setting. Both traditions see a call to hold fast to wise teaching once it is received.

Proverbs 4:3

3For I was a son to my father, tender and an only child in the sight of my mother.

Arminian

The father recalls being a tender child, an only child in his mother’s sight. Arminian readers see the father sharing his own story, showing that he too once received instruction. The personal memory makes the appeal warm and relational.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom passed down through generations, with the father remembering his own upbringing. The tender description highlights the loving setting of instruction. Both traditions see shared ground in the generational transmission of wisdom.

Proverbs 4:4

4He taught me, and said to me: “Let your heart retain my words. Keep my commandments, and live.

Arminian

The father’s father taught him to retain his words and keep his commands to live. Arminian readers see the link between receiving instruction and the flourishing life it brings. Life is connected to the willing keeping of wise counsel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see generational teaching in which life is tied to keeping commands. The grandfather’s words become part of the father’s instruction. Both traditions read life here in the wisdom sense of a full and blessed life, not a formula for earning salvation.

Proverbs 4:5

5Get wisdom. Get understanding. Don’t forget, and don’t deviate from the words of my mouth.

Arminian

Get wisdom and understanding; do not forget or turn from these words. Arminian readers see urgency in pursuing wisdom actively. Forgetting and turning aside are real dangers that require diligence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom as the supreme pursuit, urged with repeated commands. The call to remember underscores the need to retain what is taught. Both traditions see this as shared encouragement to seek and hold fast to wisdom.

Proverbs 4:6

6Don’t forsake her, and she will preserve you. Love her, and she will keep you.

Arminian

Do not forsake wisdom, and she will preserve you; love her, and she will guard you. Arminian readers see a relationship in which loving and keeping wisdom brings protection. Faithfulness to wisdom is a choice with real consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom personified as a guardian for those who love her. The relationship is reciprocal in the imagery. Both traditions see wisdom’s protective power as a general pattern encouraging devotion to her.

Proverbs 4:7

7Wisdom is supreme. Get wisdom. Yes, though it costs all your possessions, get understanding.

Arminian

Wisdom is supreme; get it, even at the cost of everything. Arminian readers see the priority of wisdom requiring sacrifice and commitment. Nothing else is worth more, so the son should pursue it wholeheartedly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom’s supreme value demanding total commitment. The image of costly acquisition underlines its worth. Both traditions see shared ground in prizing wisdom above every possession.

Proverbs 4:8

8Esteem her, and she will exalt you. She will bring you to honor when you embrace her.

Arminian

Esteem wisdom and she will exalt you; embrace her and she will honor you. Arminian readers see honor as the fruit of valuing wisdom. The proverb motivates devotion by showing its outcome.

Reformed

Reformed readers see honor flowing to those who cherish wisdom. The language of embrace makes the relationship personal. Both traditions read the promise of honor as a general pattern, not a guarantee of public recognition.

Proverbs 4:9

9She will give to your head a garland of grace. She will deliver a crown of splendor to you.”

Arminian

Wisdom will place a garland of grace and a crown of splendor on your head. Arminian readers see wisdom adorning the life of the one who embraces her. The imagery celebrates the beauty of the wise life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see crown imagery highlighting the dignity wisdom brings. Grace and splendor describe the result of wise living. Both traditions see this as an encouraging close to the grandfather’s recalled words.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
