Proverbs 4

Verses 20–27 · WEB

The father renews his call to attend to his words and keep them in the heart, the wellspring of life. Guarding the heart shows itself in honest speech, a focused gaze, level paths, and steady refusal to swerve toward evil. Both traditions see shared ground in this emphasis on the inner life shaping outward conduct within the wisdom tradition.

Proverbs 4:20

20My son, attend to my words. Turn your ear to my sayings.

Arminian

My son, attend to my words and turn your ear to my sayings. Arminian readers see another call for attentive listening, showing that wisdom must be received repeatedly. The father’s persistence reflects how easily attention can drift.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the father renewing his appeal for careful attention. Wisdom requires an open ear. Both traditions see the repeated call to listen as characteristic of these opening chapters.

Proverbs 4:21

21Let them not depart from your eyes. Keep them in the center of your heart.

Arminian

Do not let these words leave your sight; keep them in the center of your heart. Arminian readers see the need to keep wisdom close and internalized. Wisdom must shape the inner life, not remain external.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the heart as the place where wisdom must dwell. Keeping words in view and heart shows complete devotion. Both traditions share this emphasis on inward reception.

Proverbs 4:22

22For they are life to those who find them, and health to their whole body.

Arminian

These words are life to those who find them and health to their whole body. Arminian readers see wisdom bringing wholeness to the entire person. Finding wisdom involves seeking it and receiving it gladly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom bringing life and health in a holistic sense. The promise reflects general benefits of wise living. Both traditions read this as typical fruit rather than an absolute guarantee of health.

Proverbs 4:23

23Keep your heart with all diligence, for out of it is the wellspring of life.

Arminian

Guard your heart diligently, for from it flows the wellspring of life. Arminian readers see the heart as the center of choices and desires, requiring careful vigilance. What flows from the heart shapes the whole of life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the heart as the source of life’s direction, needing constant guarding. The verse underscores the inward roots of conduct. Both traditions see shared ground in the call to watch over the heart above all else.

Proverbs 4:24

24Put away from yourself a perverse mouth. Put corrupt lips far from you.

Arminian

Put away a perverse mouth and corrupt lips. Arminian readers see speech as an expression of the heart that must be disciplined. Guarding the heart includes guarding words.

Reformed

Reformed readers see crooked speech as incompatible with a wise heart. The mouth reveals what is within. Both traditions see this as practical wisdom about the power of speech.

Proverbs 4:25

25Let your eyes look straight ahead. Fix your gaze directly before you.

Arminian

Let your eyes look straight ahead and fix your gaze before you. Arminian readers see focus and resolve in following the wise path. Distraction can lead away from wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a fixed gaze as resistance to wandering after evil. Steady focus keeps a person on course. Both traditions see shared ground in this call to single-minded attention.

Proverbs 4:26

26Make the path of your feet level. Let all of your ways be established.

Arminian

Make the path of your feet level and let your ways be established. Arminian readers see careful planning and attention to one’s steps. Wise choices create stability in life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see deliberate consideration of one’s ways leading to stability. Established paths reflect a settled, wise life. Both traditions see this as practical guidance for walking carefully.

Proverbs 4:27

27Don’t turn to the right hand nor to the left. Remove your foot from evil.

Arminian

Do not turn to the right or left; remove your foot from evil. Arminian readers see steadfast commitment to the wise path. Turning from evil is a continual decision the son must make.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the call to stay on the straight path without deviation. Removing the foot from evil completes the chapter’s exhortation. Both traditions see shared ground in this closing summons to faithful, upright living.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
