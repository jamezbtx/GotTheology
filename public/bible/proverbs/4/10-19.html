Passage

Proverbs 4

Verses 10–19 · WEB

The father reminds his son that he has led him in straight paths and urges him to hold fast to instruction. He warns sharply against the path of the wicked, whose restless evil and darkness contrast with the path of the righteous, shining brighter like the dawn. Both traditions share this two-ways teaching and the call to persevere along the way of wisdom.

Proverbs 4:10

10Listen, my son, and receive my sayings. The years of your life will be many.

Arminian

Listen and receive the father’s sayings, and your years will be many. Arminian readers see attentive reception tied to the flourishing life. Long life is a general blessing associated with wisdom in Proverbs.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the link between receiving wise teaching and a full life. The promise reflects a general pattern of God’s order. Both traditions read it within the wisdom genre rather than as an absolute promise of longevity.

Proverbs 4:11

11I have taught you in the way of wisdom. I have led you in straight paths.

Arminian

The father has taught his son the way of wisdom and led him in straight paths. Arminian readers see faithful parenting that guides without forcing. The son must now walk the paths he has been shown.

Reformed

Reformed readers see instruction as a guide along upright paths. The father’s teaching provides direction for life. Both traditions see shared ground in the value of wise guidance for the young.

Proverbs 4:12

12When you go, your steps will not be hampered. When you run, you will not stumble.

Arminian

When the son walks or runs, he will not be hindered or stumble. Arminian readers see wisdom bringing freedom and stability in life’s movement. Wise choices clear the way and help avoid needless falls.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the security wisdom provides in every pace of life. The imagery of walking and running suggests both ordinary and urgent moments. Both traditions read this as a general benefit of wise living.

Proverbs 4:13

13Take firm hold of instruction. Don’t let her go. Keep her, for she is your life.

Arminian

Hold firmly to instruction and do not let it go, for it is your life. Arminian readers see the need for continual grip on wisdom. Letting go is a real risk that the son must guard against.

Reformed

Reformed readers see instruction as life-giving and to be kept firmly. The urgency underscores its importance. Both traditions share the conviction that wisdom must be held fast rather than casually received.

Proverbs 4:14

14Don’t enter into the path of the wicked. Don’t walk in the way of evil men.

Arminian

Do not enter the path of the wicked or walk the way of evil people. Arminian readers see a clear choice between two paths. The son is warned not to begin down the road of wickedness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the two-ways theme central to Proverbs. Avoiding the path of evil is a matter of wisdom and righteousness. Both traditions see shared ground in this sharp contrast between paths.

Proverbs 4:15

15Avoid it, and don’t pass by it. Turn from it, and pass on.

Arminian

Avoid it, do not pass by it, turn from it and go on. Arminian readers see decisive avoidance as the wise response to temptation. Repeated commands stress the importance of keeping distance from evil.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the urgency of turning from wickedness entirely. The rapid series of verbs shows the seriousness of the danger. Both traditions see practical wisdom in avoiding the very approach to evil.

Proverbs 4:16

16For they don’t sleep unless they do evil. Their sleep is taken away, unless they make someone fall.

Arminian

The wicked cannot sleep unless they do evil or make someone fall. Arminian readers see how wickedness can become a consuming habit. The vivid image warns of the corrupting power of persistent evil.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the restless drive of the wicked toward harm. Their unrest contrasts with the sweet sleep of the wise. Both traditions see this as a portrait of how deeply evil can grip a life.

Proverbs 4:17

17For they eat the bread of wickedness and drink the wine of violence.

Arminian

They eat the bread of wickedness and drink the wine of violence. Arminian readers see evil becoming a way of life that sustains and shapes the wicked. Their very livelihood is drawn from wrongdoing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wickedness and violence as the daily food of the wicked. The image depicts sin as habitual and nourishing their corruption. Both traditions see shared ground in this sober description.

Proverbs 4:18

18But the path of the righteous is like the dawning light that shines more and more until the perfect day.

Arminian

The path of the righteous is like dawn growing brighter until full day. Arminian readers see growth in righteousness as a process of increasing light. The righteous advance as they continue walking the path of wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see progressive growth in righteousness, moving toward fullness. The image of dawn pictures increasing clarity and maturity. Both traditions see this as a beautiful portrait of ongoing growth in the wise and righteous life.

Proverbs 4:19

19The way of the wicked is like darkness. They don’t know what they stumble over.

Arminian

The way of the wicked is like darkness, and they do not know what makes them stumble. Arminian readers see the blindness that comes from walking in wickedness. Without wisdom, people cannot see the dangers ahead.

Reformed

Reformed readers see moral darkness leading to unseen stumbling. The contrast with the righteous path is stark. Both traditions see shared ground in this picture of the confusion and danger of the wicked way.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
