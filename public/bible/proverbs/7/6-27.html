Proverbs 7

Verses 6–27 · WEB

The father describes a scene he watched from his window. A young man lacking sense wanders at dusk toward an adulterous woman’s house. Dressed like a prostitute, loud and restless, she grabs and kisses him. She speaks of offerings and vows just paid, a perfumed bed, and a husband away on a long journey. Her flattery wins, and he follows like an ox to slaughter or a bird into a snare, not knowing it will cost his life. The father closes by urging his sons not to turn aside, for her house is the way to Sheol.

Proverbs 7:6

6For at the window of my house, I looked out through my lattice.

Arminian

The father reports what he saw from his window through the lattice. Arminian readers note the vivid, observed style of the lesson: real life gives the warning its force.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom literature drawing lessons from observation of life under God. The scene is narrated as a cautionary example, and some readers take it as a typical scene rather than a single event.

Proverbs 7:7

7I saw among the simple ones. I discerned among the youths a young man void of understanding,

Arminian

The young man is among the simple and void of understanding, literally lacking heart. Arminian readers stress that the simple are not yet fixed in evil but undeveloped and open, still able to be taught or misled.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the simple as morally naïve and vulnerable apart from instruction. Lacking heart shows that the root problem is inward, not merely a lack of information.

Proverbs 7:8

8passing through the street near her corner, he went the way to her house,

Arminian

He passes near her corner and takes the way to her house. Arminian readers note that the fall begins with small, chosen steps toward temptation before any open sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the folly of drifting toward known danger, and the responsibility of avoiding occasions of sin. The way of the house is a road chosen by a foolish heart.

Proverbs 7:9

9in the twilight, in the evening of the day, in the middle of the night and in the darkness.

Arminian

Twilight, evening, middle of the night, darkness: the time words deepen. Arminian readers see darkness as the cover temptation seeks and as a picture of moral drift.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the darkness as symbolic, in line with John 3:19, where people love darkness because their deeds are evil.

Proverbs 7:10

10Behold, there a woman met him with the attire of a prostitute, and with crafty intent.

Arminian

A woman meets him dressed like a prostitute and with crafty intent, literally guarded of heart. Arminian readers note the deliberate strategy of the tempter as she comes to meet him.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the calculated nature of temptation. Her concealed heart contrasts with the open, guarded heart wisdom produces.

Proverbs 7:11

11She is loud and defiant. Her feet don’t stay in her house.

Arminian

She is loud and defiant; her feet don’t stay home. Arminian readers see restlessness and rebellion as marks of a life given to folly.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the contrast with the woman of strength in chapter 31, who manages her household well. Folly is restless and turbulent.

Proverbs 7:12

12Now she is in the streets, now in the squares, and lurking at every corner.

Arminian

She lurks in the streets, the squares, at every corner. Arminian readers see that temptation is pervasive and requires constant vigilance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note how temptation pursues people in public places. Wisdom in chapter 8 will call in the same public places, setting up a competing invitation.

Proverbs 7:13

13So she caught him, and kissed him. With an impudent face she said to him:

Arminian

She seizes him and kisses him with an impudent face. Arminian readers see the shamelessness of persistent sin, which no longer hides its face.

Reformed

Reformed readers note how sin hardens a conscience until shame is lost, as Jeremiah 3:3 describes a forehead that refuses to be ashamed.

Proverbs 7:14

14“Sacrifices of peace offerings are with me. Today I have paid my vows.

Arminian

Peace offerings and paid vows: she cloaks adultery in religious language. Peace-offering meat had to be eaten soon, so she may be offering a feast. Arminian readers see the danger of religion used as cover for sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see hypocrisy, religious observance joined to wickedness. External worship cannot sanctify a corrupt heart, as the prophets often warn.

Proverbs 7:15

15Therefore I came out to meet you, to diligently seek your face, and I have found you.

Arminian

She claims she came out diligently to seek his face and found him. Arminian readers note the flattery that makes the young man feel uniquely wanted.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the irony: wisdom will promise that those who seek her diligently will find her in 8:17, but here folly seeks its prey.

Proverbs 7:16

16I have spread my couch with carpets of tapestry, with striped cloths of the yarn of Egypt.

Arminian

She describes her couch with tapestry and Egyptian linen. Arminian readers see the appeal to the senses and luxury, sin made attractive.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sin’s deceitful packaging, as in Hebrews 3:13. Temptation promises pleasure and conceals its cost. The finery is meant to make the sin look refined rather than shameful.

Proverbs 7:17

17I have perfumed my bed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon.

Arminian

Myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon perfume the bed. Arminian readers note that the language echoes the Song of Songs, now twisted from marital love to adultery.

Reformed

Reformed readers see good gifts of creation abused. The same fragrances that belong to marital love in the Song of Songs are used here to lure into sin.

Proverbs 7:18

18Come, let’s take our fill of loving until the morning. Let’s solace ourselves with loving.

Arminian

Let’s take our fill of loving until morning. Arminian readers see the open invitation to sin presented as pleasure without consequence.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that sin promises satisfaction it cannot deliver. The language of fullness is ironic in light of the death that follows.

Proverbs 7:19

19For my husband isn’t at home. He has gone on a long journey.

Arminian

My husband isn’t at home; he has gone on a long journey. Arminian readers note the false assurance of safety, as if no one sees. Both traditions recall that God sees all.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the implicit breach of covenant: she is married, and the sin is adultery. Her calculation ignores the God who witnesses marriage, as Malachi 2:14 says.

Proverbs 7:20

20He has taken a bag of money with him. He will come home at the full moon.”

Arminian

He took money and will return at the full moon. Arminian readers see temptation offering a secure window, an assurance that consequences are far off.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the self-deception of thinking sin can be timed and hidden. Proverbs 6:34 warns of a jealous husband’s fury.

Proverbs 7:21

21With persuasive words, she led him astray. With the flattering of her lips, she seduced him.

Arminian

With persuasive words she led him astray. Arminian readers stress that he is persuaded, not forced. He yields to an appeal he could have resisted.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree he is responsible and also stress the power of sin to captivate a heart lacking wisdom. Persuasion succeeds where the heart is already inclined.

Proverbs 7:22

22He followed her immediately, as an ox goes to the slaughter, as a fool stepping into a noose.

Arminian

He follows immediately, like an ox to slaughter or a fool into a noose. Arminian readers see the speed with which a weakened will falls once it starts down the path.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the imagery of an animal led unknowing to death, showing the blindness of sin. He does not reckon with the end of the road, and the father’s point is that folly never does.

Proverbs 7:23

23Until an arrow strikes through his liver, as a bird hurries to the snare, and doesn’t know that it will cost his life.

Arminian

Until an arrow pierces his liver, like a bird hurrying to a snare, not knowing it will cost his life. Arminian readers see the hidden cost of sin and the urgency of the warning.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that sin’s wages is death, as Romans 6:23 says. The simple do not see the end of their path until it is too late.

Proverbs 7:24

24Now therefore, sons, listen to me. Pay attention to the words of my mouth.

Arminian

The father turns directly to his sons: listen to me. Arminian readers see the purpose of the story: an appeal to choose now.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the instruction pressed home as a means God uses to keep his people. A vivid example is followed by direct exhortation, a pattern that runs through Proverbs 1–9.

Proverbs 7:25

25Don’t let your heart turn to her ways. Don’t go astray in her paths,

Arminian

Don’t let your heart turn to her ways. Arminian readers stress guarding the heart, which is where choices begin, as 4:23 says.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the heart as the center of the battle, needing God’s word and grace to keep it from turning aside.

Proverbs 7:26

26for she has thrown down many wounded. Yes, all her slain are a mighty army.

Arminian

She has thrown down many wounded; her slain are a mighty army. Arminian readers note that even the strong have fallen, so no one is safe through self-confidence.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that no one should presume on strength, as 1 Corinthians 10:12 warns. The multitude of victims underlines the seriousness.

Proverbs 7:27

27Her house is the way to Sheol, going down to the rooms of death.

Arminian

Her house is the way to Sheol, going down to the chambers of death. WEB notes that Sheol is the place of the dead. Arminian readers see the end of the path as death, the real consequence of a chosen way.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the path of folly ending in death. Many read the warning as pointing beyond physical death to final ruin, the opposite of wisdom’s life in 8:35.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
