Passage

Proverbs 24

Verses 23–34 · WEB

A new heading introduces further sayings of the wise: impartial judgment, honest answers, wise ordering of work, truthful witness, and refusal of personal revenge. A closing observation of the sluggard’s ruined field shows how little neglects become poverty. Both traditions treat this as shared wisdom; the refusal to repay the neighbor in 24:29 stands beside God’s right to repay in 24:12.

Proverbs 24:23

23These also are sayings of the wise: To show partiality in judgment is not good.

Arminian

A new heading introduces more sayings of the wise; partiality in judgment is not good. Arminian readers see justice requiring fairness that does not favor the powerful or the familiar.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see impartiality reflecting God’s own justice. Both traditions note that the superscription marks a short new collection within the book. Favoritism corrupts any court.

Proverbs 24:24

24He who says to the wicked, “You are righteous,” peoples will curse him, and nations will abhor him—

Arminian

Whoever calls the wicked righteous will be cursed by peoples and abhorred by nations. Arminian readers see the communal harm of perverting justice for the guilty.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the condemnation of calling evil good. Both traditions agree that public justice depends on telling the truth about guilt and innocence.

Proverbs 24:25

25but it will go well with those who convict the guilty, and a rich blessing will come on them.

Arminian

Those who convict the guilty will prosper and receive rich blessing. Arminian readers see courage in upholding justice rewarded within God’s moral order. Truthful judgment strengthens a community.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see blessing on those who judge rightly. Both traditions agree that confronting wrongdoing is a service to the community, not merely harshness.

Proverbs 24:26

26An honest answer is like a kiss on the lips.

Arminian

An honest answer is like a kiss on the lips. Arminian readers see truthful speech as an expression of genuine love and respect. Truth spoken kindly is a mark of real friendship rather than mere politeness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see honesty as a gift that builds relationships. Both traditions agree the image conveys intimacy and warmth, valuing candor over flattery. Flattery may please, but honesty serves.

Proverbs 24:27

27Prepare your work outside, and get your fields ready. Afterwards, build your house.

Arminian

Prepare your work outside and ready your fields before building your house. Arminian readers see practical wisdom in ordering priorities and preparing a livelihood first.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see prudence in planning and sequencing tasks. Both traditions read the saying as counsel for responsible life decisions, possibly including marriage and family, without insisting on one application.

Proverbs 24:28

28Don’t be a witness against your neighbor without cause. Don’t deceive with your lips.

Arminian

Do not testify against your neighbor without cause or deceive with your lips. Arminian readers see truthfulness protecting neighbors from unjust harm. Words can wound as surely as actions.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the ninth commandment’s concern against false witness. Both traditions agree the verse condemns deceptive or baseless accusations. Truthful testimony protects the neighbor’s name and the integrity of justice.

Proverbs 24:29

29Don’t say, “I will do to him as he has done to me; I will repay the man according to his work.”

Arminian

Do not say you will repay someone as he has done to you. Arminian readers see personal vengeance renounced, anticipating New Testament teaching such as Romans 12:17.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see retaliation forbidden for individuals, leaving judgment to God. Both note the contrast with 24:12, where God, not the individual, repays according to work.

Proverbs 24:30

30I went by the field of the sluggard, by the vineyard of the man void of understanding.

Arminian

The sage passes the field of the sluggard and the vineyard of one lacking understanding. Arminian readers see a lesson drawn from observation of everyday life.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see wisdom learned through reflection on God’s ordered world. Both traditions note that laziness is linked with lack of understanding, making it a moral rather than merely practical failure.

Proverbs 24:31

31Behold, it was all grown over with thorns. Its surface was covered with nettles, and its stone wall was broken down.

Arminian

The field is overgrown with thorns and nettles and its wall broken down. Arminian readers see the visible results of neglect accumulating over time. Nothing dramatic happened; the work was simply left undone.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see decay following negligence. Both traditions read the scene as a picture of how small failures to act lead to ruin. The broken wall leaves the vineyard exposed as well as unfruitful.

Proverbs 24:32

32Then I saw, and considered well. I saw, and received instruction:

Arminian

The observer considers and receives instruction. Arminian readers see the importance of reflecting on what one sees and choosing to learn from it. Seeing becomes learning only when one stops to consider.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see wisdom gained through thoughtful observation. Both traditions agree that instruction comes not only from teachers but from attentive reflection on life.

Proverbs 24:33

33a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to sleep,

Arminian

A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands. Arminian readers see how small indulgences of laziness add up to serious consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the gradual drift of sloth. Both traditions note that these words repeat an earlier saying in Proverbs 6, reinforcing the warning.

Proverbs 24:34

34so your poverty will come as a robber and your want as an armed man.

Arminian

Poverty comes like a robber and want like an armed man. Arminian readers see sudden consequences of long neglect, urging diligence now. Delay feels harmless until its cost arrives all at once.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the certainty of ruin that follows persistent laziness. Both traditions recognize that Proverbs speaks of general patterns, not every case of poverty, and should not be used to blame the poor indiscriminately.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
