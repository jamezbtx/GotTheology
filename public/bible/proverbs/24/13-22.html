Passage

Proverbs 24

Verses 13–22 · WEB

Wisdom is sweet like honey and carries hope that will not be cut off. The wicked are warned not to attack the righteous, who rise again after many falls. The learner must not gloat over an enemy’s fall or fret over evildoers, and should fear the LORD and the king. Both traditions read these as shared counsel that restrains envy and vengeance by trust in God’s justice.

Proverbs 24:13

13My son, eat honey, for it is good, the droppings of the honeycomb, which are sweet to your taste;

Arminian

Eat honey, for it is good and sweet. Arminian readers see the goodness of God’s gifts used as an image for the delight of wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see honey as a picture of wisdom’s sweetness. Both traditions agree the verse prepares the comparison in the next verse rather than serving as dietary instruction.

Proverbs 24:14

14so you shall know wisdom to be to your soul. If you have found it, then there will be a reward: Your hope will not be cut off.

Arminian

Wisdom is sweet to the soul, and finding it brings a reward and hope that will not be cut off. Arminian readers see a real promise attached to the pursuit of wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s faithfulness securing the hope of the wise. Both traditions note the repetition of the hope not cut off from 23:18, linking the two sections.

Proverbs 24:15

15Don’t lay in wait, wicked man, against the habitation of the righteous. Don’t destroy his resting place;

Arminian

The wicked should not lie in wait against the home of the righteous. Arminian readers see a warning addressed to would-be oppressors, appealing to their conscience before judgment comes.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see protection of the righteous asserted against schemers. Both traditions agree the verse warns that attacks on the righteous will not succeed in the end.

Proverbs 24:16

16for a righteous man falls seven times and rises up again, but the wicked are overthrown by calamity.

Arminian

The righteous fall seven times and rise again, but the wicked are overthrown by calamity. Arminian readers see resilience through repeated hardship, sustained by God and continued trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God upholding the righteous through many troubles. Both traditions read the falls chiefly as calamities rather than moral lapses, and agree the verse contrasts recovery with final ruin.

Proverbs 24:17

17Don’t rejoice when your enemy falls. Don’t let your heart be glad when he is overthrown,

Arminian

Do not rejoice when your enemy falls. Arminian readers see a call to humility and mercy that anticipates Jesus’ teaching on loving enemies. Mercy extends even to those who have wronged us.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see gloating forbidden as contrary to God’s character. Both traditions agree the saying restrains vindictive pleasure even when an enemy deserves judgment.

Proverbs 24:18

18lest the LORD see it, and it displease him, and he turn away his wrath from him.

Arminian

Gloating might displease the LORD so that he turns his wrath away. Arminian readers see God watching the heart’s response to others’ misfortune, not only outward actions.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s concern for the attitude of the observer. Both traditions agree the verse is a warning against self-righteous satisfaction rather than a technique for increasing an enemy’s punishment.

Proverbs 24:19

19Don’t fret yourself because of evildoers, neither be envious of the wicked;

Arminian

Do not fret because of evildoers or envy the wicked. Arminian readers see freedom from anxiety and envy grounded in trust that God will act justly.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the call to rest in God’s governance. Both traditions note the parallel with Psalm 37, which also urges not fretting over evildoers.

Proverbs 24:20

20for there will be no reward to the evil man. The lamp of the wicked will be snuffed out.

Arminian

The evil man has no future reward, and the lamp of the wicked will be snuffed out. Arminian readers see the end of wickedness as a reason not to envy it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s judgment bringing the prosperity of the wicked to an end. Both traditions agree the extinguished lamp pictures the loss of life and legacy.

Proverbs 24:21

21My son, fear the LORD and the king. Don’t join those who are rebellious,

Arminian

Fear the LORD and the king, and do not join the rebellious. Arminian readers see reverence for God shaping respect for rightful authority. Loyalty to God comes first and shapes every other loyalty.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s order including human government. Both traditions agree the fear of the LORD comes first and that respect for rulers is not unconditional approval of injustice.

Proverbs 24:22

22for their calamity will rise suddenly. Who knows what destruction may come from them both?

Arminian

Calamity from both will rise suddenly upon the rebellious. Arminian readers see consequences for rebellion against God and legitimate authority. Rebellion carries hidden dangers that the rebellious rarely foresee.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see judgment that can come swiftly and unexpectedly. Both traditions recognize uncertainty about the exact referent of both, most naturally the LORD and the king.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
