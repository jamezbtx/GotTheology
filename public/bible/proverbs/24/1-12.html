Passage

Proverbs 24

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Sayings warn against envying evil men, commend wisdom as the builder of a house and the source of strength and counsel, and expose folly as sin. The section closes with an urgent call to rescue those led to death, since God weighs hearts and repays each according to his work. Both traditions affirm this accountability; they share the conviction that judgment according to works does not contradict salvation by grace.

Proverbs 24:1

1Don’t be envious of evil men, neither desire to be with them;

Arminian

Do not envy evil men or desire their company. Arminian readers see a call to resist the pull of those who seem successful through wrongdoing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see envy of the wicked as a recurring temptation addressed in wisdom literature. Both traditions agree the saying protects the learner from being drawn into the ways of the violent.

Proverbs 24:2

2for their hearts plot violence and their lips talk about mischief.

Arminian

Their hearts plot violence and their lips speak mischief. Arminian readers see the inner intentions of the wicked revealed, showing why their company is dangerous.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the heart as the source of violent plans and harmful speech. Both traditions note that the heart and lips work together, a common pairing in Proverbs.

Proverbs 24:3

3Through wisdom a house is built; by understanding it is established;

Arminian

By wisdom a house is built and by understanding it is established. Arminian readers see wisdom as the foundation for a stable household and life.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the order God provides through wisdom. Both traditions agree the house image covers both a literal home and the shape of a whole life, echoing the earlier picture of Wisdom building her house.

Proverbs 24:4

4by knowledge the rooms are filled with all rare and beautiful treasure.

Arminian

By knowledge the rooms are filled with rare and beautiful treasure. Arminian readers see the blessings that follow from a life built on wisdom. What wisdom builds, it also furnishes.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the riches of wisdom portrayed as filling the house. Both traditions read the treasure as including more than material goods, while recognizing that Proverbs states general patterns.

Proverbs 24:5

5A wise man has great power. A knowledgeable man increases strength,

Arminian

A wise man has great power, and knowledge increases strength. Arminian readers see wisdom as a source of genuine capability, surpassing mere physical force. Understanding multiplies what strength alone can do.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the strength that comes from understanding. Both traditions agree the saying elevates wisdom above brute power. Insight is a gift worth more than muscle.

Proverbs 24:6

6for by wise guidance you wage your war, and victory is in many advisors.

Arminian

By wise guidance you wage war, and victory comes through many advisors. Arminian readers see humility in seeking counsel rather than relying on one’s own judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the value of counsel in major decisions. Both traditions note that Proverbs repeatedly commends many advisors, applying the principle beyond warfare.

Proverbs 24:7

7Wisdom is too high for a fool. He doesn’t open his mouth in the gate.

Arminian

Wisdom is too high for a fool, who does not speak in the gate. Arminian readers see the fool excluding himself from wisdom by refusing instruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fool’s inability to contribute where community decisions are made. Both traditions note that the city gate was the place of public deliberation and judgment in ancient Israel, as the Old Testament itself depicts.

Proverbs 24:8

8One who plots to do evil will be called a schemer.

Arminian

One who plots evil will be called a schemer. Arminian readers see reputation following character, so that devious intent eventually becomes known. Names tend to follow habits of the heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the moral order exposing those who plan harm. Both traditions agree the saying warns that evil scheming defines a person in the eyes of others.

Proverbs 24:9

9The schemes of folly are sin. The mocker is detested by men.

Arminian

Foolish schemes are sin, and the mocker is detested. Arminian readers see that folly is not mere ignorance but moral failure for which a person is responsible.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see folly as sin, not simply a lack of knowledge. Both traditions agree that the mocker’s contempt for wisdom alienates him from the community.

Proverbs 24:10

10If you falter in the time of trouble, your strength is small.

Arminian

If you falter in trouble, your strength is small. Arminian readers see adversity testing the reality of one’s strength and calling for endurance grounded in God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see trials revealing what a person truly relies on. Both traditions read the saying as an exhortation to resilience rather than a condemnation of those who struggle.

Proverbs 24:11

11Rescue those who are being led away to death! Indeed, hold back those who are staggering to the slaughter!

Arminian

Rescue those being led to death and hold back those staggering to slaughter. Arminian readers see an urgent moral duty to act on behalf of those in mortal danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a summons to protect the vulnerable from injustice and death. Both traditions agree the command calls for active intervention rather than passive sympathy.

Proverbs 24:12

12If you say, “Behold, we didn’t know this,” doesn’t he who weighs the hearts consider it? He who keeps your soul, doesn’t he know it? Shall he not give to every man according to his work?

Arminian

Pleading ignorance will not excuse inaction, for God weighs hearts and repays according to work. Arminian readers see real accountability for neglected responsibility, echoed in Romans 2:6 and Matthew 16:27.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s searching knowledge and just recompense, affirming judgment according to works alongside justification by grace through faith. Both traditions agree the verse confronts willful ignorance about others’ suffering.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
