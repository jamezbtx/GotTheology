Passage

Proverbs 31

Verses 10–31 · WEB

The book closes with a poem in praise of “a worthy woman.” In Hebrew it is an alphabetic acrostic: its 22 verses begin with the 22 letters of the alphabet in order, from aleph to tav, a form that suggests completeness and helps memory. The same Hebrew phrase is used of Ruth (Ruth 3:11). The poem portrays a real woman of covenant wisdom at work in trade, land, household, charity, and speech, and it climaxes in “a woman who fears the LORD” (31:30), echoing the book’s motto (1:7). Many readers also see Lady Wisdom of chapters 1–9 embodied here, though they weigh that link differently. The portrait is neither an impossible checklist nor merely an allegory. Both traditions share this reading and see in it wisdom lived out in ordinary life before God.

Proverbs 31:10

10Who can find a worthy woman? For her value is far above rubies.

Arminian

The poem opens with a question: “Who can find a worthy woman?” The phrase can mean a woman of strength, valor, or noble character. The same phrase describes Ruth (Ruth 3:11), and Proverbs uses it earlier (12:4). Her value “far above rubies” recalls what the book says of wisdom (3:15; 8:11).

Arminian readers, with all Christian readers, read the question as praise of rarity and worth, not as despair that such a woman cannot exist. The poem sets out a real and reachable pattern of godly life.

Reformed

The acrostic begins with aleph. The question invites the reader to see such a woman as a treasure, given by the LORD (compare 18:22; 19:14).

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, note the link to wisdom in chapters 1–9: what Lady Wisdom is in poetry, this woman is in daily life. Both traditions treat the poem as a portrait of wisdom embodied, not as a doctrinal fork.

Proverbs 31:11

11The heart of her husband trusts in her. He shall have no lack of gain.

Arminian

Her husband trusts her completely, and he lacks no “gain.” The word may carry the sense of spoil or plunder, a bold image that some read as part of the poem’s heroic tone.

Arminian readers see trust as the fruit of faithful character proven over time. Marriage flourishes where faithfulness is real.

Reformed

Trust is the first thing said of the marriage. She is not managed or watched; she is relied on.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, see a picture of covenant faithfulness within the household. Both traditions read the verse as praise of trustworthy character.

Proverbs 31:12

12She does him good, and not harm, all the days of her life.

Arminian

She does him good and not harm all her life. Her faithfulness is lasting, not seasonal.

Arminian readers note the steady, daily shape of holiness here. Goodness is shown in long obedience, not in single moments.

Reformed

“All the days of her life” stresses constancy. Her goodness to her husband is steady through every season.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the lifelong pattern of a godly life. Both traditions treat the verse as a simple commendation of faithful love.

Proverbs 31:13

13She seeks wool and flax, and works eagerly with her hands.

Arminian

She seeks wool and flax and works eagerly with her hands. Her diligence is willing, even glad.

Arminian readers stress that ordinary manual work is a fitting place to serve God. Diligence is a virtue Proverbs praises throughout (10:4; 12:24).

Reformed

Wool and flax are the raw materials of clothing. She finds them and works them herself.

Reformed readers, like Arminians, see the dignity of ordinary work, a theme the Reformers stressed in their teaching on vocation. Both traditions read the verse as praise of willing labor.

Proverbs 31:14

14She is like the merchant ships. She brings her bread from afar.

Arminian

Like merchant ships, she brings food from afar. The image suggests initiative, planning, and reach beyond her own doorstep.

Arminian readers see wise enterprise as part of a godly life. Providing well for a household takes thought and effort.

Reformed

The comparison to trading ships shows a woman engaged in the wider economy, not confined to a narrow role.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see faithful stewardship of resources and opportunities. Both traditions read the verse as wisdom applied to trade and provision.

Proverbs 31:15

15She rises also while it is yet night, gives food to her household, and portions for her servant girls.

Arminian

She rises before dawn to provide food for her household and portions for her servant girls. “Portions” may mean food or the tasks assigned for the day.

Arminian readers note that her care reaches the servants as well as the family. Responsibility for others shapes her day.

Reformed

Her early rising is not driven by anxiety but by care for those under her charge.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, see her as a faithful steward of a household that includes dependents. Both traditions read the verse as a picture of diligent, caring management.

Proverbs 31:16

16She considers a field, and buys it. With the fruit of her hands, she plants a vineyard.

Arminian

She considers a field and buys it, then plants a vineyard with what her hands have earned. She weighs decisions before acting.

Arminian readers see careful judgment and initiative as gifts to be used. Wisdom is practical and forward-looking.

Reformed

The verse shows thought before purchase and investment of earnings in lasting fruit.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see prudent stewardship that builds for the future. Both traditions read the verse as a portrait of wise economic life.

Proverbs 31:17

17She arms her waist with strength, and makes her arms strong.

Arminian

“She arms her waist with strength” uses the language of preparing for battle or hard labor. Some interpreters note that the poem borrows images often found in heroic poetry and applies them to a woman’s daily work.

Arminian readers see strength of body and will offered to God’s purposes. Faithfulness requires vigor.

Reformed

The image is of a woman ready for demanding work. Strength, not fragility, marks her.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, see God-given strength used for the good of others. Both traditions read the verse as praise of energy and resolve.

Proverbs 31:18

18She perceives that her merchandise is profitable. Her lamp doesn’t go out by night.

Arminian

She sees that her trade is profitable, and her lamp does not go out at night. She knows the worth of her work and keeps at it.

Arminian readers see diligence and honest profit as compatible with godliness. Gain rightly earned can serve good ends, as verse 20 shows.

Reformed

The lamp burning at night may picture late work or a household that is never without provision.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see honest gain as part of faithful stewardship. Both traditions read the verse as practical commendation, not a rule against rest.

Proverbs 31:19

19She lays her hands to the distaff, and her hands hold the spindle.

Arminian

Her hands hold the distaff and spindle, the tools for spinning thread. The work is humble and steady.

Arminian readers see ordinary tasks as a fitting place for faithful service. No work done for God is too small.

Reformed

Many interpreters note that the hands of verse 19 lead straight into the open hands of verse 20: the hands that work are also the hands that give.

Reformed readers, like Arminians, see vocation and charity joined together. Both traditions read the verse as praise of humble diligence.

Proverbs 31:20

20She opens her arms to the poor; yes, she extends her hands to the needy.

Arminian

She opens her arms to the poor and extends her hands to the needy. Her industry is not selfish; it overflows in generosity.

Arminian readers often connect this with Wesley’s concern for the poor and with a well-known paraphrase of his sermon “The Use of Money”—earn all you can, save all you can, give all you can—which is a summary, not a verbatim quotation. Love of neighbor is the goal of diligent work.

Reformed

Her generosity is personal: she reaches out her hands to the needy. The chapter that began with the king’s duty to the poor (31:8–9) now shows a household doing the same.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see mercy to the poor as a mark of those who fear the LORD (compare 14:31; 19:17). Both traditions agree on the verse’s plain meaning.

Proverbs 31:21

21She is not afraid of the snow for her household, for all her household are clothed with scarlet.

Arminian

Snow does not frighten her, because her household is clothed in scarlet. She plans ahead for hard seasons.

Arminian readers see foresight as part of wise love. Care for others includes preparing for what may come.

Reformed

“Scarlet” may suggest fine, warm garments. Some ancient versions read the same consonants as “double” garments, so the precise sense is uncertain, though the point of thorough provision is clear.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, see calm confidence that comes from faithful preparation. Both traditions read the verse as praise of foresight.

Proverbs 31:22

22She makes for herself carpets of tapestry. Her clothing is fine linen and purple.

Arminian

She makes coverings for herself and wears fine linen and purple. Her provision for others does not mean neglect of herself.

Arminian readers see that holiness does not require drabness. Beauty and quality can be received with thanks.

Reformed

Fine linen and purple were costly materials, marks of dignity and prosperity.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the good gifts of God enjoyed within a life of wisdom and generosity. Both traditions read the verse as part of the portrait, not as a lesson on dress.

Proverbs 31:23

23Her husband is respected in the gates, when he sits among the elders of the land.

Arminian

Her husband is respected in the city gates, where the elders sat to judge and decide matters. Her faithfulness strengthens his standing.

Arminian readers note that a household’s character affects its witness in the community. Private faithfulness has public fruit.

Reformed

The gates were the place of public life and justice. Her husband sits among the elders, honored in part because of her.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, see the household as a foundation of wider social order. Both traditions read the verse as a description of the blessing her wisdom brings.

Proverbs 31:24

24She makes linen garments and sells them, and delivers sashes to the merchant.

Arminian

She makes linen garments and sells them, and supplies sashes to the merchant. Her work reaches the market.

Arminian readers see honest trade as a legitimate and godly calling. Her diligence serves both household and community.

Reformed

She produces and sells goods, engaging in commerce with skill.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the poem honoring a woman’s work in trade as part of faithful vocation. Both traditions read the verse in the same practical way.

Proverbs 31:25

25Strength and dignity are her clothing. She laughs at the time to come.

Arminian

Strength and dignity are her clothing, and she laughs at the time to come. She faces the future without fear.

Arminian readers see confidence rooted in faithful living and trust in God. Diligence and faith together free her from anxiety.

Reformed

Her true clothing is her character. Her laughter at the future is not careless but settled, grounded in her preparation and, as verse 30 shows, in the fear of the LORD.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, see a calm trust in God’s care. Both traditions read the verse as praise of steady, hopeful character.

Proverbs 31:26

26She opens her mouth with wisdom. Kind instruction is on her tongue.

Arminian

She opens her mouth with wisdom, and kind instruction is on her tongue. Her speech matches her deeds.

Arminian readers note that Proverbs ties wise speech to a good heart throughout (10:31; 15:2). Her words build up and teach.

Reformed

“Kind instruction” may be rendered “teaching of steadfast love” or “loyal kindness”; the Hebrew joins the word for teaching (torah) with a word often used for covenant love. The exact nuance is debated.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see her as a teacher of wisdom in her household, echoing the mother’s teaching earlier in the book (1:8; 6:20) and in 31:1. Both traditions read the verse as praise of wise and kind speech.

Proverbs 31:27

27She looks well to the ways of her household, and doesn’t eat the bread of idleness.

Arminian

She watches over her household and does not eat the bread of idleness. She is attentive and active.

Arminian readers see watchfulness and diligence as marks of faithful care. Idleness is contrary to wisdom throughout Proverbs.

Reformed

“Looks well to” pictures a watchman keeping careful oversight. Some interpreters have suggested a wordplay with the Greek word for wisdom, though that is debated.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, see responsible oversight as an expression of love. Both traditions read the verse as praise of attentive stewardship.

Proverbs 31:28

28Her children rise up and call her blessed. Her husband also praises her:

Arminian

Her children rise up and call her blessed, and her husband praises her. Those who know her best honor her most.

Arminian readers see the fruit of a faithful life in the gratitude of family. Godliness is recognized at home first.

Reformed

Praise comes from within the household. Her children and husband speak what they have seen.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the blessing of a godly home. Both traditions read the verse as testimony to her character.

Proverbs 31:29

29“Many women do noble things, but you excel them all.”

Arminian

Her husband’s words are quoted: many women do noble things, but she excels them all. The word for “noble” matches the “worthy” of verse 10.

Arminian readers see loving praise of a spouse as fitting and good. Honor given where it is due strengthens the household.

Reformed

The husband’s praise echoes the poem’s opening. Others act worthily; she surpasses them in his eyes.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, hear the voice of a grateful husband. Both traditions read the verse as part of the poem’s praise, not as a ranking of women.

Proverbs 31:30

30Charm is deceitful, and beauty is vain; but a woman who fears the LORD, she shall be praised.

Arminian

“Charm is deceitful, and beauty is vain; but a woman who fears the LORD, she shall be praised.” This is the key to the whole poem. Her strength, skill, and generosity grow from the fear of the LORD, which is the beginning of wisdom (1:7; 9:10).

Arminian readers see true worth in a heart turned to God, not in passing appearances. Holiness of heart shows itself in the life the poem has described.

Reformed

The verse sets lasting worth against fleeting charm and beauty. By ending with the fear of the LORD, the book closes where it began (1:7), framing all of Proverbs.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the fear of the LORD as reverent faith that shapes every part of life. Both traditions read the verse as the climax of the poem and of the book.

Proverbs 31:31

31Give her of the fruit of her hands! Let her works praise her in the gates!

Arminian

“Give her of the fruit of her hands! Let her works praise her in the gates!” Her deeds speak for her in public.

Arminian readers see works as the visible fruit of a God-fearing life. Her life is honored where it has borne fruit.

Reformed

The poem ends in the gates, where her husband sat (v 23). Her works themselves become her praise in the public square.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, see good works as the fruit of the fear of the LORD, rightly honored by others. Both traditions read the verse as a fitting close to the acrostic and the book.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
