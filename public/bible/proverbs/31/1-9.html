Passage

Proverbs 31

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Proverbs 31 opens with the words of King Lemuel, a counsel his mother taught him. Lemuel is otherwise unknown: some older Jewish and Christian interpreters identified him with Solomon, while others take the word rendered “revelation” as the name of a people or place (Massa), so his identity remains uncertain. The queen mother warns her son against squandering his strength on women and wine, because a drunken king forgets the law and perverts justice. Instead he must open his mouth for the mute, the desolate, the poor and the needy. Both traditions read the passage as wisdom for rulers: power exists to serve justice, especially for those who cannot defend themselves.

Proverbs 31:1

1The words of King Lemuel—the revelation which his mother taught him:

Arminian

Lemuel is not named elsewhere in Scripture, and his identity is uncertain. The word rendered “revelation” can mean an oracle or burden; some interpreters read it as Massa, a people descended from Ishmael (Gen 25:14), which would make Lemuel a non-Israelite king whose counsel Israel’s sages received. The name is often explained as meaning “belonging to God,” though that too is a suggestion.

Arminian readers, with all Christian readers, note that the counsel is a mother’s teaching. Wisdom is handed down in the home, and a parent’s instruction is honored here as part of Scripture.

Reformed

The heading presents what follows as weighty instruction received by a king from his mother. Whether Lemuel was an Israelite or a foreign ruler, his mother’s words were preserved and received among Israel’s wisdom.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see God using ordinary means, such as a parent’s teaching, to form those who will govern. Both traditions read the verse as an introduction, not a doctrinal text.

Proverbs 31:2

2“Oh, my son! Oh, son of my womb! Oh, son of my vows!

Arminian

The threefold cry, “my son… son of my womb… son of my vows,” is full of affection and urgency. “Son of my vows” may mean a child she prayed and vowed for, as Hannah did for Samuel (1 Sam 1:11), though the exact sense is uncertain.

Arminian readers see a parent’s love pressing for a response. The appeal is personal because the choices ahead of the son are real and his.

Reformed

The repeated address stresses the mother’s bond with the king: he is hers by birth and by vow. Her love gives her the right to speak plainly.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, hear in the vows a sense that the child was received from God and dedicated to him. Both traditions read the verse as an affectionate preface to serious counsel.

Proverbs 31:3

3Don’t give your strength to women, nor your ways to that which destroys kings.

Arminian

The first warning is against giving his strength to women, probably meaning a large harem and sexual indulgence that drain a king’s energy and judgment. The Law already warned Israel’s king not to multiply wives, “that his heart not turn away” (Deut 17:17).

Arminian readers see self-control as part of faithful stewardship of power. Desire left unchecked can ruin a life given for others.

Reformed

“That which destroys kings” sums up the danger. Solomon’s many wives turning his heart away (1 Kgs 11:1–4) is a familiar biblical example.

Reformed readers, in agreement, see a ruler’s private life as bound up with his public calling. Both traditions read the verse as a warning against sexual excess, not a disparagement of women, as the closing poem of the chapter makes clear.

Proverbs 31:4

4It is not for kings, Lemuel, it is not for kings to drink wine, nor for princes to say, ‘Where is strong drink?’

Arminian

The second warning concerns strong drink. “It is not for kings” does not condemn wine in itself, which Scripture elsewhere counts as God’s gift (Ps 104:15), but the craving that asks, “Where is strong drink?”

Arminian readers stress the special responsibility of those who hold power. What may be lawful for others becomes dangerous for one whose clear judgment others depend on.

Reformed

The repetition, “not for kings… not for kings,” underlines the point: a ruler must keep a clear head.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, see wisdom fitting conduct to calling. Both traditions read the verse as practical counsel about sobriety in office, not as a universal rule on alcohol.

Proverbs 31:5

5lest they drink, and forget the law, and pervert the justice due to anyone who is afflicted.

Arminian

The reason follows: a drinking king may forget the law and pervert justice for the afflicted. Drunkenness harms most those who most need a fair judge.

Arminian readers note that the warning ties personal habits to the welfare of the weak. A ruler is accountable to God for the poor who suffer under his neglect.

Reformed

Forgetting “the law” (or “what is decreed”) means losing hold of the standards a king must uphold. The afflicted have no one else to protect their rights.

Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the civil ruler as a servant of God for justice (compare Rom 13:4). Both traditions read the verse as a statement about the duty of rulers, not a doctrinal fork.

Proverbs 31:6

6Give strong drink to him who is ready to perish, and wine to the bitter in soul.

Arminian

By contrast, strong drink may be given to the one who is perishing and bitter in soul. Interpreters differ on the tone: some read a merciful concession, such as easing the pain of the dying; others hear a sharp contrast, meaning that drink belongs to the hopeless, not to the king who must judge.

Arminian readers, with many others, take the main point to be the contrast. The king’s duty is to stay sober so that he can help the very people whose misery drink would dull.

Reformed

The verse sets the king’s calling beside the condition of the suffering. Whatever the precise tone, the comparison makes the ruler’s sobriety a matter of service.

Reformed readers, like Arminians, are cautious about building a general rule from this verse. Both traditions read it within the mother’s argument rather than as a standalone instruction.

Proverbs 31:7

7Let him drink, and forget his poverty, and remember his misery no more.

Arminian

Drink lets the poor man forget his poverty and misery for a time. The verse is realistic about why people drink, without praising it as a remedy.

Arminian readers see an implied challenge: the king should not be the one who forgets. His task is to remember the poor and act for them.

Reformed

Forgetting misery stands in deliberate contrast with verse 5, where the king forgets the law. One kind of forgetting may be pitied; the other is a betrayal of office.

Reformed readers, sharing the reading, see the verse preparing for the positive charge in verses 8–9. Both traditions read it as part of the contrast, not as an endorsement of drunkenness.

Proverbs 31:8

8Open your mouth for the mute, in the cause of all who are left desolate.

Arminian

Now the positive command: “Open your mouth for the mute.” The king must speak for those who cannot speak for themselves, whether through weakness, poverty, or lack of standing in court.

Arminian readers often connect this with the long Wesleyan concern for the poor and the neglected. Love of neighbor takes public form in advocacy and justice.

Reformed

“All who are left desolate” may refer to those who are passing away or left destitute; the exact sense is debated. Either way, the king is to take up their cause.

Reformed readers, with Arminians, see here the calling of rulers and of God’s people to defend the defenseless (compare Ps 82:3–4). Both traditions hear the voice of the God who cares for the widow, the orphan, and the stranger.

Proverbs 31:9

9Open your mouth, judge righteously, and serve justice to the poor and needy.”

Arminian

The charge is repeated and widened: judge righteously and serve justice to the poor and needy. This is what a king’s mouth is for.

Arminian readers see justice for the poor as a fruit of true religion, not an optional extra. Faith that ignores the needy is not the faith Scripture commends.

Reformed

The oracle ends where it has been heading: righteous judgment for the poor and needy. The king’s strength, saved from women and wine, is spent in justice.

Reformed readers, sharing this ground, see the ideal king pointing beyond any earthly ruler to the Messiah who judges the poor with righteousness (Isa 11:4; Ps 72:12–14). Both traditions read the verse as the climax of the mother’s counsel.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
