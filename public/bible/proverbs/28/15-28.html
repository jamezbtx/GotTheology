Passage

Proverbs 28

Verses 15–28 · WEB

The second half of chapter 28 continues the sentence proverbs, with sharp attention to wicked rulers, blood guilt, blameless living, diligent work, greed and haste for riches, partiality, honest rebuke, trust in the LORD, and generosity to the poor. It closes by echoing verse 12 on how wicked and righteous rule affect a people. Both traditions read the promises here as general patterns of God’s moral order, not absolute guarantees.

Proverbs 28:15

15As a roaring lion or a charging bear, so is a wicked ruler over helpless people.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the terror a wicked ruler inflicts on helpless people, like a roaring lion or charging bear. Abuse of power is a grave wrong.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same vivid condemnation of tyranny. Both traditions share this ground: those in authority are accountable for how they treat the vulnerable.

Proverbs 28:16

16A tyrannical ruler lacks judgment. One who hates ill-gotten gain will have long days.

Arminian

Arminian readers see poor judgment in tyranny and long life for one who rejects unjust gain. Integrity in leadership tends toward stability.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same contrast. Both traditions read long days as a general blessing associated with righteousness, not an absolute guarantee.

Proverbs 28:17

17A man who is tormented by blood guilt will be a fugitive until death. No one will support him.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the haunting effect of blood guilt, driving the guilty to flight. Such a burden should not be eased by others shielding the offender from consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same picture of guilt and its pursuit. Both traditions read the verse as a sober statement about the weight of shed blood and the justice that follows it.

Proverbs 28:18

18Whoever walks blamelessly is kept safe; but one with perverse ways will fall suddenly.

Arminian

Arminian readers see safety in blameless living and sudden ruin for the perverse. Walking with integrity is a protective way of life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same contrast between two ways. Both traditions read it as a general pattern within wisdom literature rather than a promise that the blameless never suffer harm.

Proverbs 28:19

19One who works his land will have an abundance of food; but one who chases fantasies will have his fill of poverty.

Arminian

Arminian readers see diligent work rewarded with plenty, while chasing fantasies leads to poverty. Responsible, steady effort on what is actually in hand is contrasted with schemes that promise much and deliver little.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same practical wisdom about labor and idle dreams. Both traditions share this ground as everyday counsel about work and its fruits.

Proverbs 28:20

20A faithful man is rich with blessings; but one who is eager to be rich will not go unpunished.

Arminian

Arminian readers see faithfulness bringing blessing, while hasty greed brings judgment. Character, not speed of gain, determines true wealth, and the eagerness to get rich quickly tempts a person toward compromise.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same contrast. Both traditions read the warning against eagerness to be rich as practical moral wisdom, consistent with the chapter’s concern about wealth.

Proverbs 28:21

21To show partiality is not good, yet a man will do wrong for a piece of bread.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the wrong of partiality and how easily people compromise justice for small gain. Integrity must be guarded even in minor matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same insight into how cheaply justice can be sold. Both traditions agree the proverb exposes human weakness in the face of petty bribes.

Proverbs 28:22

22A stingy man hurries after riches, and doesn’t know that poverty waits for him.

Arminian

Arminian readers see stinginess driving a frantic pursuit of wealth that ends in want. Greed blinds a person to the consequences ahead, and the hurry itself reflects misplaced trust in riches.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same irony of greed. Both traditions read it as a general pattern: grasping for riches can lead to poverty.

Proverbs 28:23

23One who rebukes a man will afterward find more favor than one who flatters with the tongue.

Arminian

Arminian readers see honest rebuke eventually winning more favor than flattery. Truthful correction, though unwelcome at first, serves others well over time and is often recognized afterward as genuine care.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same contrast between honest rebuke and flattery. Both traditions share this ground, echoing 27:6’s praise of faithful wounds.

Proverbs 28:24

24Whoever robs his father or his mother and says, “It’s not wrong,” is a partner with a destroyer.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the seriousness of robbing one’s own father or mother and then denying any wrongdoing. Self-justification compounds the sin, closing the door to the honest admission that verse 13 commends.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same condemnation of family betrayal and excuse-making, a violation of the duty to honor parents. Both traditions agree such a person aligns himself with destruction, whatever his claims of innocence.

Proverbs 28:25

25One who is greedy stirs up strife; but one who trusts in the LORD will prosper.

Arminian

Arminian readers see greed stirring conflict, while trust in the LORD leads to prosperity. Trust is a responsive reliance that steadies life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see trust in the LORD as the right posture of the heart, contrasted with grasping. Both traditions read prosperity here as a general pattern of flourishing, not a guarantee of wealth.

Proverbs 28:26

26One who trusts in himself is a fool; but one who walks in wisdom is kept safe.

Arminian

Arminian readers see self-reliance as folly, while walking in wisdom brings safety. Humility before God and others is the wise path.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same contrast between self-trust and wisdom. Both traditions share this ground fully: trusting oneself above God is foolish, and wise living protects.

Proverbs 28:27

27One who gives to the poor has no lack; but one who closes his eyes will have many curses.

Arminian

Arminian readers see generosity to the poor bringing provision, while ignoring need brings curses. Compassion is a responsible choice with real consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same blessing for generosity. Both traditions read this as a general pattern within God’s moral order, encouraging care for the poor.

Proverbs 28:28

28When the wicked rise, men hide themselves; but when they perish, the righteous thrive.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the chapter closing with the effects of wicked and righteous rule. When the wicked perish, the righteous flourish.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same contrast that echoes verse 12. Both traditions read it as political wisdom about how leadership affects the well-being of the righteous.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
