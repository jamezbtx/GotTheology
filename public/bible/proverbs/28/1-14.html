Passage

Proverbs 28

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Chapter 28 is mixed sentence-proverb material from the Hezekiah collection, held together by recurring themes: the righteous and the wicked, keeping God’s law, justice for the poor, rulers and their effect on a land, and wealth and integrity. Verses 13–14 on confessing and forsaking sin and on fearing versus hardening the heart touch questions the traditions discuss elsewhere, but both read these proverbs as shared moral wisdom rather than a settled account of grace and the will.

Proverbs 28:1

1The wicked flee when no one pursues; but the righteous are as bold as a lion.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the contrast between a guilty conscience that flees from imagined pursuit and the boldness of the righteous. Character shapes how a person faces the world.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same contrast between fear and confidence. Both traditions read this as a general pattern: wrongdoing breeds anxiety, while integrity gives courage, without implying the righteous never feel fear.

Proverbs 28:2

2In rebellion, a land has many rulers, but order is maintained by a man of understanding and knowledge.

Arminian

Arminian readers see rebellion producing unstable leadership, while a person of understanding brings order. Wise individuals can make a real difference in a nation’s stability.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same observation about governance and the value of wise leadership. Both traditions treat it as political wisdom about the connection between social disorder and the lack of understanding.

Proverbs 28:3

3A needy man who oppresses the poor is like a driving rain which leaves no crops.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the cruelty of the needy oppressing the poor, compared to a destructive rain that ruins crops. Those who know hardship have a responsibility not to exploit others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same condemnation of oppression and its destructive effects. Both traditions share this ground: exploiting the vulnerable is wrong regardless of one’s own position.

Proverbs 28:4

4Those who forsake the law praise the wicked; but those who keep the law contend with them.

Arminian

Arminian readers see those who abandon the law siding with the wicked, while law-keepers resist them. Loyalty to God’s instruction is expressed in one’s alignments.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same moral division: attitude toward God’s law shapes how one regards wickedness. Both traditions read this as a contrast of allegiances, not a teaching on how one is justified.

Proverbs 28:5

5Evil men don’t understand justice; but those who seek the LORD understand it fully.

Arminian

Arminian readers see that seeking the LORD brings understanding of justice, while evil darkens moral perception. Those who seek him grow in discernment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see true understanding of justice coming to those who seek the LORD. Both traditions see shared ground here: moral clarity is bound up with relationship to God, and evil clouds the mind.

Proverbs 28:6

6Better is the poor who walks in his integrity than he who is perverse in his ways, and he is rich.

Arminian

Arminian readers see integrity valued above wealth gained through crooked ways. Choosing honesty in poverty is better than prospering through perversity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same ranking of integrity over riches. Both traditions treat this better-than saying as a reminder that character matters more than circumstances.

Proverbs 28:7

7Whoever keeps the law is a wise son; but he who is a companion of gluttons shames his father.

Arminian

Arminian readers see keeping the law as the mark of a wise son, while bad companions bring shame. The son’s choices of conduct and friends shape his reputation and his father’s honor.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same household wisdom. Both traditions read law here as God’s instruction, and agree that the company one keeps reflects and shapes character.

Proverbs 28:8

8He who increases his wealth by excessive interest gathers it for one who has pity on the poor.

Arminian

Arminian readers see that wealth gained through exploitative interest will ultimately pass to someone who helps the poor. Unjust gain does not secure lasting benefit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s justice working through the course of events to redirect ill-gotten wealth. Both traditions read this as a general pattern rather than a precise prediction in every case.

Proverbs 28:9

9He who turns away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer is an abomination.

Arminian

Arminian readers see that deliberately refusing to hear God’s law corrupts even prayer. Openness to God’s word and prayer belong together, and one cannot seek God while willfully turning from his instruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same connection between hearing God’s instruction and acceptable worship. Both traditions agree the verse warns against religious practice that ignores God’s teaching.

Proverbs 28:10

10Whoever causes the upright to go astray in an evil way, he will fall into his own trap; but the blameless will inherit good.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the deceiver falling into his own trap, while the blameless inherit good. Leading others astray carries a heavy responsibility and brings its own consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same justice built into God’s moral order. Both traditions read this as a general pattern of reversal, warning against misleading the upright.

Proverbs 28:11

11The rich man is wise in his own eyes; but the poor who has understanding sees through him.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the danger of self-conceit in the wealthy and the discernment of the understanding poor. Wisdom is not tied to wealth, and pride blinds.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same reversal of appearances. Both traditions share the ground that true understanding can belong to the poor and that self-reliant pride undermines wisdom.

Proverbs 28:12

12When the righteous triumph, there is great glory; but when the wicked rise, men hide themselves.

Arminian

Arminian readers see how the character of those in power affects society: righteous rule brings celebration, while wicked rule drives people into hiding.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same observation about public life. Both traditions treat it as political wisdom about the social effects of who rules, a theme repeated later in this chapter.

Proverbs 28:13

13He who conceals his sins doesn’t prosper, but whoever confesses and renounces them finds mercy.

Arminian

Arminian readers see a pattern: concealment blocks flourishing, while honest confession joined to forsaking sin finds mercy. Repentance is a real, responsible turning that God enables and graciously meets.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same pairing of confession and renouncing sin, with mercy given to the penitent. Both traditions agree that confession without forsaking falls short and that mercy is God’s gift. They differ elsewhere on whether the grace that brings repentance is resistible or effectual, but this proverb does not address that, so both read it as shared ground.

Proverbs 28:14

14Blessed is the man who always fears; but one who hardens his heart falls into trouble.

Arminian

Arminian readers see blessedness in a continual reverent fear that keeps one responsive, while hardening the heart leads to trouble. Hardening is pictured as a person’s own resistance, which is a real danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same contrast between reverent fear and self-hardening, with the person held responsible. Some passages elsewhere speak of God’s judicial hardening, but here the subject hardens himself, and both traditions read the proverb as a warning about human responsibility.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
