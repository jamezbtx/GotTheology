Passage

Proverbs 9

Verses 1–6 · WEB

Proverbs 9 closes the opening discourses (chapters 1–9) with a diptych of two hostesses. Here Lady Wisdom builds her house, prepares a feast, and sends out a public invitation to the simple: leave your simple ways, and live.

Proverbs 9:1

1Wisdom has built her house. She has carved out her seven pillars.

Arminian

Wisdom, personified as a woman since 1:20 and 8:1, now has a finished house. “Seven pillars” most likely signals completeness or a large, well-built house; interpreters have proposed many further meanings, and the text does not settle them.

Arminian readers see a house built for guests. Wisdom’s labor is for the sake of others, and the whole scene assumes a genuine offer to anyone who will come.

Reformed

The verbs are completed actions: the house is built and the pillars carved before anyone is invited. The provision is ready before the guest arrives.

Reformed readers hear an anticipation of grace that prepares everything in advance. Many in both traditions, following texts like 1 Corinthians 1:24 and 1:30, see Christ as the fulfillment of personified Wisdom; others read the figure as a literary personification that Christ fulfills more indirectly. Neither tradition requires one view.

Proverbs 9:2

2She has prepared her meat. She has mixed her wine. She has also set her table.

Arminian

The feast is lavish: meat, mixed wine, a set table. In the ancient world such a meal was hospitality, honor, and fellowship together.

Arminian readers note that the abundance is meant to attract. Wisdom does not merely issue commands; she makes the way of life desirable and sets it before people as a real good they may accept.

Reformed

Mixing wine (often with spices) and slaughtering animals describe costly preparation. Wisdom’s banquet stands in deliberate contrast to the cheap fare of Folly in 9:17.

Reformed readers connect the image to the wider biblical theme of God’s feast (Isaiah 25:6; 55:1–2) and, for many, to the gospel banquet Jesus describes (Luke 14:16–24). The richness belongs to the host, not to the guests’ merit.

Proverbs 9:3

3She has sent out her maidens. She cries from the highest places of the city:

Arminian

Wisdom sends maidens and also calls out herself from the highest places of the city, the most public spot available. Her invitation is not secret or restricted.

Arminian readers take the public setting as a picture of a universal call. The message goes where everyone can hear it, and those who hear are responsible for how they answer.

Reformed

The heights of the city echo 8:2–3, where Wisdom stands at the gates and crossroads. Her messengers resemble the servants sent out with a king’s invitation in Jesus’ parables (Matthew 22:3–4).

Reformed readers agree that the call is public and sincere. They distinguish this outward call, which goes to all, from the inward work by which God makes the call effective in those who come; that distinction is developed elsewhere in Scripture rather than here.

Proverbs 9:4

4“Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!” As for him who is void of understanding, she says to him,

Arminian

“The simple” in Proverbs are the inexperienced and easily swayed, not yet committed to folly. Wisdom aims her invitation at precisely those who lack understanding.

Arminian readers see this as a sincere, resistible summons. By God’s grace the simple are enabled to respond, and the call is meant to be answerable by those who hear it, which is why the same people can also answer Folly’s identical call in 9:16.

Reformed

Wisdom’s audience is the needy, not the accomplished. Those who already think themselves wise are not in view; the call goes to the one “void of understanding.”

Reformed readers hear in this the pattern of grace that seeks the empty-handed (cf. 1 Corinthians 1:26–29). They hold that the call is sincerely offered to all, and that where someone turns in, it is because God has opened the heart (cf. Acts 16:14).

Key contrast Both traditions read Wisdom’s invitation as a genuine, public offer to the simple, and both deny that anyone comes by native wisdom. Arminians typically understand God’s grace as enabling every hearer to respond, so that turning in is a free response that could be refused. Reformed interpreters typically understand the outward call as going to all while an inward, effectual call brings the elect to accept it. The parallel call in 9:16 makes the question of how the simple choose between two voices especially vivid.

Proverbs 9:5

5“Come, eat some of my bread, Drink some of the wine which I have mixed!

Arminian

“Come, eat... drink” turns hearing into participation. Wisdom is not only taught; she is received, like food.

Arminian readers note the open-handed imperative. Isaiah 55:1 sounds the same note (“Come, buy, and eat”), and many readers in both traditions hear Jesus’ words about himself as the bread of life (John 6:35) in the background, while recognizing that Proverbs does not spell this out.

Reformed

Wisdom’s bread and mixed wine are her own provision (“which I have mixed”). The guest brings nothing but hunger.

Reformed readers stress that wisdom is a gift received rather than an achievement earned. Many hear an anticipation of Christ, who gives himself as food (John 6:35, 51); some in the tradition also connect the image to the Lord’s Supper, though that is an application, not the passage’s original sense.

Proverbs 9:6

6Leave your simple ways, and live. Walk in the way of understanding.”

Arminian

The invitation has a moral content: “Leave your simple ways.” Coming to Wisdom’s table means turning away from something, and the promised result is life.

Arminian readers see repentance and life closely joined. The call to leave and walk presumes that the hearer, aided by grace, can and must respond, and that the outcome genuinely depends on that response.

Reformed

“Live” is the goal of the whole speech, and it contrasts sharply with the death in Folly’s house (9:18). Walking “in the way of understanding” is ongoing, not a single moment.

Reformed readers agree that the call demands real turning. They see the life promised here as God’s gift and the turning as the fruit of his work, so that the command and grace go together rather than compete.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
