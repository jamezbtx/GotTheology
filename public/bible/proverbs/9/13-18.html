Passage

Proverbs 9

Verses 13–18 · WEB

The chapter ends with Folly’s rival house. She copies Wisdom’s invitation word for word (compare 9:4 and 9:16), but her table offers stolen water and secret bread, and her guests are already among the dead. The discourses end by leaving the reader to choose between the two doors.

Proverbs 9:13

13The foolish woman is loud, undisciplined, and knows nothing.

Arminian

Literally “a woman of folly,” this figure mirrors Lady Wisdom. Where Wisdom is purposeful and generous, Folly is loud, undisciplined, and ignorant.

Arminian readers see temptation portrayed honestly. Folly is attractive and noisy, but she has nothing real to offer, and the reader is warned before the choice is made.

Reformed

Folly’s description recalls the adulterous woman of chapters 5–7 (compare 7:11, “She is loud and defiant”). The literary figure gathers up every path away from God into one person.

Reformed readers note that she “knows nothing.” Folly’s emptiness contrasts with Wisdom’s fullness, and both traditions see behind the image the competing call of sin.

Proverbs 9:14

14She sits at the door of her house, on a seat in the high places of the city,

Arminian

Folly also has a house and a seat in the high places of the city. Her bid for attention imitates Wisdom’s (9:3).

Arminian readers note that the rival call is just as public as the true one. The hearer stands between two real invitations and must weigh them.

Reformed

Unlike Wisdom, Folly has built nothing and prepared nothing; she simply sits at her door. The imitation is outward only.

Reformed readers see evil as essentially parasitic, copying the shape of the good without its substance. Both traditions treat the parallel as a warning that counterfeits look most like the real thing.

Proverbs 9:15

15to call to those who pass by, who go straight on their ways,

Arminian

Folly calls to those “who go straight on their ways,” people who are not looking for trouble. Temptation often comes to the ordinary passer-by.

Arminian readers stress vigilance. Even those currently on a straight path can be drawn aside, which is why Proverbs repeatedly urges the reader to guard the heart (4:23).

Reformed

The targets are people simply going about life. Folly interrupts them and tries to turn them aside.

Reformed readers agree that the warning is real and needed. They teach that believers persevere through watchfulness and the means God provides, not apart from them, and they read this verse as a call to that watchfulness.

Proverbs 9:16

16“Whoever is simple, let him turn in here.” As for him who is void of understanding, she says to him,

Arminian

Folly repeats Wisdom’s invitation (9:4) almost word for word. The simple and those lacking understanding are courted by both houses.

Arminian readers see here the moral seriousness of choice. Both calls are addressed to the same people, and which door they enter matters eternally (see the note on 9:4).

Reformed

The identical wording is deliberate. The simple are not neutral; they will be shaped by one voice or the other.

Reformed readers stress that the simple, left to themselves, are vulnerable to Folly’s call, which is why Wisdom’s invitation is grace. Both traditions agree that the passage calls the reader to recognize and refuse the counterfeit.

Proverbs 9:17

17“Stolen water is sweet. Food eaten in secret is pleasant.”

Arminian

Folly’s menu is the thrill of the forbidden: stolen water, food eaten in secret. The imagery recalls 5:15–18, where water from one’s own cistern stands for faithfulness in marriage.

Arminian readers note how sin advertises itself as sweet precisely because it is forbidden. The hearer is invited to see through the appeal before acting on it.

Reformed

Wisdom offers meat and wine she has prepared; Folly offers water and bread that are stolen. Even her feast is taken from someone else.

Reformed readers see a portrait of how sin deceives, promising pleasure while hiding its cost (cf. Hebrews 3:13). Both traditions draw the same pastoral lesson about the deceitfulness of secret sin.

Proverbs 9:18

18But he doesn’t know that the departed spirits are there, that her guests are in the depths of Sheol.

Arminian

The guest “doesn’t know” what the reader now knows: Folly’s guests are already among the “departed spirits” in Sheol. The WEB footnotes Sheol as “the place of the dead.”

Arminian readers see the danger as real for anyone who accepts Folly’s invitation. The discourses close with life and death set side by side, and with an urgent appeal to choose life.

Reformed

“Departed spirits” translates a Hebrew word for the shades of the dead. Folly’s house leads to the same end as the adulteress’s house in 2:18 and 7:27.

Reformed readers read the closing verse as a sober picture of where every way without God leads. Both traditions agree that the warning is meant to drive the reader back to Wisdom’s table, the house of life.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
