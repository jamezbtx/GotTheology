Proverbs 9

Verses 7–12 · WEB

Between the two invitations stands a short interlude on who can be taught. The scoffer resents correction while the wise grow wiser, and at the center stands the book’s motto restated: the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom.

Proverbs 9:7

7One who corrects a mocker invites insult. One who reproves a wicked man invites abuse.

Arminian

Proverbs often uses “mocker” or “scoffer” for someone who has hardened against instruction. Correcting such a person tends only to bring insult.

Arminian readers note that the scoffer’s condition is self-chosen and reinforced over time. The verse is practical counsel about where correction can help, not a statement that anyone is beyond God’s reach.

Reformed

The interlude is practical: some people will not receive correction, and pressing it only brings abuse on the one who corrects.

Reformed readers read this as wise discernment, much like Jesus’ warning about casting pearls before swine (Matthew 7:6). Like Arminians, they do not take it as a final verdict on any individual; God can soften even a mocker.

Proverbs 9:8

8Don’t reprove a scoffer, lest he hate you. Reprove a wise person, and he will love you.

Arminian

The contrast is in how each person receives a rebuke. The scoffer hates the one who reproves; the wise person loves him.

Arminian readers see character displayed in response. Receiving correction is a mark of wisdom that people grow into through repeated choices, and the verse encourages readers to examine their own reaction to reproof.

Reformed

Proverbs measures wisdom partly by teachability (cf. 12:1; 15:31–32). The wise are not those who need no correction but those who welcome it.

Reformed readers agree and add that a teachable spirit is itself evidence of God’s grace at work. Both traditions draw the same pastoral lesson for churches and families: correction should be given wisely and received humbly.

Proverbs 9:9

9Instruct a wise person, and he will be still wiser. Teach a righteous person, and he will increase in learning.

Arminian

Wisdom compounds. The wise and righteous person receives instruction and keeps growing in learning.

Arminian readers stress growth and continued cooperation. Wisdom is not a fixed possession but a path along which one keeps walking, and neglecting it is a real danger.

Reformed

“Wise” and “righteous” are paired, showing that wisdom in Proverbs is moral and relational, not only intellectual.

Reformed readers connect this to sanctification as progressive growth (cf. 1:5; 2 Peter 3:18). Both traditions agree that the beginning of wisdom leads into a lifetime of increase.

Proverbs 9:10

10The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom. The knowledge of the Holy One is understanding.

Arminian

This verse echoes the motto in 1:7 and forms a bookend for the discourses. Wisdom begins with reverent submission to the LORD, Israel’s covenant God. “The Holy One” is plural in Hebrew, most likely a plural of majesty (compare 30:3).

Arminian readers see the fear of the LORD as a response of the whole person, awakened by grace and freely embraced, that opens the way to every other kind of understanding.

Reformed

“Beginning” can mean both starting point and foundation. True wisdom is not reached apart from knowing God; it starts there and rests there.

Reformed readers also see this fear as a gift that God puts in the heart (cf. Jeremiah 32:40). Both traditions agree that the fear meant here is reverent trust and worship, not cringing terror, and that knowing God is the heart of wisdom.

Proverbs 9:11

11For by me your days will be multiplied. The years of your life will be increased.

Arminian

“By me” returns to Wisdom’s own voice (as in 8:35). Long life is presented as wisdom’s typical fruit.

Arminian readers, like Reformed ones, take this as a general pattern rather than an unconditional promise to each person. Wise living tends to lengthen life, but Scripture also records godly people who die young.

Reformed

The promise of multiplied days repeats a theme from 3:2, 3:16, and 4:10. It describes how wisdom generally works in God’s ordered world.

Reformed readers agree that the verse is not a guarantee about any individual’s lifespan. Many in both traditions also see it as pointing ultimately beyond this life to the life God gives in the age to come.

Proverbs 9:12

12If you are wise, you are wise for yourself. If you mock, you alone will bear it.

Arminian

Each person reaps the outcome of their own response. Wisdom benefits the one who has it; mockery harms the mocker most.

Arminian readers find a strong statement of personal responsibility. Each hearer must answer Wisdom for themselves, and no one can be wise or foolish on another’s behalf.

Reformed

The verse sharpens the choice before the reader just as Folly’s speech begins. The consequences are personal, not merely social.

Reformed readers fully affirm this responsibility. They hold it together with God’s sovereignty without seeing a contradiction, since Scripture teaches both side by side. Neither tradition reads the verse as denying that wisdom also blesses others (as 10:21 shows).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
