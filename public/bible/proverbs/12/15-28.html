Passage

Proverbs 12

Verses 15–28 · WEB

The second half of chapter 12 focuses on speech and diligence: the fool who is right in his own eyes, rash words that wound and wise words that heal, lying lips that the LORD abhors, prudent silence, and the contrast between the diligent and the lazy. The unit closes with the way of righteousness as the way of life. Both traditions treat these sayings as general wisdom, not unconditional promises, and read the shared moral teaching as binding on all God’s people.

Proverbs 12:15

15The way of a fool is right in his own eyes, but he who is wise listens to counsel.

Arminian

The fool thinks his way is right, but the wise listen to counsel. Self-assurance without listening is folly.

Arminian readers stress the responsibility to seek and heed advice. The fool’s certainty keeps him from learning, while the wise remain willing to be corrected.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-righteousness as a mark of folly, and openness to counsel as humility (cf. 14:12).

Shared ground: no one is wise alone, and the wise test their judgment by listening to others. Listening is itself a mark of wisdom.

Proverbs 12:16

16A fool shows his annoyance the same day, but one who overlooks an insult is prudent.

Arminian

A fool shows annoyance immediately, but the prudent overlook an insult. Self-control in the face of provocation is wise.

Arminian readers emphasize the choice to respond with restraint. Quick displays of irritation inflame conflict, while patience can defuse it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see patience as a fruit of grace, and quick anger as folly (cf. James 1:19–20).

Both agree that overlooking an insult is often the prudent and loving response, while quick displays of irritation reveal folly.

Proverbs 12:17

17He who is truthful testifies honestly, but a false witness lies.

Arminian

The truthful testify honestly; the false witness lies. Honesty in testimony is foundational to justice.

Arminian readers see truthfulness as a moral responsibility that sustains community life. Courts, families, and communities depend on honest witness, and lying testimony undermines them all.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this to the ninth commandment against false witness (Exodus 20:16).

Shared ground: truth-telling is essential to justice, and false testimony corrupts the community and dishonors God. Honest testimony reflects the God of truth.

Proverbs 12:18

18There is one who speaks rashly like the piercing of a sword, but the tongue of the wise heals.

Arminian

Rash words pierce like a sword, but the tongue of the wise brings healing. Speech can wound or restore.

Arminian readers emphasize the responsibility to speak carefully. Words chosen with care can bind up wounds that rash words have made.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the tongue’s power and the need for grace to speak words that heal (cf. Ephesians 4:29).

Both agree that careless words cause real damage, while wise words bring healing and peace. The wise use speech to mend.

Proverbs 12:19

19Truth’s lips will be established forever, but a lying tongue is only momentary.

Arminian

Truthful lips endure forever, but a lying tongue lasts only a moment. Truth outlasts lies.

Arminian readers see truth as aligned with God’s eternal character, while falsehood is temporary. Lies may seem to work, but their success is brief.

Reformed

Reformed readers see truth as grounded in God and lies as ultimately exposed.

Shared ground: deception may succeed briefly, but truth endures, because God himself is true and will expose falsehood. A lying tongue cannot stand before him.

Proverbs 12:20

20Deceit is in the heart of those who plot evil, but joy comes to the promoters of peace.

Arminian

Deceit fills the hearts of those who plot evil, but joy comes to those who promote peace. Inner disposition shapes outcomes.

Arminian readers note that peacemaking is a choice that brings joy. Plotting evil fills the heart with deceit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see peacemaking as a mark of God’s children (cf. Matthew 5:9).

Both agree that scheming corrupts the heart, while pursuing peace brings joy to the peacemaker. Deceit, by contrast, corrupts the schemer’s own heart, while peace brings joy.

Proverbs 12:21

21No mischief shall happen to the righteous, but the wicked shall be filled with evil.

Arminian

No harm befalls the righteous, but the wicked are filled with trouble. This is a general statement, not an unconditional promise.

Arminian readers read it alongside Job and the Psalms, which show the righteous suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s ultimate protection of his people, even when they suffer temporarily (cf. Romans 8:28).

Shared ground: this proverb describes a general pattern and the final outcome, not a guarantee against every hardship in this life.

Proverbs 12:22

22Lying lips are an abomination to the LORD, but those who do the truth are his delight.

Arminian

Lying lips are an abomination to the LORD, but those who practice truth delight him. God’s response to speech is personal.

Arminian readers stress God’s genuine delight in truthful living. Truth is something done as well as said.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s holiness opposed to falsehood, and truthfulness as fruit of his Spirit.

Both agree that God hates deceit and delights in truth, so honesty is an act of worship, not merely good manners.

Proverbs 12:23

23A prudent man keeps his knowledge, but the hearts of fools proclaim foolishness.

Arminian

The prudent keep their knowledge to themselves, but fools broadcast their folly. Discretion is wise.

Arminian readers see restraint as a mark of maturity. Fools, by contrast, proclaim their foolishness without restraint, while the prudent know when silence serves better than display.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wise silence as fruit of humility, and foolish talk as revealing the heart.

Shared ground: not every thought needs to be spoken, and discretion protects both speaker and hearer. Silence can be a form of wisdom.

Proverbs 12:24

24The hands of the diligent ones shall rule, but laziness ends in slave labor.

Arminian

The diligent will rule, but laziness ends in forced labor. Effort brings authority; sloth brings servitude.

Arminian readers emphasize responsibility and the real consequences of choices. Diligence tends toward freedom and leadership, while laziness tends toward dependence on others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see diligence as faithful stewardship, and laziness as a moral failure (cf. 2 Thessalonians 3:10).

Both agree that diligence generally leads to responsibility and freedom, while laziness leads to dependence. Work is part of faithful living.

Proverbs 12:25

25Anxiety in a man’s heart weighs it down, but a kind word makes it glad.

Arminian

Anxiety weighs down the heart, but a kind word makes it glad. Words can lift burdens.

Arminian readers see the call to encourage others as a practical expression of love. Even a brief word of kindness can lift a heavy heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers see encouragement as a means of grace, and anxiety as something God’s people bring to him (cf. 1 Peter 5:7).

Shared ground: kind words have real power to relieve anxiety and gladden a burdened heart.

Proverbs 12:26

26A righteous person is cautious in friendship, but the way of the wicked leads them astray.

Arminian

The righteous are cautious in friendship, but the way of the wicked leads them astray. The first line is difficult in Hebrew, and translations vary.

Arminian readers note the call to choose companions wisely. Influence can lead people astray.

Reformed

Reformed readers acknowledge the textual uncertainty and emphasize the clearer point: the righteous exercise discernment, while the wicked go astray.

Both agree that companions shape character (cf. 13:20), and that discernment in friendship is wise.

Proverbs 12:27

27The slothful man doesn’t roast his game, but the possessions of diligent men are prized.

Arminian

The lazy do not roast their game, but the possessions of the diligent are prized. Even what is gained is wasted by sloth.

Arminian readers note that laziness squanders opportunity. The diligent value and care for what they possess.

Reformed

Reformed readers see stewardship as valuing and using what God provides.

Shared ground: diligence includes finishing the task and caring for what one has, while laziness wastes even the gifts already in hand. Faithfulness includes using well what God gives.

Proverbs 12:28

28In the way of righteousness is life; in its path there is no death.

Arminian

In the way of righteousness is life, and in its path there is no death. The second line is debated in Hebrew; some read it as a promise of deathlessness, others as describing the contrast with a path leading to death.

Arminian readers often hear a hint of life beyond death.

Reformed

Reformed readers acknowledge the debate and many see in the verse a glimpse of eternal life, fully revealed in the New Testament (cf. John 11:25–26).

Shared ground: righteousness leads to life, and both traditions read the final hope in light of Christ’s resurrection.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
