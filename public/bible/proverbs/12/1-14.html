Passage

Proverbs 12

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Chapter 12 continues the Solomonic sentences (10:1–22:16) in antithetic pairs. This unit contrasts the stability of the righteous with the collapse of the wicked, touching on receiving correction, the worthy wife, honest thoughts and words, kindness to animals, and diligent work. Both traditions read the sayings as shared wisdom about how God’s world usually works, with the righteous rooted and the wicked overthrown.

Proverbs 12:1

1Whoever loves correction loves knowledge, but he who hates reproof is stupid.

Arminian

Loving correction is loving knowledge; hating reproof is stupid. The blunt word underlines the point: refusing correction makes learning impossible.

Arminian readers emphasize the responsibility to welcome correction, a choice that leads to growth. Rebuke received with humility becomes a path to knowledge.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the teachable heart as evidence of grace, and the hatred of reproof as a mark of folly (cf. Hebrews 12:5–11).

Shared ground: openness to correction is essential to wisdom, and the wise welcome rebuke.

Proverbs 12:2

2A good man shall obtain favor from the LORD, but he will condemn a man of wicked plans.

Arminian

A good person obtains favor from the LORD, but he condemns the one who plots evil. God is actively involved in moral outcomes.

Arminian readers stress God’s genuine responsiveness to human goodness and wickedness. Goodness and wicked schemes do not meet the same response from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s favor as grace toward the good, with goodness itself his gift, and his condemnation of wicked schemes as just.

Both agree that God is not indifferent: he favors goodness and opposes evil plans.

Proverbs 12:3

3A man shall not be established by wickedness, but the root of the righteous shall not be moved.

Arminian

Wickedness cannot establish anyone, but the root of the righteous remains unmoved. The image is of deep roots versus shallow foundations.

Arminian readers see stability as the fruit of a righteous life, while wicked gains remain precarious.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the security of those God plants and sustains (cf. Psalm 1:3).

Shared ground: wickedness provides no lasting security, and righteousness gives stability under God’s care. Wickedness may appear strong for a time, but it has no root that can hold when trouble comes.

Proverbs 12:4

4A worthy woman is the crown of her husband, but a disgraceful wife is as rottenness in his bones.

Arminian

A worthy wife is her husband’s crown; a disgraceful one is like rot in his bones. The phrase anticipates the worthy woman of 31:10.

Arminian readers see character as the true measure of a spouse, and marriage as shaped by mutual faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see marriage as a covenant in which character brings honor or shame.

Both agree that the proverb highlights the profound influence of a spouse’s character, and its principle applies to husbands as well as wives.

Proverbs 12:5

5The thoughts of the righteous are just, but the advice of the wicked is deceitful.

Arminian

The thoughts of the righteous are just, but the advice of the wicked is deceitful. Character shapes counsel.

Arminian readers stress that righteousness begins in the mind and that deceitful advice should be recognized and refused.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a renewed mind producing just thoughts, while the wicked heart produces deceit.

Shared ground: what flows from the inner life reveals character, and counsel should be weighed by the character of those who give it.

Proverbs 12:6

6The words of the wicked are about lying in wait for blood, but the speech of the upright rescues them.

Arminian

The words of the wicked lie in wait for blood, but the speech of the upright rescues. Words can kill or save.

Arminian readers emphasize the moral responsibility of speech and the power of upright words to deliver others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wicked speech as an expression of a violent heart, and upright speech as a means God uses to rescue.

Both agree that speech has life-and-death power, a theme throughout Proverbs (cf. 18:21).

Proverbs 12:7

7The wicked are overthrown, and are no more, but the house of the righteous shall stand.

Arminian

The wicked are overthrown and vanish, but the house of the righteous stands. The image contrasts collapse with stability.

Arminian readers see the general pattern of God’s justice, while acknowledging that the righteous sometimes suffer in this life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s preservation of the righteous household and the certainty of judgment.

Shared ground: righteousness builds lasting houses, while wickedness collapses (cf. Matthew 7:24–27). The wicked may seem secure, but their overthrow is sure, and they leave nothing behind, while the righteous household endures under God’s hand.

Proverbs 12:8

8A man shall be commended according to his wisdom, but he who has a warped mind shall be despised.

Arminian

A person is praised for wisdom, but the one with a warped mind is despised. Reputation tends to follow character.

Arminian readers note that wisdom is pursued and displayed in choices, earning genuine respect. Folly, by contrast, tends to bring disrespect.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom as God’s gift that brings commendation, and a twisted mind as revealing a heart turned from God.

Both agree that wisdom tends to earn honor and folly contempt, though Scripture also shows cases where the world despises the wise.

Proverbs 12:9

9Better is he who is little known, and has a servant, than he who honors himself and lacks bread.

Arminian

Better to be little known and have a servant than to boast and lack bread. Substance matters more than self-promotion.

Arminian readers see a warning against vanity and a call to honest, modest living. Pretending to status one does not have is foolish.

Reformed

Reformed readers see contentment and humility as virtues, and self-honor as empty.

Shared ground: real provision is better than a show of status, and pretension cannot fill an empty table. The “better than” form is typical of Proverbs.

Proverbs 12:10

10A righteous man respects the life of his animal, but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel.

Arminian

The righteous care for their animals, but even the kindness of the wicked is cruel. Righteousness extends to how we treat creatures.

Arminian readers see compassion for all creation as a mark of righteousness. Even the “mercies” of the wicked carry cruelty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see stewardship of creation reflecting the Creator’s care (cf. Deuteronomy 25:4).

Both agree that cruelty to animals reveals a disordered heart, and that righteousness shows itself in small acts of care. Care for animals reflects the Creator’s own kindness.

Proverbs 12:11

11He who tills his land shall have plenty of bread, but he who chases fantasies is void of understanding.

Arminian

Whoever works the land will have plenty, but the one who chases fantasies lacks sense. Diligent work is contrasted with empty pursuits.

Arminian readers emphasize responsibility and steady effort. Fantasies promise quick gain but leave the dreamer empty, while patient tilling yields bread.

Reformed

Reformed readers see labor as part of the creation mandate, and idle schemes as folly.

Shared ground: faithful work in ordinary callings is wise, while chasing illusions leads to want (cf. 28:19, a near repetition).

Proverbs 12:12

12The wicked desires the plunder of evil men, but the root of the righteous flourishes.

Arminian

The wicked desire the plunder of evil men, but the root of the righteous flourishes. The first line is difficult in Hebrew, and translations vary.

Arminian readers note the contrast between grasping after illicit gain and the quiet fruitfulness of righteousness.

Reformed

Reformed readers acknowledge the textual difficulty and focus on the clear second line: the righteous are rooted and fruitful.

Both agree that coveting the spoils of evil is folly, and that righteousness yields stable growth.

Proverbs 12:13

13An evil man is trapped by sinfulness of lips, but the righteous shall come out of trouble.

Arminian

The evil person is trapped by sinful words, but the righteous escape trouble. Speech can ensnare the speaker.

Arminian readers note that careless or deceitful words have consequences for the one who speaks them. Lips that sin often snare their owner.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sinful speech as a snare, and deliverance of the righteous as God’s work.

Shared ground: the tongue can trap its owner, and righteous speech helps keep one out of trouble. Honest words keep a person out of many traps.

Proverbs 12:14

14A man shall be satisfied with good by the fruit of his mouth. The work of a man’s hands shall be rewarded to him.

Arminian

People are satisfied by the fruit of their words, and the work of their hands returns to them. Speech and labor both bear fruit.

Arminian readers see a moral order in which effort and good speech are rewarded.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence rewarding faithful speech and work, while recognizing that rewards are grace.

Both agree that words and deeds produce results, and that faithful living generally brings satisfaction. Good words and honest work both yield their fruit.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
