Passage

Proverbs 27

Verses 11–22 · WEB

This unit continues the sentence-proverb collection. It opens with a father’s appeal to his son and moves through prudence, surety, tactless speech, a contentious household, mutual sharpening, faithful service, the heart as a mirror, insatiable desire, praise as a test, and entrenched folly. The sayings are observational wisdom, not doctrinal statements. Both traditions read them as shared counsel for ordinary life before God.

Proverbs 27:11

11Be wise, my son, and bring joy to my heart, then I can answer my tormentor.

Arminian

Arminian readers hear the father’s voice again, appealing to the son to become wise. The son’s choices bring joy to the father and vindicate his teaching before critics.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same appeal and the shared honor of father and son. Both traditions see shared ground in the household setting of wisdom, where a child’s wise life answers those who would mock the parent’s instruction.

Proverbs 27:12

12A prudent man sees danger and takes refuge; but the simple pass on, and suffer for it.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the prudent person’s foresight and willingness to act, contrasted with the simple who walk into harm. Wisdom involves noticing danger and responding to it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same contrast between prudence and naivety. This saying closely repeats 22:3, and both traditions read it as a general pattern: foresight protects, and heedlessness suffers.

Proverbs 27:13

13Take his garment when he puts up collateral for a stranger. Hold it for a wayward woman!

Arminian

Arminian readers see practical caution about guaranteeing the debts of strangers. A lender should secure the pledge from someone who has made such a risky commitment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same financial prudence; a nearly identical saying appears at 20:16. Both traditions read this as everyday wisdom about debt and surety, without drawing theological conclusions from it.

Proverbs 27:14

14He who blesses his neighbor with a loud voice early in the morning, it will be taken as a curse by him.

Arminian

Arminian readers see that even blessing can be misused: loud praise at the wrong time comes across as a curse. Timing and sincerity matter in how we speak to others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same insight into the gap between appearance and reality in speech. Both traditions treat it as practical social wisdom about tact, sincerity, and the effect words actually have.

Proverbs 27:15

15A continual dropping on a rainy day and a contentious wife are alike:

Arminian

Arminian readers see a vivid image of persistent conflict at home, likened to a steady drip on a rainy day. Contentious behavior wears others down, and each spouse bears responsibility for the peace of the household.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same picture of domestic strife. Both traditions read it as an observation about how quarrelsomeness erodes peace, applicable to any member of a household and not a statement about women in general.

Proverbs 27:16

16restraining her is like restraining the wind, or like grasping oil in his right hand.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the futility of trying to control a contentious person by force, as hard as restraining wind or grasping oil. The verse completes the image begun in the previous one.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same continuation and its point about the limits of control. Both traditions read it as practical wisdom: some conflicts cannot simply be suppressed, and peace requires more than restraint.

Proverbs 27:17

17Iron sharpens iron; so a man sharpens his friend’s countenance.

Arminian

Arminian readers see mutual growth through relationships. As iron sharpens iron, people improve one another through honest interaction, and each must be willing to engage and be shaped.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see friendship as a means of refinement. Both traditions share this ground fully: wise relationships sharpen character, and the proverb is widely applied to mutual encouragement and accountability.

Proverbs 27:18

18Whoever tends the fig tree shall eat its fruit. He who looks after his master shall be honored.

Arminian

Arminian readers see diligent care bringing its fitting reward, whether tending a fig tree or serving a master. Faithful work is generally honored.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same pattern of faithful service and its fruit. Both traditions read the proverb as a general observation about diligence and reward, not a guarantee in every case.

Proverbs 27:19

19Like water reflects a face, so a man’s heart reflects the man.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the heart revealing the true person, just as water reflects a face. Inner character, shaped by choices, shows who we really are.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the heart as the core of a person, which conduct and self-examination reveal. Both traditions agree the saying points inward: a person is known most truly by the heart, and wisdom attends to it.

Proverbs 27:20

20Sheol and Abaddon are never satisfied; and a man’s eyes are never satisfied.

Arminian

Arminian readers see insatiable human desire compared to Sheol and Abaddon, the realm of the dead and destruction, which never say enough. Unchecked longing must be recognized and restrained.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same portrait of restless craving. The WEB footnote explains Sheol as the place of the dead. Both traditions read this as a sober observation about human desire that only true contentment can address.

Proverbs 27:21

21The crucible is for silver, and the furnace for gold; but man is refined by his praise.

Arminian

Arminian readers see praise as a test of character, like a crucible for silver. How a person responds to praise reveals whether pride or humility governs them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same refining test. Both traditions agree that praise exposes the heart, and the wise person receives honor without being puffed up by it.

Proverbs 27:22

22Though you grind a fool in a mortar with a pestle along with grain, yet his foolishness will not be removed from him.

Arminian

Arminian readers see a stark image of entrenched folly that even harsh measures cannot remove. Persistent refusal of wisdom can harden into a settled condition, a sober warning about repeated choices.

Reformed

Reformed readers see folly as deeply rooted in the human heart, beyond the reach of mere external pressure. Both traditions agree the proverb is descriptive of the hardened fool and that outward force alone does not produce wisdom.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
