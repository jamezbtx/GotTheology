Passage

Proverbs 27

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Chapter 27 belongs to the Hezekiah collection (Proverbs 25–29) and is made up of short, mostly self-contained sentence proverbs. This unit gathers sayings on humility about tomorrow, self-praise, provocation and jealousy, and above all friendship: honest rebuke, faithful wounds, earnest counsel, and loyalty to a near neighbor. As wisdom literature, these sayings describe general patterns of life. Arminian and Reformed readers share nearly all of their practical teaching.

Proverbs 27:1

1Don’t boast about tomorrow; for you don’t know what a day may bring.

Arminian

Arminian readers hear a warning against presuming on a future that is open and uncertain to us. Planning is not condemned; boasting is. Humble dependence suits creatures who cannot see what a day will bring.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same humility grounded in God’s ordering of every day, known to him though hidden from us. Both traditions agree the proverb targets arrogant presumption, not ordinary planning, and calls for modest speech about tomorrow.

Proverbs 27:2

2Let another man praise you, and not your own mouth; a stranger, and not your own lips.

Arminian

Arminian readers see a call to let character speak for itself rather than seeking honor through self-promotion. Praise from others is more trustworthy because it is not self-interested, and humility is a disposition to be cultivated.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see humility commended and self-praise exposed as pride. Both traditions read this as practical social wisdom: reputation is rightly given by others, and the urge to commend oneself reveals a heart turned inward.

Proverbs 27:3

3A stone is heavy, and sand is a burden; but a fool’s provocation is heavier than both.

Arminian

Arminian readers note the vivid comparison: a fool’s provocation weighs more than stone or sand. Foolish behavior imposes real burdens on others, which underlines the responsibility each person bears for how their conduct affects neighbors.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same picture of folly’s heavy social cost. Both traditions treat this as an observation about life together: the irritation caused by a fool can be harder to bear than any physical load.

Proverbs 27:4

4Wrath is cruel, and anger is overwhelming; but who is able to stand before jealousy?

Arminian

Arminian readers see a ranking of destructive passions, with jealousy as the hardest to withstand. Wrath and anger are fierce, but jealousy runs deeper and longer. The proverb warns against nurturing such passions.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the same ranking as insight into how powerfully disordered passions grip the heart. Both traditions agree the saying is descriptive wisdom about human emotion and a warning to guard against jealousy in oneself and to be wary of provoking it.

Proverbs 27:5

5Better is open rebuke than hidden love.

Arminian

Arminian readers see love that speaks up when correction is needed. Love that stays hidden and never acts helps no one. Honest rebuke, offered well, is a form of care that respects the other as responsible to change.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see open rebuke as an expression of genuine love. Both traditions share this ground: friendship includes truthful correction, and affection that never shows itself in honest words falls short of what love requires.

Proverbs 27:6

6The wounds of a friend are faithful, although the kisses of an enemy are profuse.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the faithful friend whose painful honesty comes from goodwill, in contrast to the enemy’s lavish but empty affection. Discernment is needed to value the wound that heals over the kiss that flatters.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same contrast between trustworthy correction and deceptive display. Both traditions read the saying as practical wisdom about friendship: sincerity is measured by faithfulness, not by how pleasant the words feel.

Proverbs 27:7

7A full soul loathes a honeycomb; but to a hungry soul, every bitter thing is sweet.

Arminian

Arminian readers see how appetite shapes perception: the satisfied despise even sweetness, while the hungry welcome what is bitter. The proverb invites reflection on contentment and on how need changes what we value.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same observation about desire and satisfaction. Both traditions treat it as a general truth about human experience, with possible wider application to how fullness can dull gratitude and want can sharpen it.

Proverbs 27:8

8As a bird that wanders from her nest, so is a man who wanders from his home.

Arminian

Arminian readers see the danger and vulnerability of leaving one’s rightful place, like a bird leaving its nest. Wandering is pictured as a choice with consequences for security and belonging.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same image of displacement and the instability it brings. Both traditions read the proverb broadly, as a reflection on the value of home and settled responsibilities, without assuming every departure is wrong.

Proverbs 27:9

9Perfume and incense bring joy to the heart; so does earnest counsel from a man’s friend.

Arminian

Arminian readers see earnest counsel from a friend compared to perfume and incense that gladden the heart. Good advice freely given is a gift, and wise people welcome it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see heartfelt counsel valued as something sweet and refreshing. Both traditions find shared ground here in the importance of honest friendship and the joy that sincere advice brings.

Proverbs 27:10

10Don’t forsake your friend and your father’s friend. Don’t go to your brother’s house in the day of your disaster. A neighbor who is near is better than a distant brother.

Arminian

Arminian readers see loyalty to longstanding friendships and the practical value of a nearby neighbor in times of trouble. Faithfulness in relationships is a choice that pays off when disaster comes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same wisdom about loyalty across generations and the help a near neighbor can give. Both traditions read the comparison with a distant brother as practical observation, not a dismissal of family obligations.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
