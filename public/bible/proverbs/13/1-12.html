Proverbs 13

Verses 1–12 · WEB

These verses belong to the first Solomonic sentence collection (10:1–22:16). Each proverb is mostly a self-contained two-line saying, often set as a contrast joined by “but.” This group gathers sayings on teachability, guarded speech, diligence, honesty, wealth, and hope. Like the rest of the collection, they are general observations about how life in God’s world ordinarily works, not unconditional promises for every case. They set the wise and righteous against the scoffer, sluggard, and wicked. Arminian and Reformed readers largely agree on how to read them: wisdom begins in the fear of the LORD (1:7), and the righteousness Proverbs commends is a life of covenant faithfulness, not merit that earns salvation.

Proverbs 13:1

1A wise son listens to his father’s instruction, but a scoffer doesn’t listen to rebuke.

Arminian

A wise son receives his father’s instruction, while a scoffer refuses rebuke. Teachability is the first mark of wisdom in this chapter.

Arminian readers note that receiving or rejecting correction is a real choice for which the son is accountable.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a teachable spirit as the fruit of the fear of the LORD at work in the heart.

Both traditions agree that wisdom starts with listening and that scorn closes the door to growth.

Proverbs 13:2

2By the fruit of his lips, a man enjoys good things, but the unfaithful crave violence.

Arminian

Good speech bears fruit that the speaker enjoys, while the unfaithful hunger for violence. The Hebrew of the second line is compact, roughly “the soul of the treacherous is violence.”

Arminian readers note that words and desires shape the course of a life.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the proverb exposes what the heart craves, not only what the hands do.

Both traditions read the verse as a general pattern: wholesome speech tends to bless, and violent desire tends to destroy.

Proverbs 13:3

3He who guards his mouth guards his soul. One who opens wide his lips comes to ruin.

Arminian

Guarding one’s mouth protects one’s life, while careless talk brings ruin. Restraint in speech is a recurring theme in Proverbs (compare 10:19; 21:23).

Arminian readers stress the daily responsibility to govern one’s tongue.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the tongue reveals the heart and that self-control is a gift of grace to be exercised (compare James 3:2–12).

Both traditions agree on the practical wisdom of careful speech.

Proverbs 13:4

4The soul of the sluggard desires, and has nothing, but the desire of the diligent shall be fully satisfied.

Arminian

The sluggard wants much and gets nothing, while the diligent are satisfied. Desire without effort leaves a person empty.

Arminian readers note that wishing is no substitute for faithful work.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that diligence is itself shaped by God’s providence and blessing, without making effort pointless.

Both traditions see here a general observation about work, not a promise that diligence always brings plenty.

Proverbs 13:5

5A righteous man hates lies, but a wicked man brings shame and disgrace.

Arminian

The righteous hate lies, while the wicked bring shame and disgrace. Honesty is a matter of what one loves and hates.

Arminian readers note that integrity is chosen and practiced over time.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that hatred of falsehood reflects God’s own character (compare 6:16–19).

Both traditions agree that deceit damages both the liar and those around him.

Proverbs 13:6

6Righteousness guards the way of integrity, but wickedness overthrows the sinner.

Arminian

Righteousness guards the person of integrity, while wickedness brings the sinner down. The way a person lives tends to protect or to overthrow.

Arminian readers note that moral choices have consequences built into God’s world.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the moral order upheld by God’s providence, so that wickedness carries the seeds of its own downfall.

Both traditions read the verse as a general pattern rather than a promise of immediate outcomes.

Proverbs 13:7

7There are some who pretend to be rich, yet have nothing. There are some who pretend to be poor, yet have great wealth.

Arminian

Some pretend to be rich and have nothing; others pretend to be poor and have great wealth. Appearances can mislead in both directions.

Arminian readers draw the lesson that outward show is no measure of a person’s real condition.

Reformed

Reformed readers sometimes extend the saying to spiritual riches, though the proverb itself speaks of material pretense.

Both traditions agree that wisdom looks beneath appearances, and they hold any spiritual application as secondary.

Proverbs 13:8

8The ransom of a man’s life is his riches, but the poor hear no threats.

Arminian

Wealth may ransom a rich man’s life, but the poor “hear no threats.” The second line is debated: it may mean the poor are not worth threatening, or that they do not heed rebuke.

Arminian readers note the ambiguity and avoid building much on it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that riches bring their own dangers, even as they can buy a way out of some trouble.

Both traditions read the verse as a realistic observation about wealth, with its meaning held cautiously.

Proverbs 13:9

9The light of the righteous shines brightly, but the lamp of the wicked is snuffed out.

Arminian

The light of the righteous shines, while the lamp of the wicked goes out. Light and lamp picture life and flourishing.

Arminian readers hear encouragement to persevere in righteousness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the lamp going out is a common image for death or the end of a family line (compare 20:20; 24:20).

Both traditions see a general contrast between lasting joy and fading prosperity.

Proverbs 13:10

10Pride only breeds quarrels, but wisdom is with people who take advice.

Arminian

Pride breeds quarrels, while wisdom belongs to those who take advice. Humility makes room for counsel.

Arminian readers note that seeking advice is a choice that guards against conflict.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pride as a root sin that spoils relationships, and teachability as a sign of grace.

Both traditions agree that a willingness to be counseled is central to wisdom.

Proverbs 13:11

11Wealth gained dishonestly dwindles away, but he who gathers by hand makes it grow.

Arminian

Wealth gained dishonestly dwindles, while wealth gathered steadily grows. The Hebrew behind “dishonestly” may suggest wealth that comes from nothing, or quickly; translations differ.

Arminian readers note the value of patient, honest work.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s providence tends to frustrate ill-gotten gain over time.

Both traditions read the verse as a general pattern, not a guarantee about every fortune.

Proverbs 13:12

12Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but when longing is fulfilled, it is a tree of life.

Arminian

Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but fulfilled longing is a tree of life. The “tree of life” image also appears in 3:18; 11:30; and 15:4.

Arminian readers note the honesty of the proverb about disappointment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Scripture does not shame the heartsick, and that believers wait for promises God will fulfill.

Both traditions take pastoral comfort here: delay is painful, and fulfillment brings new life.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
