Passage

Proverbs 13

Verses 13–25 · WEB

The second half of chapter 13 continues the sentence collection with sayings on receiving instruction, the company we keep, discipline, inheritance, and justice for the poor. Many of these proverbs describe consequences: favor or hardship, honor or shame, satisfaction or hunger. Both traditions read these as general patterns under God’s providence rather than guarantees for every individual. Job, Ecclesiastes, and many psalms show the same God allowing the righteous to suffer and the wicked to prosper for a time. Both traditions also see the final vindication of these patterns at God’s judgment, while being cautious about reading that horizon into every line.

Proverbs 13:13

13Whoever despises instruction will pay for it, but he who respects a command will be rewarded.

Arminian

Despising instruction brings a cost, while respecting a command is rewarded. The verse frames the second half of the chapter around receiving teaching.

Arminian readers note that how one responds to instruction is a real and accountable choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that a reverent response to God’s word is the fruit of a heart he has shaped.

Both traditions agree that despising instruction is self-harm, and that “reward” here is a general pattern, not a wage that earns God’s favor.

Proverbs 13:14

14The teaching of the wise is a spring of life, to turn from the snares of death.

Arminian

The teaching of the wise is a spring of life that turns people from deadly snares. The same image returns at 14:27 for the fear of the LORD.

Arminian readers note that wise teaching offers a real way of escape for those who heed it.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the parallel with 14:27 roots wise teaching in reverence for God.

Both traditions see sound teaching as a life-giving gift that protects from destructive paths.

Proverbs 13:15

15Good understanding wins favor, but the way of the unfaithful is hard.

Arminian

Good understanding wins favor, while the way of the unfaithful is hard. The Hebrew word behind “hard” is unusual, and the ancient Greek translation reads differently.

Arminian readers note that wisdom tends to build trust with others.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the hardness of the treacherous way is part of how God’s moral order works.

Both traditions read the verse as a general contrast, holding the exact sense of the second line with some caution.

Proverbs 13:16

16Every prudent man acts from knowledge, but a fool exposes folly.

Arminian

The prudent act from knowledge, while a fool parades his folly. What is inside eventually shows.

Arminian readers note the value of thinking before acting.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that conduct exposes the heart, whether wise or foolish.

Both traditions agree that prudence is knowledge put into practice, and that folly cannot stay hidden for long.

Proverbs 13:17

17A wicked messenger falls into trouble, but a trustworthy envoy gains healing.

Arminian

A wicked messenger falls into trouble, while a faithful envoy brings healing. Messengers carried great responsibility in the ancient world.

Arminian readers note the importance of faithfulness in any trust given to us.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that faithful service brings healing to others as well as to the servant.

Both traditions apply the verse to anyone entrusted with a message, including those who speak God’s word.

Proverbs 13:18

18Poverty and shame come to him who refuses discipline, but he who heeds correction shall be honored.

Arminian

Refusing discipline leads to poverty and shame, while heeding correction brings honor. Teachability again proves decisive.

Arminian readers note that accepting correction is a choice that shapes one’s future.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God uses correction as a means of grace (compare Hebrews 12:5–11).

Both traditions read poverty and honor here as typical outcomes, not as a guarantee in every case.

Proverbs 13:19

19Longing fulfilled is sweet to the soul, but fools detest turning from evil.

Arminian

Fulfilled longing is sweet, but fools hate turning from evil. The fool wants the sweetness without the change.

Arminian readers note that the fool’s refusal to turn is his own responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the fool’s love of evil shows how deep the problem lies.

Both traditions agree that true satisfaction cannot be found while clinging to evil.

Proverbs 13:20

20One who walks with wise men grows wise, but a companion of fools suffers harm.

Arminian

Walking with the wise makes one wise, while the companion of fools suffers harm. Companions shape character.

Arminian readers stress the responsibility to choose one’s company wisely.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God often uses the community of the wise as a means of growth.

Both traditions agree on the practical importance of good companionship (compare 1 Corinthians 15:33).

Proverbs 13:21

21Misfortune pursues sinners, but prosperity rewards the righteous.

Arminian

Misfortune pursues sinners, while prosperity rewards the righteous. This states a general pattern of retribution.

Arminian readers note that the pattern holds generally but not always, as Job and the Psalms show.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the pattern is general and finds its full outworking at God’s judgment.

Both traditions agree that this verse is not a promise of prosperity for every righteous person, and they read it in the same way.

Proverbs 13:22

22A good man leaves an inheritance to his children’s children, but the wealth of the sinner is stored for the righteous.

Arminian

A good man leaves an inheritance to his grandchildren, while the sinner’s wealth is stored up for the righteous. Faithfulness looks beyond one generation.

Arminian readers note the long-term blessing of a faithful life.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s providence can redirect wealth over time.

Both traditions read the verse as a general pattern rather than a guarantee for every estate.

Proverbs 13:23

23An abundance of food is in poor people’s fields, but injustice sweeps it away.

Arminian

Poor people’s fields may produce abundant food, but injustice sweeps it away. The Hebrew of this verse is difficult, and translations differ.

Arminian readers note that poverty is sometimes caused by injustice, not laziness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that the proverb recognizes structural wrongs.

Both traditions agree that Proverbs does not blame all poverty on the poor, and that injustice is condemned.

Proverbs 13:24

24One who spares the rod hates his son, but one who loves him is careful to discipline him.

Arminian

Sparing the rod is a failure of love, while loving parents discipline their children. The “rod” is the ancient image of correction.

Arminian readers note that discipline is an expression of love, not anger.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the verse reflects God’s own loving discipline of his children (Hebrews 12:6).

Both traditions agree on the principle of loving correction, and Christians in both differ on how the image of the rod applies to methods today.

Proverbs 13:25

25The righteous one eats to the satisfying of his soul, but the belly of the wicked goes hungry.

Arminian

The righteous eat to satisfaction, while the wicked go hungry. The chapter ends with a familiar contrast.

Arminian readers note that the verse encourages trust in God’s provision for those who walk in his ways.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that this is a general pattern under God’s providence, not a promise against hunger.

Both traditions read it alongside other Scriptures where the righteous suffer want.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
