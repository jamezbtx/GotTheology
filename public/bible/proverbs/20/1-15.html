Passage

Proverbs 20

Verses 1–15 · WEB

Chapter 20 gathers sayings on drink, royal authority, strife, laziness, hidden motives, integrity, and honest dealing. A central question in verse 9, who can claim a pure heart, stands among practical counsel. Both traditions read the chapter as wisdom for life before the LORD, sharing the conviction that no one is sinless and that God sees and weighs human conduct.

Proverbs 20:1

1Wine is a mocker and beer is a brawler. Whoever is led astray by them is not wise.

Arminian

Wine mocks and beer brawls, and those led astray by them are not wise. Arminian readers see warning about drink’s power to mislead. Christians in both traditions differ on whether this calls for moderation or abstinence.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the danger of drunkenness and loss of self-control. The proverb personifies alcohol as a deceiver. Views on drinking vary within both traditions, so they should not be assigned to one camp.

Proverbs 20:2

2The terror of a king is like the roaring of a lion. He who provokes him to anger forfeits his own life.

Arminian

A king’s anger is like a lion’s roar, and provoking him endangers life. Arminian readers see prudence in dealing with powerful authorities. The proverb repeats a theme from 19:12.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see respect for governing power, while other Scripture limits obedience where rulers command sin. Both traditions read the verse as practical wisdom.

Proverbs 20:3

3It is an honor for a man to keep aloof from strife, but every fool will be quarreling.

Arminian

Avoiding strife is honorable, while fools rush into quarrels. Arminian readers see peacemaking as a mark of wisdom. Restraint brings honor, not shame. Fools mistake contention for strength.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see quarrelsomeness as folly and peace as wisdom. Both traditions share this reading. Honor comes from restraint rather than from winning arguments, a theme found throughout Proverbs.

Proverbs 20:4

4The sluggard will not plow by reason of the winter; therefore he shall beg in harvest, and have nothing.

Arminian

The sluggard will not plow in season and has nothing at harvest. Arminian readers see consequences of neglecting timely work. Opportunities can be missed through laziness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s order of seedtime and harvest requiring diligence. Both traditions read the proverb as shared wisdom. The image comes from an agricultural setting where timing determined survival.

Proverbs 20:5

5Counsel in the heart of man is like deep water, but a man of understanding will draw it out.

Arminian

Purposes in the heart are like deep water, but the discerning can draw them out. Arminian readers see the value of wise counsel in understanding motives. Insight requires patience and skill.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the depths of the human heart and the gift of discernment. Both traditions share this reading. Wise counselors help others bring hidden purposes to light without manipulation.

Proverbs 20:6

6Many men claim to be men of unfailing love, but who can find a faithful man?

Arminian

Many claim faithful love, but a faithful person is hard to find. Arminian readers see the gap between profession and practice. True faithfulness is proven over time.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see human unreliability exposed, which highlights God’s perfect faithfulness. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. Faithfulness is rare among people but constant in God, a contrast both traditions affirm.

Proverbs 20:7

7A righteous man walks in integrity. Blessed are his children after him.

Arminian

The righteous walk in integrity, and their children are blessed. Arminian readers see a godly life influencing the next generation. Children benefit from a parent’s example and reputation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s covenant blessing often extending to families. The proverb states a general pattern rather than a guarantee for each child. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 20:8

8A king who sits on the throne of judgment scatters away all evil with his eyes.

Arminian

A king judging from his throne scatters evil with his eyes. Arminian readers see the ideal of just rulers who discern and restrain wrongdoing. Justice depends on attentive leadership.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the calling of rulers to uphold justice. Many Christians also see this ideal fulfilled in Christ’s perfect judgment. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 20:9

9Who can say, “I have made my heart pure. I am clean and without sin”?

Arminian

No one can claim to have made their heart pure and sinless. Arminian readers agree that all are sinners needing grace. Wesleyan Arminians teach a real heart-cleansing by grace, yet deny any claim to self-made purity or sinless perfection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the universal reach of sin and the impossibility of self-cleansing. Sanctification remains incomplete in this life. Both traditions agree that no one purifies their own heart; differences about entire sanctification exist within the Arminian family itself.

Proverbs 20:10

10Differing weights and differing measures, both of them alike are an abomination to the LORD.

Arminian

Differing weights and measures are an abomination to the LORD. Arminian readers see God’s concern for honesty in everyday business. Economic fraud is a spiritual matter.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see justice in commerce as part of God’s law, as in Deuteronomy 25:13–16. Both traditions share this reading. Honest measures protect the poor and vulnerable from exploitation in trade.

Proverbs 20:11

11Even a child makes himself known by his doings, whether his work is pure, and whether it is right.

Arminian

Even a child is known by his actions. Arminian readers see conduct revealing character from an early age. Behavior shows what a person truly values.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see deeds as evidence of the heart. Both traditions read the proverb as shared wisdom about character. Character is visible in practice even before maturity is reached.

Proverbs 20:12

12The hearing ear, and the seeing eye, the LORD has made even both of them.

Arminian

The LORD made both the hearing ear and the seeing eye. Arminian readers see human faculties as gifts from the Creator. Our abilities to perceive come from God and should be used for him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God as Creator of every capacity. Some readers also connect this with spiritual hearing and sight given by God. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 20:13

13Don’t love sleep, lest you come to poverty. Open your eyes, and you shall be satisfied with bread.

Arminian

Loving sleep leads to poverty, but opening one’s eyes brings bread. Arminian readers see diligence rewarded and idleness warned against. The proverb calls for wakeful effort.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see work as part of God’s good order. Both traditions share this reading. The contrast between sleep and open eyes recalls the call to diligence throughout the book.

Proverbs 20:14

14“It’s no good, it’s no good,” says the buyer; but when he is gone his way, then he boasts.

Arminian

A buyer complains about goods, then boasts after the purchase. Arminian readers see humor exposing dishonest bargaining. Even small deceptions in trade matter. The buyer’s two faces expose bad faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see wisdom observing everyday dishonesty. Both traditions read the proverb as shared counsel for truthful dealing. Wisdom pays attention to how ordinary transactions can reveal integrity or its absence.

Proverbs 20:15

15There is gold and abundance of rubies, but the lips of knowledge are a rare jewel.

Arminian

Gold and rubies abound, but knowledgeable lips are a rare jewel. Arminian readers see wise speech valued above material wealth. Good counsel is precious. Wisdom outweighs precious stones.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see wisdom as more valuable than riches. Both traditions share this reading. The rarity of wise speech makes it more precious than abundant gold, a theme repeated in 3:14–15 and 8:11.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
