Passage

Proverbs 20

Verses 16–30 · WEB

The chapter closes with sayings about pledges, deceit, counsel, gossip, honoring parents, patience instead of revenge, honest scales, and the king’s justice. Verse 24 raises the relation between the LORD’s direction and human understanding. Both traditions affirm God’s sovereign care and real human responsibility; they explain how the two relate in different ways.

Proverbs 20:16

16Take the garment of one who puts up collateral for a stranger; and hold him in pledge for a wayward woman.

Arminian

Take the garment of one who guarantees a stranger’s debt. Arminian readers see warning against rash financial pledges. Those who risk security for strangers should expect consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see prudence in financial commitments. The second line’s wording varies among translations. Both traditions read the verse as practical wisdom. Several proverbs warn against careless surety.

Proverbs 20:17

17Fraudulent food is sweet to a man, but afterwards his mouth is filled with gravel.

Arminian

Food gained by fraud tastes sweet but turns to gravel. Arminian readers see sin’s pleasures as short-lived. Deceit brings bitter results. The image contrasts initial pleasure with lasting harm.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the deceitfulness of sin. Both traditions share this reading. What is gained by fraud may taste sweet at first, but its end is bitter and painful.

Proverbs 20:18

18Plans are established by advice; by wise guidance you wage war!

Arminian

Plans succeed through advice, and war should be waged with guidance. Arminian readers see the value of consultation. Wise decisions rely on counsel. Even great undertakings need careful planning.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see humility in seeking advice. Both traditions read the verse as shared wisdom. Seeking counsel guards against pride and rash action, whether in war or in ordinary decisions.

Proverbs 20:19

19He who goes about as a tale-bearer reveals secrets; therefore don’t keep company with him who opens wide his lips.

Arminian

A gossip reveals secrets, so avoid those who talk too much. Arminian readers see protection of trust in community. Careless speech harms relationships. Trust is easily broken by loose talk.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see gossip as destructive. Both traditions share this reading. Those who reveal secrets to us will likely reveal ours, so wise people avoid such company.

Proverbs 20:20

20Whoever curses his father or his mother, his lamp shall be put out in blackness of darkness.

Arminian

Cursing father or mother leads to one’s lamp going out in darkness. Arminian readers see grave consequences for dishonoring parents. Life and light are tied to honoring them.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fifth commandment and the seriousness of contempt for parents. Both traditions share this reading. The image of a lamp extinguished in darkness signals loss of life and posterity.

Proverbs 20:21

21An inheritance quickly gained at the beginning won’t be blessed in the end.

Arminian

An inheritance gained hastily will not be blessed in the end. Arminian readers see warning against greed and impatience. Ill-gotten or rushed gain lacks lasting blessing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the folly of grasping. The story of the prodigal son illustrates a similar pattern. Both traditions share this reading. Patient, honest gain is preferred.

Proverbs 20:22

22Don’t say, “I will pay back evil.” Wait for the LORD, and he will save you.

Arminian

Do not repay evil; wait for the LORD to save. Arminian readers see trust in God’s justice replacing personal revenge. Paul echoes this in Romans 12:17–19.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see waiting on God as an act of faith. Vengeance belongs to the LORD. Both traditions share this reading. Waiting expresses trust in God’s justice.

Proverbs 20:23

23The LORD detests differing weights, and dishonest scales are not pleasing.

Arminian

The LORD detests differing weights and dishonest scales. Arminian readers see repetition emphasizing God’s concern for honesty. Fraud offends God. God takes notice of hidden dishonesty.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the seriousness of economic injustice. The repetition of verse 10 underlines it. Both traditions share this reading. Dishonest scales cheat neighbors in ways they may never detect, but God sees.

Proverbs 20:24

24A man’s steps are from the LORD; how then can man understand his way?

Arminian

A person’s steps are from the LORD, so how can anyone fully understand their way? Arminian readers affirm God’s guidance and the limits of human understanding. Many see God’s direction as wise governance and overruling that does not determine every free choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s meticulous providence directing human steps, with human choices real and responsible within that order. Both traditions call for humble trust rather than self-reliance. The verse is mostly shared ground, but it touches the classic difference over how providence relates to human freedom.

Key contrast Both affirm that God directs human steps and that humans remain responsible. Reformed readers commonly see meticulous providence and concurrence underlying every choice. Arminian readers commonly see providence that guides, governs, and overrules without determining free choices. Views on the details vary within each tradition.

Proverbs 20:25

25It is a snare to a man to make a rash dedication, then later to consider his vows.

Arminian

It is a trap to make a rash vow and only later consider it. Arminian readers see the need for thoughtful commitments before God. Promises should be made with care.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the seriousness of vows, as in Ecclesiastes 5:4–6. Both traditions share this reading. Rash dedications can trap a person between broken promises and unwise obligations.

Proverbs 20:26

26A wise king winnows out the wicked, and drives the threshing wheel over them.

Arminian

A wise king winnows out the wicked like grain on a threshing floor. Arminian readers see justice separating evil from good. Leaders bear responsibility to restrain wrongdoing.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the vocation of rulers to execute justice. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground. The threshing image pictures decisive separation of the wicked from the community.

Proverbs 20:27

27The spirit of man is the LORD’s lamp, searching all his innermost parts.

Arminian

The human spirit is the LORD’s lamp, searching inward parts. Arminian readers see conscience as a means through which God examines and guides. Inner life is open to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God searching the heart, sometimes through conscience. Interpreters discuss whether spirit here means breath, conscience, or the whole inner person. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 20:28

28Love and faithfulness keep the king safe. His throne is sustained by love.

Arminian

Love and faithfulness preserve the king, and his throne is upheld by love. Arminian readers see covenant virtues as the foundation of stable rule. Power rests best on loyal love.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see steadfast love as essential to just government. Many Christians also see these qualities perfected in Christ’s reign. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 20:29

29The glory of young men is their strength. The splendor of old men is their gray hair.

Arminian

Young men’s glory is strength, and old men’s splendor is gray hair. Arminian readers see each stage of life with its own honor. Communities need both energy and experience.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the value of every season of life. Both traditions share this reading. Strength and gray hair are both gifts, and a healthy community honors both together.

Proverbs 20:30

30Wounding blows cleanse away evil, and beatings purge the innermost parts.

Arminian

Wounding blows cleanse evil and beatings purge the inner parts. Arminian readers see painful correction as sometimes producing inward change. The saying reflects ancient discipline and should be read in that setting.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see discipline as a means of moral cleansing, a theme also in Hebrews 12:11. The verse does not authorize abuse. Both traditions read it in its context.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
