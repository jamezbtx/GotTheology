Passage

Proverbs 16

Verses 16–33 · WEB

The rest of Proverbs 16 returns to varied sayings on wisdom, pride, speech, violence, age, and self-control. It closes with the lot cast into the lap, every decision coming from the LORD. Arminian and Reformed readers share the teaching of these verses, giving providence different emphasis in the final saying without treating it as a point of real dispute.

Proverbs 16:16

16How much better it is to get wisdom than gold! Yes, to get understanding is to be chosen rather than silver.

Arminian

Getting wisdom is better than gold, and understanding than silver. Wisdom is worth more than wealth.

Arminian readers see an invitation to pursue wisdom as the greatest treasure. Effort to gain it is worthwhile.

Reformed

The proverb ranks wisdom above precious metals. Its value is beyond comparison.

Reformed readers see wisdom as God’s gift, to be sought diligently. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 16:17

17The highway of the upright is to depart from evil. He who keeps his way preserves his soul.

Arminian

The highway of the upright turns away from evil, and keeping one’s way preserves one’s life. The upright path avoids evil.

Arminian readers see guarding one’s way as a continuing responsibility. Faithfulness preserves life.

Reformed

The upright road departs from evil. Watchfulness over one’s path protects the soul.

Reformed readers see vigilance as part of a life shaped by grace. Both traditions agree on this teaching.

Proverbs 16:18

18Pride goes before destruction, and an arrogant spirit before a fall.

Arminian

Pride comes before destruction, and arrogance before a fall. Pride is dangerous.

Arminian readers see a warning to choose humility. Pride blinds people to their danger.

Reformed

The famous saying links pride with ruin. Arrogance precedes collapse.

Reformed readers see pride as a root of many sins. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 16:19

19It is better to be of a lowly spirit with the poor, than to divide the plunder with the proud.

Arminian

It is better to be humble with the poor than to share plunder with the proud. Humility outweighs ill-gotten gain.

Arminian readers see a choice of company and values. Joining the proud for gain is not worth it.

Reformed

The saying prefers lowliness to proud prosperity. God sides with the humble.

Reformed readers see humility as a mark of those who fear God. Both traditions agree.

Proverbs 16:20

20He who heeds the Word finds prosperity. Whoever trusts in the LORD is blessed.

Arminian

Whoever heeds the word prospers, and whoever trusts in the LORD is blessed. Listening and trust go together.

Arminian readers see blessing linked with a responsive heart. Heeding instruction is a real choice.

Reformed

Attention to instruction and trust in the LORD bring blessing. Both are part of wisdom.

Reformed readers see faith as the root of true blessing. Both traditions read this as a general truth, not a guarantee of material prosperity.

Proverbs 16:21

21The wise in heart shall be called prudent. Pleasantness of the lips promotes instruction.

Arminian

The wise in heart are called prudent, and pleasant speech promotes learning. Wisdom shows itself and persuades.

Arminian readers see gracious speech as helping others to learn.

Reformed

The wise are recognised as discerning. Sweetness of speech makes teaching effective.

Reformed readers see wise teaching marked by grace. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 16:22

22Understanding is a fountain of life to one who has it, but the punishment of fools is their folly.

Arminian

Understanding is a fountain of life to those who have it, but fools are punished by their folly. Folly carries its own penalty.

Arminian readers see consequences following from choices. Wisdom refreshes; folly harms.

Reformed

Understanding gives life, while folly is its own discipline. Fools reap what they sow.

Reformed readers see God’s moral order built into life. Both traditions agree.

Proverbs 16:23

23The heart of the wise instructs his mouth, and adds learning to his lips.

Arminian

The heart of the wise instructs the mouth and adds learning to the lips. Speech flows from the heart.

Arminian readers see wisdom shaping words from within.

Reformed

A wise heart teaches its own mouth. Inner wisdom produces persuasive speech.

Reformed readers see the heart as the source of what we say. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 16:24

24Pleasant words are a honeycomb, sweet to the soul, and health to the bones.

Arminian

Pleasant words are like honeycomb, sweet to the soul and healing to the bones. Kind words do good.

Arminian readers see gracious speech as a gift to others. Words can heal.

Reformed

The image of honey shows the sweetness and benefit of kind speech. Body and soul are refreshed.

Reformed readers see wise and gracious words as part of godly character. Both traditions agree.

Proverbs 16:25

25There is a way which seems right to a man, but in the end it leads to death.

Arminian

A way may seem right but lead to death. Appearances can deceive.

Arminian readers see the need for humility and God’s guidance. Self-confidence is not enough.

Reformed

The saying warns that what seems right to us can be deadly. Human judgment needs God’s wisdom.

Reformed readers see the limits of human reasoning apart from God. Both traditions share this reading, which repeats 14:12.

Proverbs 16:26

26The appetite of the laboring man labors for him, for his mouth urges him on.

Arminian

The labourer’s hunger works for him, since his appetite drives him on. Need motivates work.

Arminian readers see an honest observation about human motivation. Work meets real needs.

Reformed

Appetite is a spur to labour. God has made human need a reason to work.

Reformed readers see work as part of God’s ordering of life. Both traditions read this as observation.

Proverbs 16:27

27A worthless man devises mischief. His speech is like a scorching fire.

Arminian

A worthless person plots trouble, and his speech burns like fire. Evil plans spread harm.

Arminian readers see the destructive power of malicious speech.

Reformed

The worthless man devises evil, and his words scorch. Speech can be destructive.

Reformed readers see the tongue as capable of great harm. Both traditions agree.

Proverbs 16:28

28A perverse man stirs up strife. A whisperer separates close friends.

Arminian

A perverse person stirs up strife, and a gossip separates close friends. Gossip destroys friendships.

Arminian readers see responsibility for the effect of our words on relationships.

Reformed

Perversity and whispering divide people. Friendship is fragile.

Reformed readers see gossip as a serious sin against community. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 16:29

29A man of violence entices his neighbor, and leads him in a way that is not good.

Arminian

A violent person entices a neighbour and leads him astray. Violence draws others in.

Arminian readers see the danger of being influenced by violent people. Choosing companions matters.

Reformed

The violent man lures others into his path. Evil recruits.

Reformed readers see the corrupting influence of bad company. Both traditions agree.

Proverbs 16:30

30One who winks his eyes to plot perversities, one who compresses his lips, is bent on evil.

Arminian

Winking eyes and pressed lips signal plots of evil. Body language can reveal intent.

Arminian readers see a warning to watch for signs of deceit.

Reformed

The gestures describe someone scheming. Outward signs can betray inward plans.

Reformed readers see wisdom in discerning character. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 16:31

31Gray hair is a crown of glory. It is attained by a life of righteousness.

Arminian

Gray hair is a crown of glory, gained by a righteous life. Age with righteousness is honourable.

Arminian readers see the honour of a long, faithful life. It is a general truth, not a promise that the righteous all live long.

Reformed

Gray hair is a crown when it accompanies righteousness. Age is honoured when it is godly.

Reformed readers read it as a general pattern under God’s blessing. Both traditions agree it is not an unconditional promise.

Proverbs 16:32

32One who is slow to anger is better than the mighty; one who rules his spirit, than he who takes a city.

Arminian

One slow to anger is better than the mighty, and self-control is better than conquering a city. Inner mastery is greater than military victory.

Arminian readers see self-control as a disciplined choice, aided by grace.

Reformed

Ruling one’s spirit is greater than taking a city. Self-control is true strength.

Reformed readers see self-mastery as a fruit of the Spirit’s work. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 16:33

33The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD.

Arminian

The lot is cast, but every decision is from the LORD. Even apparent chance lies under God’s rule.

Arminian readers affirm God’s providence over all things, including what seems random. Many Arminians understand this as God’s governance that does not override genuine human freedom elsewhere.

Reformed

The lot falls into the lap, yet its outcome is from the LORD. Nothing is truly random to God.

Reformed readers see God’s detailed providence over all events. Both traditions affirm God’s rule here, differing mainly in how they describe its relation to human choices more generally.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
