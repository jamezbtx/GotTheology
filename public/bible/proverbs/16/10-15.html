Proverbs 16

Verses 10–15 · WEB

These verses form a cluster of royal sayings about the king’s judgments, honest measures, righteous rule, and the king’s anger and favour. They describe kingship as it should be under God, with justice and truth at its foundation. Both traditions read them as shared wisdom about rulers, and many Christians see the ideal ultimately fulfilled in the Messiah.

Proverbs 16:10

10Inspired judgments are on the lips of the king. He shall not betray his mouth.

Arminian

The king’s lips carry decisive judgments, and he should not be false in judgment. The royal sayings begin here.

Arminian readers see the ideal of a king who judges justly. It describes what a king should be, not that every king does so.

Reformed

The verse presents the king as one whose decisions should reflect God’s justice. The ideal is righteous rule.

Reformed readers see governing authority as answerable to God. Both traditions read this as an ideal of kingship, often seen fulfilled in the Messiah.

Proverbs 16:11

11Honest balances and scales are the LORD’s; all the weights in the bag are his work.

Arminian

Honest scales and weights belong to the LORD. Fair trade is his concern.

Arminian readers see God’s care for ordinary economic justice. Cheating in business offends him.

Reformed

God claims honest measures as his own. Justice in commerce reflects his character.

Reformed readers see all of life, including trade, under God’s law. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 16:12

12It is an abomination for kings to do wrong, for the throne is established by righteousness.

Arminian

It is detestable for kings to do wrong, because the throne is established by righteousness. Power must rest on justice.

Arminian readers see rulers held to God’s standard. Righteousness is the foundation of legitimate rule.

Reformed

Righteousness, not force, establishes a throne. Kings are accountable to God.

Reformed readers see government as ordained by God for justice. Both traditions agree on this point.

Proverbs 16:13

13Righteous lips are the delight of kings. They value one who speaks the truth.

Arminian

Kings delight in righteous lips and value truthful speakers. Good rulers want honest advisers.

Arminian readers see the importance of truth-telling in public life. Flattery is not wisdom.

Reformed

The ideal king values truth over flattery. Honest counsel strengthens rule.

Reformed readers see truthfulness as a virtue in every sphere. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 16:14

14The king’s wrath is a messenger of death, but a wise man will pacify it.

Arminian

A king’s anger can bring death, but a wise person can calm it. Wisdom navigates power.

Arminian readers see prudence in dealing with those in authority. Wisdom can turn away danger.

Reformed

The saying recognises the danger of royal anger and the value of wisdom. Tact can preserve life.

Reformed readers see wisdom as useful in every relationship, including with rulers. Both traditions read it as practical counsel.

Proverbs 16:15

15In the light of the king’s face is life. His favor is like a cloud of the spring rain.

Arminian

The king’s favour is life, like a spring rain cloud. Good rule brings blessing.

Arminian readers see the benefits of just and gracious leadership. The image is of refreshment.

Reformed

The king’s favour is compared to the late rain that ripens the harvest. Righteous rule is a blessing to the people.

Reformed readers often see this ideal pointing beyond any human king. Both traditions read the royal sayings as describing kingship as it should be.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
