Passage

Proverbs 16

Verses 1–9 · WEB

Proverbs 16:1–9 gathers sayings about human plans and the LORD’s direction, with the LORD named in nearly every verse. People plan, think, and commit their work, while God weighs motives, directs steps, and governs outcomes. Both traditions agree that God’s rule and human responsibility stand together, though they explain their relationship differently, especially in 16:4 and 16:9.

Proverbs 16:1

1The plans of the heart belong to man, but the answer of the tongue is from the LORD.

Arminian

People make plans in their hearts, but the answer of the tongue comes from the LORD. Human thinking and divine involvement are set side by side.

Arminian readers see real human planning that God guides and can overrule. The final word belongs to God, while people remain responsible for their plans.

Reformed

The saying places even the outcome of speech under the LORD. Human plans are real, yet God has the last word.

Reformed readers see God’s providence reaching into the details of human words and decisions. Both traditions agree that people plan and God governs.

Proverbs 16:2

2All the ways of a man are clean in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the motives.

Arminian

People think their ways are clean, but the LORD weighs the motives. Self-assessment is not the final judgment.

Arminian readers see a call to honest self-examination before God. He knows what we may hide from ourselves.

Reformed

God weighs the spirit, not just the outward act. Self-justification does not satisfy him.

Reformed readers see the heart’s capacity for self-deception and the need for God’s searching judgment. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 16:3

3Commit your deeds to the LORD, and your plans shall succeed.

Arminian

Commit your deeds to the LORD, and your plans will succeed. Trust is expressed by entrusting work to God.

Arminian readers see an invitation to active trust that shapes how plans are made. It is a general truth, not a promise that every plan will succeed as we imagine.

Reformed

The verb pictures rolling one’s works onto the LORD. Plans grounded in trust are established.

Reformed readers see dependence on God as the setting for wise planning. Both traditions read it as wisdom about trust, not a formula for success.

Proverbs 16:4

4The LORD has made everything for its own end— yes, even the wicked for the day of evil.

Arminian

The WEB reads: “The LORD has made everything for its own end—yes, even the wicked for the day of evil.” Many Arminian readers take this as God’s purpose in judgment: everything has its place in God’s order, and the wicked, who choose evil, will meet a fitting day of reckoning. God does not create anyone in order to make them wicked.

The verse affirms that wickedness does not escape God’s purposes. Interpreters differ over whether ‘its own end’ means its purpose or its answer and consequence.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly see here God’s comprehensive purpose, including the wicked and their judgment, within his decree. Many hold that God ordains the end of the wicked justly, without being the author of their sin.

Reformed interpreters differ on how strongly to press the verse; some read it mainly as judgment fitting the wicked, others connect it with a broader doctrine of decree. Both traditions agree that evil does not lie outside God’s rule.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm that evil does not lie outside God’s purposes and that God is not the author of sin. Arminian readers commonly read the verse as God’s fitting judgment on those who choose wickedness; Reformed readers commonly see God’s comprehensive decree including the wicked and their end. Interpreters in both traditions differ on how much doctrine the verse can bear.

Proverbs 16:5

5Everyone who is proud in heart is an abomination to the LORD; they shall certainly not be unpunished.

Arminian

Everyone proud in heart is detestable to the LORD and will not go unpunished. Pride is a serious offence.

Arminian readers see God’s opposition to arrogance and his justice. The warning invites humility.

Reformed

Pride is named an abomination, and its punishment is certain. God’s justice reaches inner attitudes.

Reformed readers see pride as a root sin that God opposes. Both traditions agree on this teaching.

Proverbs 16:6

6By mercy and truth iniquity is atoned for. By the fear of the LORD men depart from evil.

Arminian

By mercy and truth iniquity is atoned for, and by the fear of the LORD people turn from evil. Steadfast love and faithfulness are central.

Arminian readers do not read this as earning forgiveness by good works. Mercy and truth describe God’s covenant character, and they also shape a repentant life.

Reformed

The verse links atonement with covenant love and faithfulness. Interpreters differ on whether it refers mainly to God’s mercy or to human conduct, or both.

Reformed readers read it in light of God’s provision for sin rather than human merit. Both traditions agree it does not teach salvation by works.

Proverbs 16:7

7When a man’s ways please the LORD, he makes even his enemies to be at peace with him.

Arminian

When a person’s ways please the LORD, he makes even enemies be at peace with him. God can bring peace from hostility.

Arminian readers see God honouring faithful lives. It is a general truth; the righteous may still face opposition.

Reformed

God is able to turn enemies toward peace. His favour affects human relationships.

Reformed readers see God’s providence governing even the hearts of enemies. Both traditions read it as a pattern, not an unconditional promise.

Proverbs 16:8

8Better is a little with righteousness, than great revenues with injustice.

Arminian

A little with righteousness is better than great income with injustice. Integrity outweighs gain.

Arminian readers see a choice of values. Honest poverty is preferable to unjust wealth.

Reformed

The ‘better than’ saying ranks righteousness above wealth. How we gain matters more than how much.

Reformed readers see justice as essential to godly living. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 16:9

9A man’s heart plans his course, but the LORD directs his steps.

Arminian

A person plans a course, but the LORD directs the steps. Planning and providence work together.

Arminian readers see God guiding and sometimes redirecting human choices without removing their freedom. People truly plan; God sovereignly guides outcomes.

Reformed

Human planning is real, yet the LORD establishes the actual path. God’s direction is decisive.

Reformed readers see God’s providence ordering human steps, including through human choices. Both traditions affirm that people plan responsibly and that God directs.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm real human planning and real divine direction. Arminian readers commonly stress that God guides, permits, and sometimes overrules free human choices; Reformed readers commonly stress that God’s providence determines the path even through human choices. The difference is in how divine direction relates to human freedom, not whether God directs.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
