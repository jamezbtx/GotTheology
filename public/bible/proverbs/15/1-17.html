Passage

Proverbs 15

Verses 1–17 · WEB

Proverbs 15 sits within the Solomonic collection that begins at 10:1, where most sayings set two lines in contrast. These verses weigh gentle and harsh speech, wise and foolish hearers, and the LORD’s watchful presence over human life. They are general truths, not unconditional promises, and Arminian and Reformed readers share nearly all of their teaching.

Proverbs 15:1

1A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger.

Arminian

A gentle answer calms anger, while a harsh word inflames it. The saying contrasts two kinds of speech and their likely effects.

Arminian readers see a practical call to choose words that make peace. Gentleness is a habit formed by grace and practice, not weakness.

Reformed

The proverb describes how speech usually works in human relationships. It is a general truth, not a guarantee that every angry person will calm down.

Reformed readers see the tongue as an area where renewed character shows. Both traditions read this as shared wisdom about speech.

Proverbs 15:2

2The tongue of the wise commends knowledge, but the mouths of fools gush out folly.

Arminian

The wise use knowledge well, while fools pour out folly. Speech reveals what kind of person is speaking.

Arminian readers stress that wisdom grows through choosing to learn and to speak carefully. The contrast invites the reader to pursue wisdom.

Reformed

The saying sets wise and foolish speech side by side. What comes out of the mouth shows the heart.

Reformed readers see wisdom as God’s gift received through instruction. Both traditions agree that speech is a test of character.

Proverbs 15:3

3The LORD’s eyes are everywhere, keeping watch on the evil and the good.

Arminian

The LORD sees everywhere, watching both evil and good. Nothing escapes his notice.

Arminian readers find both warning and comfort: God sees wrongdoing, and he sees faithfulness that others overlook. His watchfulness invites honest living.

Reformed

God’s eyes are on every place and every person. The proverb affirms his complete knowledge.

Reformed readers see God’s omniscience as part of his providential rule. Both traditions share the confession that the LORD sees all.

Proverbs 15:4

4A gentle tongue is a tree of life, but deceit in it crushes the spirit.

Arminian

A gentle tongue gives life, while deceitful speech crushes the spirit. Words can heal or wound.

Arminian readers see responsibility for the effect our words have on others. The image of a tree of life ties speech to flourishing.

Reformed

The saying contrasts life-giving and spirit-breaking speech. Deceit damages people inwardly.

Reformed readers see in this the power of words, for good or ill, within God’s ordering of human life. Both traditions treat it as shared wisdom.

Proverbs 15:5

5A fool despises his father’s correction, but he who heeds reproof shows prudence.

Arminian

A fool rejects a father’s correction, but the one who heeds reproof is prudent. Openness to correction marks wisdom.

Arminian readers stress the choice to receive or refuse instruction. Teachability is a responsibility as well as a gift.

Reformed

The proverb places wisdom in humble reception of discipline. The family is a setting for learning.

Reformed readers see correction as a means God uses to form his people. Both traditions agree on the value of listening to reproof.

Proverbs 15:6

6In the house of the righteous is much treasure, but the income of the wicked brings trouble.

Arminian

The house of the righteous holds treasure, while the income of the wicked brings trouble. Righteousness and gain are not the same thing.

Arminian readers note that ‘treasure’ may include more than money. The saying is a general observation, not a promise that the righteous will always be wealthy.

Reformed

Wealth gained wickedly carries trouble with it. The righteous household is blessed in ways that last.

Reformed readers read the verse as a general pattern under God’s providence, not a guarantee of prosperity. Both traditions resist a simple prosperity reading.

Proverbs 15:7

7The lips of the wise spread knowledge; not so with the heart of fools.

Arminian

The wise spread knowledge; fools do not. Wisdom tends to be shared.

Arminian readers see wisdom as something to give away for the good of others. The fool’s heart has nothing worth spreading.

Reformed

The contrast is between lips that spread knowledge and a heart that cannot. Speech flows from the heart.

Reformed readers see a heart shaped by wisdom producing fruitful speech. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 15:8

8The sacrifice made by the wicked is an abomination to the LORD, but the prayer of the upright is his delight.

Arminian

The sacrifice of the wicked is detestable to the LORD, but the prayer of the upright delights him. Religious acts do not cover an unrepentant life.

Arminian readers see that God looks at the heart behind worship. Prayer from a sincere, upright person pleases him.

Reformed

Outward worship without righteousness is rejected. The prophets make the same point.

Reformed readers see that acceptable worship comes from a heart made right with God. Both traditions agree that ritual cannot replace a faithful life.

Proverbs 15:9

9The way of the wicked is an abomination to the LORD, but he loves him who follows after righteousness.

Arminian

The way of the wicked is detestable to the LORD, but he loves those who pursue righteousness. The verb ‘follows after’ suggests effort.

Arminian readers see God’s love expressed toward those who seek righteousness. The pursuit is real and responsive.

Reformed

God’s attitude to a person’s way of life is described plainly. He opposes wickedness and delights in the pursuit of righteousness.

Reformed readers see that pursuit as the fruit of God’s grace. Both traditions affirm that God loves righteousness and those who seek it.

Proverbs 15:10

10There is stern discipline for one who forsakes the way. Whoever hates reproof shall die.

Arminian

Stern discipline awaits the one who leaves the way, and the one who hates reproof will die. The warning is serious.

Arminian readers hear a call to stay on the path and accept correction. Refusing discipline leads to ruin.

Reformed

The saying ties rejection of reproof to death. In Proverbs, death often means a ruined life as well as physical death.

Reformed readers see God’s discipline as a means of keeping people on the path. Both traditions read this as a sober general warning.

Proverbs 15:11

11Sheol and Abaddon are before the LORD— how much more then the hearts of the children of men!

Arminian

Sheol and Abaddon, the realm of the dead, lie open before the LORD; how much more human hearts. The WEB footnote explains that Sheol is the place of the dead.

Arminian readers see that no hidden motive is concealed from God. This calls for sincerity before him.

Reformed

If even the depths of death are known to God, human hearts certainly are. The argument moves from greater to lesser.

Reformed readers see God’s exhaustive knowledge of every heart. Both traditions share this confession.

Proverbs 15:12

12A scoffer doesn’t love to be reproved; he will not go to the wise.

Arminian

A scoffer dislikes correction and avoids the wise. He shuts himself off from help.

Arminian readers see the scoffer’s choice to stay away from those who could help him. His isolation is self-chosen.

Reformed

The scoffer resists reproof and avoids wise company. Pride keeps him from learning.

Reformed readers see the hardness of a heart that will not be taught. Both traditions agree on the danger described.

Proverbs 15:13

13A glad heart makes a cheerful face, but an aching heart breaks the spirit.

Arminian

A glad heart shows in a cheerful face, but an aching heart breaks the spirit. Inner life and outward appearance are linked.

Arminian readers note the realism of the saying: sorrow is real and can crush. The verse does not blame those who grieve.

Reformed

The proverb observes how joy and sorrow affect a person. The heart shapes the whole person.

Reformed readers see wisdom that acknowledges human emotion honestly. Both traditions read it as observation, not a command to hide grief.

Proverbs 15:14

14The heart of one who has understanding seeks knowledge, but the mouths of fools feed on folly.

Arminian

The person with understanding seeks knowledge, while fools feed on folly. People are shaped by what they take in.

Arminian readers stress the active search for knowledge. Wisdom grows by seeking it.

Reformed

The contrast is between hunger for knowledge and appetite for folly. What we feed on forms us.

Reformed readers see a renewed heart seeking wisdom. Both traditions read this as shared ground.

Proverbs 15:15

15All the days of the afflicted are wretched, but one who has a cheerful heart enjoys a continual feast.

Arminian

The days of the afflicted are hard, but a cheerful heart has a continual feast. Outlook affects how life is experienced.

Arminian readers do not take this as dismissing real suffering. It encourages a joy that can sustain people even in hardship.

Reformed

The saying sets affliction beside a cheerful heart. Contentment can turn little into a feast.

Reformed readers see contentment as a gift God gives in every circumstance. Both traditions read the verse with compassion for the afflicted.

Proverbs 15:16

16Better is little, with the fear of the LORD, than great treasure with trouble.

Arminian

Little with the fear of the LORD is better than great wealth with trouble. Reverence for God outweighs riches.

Arminian readers see a call to value relationship with God above possessions. The ‘better than’ form invites a choice of priorities.

Reformed

The fear of the LORD is worth more than treasure. Wealth without God brings anxiety.

Reformed readers see the fear of the LORD as the root of true contentment. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 15:17

17Better is a dinner of herbs, where love is, than a fattened calf with hatred.

Arminian

A simple meal with love is better than a feast with hatred. Relationships matter more than luxury.

Arminian readers see a practical lesson for homes and communities. Love makes ordinary things rich.

Reformed

The proverb weighs love against plenty. A household’s character matters more than its table.

Reformed readers see love as the fruit of a life shaped by God. Both traditions agree on this shared wisdom.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
