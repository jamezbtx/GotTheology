Passage

Proverbs 15

Verses 18–33 · WEB

The second half of Proverbs 15 continues its contrasts: patience and anger, counsel and folly, the proud and the widow, prayer and distance from God. The chapter closes with the fear of the LORD as the teacher of wisdom and humility before honour. These sayings describe general patterns of life under God and are largely shared ground for both traditions.

Proverbs 15:18

18A wrathful man stirs up contention, but one who is slow to anger appeases strife.

Arminian

A hot-tempered person stirs up conflict, but one slow to anger calms it. Temperament affects community.

Arminian readers see patience as a choice and a discipline. The slow to anger make peace.

Reformed

The saying contrasts two temperaments and their effects. Patience reflects God’s own character, who is slow to anger.

Reformed readers see self-control as a fruit of grace. Both traditions read this as shared wisdom.

Proverbs 15:19

19The way of the sluggard is like a thorn patch, but the path of the upright is a highway.

Arminian

The lazy person’s way is like thorns, but the upright have a clear road. Laziness makes life harder.

Arminian readers see diligence as part of responsible living. The upright are not spared effort, but their way is open.

Reformed

The proverb links laziness with obstruction and uprightness with a straight path. Character shapes circumstances.

Reformed readers see diligence as part of faithful stewardship. Both traditions read it as general wisdom, not a promise of an easy life.

Proverbs 15:20

20A wise son makes a father glad, but a foolish man despises his mother.

Arminian

A wise son gladdens his father; a foolish man despises his mother. Wisdom and folly affect families.

Arminian readers see children’s choices as genuinely affecting their parents. Honouring parents is a responsibility.

Reformed

The saying links wisdom with family joy and folly with contempt. The fifth commandment stands in the background.

Reformed readers see the household as a setting for learning godliness. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 15:21

21Folly is joy to one who is void of wisdom, but a man of understanding keeps his way straight.

Arminian

Folly delights the one without wisdom, but the person of understanding walks straight. Pleasure in folly is a warning sign.

Arminian readers see that what we enjoy reveals what we are becoming. Choosing a straight path takes understanding.

Reformed

The fool enjoys foolishness, while the wise keeps his way straight. Delight and direction are linked.

Reformed readers see a renewed heart finding joy in what is right. Both traditions agree on the contrast.

Proverbs 15:22

22Where there is no counsel, plans fail; but in a multitude of counselors they are established.

Arminian

Plans fail without counsel but succeed with many advisers. Wisdom welcomes advice.

Arminian readers see humility in seeking counsel. Good decisions are often shared ones.

Reformed

The proverb commends taking advice before acting. Isolation in decision-making is risky.

Reformed readers see God using counsel as a means of guiding his people. Both traditions read this as practical shared wisdom.

Proverbs 15:23

23Joy comes to a man with the reply of his mouth. How good is a word at the right time!

Arminian

A fitting reply brings joy, and a word at the right time is good. Timing matters in speech.

Arminian readers see skill in speaking well as something to cultivate. A timely word can help another greatly.

Reformed

The saying praises speech that fits the moment. Words have their proper season.

Reformed readers see wise speech as a gift used for others’ good. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 15:24

24The path of life leads upward for the wise, to keep him from going downward to Sheol.

Arminian

The path of life leads the wise upward, away from Sheol below. The WEB footnote explains that Sheol is the place of the dead.

Arminian readers see wisdom as a way of life that leads away from death. The image is of a path chosen and followed.

Reformed

The upward path contrasts with the descent to Sheol. In Proverbs this mainly concerns life and death in this world, though later readers have seen wider hope.

Reformed readers see the way of wisdom as God’s gift that preserves life. Both traditions read the verse with care, without forcing a full doctrine of the afterlife onto it.

Proverbs 15:25

25The LORD will uproot the house of the proud, but he will keep the widow’s borders intact.

Arminian

The LORD uproots the house of the proud but protects the widow’s boundary. God defends the vulnerable.

Arminian readers see God’s justice acting on behalf of those with no power. Pride is opposed; the weak are protected.

Reformed

The saying sets God against the proud and for the widow. Boundary stones were a matter of livelihood.

Reformed readers see God’s providential care for the defenceless. Both traditions affirm this shared truth about God’s justice.

Proverbs 15:26

26The LORD detests the thoughts of the wicked, but the thoughts of the pure are pleasing.

Arminian

The LORD detests the thoughts of the wicked, but pure thoughts please him. God judges inner life as well as actions.

Arminian readers see a call to guard thoughts as well as deeds. Purity of heart pleases God.

Reformed

God sees and weighs thoughts. Inner life matters to him.

Reformed readers see the need for a renewed heart. Both traditions agree on this point.

Proverbs 15:27

27He who is greedy for gain troubles his own house, but he who hates bribes will live.

Arminian

Greed for gain brings trouble on one’s house, but hatred of bribes leads to life. Corruption harms families.

Arminian readers see integrity in money matters as a real choice with consequences. Refusing bribes protects life.

Reformed

The greedy damage their own households. Honesty in dealings brings life.

Reformed readers see justice in economic life as part of godliness. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 15:28

28The heart of the righteous weighs answers, but the mouth of the wicked gushes out evil.

Arminian

The righteous think before answering, but the wicked pour out evil. Careful speech marks righteousness.

Arminian readers see thoughtfulness in speech as a discipline. Weighing words before speaking is wise.

Reformed

The heart of the righteous considers its answers. The wicked speak without restraint.

Reformed readers see a heart shaped by God restraining the tongue. Both traditions agree on this contrast.

Proverbs 15:29

29The LORD is far from the wicked, but he hears the prayer of the righteous.

Arminian

The LORD is far from the wicked but hears the prayer of the righteous. Relationship with God affects prayer.

Arminian readers see God’s nearness to those who seek him in righteousness. Prayer is a real relationship.

Reformed

God’s distance from the wicked and nearness to the righteous are contrasted. He hears those who are right with him.

Reformed readers see this as describing relationship rather than physical distance, since God is everywhere present. Both traditions read it this way.

Proverbs 15:30

30The light of the eyes rejoices the heart. Good news gives health to the bones.

Arminian

Bright eyes gladden the heart, and good news refreshes the body. Joy affects the whole person.

Arminian readers see simple joys and good news as God’s gifts. The saying is observational.

Reformed

The proverb links seeing and hearing good things with health. Body and spirit are connected.

Reformed readers see God’s kindness in ordinary joys. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 15:31

31The ear that listens to reproof lives, and will be at home among the wise.

Arminian

The ear that listens to life-giving correction will dwell among the wise. Listening leads to belonging.

Arminian readers see the choice to accept reproof as a path into wise company.

Reformed

Receiving correction brings a person among the wise. Teachability is the doorway to wisdom.

Reformed readers see correction as a means of grace. Both traditions agree on the value of listening.

Proverbs 15:32

32He who refuses correction despises his own soul, but he who listens to reproof gets understanding.

Arminian

The one who refuses correction despises himself, but the one who listens gains understanding. Rejecting discipline is self-harm.

Arminian readers stress responsibility to receive correction. Refusal is a choice against one’s own good.

Reformed

The proverb shows that rejecting correction harms the soul. Listening brings understanding.

Reformed readers see humility before correction as a sign of grace. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 15:33

33The fear of the LORD teaches wisdom. Before honor is humility.

Arminian

The fear of the LORD instructs in wisdom, and humility comes before honour. Reverence and humility lead the way.

Arminian readers see wisdom beginning with a right relationship to God. Humility is the path to honour.

Reformed

The fear of the LORD is the school of wisdom. Honour follows humility, not pride.

Reformed readers see reverence for God at the root of all true wisdom. Both traditions share this conclusion, which echoes the theme of Proverbs 1:7.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
