Passage

Proverbs 26

Verses 1–12 · WEB

This cluster is devoted to the fool: honor does not fit him, correction must be firm, he should not be trusted with messages or proverbs, and he returns to his folly like a dog to its vomit. The paired sayings about answering a fool in verses 4 and 5 are complementary wisdom about different situations, and the cluster ends by saying there is more hope for a fool than for someone wise in his own eyes. Both traditions find broad common ground here.

Proverbs 26:1

1Like snow in summer, and as rain in harvest, so honor is not fitting for a fool.

Arminian

Honor fits a fool as badly as snow in summer or rain at harvest. Arminian readers note that the cluster opens by naming what is out of place.

Rain at harvest could ruin a crop and appears elsewhere as a sign of judgment (1 Sam 12:17–18).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the moral order of the world: honor belongs to wisdom and righteousness, not to folly.

Giving honor to fools confuses the community about what is good.

Proverbs 26:2

2Like a fluttering sparrow, like a darting swallow, so the undeserved curse doesn’t come to rest.

Arminian

An undeserved curse flutters away like a bird without landing. Arminian readers find comfort here: unjust curses have no power.

The innocent need not fear words spoken against them without cause.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereignty over blessing and cursing (Num 23:8; Deut 23:5).

No curse can harm those whom God protects unless he permits it (cf. 2 Sam 16:12).

Proverbs 26:3

3A whip is for the horse, a bridle for the donkey, and a rod for the back of fools!

Arminian

A whip for the horse, a bridle for the donkey, and a rod for fools. Arminian readers note that the fool does not respond to reason and must be restrained (cf. Ps 32:9).

The saying describes what folly brings on itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the rod as fitting discipline for the fool (Prov 10:13; 19:29).

The image is harsh, reflecting the seriousness of folly.

Proverbs 26:4

4Don’t answer a fool according to his folly, lest you also be like him.

Arminian

Don’t answer a fool according to his folly, or you will become like him. Arminian readers see a warning against being drawn into the fool’s way of arguing.

The next verse gives the complementary counsel. Wisdom judges which applies.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the danger of descending to the fool’s level.

This verse and the next stand together on purpose. Proverbs expects discernment about situations, not mechanical rule-following.

Proverbs 26:5

5Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own eyes.

Arminian

Answer a fool according to his folly, so he won’t be wise in his own eyes. Arminian readers see this as complementary to verse 4: sometimes folly must be exposed.

The pair shows that wisdom is practical judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the two sayings are complementary. Jesus sometimes answered with a question that exposed his questioners (Matt 21:23–27), and Paul spoke “as a fool” to expose boasting (2 Cor 11:16–17).

Shared ground: the wise must know when to stay silent and when to answer.

Proverbs 26:6

6One who sends a message by the hand of a fool is cutting off feet and drinking violence.

Arminian

Sending a message through a fool is like cutting off your own feet. Arminian readers note the self-harm of trusting the wrong person.

The contrast with the faithful messenger of 25:13 is clear.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see 25:13. Entrusting important tasks to fools brings trouble.

The saying calls for wise choices about whom to rely on.

Proverbs 26:7

7Like the legs of the lame that hang loose, so is a parable in the mouth of fools.

Arminian

A proverb in a fool’s mouth is as useless as a lame person’s legs. Arminian readers note that wisdom sayings require wisdom to use.

Knowing proverbs does not make one wise.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that truth misapplied is powerless. The fool can quote but not apply.

The verse warns against using Scripture without understanding.

Proverbs 26:8

8As one who binds a stone in a sling, so is he who gives honor to a fool.

Arminian

Giving honor to a fool is like binding a stone in a sling. Arminian readers note the absurdity: the stone is meant to fly, and honor given to a fool is wasted or dangerous.

The saying repeats verse 1’s theme.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the folly of honoring folly. It defeats the purpose of honor.

The image may suggest that such honor will come back to harm someone.

Proverbs 26:9

9Like a thorn bush that goes into the hand of a drunkard, so is a parable in the mouth of fools.

Arminian

A proverb in a fool’s mouth is like a thorn bush in a drunkard’s hand. Arminian readers note that this escalates verse 7: misused wisdom can actually harm.

The fool may wound himself or others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the danger of truth wielded carelessly. It can do damage.

The repetition of verse 7 stresses the point.

Proverbs 26:10

10As an archer who wounds all, so is he who hires a fool or he who hires those who pass by.

Arminian

Hiring a fool or random passers-by is like an archer wounding everyone. Arminian readers note that the Hebrew of this verse is notoriously difficult, and translations differ widely.

The general sense in the WEB is that careless hiring causes widespread harm.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise acknowledge the difficulty of the verse.

Whatever the exact sense, it fits the cluster’s warning against entrusting tasks to fools.

Proverbs 26:11

11As a dog that returns to his vomit, so is a fool who repeats his folly.

Arminian

A fool returning to his folly is like a dog returning to its vomit. Arminian readers note that 2 Peter 2:22 applies this proverb to those who turn back after knowing the way of righteousness, which Arminian readers take as a warning that real believers can fall away.

In Proverbs itself, the saying describes the fool’s habitual folly.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note 2 Peter 2:22, but generally read those described there as never truly regenerate, whatever they once professed.

In Proverbs, the image shows how folly is repeated by habit and nature. Both traditions agree it is a vivid warning against returning to sin.

Proverbs 26:12

12Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him.

Arminian

There is more hope for a fool than for one wise in his own eyes. Arminian readers see pride as more dangerous than ignorance because it blocks teachability (cf. 26:5, 16).

The cluster ends by turning the warning on the reader.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with warnings against self-righteousness (Isa 5:21; Luke 18:9–14; 1 Cor 3:18).

The proud cannot receive grace they think they do not need (cf. Prov 3:7).

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
