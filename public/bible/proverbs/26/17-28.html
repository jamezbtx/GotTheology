Passage

Proverbs 26

Verses 17–28 · WEB

The final cluster deals with meddlers, deceivers, gossips, and quarrelers: meddling in others’ fights, deceiving and then claiming to joke, feeding quarrels with gossip, and hiding malice behind charming speech. It ends by saying hidden wickedness will be exposed and that those who dig pits fall into them. Both traditions find shared ground in this teaching about honest speech and peace.

Proverbs 26:17

17Like one who grabs a dog’s ears is one who passes by and meddles in a quarrel not his own.

Arminian

Meddling in someone else’s quarrel is like grabbing a dog’s ears. Arminian readers note the danger of interfering where one has no place (cf. 1 Pet 4:15).

This is different from peacemaking, which Jesus blesses (Matt 5:9).

Reformed

Reformed readers also distinguish meddling from peacemaking. Busybodies are warned against (2 Thess 3:11).

Wisdom knows when involvement helps and when it inflames.

Proverbs 26:18

18Like a madman who shoots torches, arrows, and death,

Arminian

A madman shooting torches and arrows is the first half of a comparison completed in verse 19. Arminian readers note the vivid picture of reckless harm.

The damage is real regardless of intent.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the image of indiscriminate harm.

The comparison prepares for the deceiver’s excuse in verse 19.

Proverbs 26:19

19is the man who deceives his neighbor and says, “Am I not joking?”

Arminian

Deceiving a neighbor and then asking “Am I not joking?” does as much damage as the madman’s arrows. Arminian readers stress responsibility for the effects of words.

The excuse does not undo the harm.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with warnings against careless and deceptive speech (Eph 4:25; 5:4).

Truthfulness is part of loving the neighbor.

Proverbs 26:20

20For lack of wood a fire goes out. Without gossip, a quarrel dies down.

Arminian

Without wood a fire goes out; without gossip a quarrel dies. Arminian readers see practical wisdom: refusing to pass on gossip helps end conflict.

Each person can choose not to add fuel.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the tongue as a fire (James 3:5–6; Prov 16:28).

Silence can be an act of peacemaking.

Proverbs 26:21

21As coals are to hot embers, and wood to fire, so is a contentious man to kindling strife.

Arminian

A contentious person kindles strife like coals and wood. Arminian readers note that some people habitually stir conflict (cf. 15:18).

Wisdom avoids feeding such fires.

Reformed

Reformed readers see contentiousness as a work of the flesh opposed to peace (cf. Gal 5:20).

The image continues the fire theme of verse 20.

Proverbs 26:22

22The words of a whisperer are as dainty morsels, they go down into the innermost parts.

Arminian

Gossip tastes like dainty morsels and goes deep. Arminian readers note the attraction of gossip and its lasting effect (cf. 18:8).

What is easy to hear is hard to forget.

Reformed

Reformed readers also note 18:8. Gossip appeals to the corrupt appetite of the heart.

Its damage reaches the innermost parts.

Proverbs 26:23

23Like silver dross on an earthen vessel are the lips of a fervent one with an evil heart.

Arminian

Fervent lips with an evil heart are like silver dross coating clay. Arminian readers see the deception of a shiny surface over cheap material.

Some scholars suggest a different reading of “silver dross” as a kind of glaze; the point of the image is the same.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with Jesus’ rebuke of outward show hiding inward corruption (Matt 23:27).

Warm words can mask an evil heart.

Proverbs 26:24

24A malicious man disguises himself with his lips, but he harbors evil in his heart.

Arminian

A malicious person disguises himself with his lips but harbors evil inside. Arminian readers stress discernment about smooth speech (cf. Ps 28:3).

Words and heart can be at odds.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the deceitfulness of the heart (Jer 17:9) and the hypocrisy of speaking peace while planning harm (Ps 55:21).

God sees what words conceal.

Proverbs 26:25

25When his speech is charming, don’t believe him, for there are seven abominations in his heart.

Arminian

Don’t believe charming speech when the heart holds seven abominations. Arminian readers note the call to wise caution, not cynicism toward all kindness.

Seven suggests the fullness of the evil hidden.

Reformed

Reformed readers recall the list of things the LORD hates in Proverbs 6:16–19.

Discernment is needed because the wicked can sound pleasant (Jer 9:8).

Proverbs 26:26

26His malice may be concealed by deception, but his wickedness will be exposed in the assembly.

Arminian

Malice concealed by deception will be exposed in the assembly. Arminian readers see confidence that deception does not last.

Truth comes out in public eventually.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with Jesus’ teaching that hidden things will be revealed (Luke 12:2–3; 1 Cor 4:5).

God’s justice brings concealed sin to light.

Proverbs 26:27

27Whoever digs a pit shall fall into it. Whoever rolls a stone, it will come back on him.

Arminian

Whoever digs a pit falls into it, and a rolled stone comes back on the one who rolls it. Arminian readers see the moral logic of consequences (cf. Ps 7:15–16; Eccl 10:8).

Evil plans turn back on their makers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence ordering just recompense, as when Haman was hanged on the gallows he had prepared for Mordecai (Esther 7:10).

The wicked are caught in their own schemes.

Proverbs 26:28

28A lying tongue hates those it hurts; and a flattering mouth works ruin.

Arminian

A lying tongue hates those it hurts, and flattery works ruin. Arminian readers note that lies reveal hostility, even when dressed as friendliness (cf. 29:5).

The chapter ends by exposing the hatred behind deceit.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with warnings against flattering lips (Ps 12:2–3).

True love speaks truth; flattery destroys.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
