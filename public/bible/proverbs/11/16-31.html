Passage

Proverbs 11

Verses 16–31 · WEB

The Solomonic sentences (10:1–22:16) continue with sayings on generosity and its fruit: mercy, sowing righteousness, open-handed giving, and the folly of trusting in riches. The LORD’s delight in the blameless and the certainty of judgment on the wicked frame the unit, which closes with the righteous as a tree of life. Both traditions read these as general patterns of God’s moral order, not guarantees for every case, and both see their final outcome in God’s judgment.

Proverbs 11:16

16A gracious woman obtains honor, but violent men obtain riches.

Arminian

A gracious woman gains honor, while violent men gain riches. The contrast compares kinds of gain: honor won by grace versus wealth seized by force.

Arminian readers see the lasting value of gracious character over ill-gotten gain.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that honor gained through graciousness differs in kind from riches seized by violence.

Both agree the proverb commends graciousness and implicitly questions wealth obtained by ruthless means, without implying that violence is admirable because it pays.

Proverbs 11:17

17The merciful man does good to his own soul, but he who is cruel troubles his own flesh.

Arminian

The merciful benefit themselves, but the cruel bring trouble on their own bodies. Kindness has an inward reward.

Arminian readers stress that mercy shapes the one who shows it, and cruelty corrodes the cruel. Kindness is not a loss to the giver but a gain.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the moral order God built into creation: mercy blesses the merciful, and cruelty rebounds (cf. Matthew 5:7).

Shared ground: how we treat others affects our own souls. Mercy and cruelty both leave their mark on the one who practices them.

Proverbs 11:18

18Wicked people earn deceitful wages, but one who sows righteousness reaps a sure reward.

Arminian

The wicked earn deceptive wages, but the one who sows righteousness reaps a sure reward. Sowing and reaping is a frequent biblical image.

Arminian readers emphasize that choices bear fruit, and that wicked gain is ultimately illusory.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness in rewarding righteousness, while acknowledging that the reward is grace, not merit.

Both agree that wickedness pays in false coin and righteousness yields lasting fruit (cf. Galatians 6:7–8). What wickedness promises, it cannot deliver.

Proverbs 11:19

19He who is truly righteous gets life. He who pursues evil gets death.

Arminian

True righteousness leads to life; pursuing evil leads to death. Proverbs presents two ways with two destinations.

Arminian readers see the real choice between life and death set before every person, echoing Deuteronomy 30:19. The verse presses the reader to consider where each path ends.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same two ways and stress that the righteous walk in life by God’s grace, while those who pursue evil reap death.

Shared ground: life and death are bound to the paths people walk, and Proverbs describes these as patterns under God’s justice.

Proverbs 11:20

20Those who are perverse in heart are an abomination to the LORD, but those whose ways are blameless are his delight.

Arminian

The perverse in heart are an abomination to the LORD; the blameless in their ways are his delight. God looks at the heart and the life together.

Arminian readers stress that God’s delight in the blameless reflects his genuine pleasure in faithful living.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s holiness that hates perversity and loves integrity, with blamelessness as the fruit of his work in his people.

Both agree that God is not neutral about character: he is grieved by perversity and delights in integrity.

Proverbs 11:21

21Most certainly, the evil man will not be unpunished, but the offspring of the righteous will be delivered.

Arminian

The evil will certainly be punished, but the offspring of the righteous will be delivered. The WEB footnote gives “seed” as the literal sense of “offspring.”

Arminian readers read the generational blessing as a general pattern, not a guarantee overriding each person’s response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s covenant faithfulness extending through generations, while recognizing that each person must still believe.

Shared ground: judgment on evil is sure, and the righteous often leave a blessing to their children (cf. 20:7), though Proverbs states general patterns.

Proverbs 11:22

22Like a gold ring in a pig’s snout, is a beautiful woman who lacks discretion.

Arminian

A beautiful woman without discretion is like a gold ring in a pig’s snout. The image is deliberately jarring: beauty is wasted without good judgment.

Arminian readers see a call to value character over appearance, applicable to men and women alike.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priority of inner wisdom over outward beauty, a theme echoed in 31:30.

Both agree the proverb is about the worth of discretion, not a dismissal of beauty as such. Discretion gives beauty its proper setting.

Proverbs 11:23

23The desire of the righteous is only good. The expectation of the wicked is wrath.

Arminian

The desire of the righteous ends only in good; the expectation of the wicked ends in wrath. Desires reveal and determine destinies.

Arminian readers note that what a person longs for shapes what they become, and that the wicked’s hopes lead to judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see renewed desires as evidence of grace, and the wicked’s expectation as exposed to God’s wrath.

Shared ground: what we desire matters, and righteous desires lead to good outcomes under God. The heart’s longings point toward its end.

Proverbs 11:24

24There is one who scatters, and increases yet more. There is one who withholds more than is appropriate, but gains poverty.

Arminian

Some give freely and gain more; others hoard and end in poverty. The paradox of generosity is a recurring theme.

Arminian readers see generosity as a free response to God’s goodness, which God delights to bless.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence rewarding open-handed giving, though not as a mechanical formula.

Both agree that stinginess impoverishes and generosity enriches, often in ways not measured by money alone (cf. 2 Corinthians 9:6). The open hand reflects trust in God’s provision.

Proverbs 11:25

25The liberal soul shall be made fat. He who waters shall be watered also himself.

Arminian

The generous will prosper, and the one who refreshes others will be refreshed. Generosity circles back.

Arminian readers emphasize that giving is a choice that opens a person to God’s blessing. Those who water others are themselves refreshed, often in unexpected ways.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to those who share, while insisting that blessing is grace, not payment.

Shared ground: generosity is commended, and its fruit includes both material and relational blessing, though Proverbs does not promise a fixed return.

Proverbs 11:26

26People curse someone who withholds grain, but blessing will be on the head of him who sells it.

Arminian

People curse the one who withholds grain to drive up prices, but bless the one who sells it. Hoarding in time of need is condemned.

Arminian readers see economic justice as part of loving the neighbor.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s concern for the poor reflected in the community’s judgment on profiteering.

Both agree that business decisions have moral weight, and that exploiting scarcity for gain is wrong, while honest trade is a blessing to the community.

Proverbs 11:27

27He who diligently seeks good seeks favor, but he who searches after evil, it shall come to him.

Arminian

Whoever seeks good finds favor, but trouble comes to the one who searches for evil. People tend to find what they pursue.

Arminian readers emphasize human responsibility for what one seeks, and the corresponding outcomes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s moral order in which pursuing evil brings evil, and seeking good is met with favor.

Shared ground: the direction of one’s pursuits shapes one’s life, and evil sought eventually arrives at the seeker’s door.

Proverbs 11:28

28He who trusts in his riches will fall, but the righteous shall flourish as the green leaf.

Arminian

Whoever trusts in riches will fall, but the righteous flourish like green leaves. The image echoes the tree planted by streams (Psalm 1:3).

Arminian readers stress that misplaced trust is a choice with consequences. Riches promise security they cannot give.

Reformed

Reformed readers see trust in riches as idolatry, and flourishing as rooted in God (cf. Jeremiah 17:7–8).

Both agree that wealth is an unreliable foundation and that righteousness rooted in God produces lasting vitality. Leaves stay green when roots draw from a lasting source.

Proverbs 11:29

29He who troubles his own house shall inherit the wind. The foolish shall be servant to the wise of heart.

Arminian

Whoever troubles their own household inherits the wind, and the fool ends up serving the wise. Family conflict leaves nothing of value.

Arminian readers see responsibility for the health of one’s household and the folly of destructive behavior.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the futility of disordered family life, and God’s ordering of society so that wisdom ultimately leads.

Shared ground: those who sow strife at home reap emptiness, and folly ends in dependence on the wise.

Proverbs 11:30

30The fruit of the righteous is a tree of life. He who is wise wins souls.

Arminian

The righteous bear fruit that is a tree of life, and the wise win souls. The Hebrew of the second line, often rendered as taking or gaining lives, is debated; it likely refers to drawing people to wisdom.

Arminian readers often apply it to evangelism, appealing to others to choose life.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same debated phrasing and the tree-of-life image recalling Eden (Genesis 2:9). Many apply the verse to evangelism, with conversion ultimately God’s work.

Shared ground: the wise draw others toward life, and the proverb’s own setting is wisdom’s influence broadly, which evangelistic application builds on.

Proverbs 11:31

31Behold, the righteous shall be repaid on the earth, how much more the wicked and the sinner!

Arminian

If the righteous are repaid on earth, how much more the wicked and the sinner. The argument moves from lesser to greater.

Arminian readers see God’s even-handed justice: even the righteous face discipline, so the wicked cannot expect to escape.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that 1 Peter 4:18 echoes this verse in the Greek tradition, applying it to judgment beginning with God’s household.

Shared ground: God’s justice operates in this life, and if the righteous are disciplined, the wicked will certainly face judgment.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
