Passage

Proverbs 11

Verses 1–15 · WEB

These sayings belong to the Solomonic collection of sentence proverbs (10:1–22:16), most built on antithetic parallelism, where the second line turns the first around with “but.” This unit gathers around integrity in public life: honest weights, humility, the city’s welfare under righteous or wicked citizens, guarded speech, counsel, and caution about guaranteeing debts. Both traditions read these proverbs as shared wisdom for life under God, observations of how his world usually works rather than unconditional promises.

Proverbs 11:1

1A false balance is an abomination to the LORD, but accurate weights are his delight.

Arminian

Dishonest scales cheat the neighbor, and the LORD calls them an abomination; honest weights delight him. Business ethics are a matter of worship.

Arminian readers stress that honesty is a real moral choice God expects of everyone, with God’s grace enabling it (cf. Leviticus 19:35–36).

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s law reaching into the marketplace: the LORD who owns all things cares about fair measure (Deuteronomy 25:13–16).

Shared ground: honest dealing pleases God, and fraud is not a small matter but an offense against him.

Proverbs 11:2

2When pride comes, then comes shame, but with humility comes wisdom.

Arminian

Pride leads to disgrace, but humility brings wisdom. The proverb ties character to outcome: the proud overreach and fall.

Arminian readers see humility as a disposition to cultivate in response to grace, and pride as a choice that invites shame (cf. 16:18).

Reformed

Reformed readers see pride as the root sin that sets the self against God, and humility as fruit of grace that opens the way to wisdom (cf. James 4:6).

Both agree: humility and wisdom belong together.

Proverbs 11:3

3The integrity of the upright shall guide them, but the perverseness of the treacherous shall destroy them.

Arminian

Integrity guides the upright like a reliable path, while crookedness destroys the treacherous from within. Character shapes direction.

Arminian readers note that the upright keep choosing integrity, and that perverseness is self-destructive, not imposed from outside.

Reformed

Reformed readers see integrity as a gift that God uses to guide his people, and treachery as carrying its own ruin under God’s just order.

Shared ground: integrity is a safer guide than cleverness, and deceit eventually undoes the deceiver.

Proverbs 11:4

4Riches don’t profit in the day of wrath, but righteousness delivers from death.

Arminian

Wealth cannot buy rescue in the day of wrath; righteousness delivers from death. The day of wrath may be any time of calamity, though readers also hear final judgment.

Arminian readers stress that what counts before God is righteousness, not possessions.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that riches cannot ransom a life, and read the righteousness that finally delivers in light of the righteousness God gives in Christ (cf. Romans 3:21–22).

Both agree that money offers no protection when God judges, and that righteousness matters more.

Proverbs 11:5

5The righteousness of the blameless will direct his way, but the wicked shall fall by his own wickedness.

Arminian

The righteousness of the blameless keeps their path straight, while the wicked trip over their own wickedness. Sin carries its own snare.

Arminian readers see a consistent moral order in which choices bring their own consequences, and the blameless are guided by the way they live.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence working through moral cause and effect: righteousness directs, and wickedness becomes its own downfall.

Shared ground: the wicked are not ruined by bad luck but by the evil they pursue.

Proverbs 11:6

6The righteousness of the upright shall deliver them, but the unfaithful will be trapped by evil desires.

Arminian

Righteousness rescues the upright, while the unfaithful are caught by their own cravings. Desire becomes a trap.

Arminian readers stress the role of desire and choice: the unfaithful are ensnared by what they want, and the upright are preserved as they walk in righteousness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the bondage of sinful desire, which entangles those who give themselves to it, and God’s deliverance of the upright.

Both agree that sin enslaves through desire, and that righteousness, not cleverness, keeps a person safe.

Proverbs 11:7

7When a wicked man dies, hope perishes, and expectation of power comes to nothing.

Arminian

When the wicked die, their hope dies with them; expectation of power comes to nothing. Ambition built without God has no future.

Arminian readers see the tragedy of a life invested in the wrong hopes, and a warning while there is still time to turn.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the emptiness of hope placed anywhere but in God; death exposes it.

Shared ground: hopes built on power or self come to nothing at death, while hope in God endures (cf. 10:28).

Proverbs 11:8

8A righteous person is delivered out of trouble, and the wicked takes his place.

Arminian

The righteous are rescued from trouble, and the wicked end up in their place. The narrative of Haman (Esther 7:10) is one biblical example of this reversal.

Arminian readers read this as a general pattern of God’s justice, not a promise that the righteous never suffer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providential reversals, where the trap meant for the righteous falls on the wicked.

Both agree that this describes how God’s justice often works, while the rest of Scripture shows that full vindication may wait for the end.

Proverbs 11:9

9With his mouth the godless man destroys his neighbor, but the righteous will be delivered through knowledge.

Arminian

The godless destroy neighbors with their words, but the righteous escape through knowledge. Speech can be a weapon.

Arminian readers note the responsibility to guard the tongue and to seek knowledge that enables discernment against slander and deceit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the destructive power of a godless mouth and the protective gift of knowledge that God grants his people.

Shared ground: words have real power to harm, and wisdom equips the righteous to see through them.

Proverbs 11:10

10When it goes well with the righteous, the city rejoices. When the wicked perish, there is shouting.

Arminian

A city celebrates when the righteous prosper and shouts for joy when the wicked perish. Public life is affected by the character of its citizens.

Arminian readers see the social dimension of righteousness: good people benefit their communities.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s common grace: even a mixed community recognizes the value of righteousness and the relief of justice.

Both agree that righteousness is not merely private, and that communities flourish when justice prevails.

Proverbs 11:11

11By the blessing of the upright, the city is exalted, but it is overthrown by the mouth of the wicked.

Arminian

The blessing of the upright lifts a city, but the words of the wicked tear it down. Righteous people are a public good.

Arminian readers emphasize the influence of the upright, whose blessing and conduct shape civic life for good.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the upright as salt and light in society, and the wicked mouth as a corrupting force.

Shared ground: a city’s welfare depends partly on the speech and character of its people, and believers are called to seek its good (cf. Jeremiah 29:7).

Proverbs 11:12

12One who despises his neighbor is void of wisdom, but a man of understanding holds his peace.

Arminian

Despising a neighbor reveals a lack of wisdom; the person of understanding keeps quiet. Contempt is foolish, and restraint is wise.

Arminian readers see a call to love the neighbor in speech and attitude, a responsibility God’s grace enables.

Reformed

Reformed readers see contempt as a failure to honor the image of God in others, and silence as wisdom’s restraint.

Both agree: holding one’s tongue is often the wiser path than voicing scorn. Words of scorn rarely heal anything.

Proverbs 11:13

13One who brings gossip betrays a confidence, but one who is of a trustworthy spirit is one who keeps a secret.

Arminian

A gossip betrays confidences; a trustworthy person keeps a secret. Faithfulness in speech protects relationships.

Arminian readers emphasize the responsibility to be trustworthy, a character trait formed by repeated choices. Gossip may feel harmless, but it breaks trust that is hard to rebuild, and the trustworthy choose loyalty instead.

Reformed

Reformed readers see gossip as a breach of love and faithfulness, and trustworthiness as a fruit of a renewed spirit.

Shared ground: keeping confidences is a basic form of love and faithfulness toward a neighbor.

Proverbs 11:14

14Where there is no wise guidance, the nation falls, but in the multitude of counselors there is victory.

Arminian

Without guidance a nation falls; with many counselors there is victory. Leaders need wise advice.

Arminian readers note the value of shared wisdom and accountability, and the danger of leaders who listen to no one.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the value of counsel as part of God’s ordinary means of guidance, against proud self-reliance.

Both agree that wisdom often comes through many voices, and that seeking counsel is a mark of humility (cf. 15:22).

Proverbs 11:15

15He who is collateral for a stranger will suffer for it, but he who refuses pledges of collateral is secure.

Arminian

Guaranteeing a stranger’s debt brings trouble; refusing such pledges brings security. Proverbs repeatedly warns against rash surety (cf. 6:1–5).

Arminian readers see practical prudence: generosity is good, but rash financial commitments harm the giver and family.

Reformed

Reformed readers see stewardship: God’s people are to manage resources wisely and avoid reckless obligations.

Shared ground: this is practical wisdom about debt and risk, not a prohibition on helping others in need. Prudence and generosity are meant to work together.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
