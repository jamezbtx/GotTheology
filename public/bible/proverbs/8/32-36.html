Proverbs 8

Verses 32–36 · WEB

Wisdom closes with an appeal. Her sons should listen, for those who keep her ways are blessed. Blessed is the one who watches daily at her gates. Whoever finds her finds life and obtains favor from the LORD, while whoever sins against her harms himself, and all who hate her love death.

Proverbs 8:32

32“Now therefore, my sons, listen to me, for blessed are those who keep my ways.

Arminian

Now therefore, my sons, listen; blessed are those who keep my ways. Arminian readers see wisdom’s final appeal to choose.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the call to listen as the means through which God blesses his people. The blessing is pronounced on those who keep wisdom’s ways, echoing the opening of Psalm 1.

Proverbs 8:33

33Hear instruction, and be wise. Don’t refuse it.

Arminian

Hear instruction and be wise; don’t refuse it. Arminian readers note the real possibility of refusal. Wisdom’s invitation is not forced on anyone, which is why the warning not to refuse is needed.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see the warning as real and as a means God uses to keep his people. Refusing instruction is a real sin with real consequences, as 1:24–31 warns.

Proverbs 8:34

34Blessed is the man who hears me, watching daily at my gates, waiting at my door posts.

Arminian

Blessed is the one who watches daily at my gates. Arminian readers see persistent, daily seeking. The picture is of a servant or suitor waiting eagerly at the door, ready to hear.

Reformed

Reformed readers see daily waiting on God through his word as the posture of the blessed. Watching daily at the gates pictures steady devotion rather than occasional interest.

Proverbs 8:35

35For whoever finds me finds life, and will obtain favor from the LORD.

Arminian

Whoever finds me finds life and obtains favor from the LORD. Arminian readers see life and favor given to those who find wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers often connect this with Christ, whom John 1:4 calls life. Favor from the LORD is God’s gift, not earned.

Proverbs 8:36

36But he who sins against me wrongs his own soul. All those who hate me love death.”

Arminian

He who sins against me wrongs his own soul; all who hate me love death. Arminian readers stress that refusing wisdom is self-harm and a choice of death.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that rejecting wisdom is self-destructive and reveals a heart that loves death. The closing contrast of life and death frames the whole of chapters 1–9 as a choice between two ways.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
