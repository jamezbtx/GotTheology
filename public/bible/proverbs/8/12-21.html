Proverbs 8

Verses 12–21 · WEB

Wisdom describes herself. She dwells with prudence, knowledge, and discretion. The fear of the LORD is to hate evil, and she hates pride and perversity. Counsel, understanding, and power are hers; by her kings reign and rulers decree justice. She loves those who love her, is found by those who seek her, and walks in righteousness, giving lasting riches to those who love her.

Proverbs 8:12

12“I, wisdom, have made prudence my dwelling. Find out knowledge and discretion.

Arminian

I, wisdom, dwell with prudence and find knowledge and discretion. Arminian readers see wisdom as practical skill for living, not abstract theory.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom as practical godliness joined with sound judgment. Prudence and discretion are the skill of applying God’s truth wisely in particular situations.

Proverbs 8:13

13The fear of the LORD is to hate evil. I hate pride, arrogance, the evil way, and the perverse mouth.

Arminian

The fear of the LORD is to hate evil. Arminian readers see fear of the LORD shown in moral choices: rejecting pride and perverse speech.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the fear of the LORD as the root of wisdom, as 1:7 says, expressed in hatred of evil. Wisdom hates what God hates, so pride and perverse speech cannot coexist with her.

Proverbs 8:14

14Counsel and sound knowledge are mine. I have understanding and power.

Arminian

Counsel, sound knowledge, understanding, and power are mine. Arminian readers see wisdom as the resource for every decision.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the parallels with Isaiah 11:2, the Spirit of counsel and might resting on the Messiah, which some read christologically.

Proverbs 8:15

15By me kings reign, and princes decree justice.

Arminian

By me kings reign and princes decree justice. Arminian readers see wisdom as the standard for just rule. Rulers who govern justly are drawing, knowingly or not, on the wisdom God makes available.

Reformed

Reformed readers see all legitimate authority as established under God, as Romans 13:1 says, and accountable to his wisdom.

Proverbs 8:16

16By me princes rule, nobles, and all the righteous rulers of the earth.

Arminian

By me princes and nobles rule. Arminian readers note the breadth of wisdom’s influence on rulers. No office is so high that it stands outside wisdom’s claim.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s wisdom governing human authority at every level. Rulers are stewards answerable to God, and good government depends on his wisdom.

Proverbs 8:17

17I love those who love me. Those who seek me diligently will find me.

Arminian

I love those who love me; those who seek me diligently will find me. Arminian readers see a reciprocal relationship: wisdom answers those who seek her, and seeking matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm that those who seek wisdom find her. Many add that such seeking is itself a fruit of grace, since God first loves his people, as 1 John 4:19 says.

Key contrast Both affirm that diligent seekers find wisdom. Arminian readers typically see seeking as a free response enabled by grace that God meets. Reformed readers typically see the desire to seek as itself God’s gift preceding the finding.

Proverbs 8:18

18With me are riches, honor, enduring wealth, and prosperity.

Arminian

Riches, honor, enduring wealth, and prosperity are with wisdom. Arminian readers see wisdom’s rewards, while noting that Proverbs speaks in general patterns, not guarantees.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read the promises as general wisdom, not formulas. Many stress that the truest wealth is spiritual.

Proverbs 8:19

19My fruit is better than gold, yes, than fine gold, my yield than choice silver.

Arminian

Wisdom’s fruit is better than gold. Arminian readers see wisdom’s worth exceeding material gain. The comparison invites the reader to weigh lasting goods against passing ones.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree: wisdom’s yield surpasses any earthly wealth. What wisdom produces lasts, while gold and silver pass away, a theme that Jesus’ teaching on treasure in heaven echoes.

Proverbs 8:20

20I walk in the way of righteousness, in the middle of the paths of justice,

Arminian

I walk in the way of righteousness, in the middle of the paths of justice. Arminian readers see wisdom’s path as straight and moral.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom inseparable from righteousness and justice. Her gifts are never obtained by unjust means, unlike the ill-gotten gain Proverbs condemns elsewhere.

Proverbs 8:21

21that I may give wealth to those who love me. I fill their treasuries.

Arminian

That I may give wealth to those who love me. Arminian readers see wisdom’s generosity toward those who respond in love. The treasuries she fills are not limited to money but include the goods of a well-ordered life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom’s gifts as gracious, given to her lovers. Like 8:18, the promise describes the general fruit of wisdom rather than a formula for prosperity.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
