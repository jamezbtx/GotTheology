Passage

Proverbs 23

Verses 12–28 · WEB

A father urges his son to apply his heart to instruction, accept discipline, fear the LORD instead of envying sinners, avoid drunkards and gluttons, honor aging parents, buy the truth, and flee sexual unfaithfulness. Both traditions see an appeal to the heart that calls for real response, framed by parental love and hope that will not be cut off. The discipline sayings are read as loving correction, not warrant for harm.

Proverbs 23:12

12Apply your heart to instruction, and your ears to the words of knowledge.

Arminian

The learner is urged to apply heart and ears to instruction. Arminian readers see a real summons to respond to wisdom, assuming the hearer can and should engage the teaching offered.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the call to attentive learning, with the heart as the center of the person. Both traditions agree that wisdom requires receptive hearing and inward commitment, not mere information.

Proverbs 23:13

13Don’t withhold correction from a child. If you punish him with the rod, he will not die.

Arminian

Correction should not be withheld from a child; the rod will not kill him. Arminian readers see loving discipline aimed at the child’s good, while recognizing debate about applying ancient methods today.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see discipline as an expression of care rather than neglect. Both traditions read the saying within its setting and agree it gives no warrant for harsh or harmful treatment of children.

Proverbs 23:14

14Punish him with the rod, and save his soul from Sheol.

Arminian

Discipline can save the child’s soul from Sheol. Arminian readers see formation in wisdom as protection from paths that lead to death, with the child still responsible to respond.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see discipline as a means God uses to turn the young from destruction. Both note the WEB footnote identifying Sheol as the place of the dead, and agree the verse addresses deadly folly rather than a mechanism of salvation.

Proverbs 23:15

15My son, if your heart is wise, then my heart will be glad, even mine.

Arminian

A wise son gladdens his father’s heart. Arminian readers see the relational joy that wisdom brings, motivating the learner through love rather than fear alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the affection between parent and child framing instruction. Both traditions agree the father’s gladness reflects the deeper delight of God in those who walk in wisdom.

Proverbs 23:16

16Yes, my heart will rejoice when your lips speak what is right.

Arminian

The father rejoices when his son’s lips speak what is right. Arminian readers see inward wisdom expressed outwardly in speech that honors God and others.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see speech as the fruit of a heart shaped by wisdom. Both note the parallel between a wise heart in the previous verse and right words here.

Proverbs 23:17

17Don’t let your heart envy sinners, but rather fear the LORD all day long.

Arminian

The learner should not envy sinners but fear the LORD all day long. Arminian readers see a choice of orientation, turning from envy toward continual reverence for God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fear of the LORD as the antidote to envy. Both traditions agree that this fear is the foundation of wisdom throughout Proverbs, not a passing emotion.

Proverbs 23:18

18Indeed surely there is a future hope, and your hope will not be cut off.

Arminian

There is a future hope that will not be cut off. Arminian readers see encouragement that faithfulness has a real outcome, even when the wicked seem to prosper now.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s promise securing the hope of those who fear him. Both traditions find in this verse confidence that God will vindicate righteousness, without specifying details of the future.

Proverbs 23:19

19Listen, my son, and be wise, and keep your heart on the right path!

Arminian

The son is urged to listen, be wise, and keep his heart on the right path. Arminian readers see a genuine call to choose and continue in the way of wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the necessity of guarding the heart’s direction. Both traditions agree wisdom involves an ongoing commitment, not a single decision. The direction of the heart determines the path of the life.

Proverbs 23:20

20Don’t be among ones drinking too much wine, or those who gorge themselves on meat;

Arminian

The learner should avoid the company of heavy drinkers and gluttons. Arminian readers see the influence of companions on character and the need to choose associations wisely.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the danger of habits formed in company. Both note that Deuteronomy pairs the glutton and drunkard as a picture of a rebellious son, giving the warning moral weight.

Proverbs 23:21

21for the drunkard and the glutton shall become poor; and drowsiness clothes them in rags.

Arminian

Drunkards and gluttons become poor, and drowsiness clothes them in rags. Arminian readers see natural consequences following from undisciplined living, a warning meant to turn the learner away.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the order God built into life, where excess leads to ruin. Both traditions recognize that Proverbs states general patterns rather than guaranteeing every outcome.

Proverbs 23:22

22Listen to your father who gave you life, and don’t despise your mother when she is old.

Arminian

The learner should listen to his father and not despise his aged mother. Arminian readers see honor for parents continuing into their old age as a mark of wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fifth commandment’s concern for honoring father and mother. Both traditions agree that respect for parents does not end when they become weak.

Proverbs 23:23

23Buy the truth, and don’t sell it. Get wisdom, discipline, and understanding.

Arminian

Buy the truth and do not sell it; acquire wisdom, discipline, and understanding. Arminian readers see truth as worth any cost, calling for deliberate pursuit and commitment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the surpassing value of wisdom and truth. Both traditions agree the metaphor of buying stresses priority and commitment, not that wisdom is earned as a commodity from God.

Proverbs 23:24

24The father of the righteous has great joy. Whoever fathers a wise child delights in him.

Arminian

The father of the righteous has great joy. Arminian readers see the fruit of faithful instruction bringing delight to those who invested in the child.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see righteousness in children as a gift that gladdens parents. Both traditions recognize that parents rejoice over children who walk in wisdom while acknowledging that outcomes are not guaranteed.

Proverbs 23:25

25Let your father and your mother be glad! Let her who bore you rejoice!

Arminian

Father and mother should be glad, especially the mother who bore the child. Arminian readers see the child’s wise conduct as a way of honoring both parents.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the joy of both parents as motivation for the child’s wisdom. Both traditions note the explicit mention of the mother, affirming her role in instruction throughout Proverbs.

Proverbs 23:26

26My son, give me your heart; and let your eyes keep in my ways.

Arminian

My son, give me your heart, and let your eyes keep my ways. Arminian readers see an invitation to wholehearted trust and attention, freely given in response to loving instruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the heart as the center of devotion that wisdom seeks. Both traditions agree that the teacher asks for inward commitment, not mere outward compliance.

Proverbs 23:27

27For a prostitute is a deep pit; and a wayward wife is a narrow well.

Arminian

A prostitute is a deep pit, and a wayward wife a narrow well. Arminian readers see vivid warnings about sexual immorality as a trap that can destroy a life.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the deadly danger of unfaithfulness described in strong images. Both traditions read the warning as protective counsel, consistent with the extended teaching on adultery earlier in Proverbs.

Proverbs 23:28

28Yes, she lies in wait like a robber, and increases the unfaithful among men.

Arminian

She lies in wait like a robber and increases the unfaithful among men. Arminian readers see sexual sin spreading unfaithfulness through a community, calling for vigilance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see temptation portrayed as predatory and destructive. Both traditions agree the verse warns of the social and personal damage of adultery, while holding men accountable for their own unfaithfulness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
