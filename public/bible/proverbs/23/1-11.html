Passage

Proverbs 23

Verses 1–11 · WEB

The words of the wise continue with counsel about dining with rulers, the stingy host, the futility of exhausting oneself for riches, speaking to fools, and protecting the boundary stones and fields of the fatherless. Their strong Defender will plead their case. Both traditions read these sayings as shared practical wisdom rooted in the fear of the LORD, with special concern for integrity, contentment, and the vulnerable.

Proverbs 23:1

1When you sit to eat with a ruler, consider diligently what is before you;

Arminian

At a ruler’s table, the wise guest pays careful attention to the setting and the host. Arminian readers see practical wisdom for navigating power, where self-control protects integrity in tempting situations.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see prudence required in the presence of the powerful. Both traditions read the saying as counsel for everyday conduct, grounded in the fear of the LORD that shapes the whole book.

Proverbs 23:2

2put a knife to your throat if you are a man given to appetite.

Arminian

The vivid image of a knife to the throat urges drastic restraint of appetite. Arminian readers see a call to discipline desires that could compromise a person in a position of dependence.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see hyperbole urging mastery of appetite rather than literal self-harm. Both traditions agree the proverb addresses the danger of being ruled by desire at a powerful person’s table.

Proverbs 23:3

3Don’t be desirous of his dainties, since they are deceitful food.

Arminian

The ruler’s delicacies are called deceitful food. Arminian readers see hospitality that may carry hidden expectations, calling for discernment rather than naive enjoyment. The appetite is not the only thing at stake.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a warning that generosity from the powerful can conceal manipulation. Both traditions see wisdom recognizing that appearances at a feast may not match the host’s intentions.

Proverbs 23:4

4Don’t weary yourself to be rich. In your wisdom, show restraint.

Arminian

The sage warns against exhausting oneself to become rich and urges restraint. Arminian readers see freedom from the anxious pursuit of wealth as part of a life ordered by trust in God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see covetous striving rejected in favor of contentment. Both traditions agree that diligent work is commended elsewhere in Proverbs, so the target here is consuming ambition for riches.

Proverbs 23:5

5Why do you set your eyes on that which is not? For it certainly sprouts wings like an eagle and flies in the sky.

Arminian

Riches sprout wings and fly away like an eagle. Arminian readers see the instability of wealth as a reason not to build one’s security on it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the vanity of trusting what cannot last. Both traditions connect this saying with the broader biblical warning against storing up treasure that does not endure.

Proverbs 23:6

6Don’t eat the food of him who has a stingy eye, and don’t crave his delicacies,

Arminian

The learner should not eat the food of a stingy host or crave his delicacies. Arminian readers see discernment about relationships where outward generosity hides a grudging heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a warning about hospitality that is not sincere. Both note the Hebrew idiom of an evil or stingy eye, which describes a begrudging disposition.

Proverbs 23:7

7for as he thinks about the cost, so he is. “Eat and drink!” he says to you, but his heart is not with you.

Arminian

The stingy host counts the cost while urging his guest to eat, and his heart is not with you. Arminian readers see the gap between words and heart exposed by wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the heart as the true measure of a person’s character. Both traditions agree the saying warns against accepting outward courtesy as proof of genuine goodwill.

Proverbs 23:8

8You will vomit up the morsel which you have eaten and waste your pleasant words.

Arminian

The guest ends up vomiting the morsel and wasting pleasant words. Arminian readers see the bitter aftertaste of fellowship with insincere people, where kindness is spent in vain.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the futility of investing in relationships built on pretense. Both traditions read the image as memorable counsel to choose company carefully.

Proverbs 23:9

9Don’t speak in the ears of a fool, for he will despise the wisdom of your words.

Arminian

Wisdom should not be spoken to a fool who will despise it. Arminian readers see discernment about timing and audience, without concluding that the fool is beyond all hope of change.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a practical limit on persuasion with those who scorn instruction. Both traditions note that Proverbs elsewhere urges answering a fool in some situations, so wisdom requires judgment about circumstances.

Proverbs 23:10

10Don’t move the ancient boundary stone. Don’t encroach on the fields of the fatherless,

Arminian

Moving ancient boundary stones and encroaching on orphans’ fields are forbidden. Arminian readers see God’s concern for justice toward the vulnerable expressed in ordinary property rights.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the protection of inheritance and the defenseless, echoing Deuteronomy’s prohibition on moving boundary markers. Both agree the saying condemns exploiting those least able to resist.

Proverbs 23:11

11for their Defender is strong. He will plead their case against you.

Arminian

The orphans’ Defender is strong and will plead their case. Arminian readers see God personally taking the side of the oppressed, warning those who would exploit them.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God as the powerful advocate of the helpless. Both note that the Hebrew term behind Defender is the word for a kinsman-redeemer, emphasizing family-like protection from God himself.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
