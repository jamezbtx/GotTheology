Passage

Proverbs 23

Verses 29–35 · WEB

A vivid poem portrays the drunkard: woe, strife, and bruises; wine that sparkles and then bites like a snake; confusion, instability, and the compulsive longing for more. Both traditions read it as a realistic warning about excess and bondage rather than a blanket condemnation of wine, and both differ internally on abstinence. The poem calls for wisdom and compassion toward those caught in the trap.

Proverbs 23:29

29Who has woe? Who has sorrow? Who has strife? Who has complaints? Who has needless bruises? Who has bloodshot eyes?

Arminian

A series of questions asks who has woe, sorrow, strife, and bruises. Arminian readers see the poem drawing the learner into reflection on consequences before naming the cause.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a rhetorical strategy that exposes the misery behind drunkenness. Both traditions appreciate the poem’s vivid realism as a way of warning rather than merely forbidding.

Proverbs 23:30

30Those who stay long at the wine; those who go to seek out mixed wine.

Arminian

The answer is those who linger over wine and seek out mixed wine. Arminian readers see habitual excess as the source of the miseries just listed.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see persistent drinking named as the cause of ruin. Both traditions note that Scripture elsewhere receives wine as a gift, so the concern here is lingering and seeking excess.

Proverbs 23:31

31Don’t look at the wine when it is red, when it sparkles in the cup, when it goes down smoothly.

Arminian

Do not look at the wine when it is red and sparkling and goes down smoothly. Arminian readers see temptation working through attractive appearances, calling for resistance at the first look.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see desire beginning with the eyes. Both traditions agree the verse urges avoiding the allure that leads to drunkenness, though they differ internally on whether total abstinence is required.

Proverbs 23:32

32In the end, it bites like a snake, and poisons like a viper.

Arminian

In the end it bites like a snake and poisons like a viper. Arminian readers see the hidden cost of indulgence revealed only later, when harm is already done.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see sin’s deceptive pleasure turning into destruction. Both traditions read the image as a sober warning about the deadly consequences of drunkenness.

Proverbs 23:33

33Your eyes will see strange things, and your mind will imagine confusing things.

Arminian

The drinker’s eyes see strange things and the mind imagines confusion. Arminian readers see the loss of clear judgment that makes the drunkard vulnerable to further sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see impaired perception and moral confusion. Both traditions agree the poem portrays drunkenness as the undoing of the wisdom Proverbs prizes. Clear sight gives way to confusion.

Proverbs 23:34

34Yes, you will be as he who lies down in the middle of the sea, or as he who lies on top of the rigging:

Arminian

The drinker is like one lying in the middle of the sea or atop the rigging. Arminian readers see instability and danger pictured in the image of a reeling sailor.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the helplessness of the intoxicated person. Both traditions read the comparison as a memorable picture of disorientation rather than a literal scene.

Proverbs 23:35

35“They hit me, and I was not hurt! They beat me, and I don’t feel it! When will I wake up? I can do it again. I will look for more.”

Arminian

The drinker boasts he felt no blows and seeks more as soon as he wakes. Arminian readers see the bondage of addiction, where a person returns to what harms him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see enslavement to sin portrayed with sad realism. Both traditions agree the poem ends without resolution to show the trap of compulsive drinking, calling readers to compassion and to seek deliverance.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
