Passage

Proverbs 5

Verses 15–23 · WEB

The father turns from warning to the good of faithful married love. Using images of water, a cistern, and a spring, he calls the son to drink from his own well and rejoice in the wife of his youth. Adultery is folly, because the LORD sees every path, and the wicked man is held by the cords of his own sin and dies for lack of instruction. Both traditions read married love as God’s good gift and adultery as destructive.

Proverbs 5:15

15Drink water out of your own cistern, running water out of your own well.

Arminian

The father turns from warning to commendation. The cistern and well are images for a man’s own wife: faithful married love is refreshing and good. Both traditions read this as the positive center of the chapter, with marriage presented as God’s good gift.

Reformed

Water from one’s own cistern pictures delight found within marriage. Reformed readers, sharing the covenant-marriage ethic with Arminians, note that Scripture does not answer temptation with a suspicion of desire but by directing desire to its proper, God-given place (compare Gen 2:24).

Proverbs 5:16

16Should your springs overflow in the streets, streams of water in the public squares?

Arminian

The rhetorical question asks whether one’s springs should flow out into the streets. Interpreters take it as a warning against scattering one’s affection and life outside marriage, with the answer expected to be no, as verse 17 confirms.

Reformed

Some translations read this verse as a question and some as a statement; the WEB’s question form fits the flow into verse 17. Reformed and Arminian readers agree on the sense: married love is not to be made common or public.

Proverbs 5:17

17Let them be for yourself alone, not for strangers with you.

Arminian

“For yourself alone” expresses the exclusive bond of marriage. Faithfulness belongs to the shape of covenant love, not as a restriction on joy but as its protection. Both traditions draw the same marriage ethic from the verse.

Reformed

The exclusivity of the marriage bond reflects the covenant character of marriage, which Scripture uses as a picture of the LORD’s bond with his people and of Christ and the church (Eph 5:31–32). Reformed readers share this reading with Arminians.

Proverbs 5:18

18Let your spring be blessed. Rejoice in the wife of your youth.

Arminian

“Let your spring be blessed” and “rejoice in the wife of your youth” call the husband to lasting delight in the wife he married when young. Joy within marriage is commanded, not merely allowed. Arminian readers see the call to cultivate love as an ongoing, willing act.

Reformed

The blessing on the spring may include the gift of children as well as delight. “The wife of your youth” recalls the covenant made at marriage (compare Mal 2:14). Reformed readers, in agreement with Arminians, see married joy as a gift to be received with thanksgiving.

Proverbs 5:19

19A loving doe and a graceful deer— let her breasts satisfy you at all times. Be captivated always with her love.

Arminian

The poetry uses images of a graceful doe to express tender, admiring love for one’s wife. The point is that a husband’s affection and desire should stay with her “at all times” and “always.” Married love is good and meant to last.

Reformed

The poetic imagery affirms physical and emotional love within marriage, as the Song of Songs also does. Reformed readers, with Arminians, read the verse as a celebration of married love as God’s good gift, set against the false sweetness of verse 3.

Proverbs 5:20

20For why should you, my son, be captivated with an adulteress? Why embrace the bosom of another?

Arminian

The father asks why his son would be captivated by an adulteress when such love is his at home. The repeated word “captivated” (verses 19–20) shows that the same capacity for delight can be rightly or wrongly directed. The choice is real and set before the son.

Reformed

The question exposes the folly of adultery by contrast with the good already given. Reformed readers see the human heart’s tendency to seek forbidden things. Both traditions read the verse as an appeal to see adultery’s emptiness.

Proverbs 5:21

21For the ways of man are before the LORD’s eyes. He examines all his paths.

Arminian

The reason given is God’s sight: the LORD sees all a man’s ways and examines his paths. Nothing is hidden. Arminian readers stress that God’s knowledge of every path makes each person truly accountable for the paths he chooses.

Reformed

The LORD’s all-seeing eye grounds the whole warning in God, not only in consequences. Reformed readers stress God’s full knowledge and moral government of every life (compare Ps 139:1–4; Heb 4:13). Both traditions affirm that God sees and judges what is done in secret.

Proverbs 5:22

22The evil deeds of the wicked ensnare him. The cords of his sin hold him firmly.

Arminian

The wicked man’s own evil deeds trap him, and the cords of his sin hold him fast. Sin is self-binding: repeated choices become chains. Arminian readers affirm the reality of this bondage and the sinner’s full responsibility for it.

Reformed

Sin traps the one who commits it; the image is of a hunter caught in his own snare. Reformed readers affirm the same self-binding and see in it both the fallen will’s bondage to sin and God’s just judgment, by which he gives sinners over to the sin they choose (compare Rom 1:24). Both traditions agree that only God’s grace sets the sinner free.

Proverbs 5:23

23He will die for lack of instruction. In the greatness of his folly, he will go astray.

Arminian

He dies for lack of instruction and goes astray through great folly. The chapter ends where verse 1 began: instruction refused leads to death. Arminian readers read the end as the result of a long refusal of wisdom freely offered.

Reformed

The final verse ties death to the absence of instruction and to folly. “Go astray” uses the same verb as “captivated” in verses 19–20, so the man who would not be captivated by his wife is led astray by folly. Reformed readers, with Arminians, see the end of the way of the fool, which the father’s words were given to prevent.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
