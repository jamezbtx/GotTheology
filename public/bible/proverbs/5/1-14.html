Proverbs 5

Verses 1–14 · WEB

The father calls his son to attend to wisdom so that he may keep discretion, then warns him against the adulteress. Her words are sweet, but her path ends in bitterness and death. The son is told to keep far from her door, because unfaithfulness wastes his honor, years, and labor and ends in the regret of one who refused instruction. Both traditions read the chapter as father-to-son wisdom instruction that assumes the covenant ethic of marriage.

Proverbs 5:1

1My son, pay attention to my wisdom. Turn your ear to my understanding,

Arminian

The father calls his son to attend to wisdom and understanding. In Proverbs 1–9 the teacher’s instruction is a channel of the LORD’s wisdom (2:6), so listening is the first step of godliness. Arminian readers stress the real appeal to the son’s will: instruction can be heeded or refused, and the call is sincere.

Reformed

The father’s plea frames the chapter as covenant instruction handed down in the home (compare Deut 6:6–7). Reformed readers, sharing the wisdom-genre reading, note that the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge (1:7) and that the son’s attentive heart is itself a gift God gives through the means of teaching.

Proverbs 5:2

2that you may maintain discretion, that your lips may preserve knowledge.

Arminian

The goal is discretion and lips that keep knowledge. Wisdom is not only information but a guarded life and guarded speech. Both traditions read this as the moral formation wisdom literature aims at, in which choices grow into settled character.

Reformed

Discretion is the practical skill to see danger and turn from it. Its pairing with the son’s own lips contrasts with the adulteress’s lips in the next verse: whose words a person keeps shapes where he goes. Reformed and Arminian readers alike see here the ordinary means by which God forms a wise life.

Proverbs 5:3

3For the lips of an adulteress drip honey. Her mouth is smoother than oil,

Arminian

The danger is first verbal. The adulteress, a woman outside the covenant bond of marriage, offers words that are sweet and smooth. Temptation comes dressed as pleasure and flattery, which is why it needs discretion, not only good intentions.

Reformed

The honeyed lips and smooth mouth describe persuasive speech, the same pattern as the forbidden woman of 2:16 and 7:5. Reformed readers note how sin deceives by appearing good, and the father’s warning gives the son words truer than hers.

Proverbs 5:4

4but in the end she is as bitter as wormwood, and as sharp as a two-edged sword.

Arminian

“In the end” is the key phrase. The sweetness of verse 3 turns to bitterness and a wound. Wisdom judges an act by where it leads, not by how it first tastes. Arminian readers see a warning meant to move the will before the step is taken.

Reformed

Wormwood, a proverbially bitter plant, and the two-edged sword show adultery as destructive to the one who enters it. Reformed readers, in common with Arminians, read this as the honest end of sin set against its first appeal (compare Heb 3:13 on sin’s deceitfulness).

Proverbs 5:5

5Her feet go down to death. Her steps lead straight to Sheol.

Arminian

Her path goes down to death and Sheol, the realm of the dead. In Proverbs the two ways lead to life or death. Many interpreters hear both literal danger (ruined life, violence from a wronged husband) and a deeper moral and spiritual death.

Reformed

The language of death and Sheol is the strongest in the chapter. Reformed readers read it as the wages of sin within the covenant (compare Rom 6:23), while acknowledging with Arminians that the immediate sense in Proverbs concerns ruin and premature death in this life.

Proverbs 5:6

6She gives no thought to the way of life. Her ways are crooked, and she doesn’t know it.

Arminian

She does not consider the way of life, and her paths wander without her knowing it. Sin blinds those who walk in it. Arminian readers note that the father warns his son precisely so that he will not share her blindness: there is still time to choose the path of life.

Reformed

Her unsteady, crooked ways and her lack of awareness show sin’s darkening of the mind. Reformed readers see in this the self-deceiving power of sin that only instruction and grace can expose. Both traditions take the verse as a warning, not a portrait of the son’s fate.

Proverbs 5:7

7Now therefore, my sons, listen to me. Don’t depart from the words of my mouth.

Arminian

The father now addresses his “sons,” widening the appeal. The instruction is meant for every hearer. To “not depart” from his words pictures wisdom as a path to stay on day by day, which fits the Arminian stress on persevering obedience.

Reformed

The renewed summons marks a new section of the lesson. Reformed readers see the same covenant instruction pressed on each generation, with perseverance in the father’s words as the expected fruit of a heart taught by God.

Proverbs 5:8

8Remove your way far from her. Don’t come near the door of her house,

Arminian

The counsel is practical: keep far away and do not go near her door. Wisdom does not rely on strength in the moment but avoids the place of temptation altogether. Both traditions read this as sound pastoral wisdom (compare 1 Cor 6:18, “flee sexual immorality”).

Reformed

Distance is the first defense. Reformed readers note that knowing the weakness of the heart, the wise man guards his steps before temptation is near. This shared ground of watchfulness runs through both traditions’ teaching on the Christian life.

Proverbs 5:9

9lest you give your honor to others, and your years to the cruel one;

Arminian

Verses 9–10 count the cost. Adultery hands one’s honor and best years to others, including “the cruel one,” perhaps a wronged husband or others who exploit the man. The loss is public as well as private.

Reformed

The “cruel one” is not specified; interpreters suggest the offended husband, the woman herself, or those who profit from the man’s fall. Reformed and Arminian readers agree on the point: sin squanders the honor and years God gave for faithful use.

Proverbs 5:10

10lest strangers feast on your wealth, and your labors enrich another man’s house.

Arminian

Strangers consume his wealth, and his labors enrich another house. In Israel’s setting adultery could bring compensation demands, loss of standing, and ruin of a household. The verse makes plain that unfaithfulness wastes a man’s whole life, not only one moment.

Reformed

The man’s strength and labor, meant to build his own household, go to strangers. Reformed readers see a reversal of the blessing of a godly home (Ps 128). Both traditions read the verse as a sober account of earthly consequences.

Proverbs 5:11

11You will groan at your latter end, when your flesh and your body are consumed,

Arminian

The scene moves to the end of such a life: groaning when the body is worn out. The text does not need to specify a cause; it describes a life spent and exhausted. The father wants the son to see the end before he begins.

Reformed

“Your latter end” returns to the theme of verse 4. Reformed readers note that the remorse described is the sorrow of loss, not necessarily repentance. Both traditions agree that sin’s consequences can remain even where forgiveness is later sought.

Proverbs 5:12

12and say, “How I have hated instruction, and my heart despised reproof.

Arminian

The ruined man confesses that he hated instruction and despised reproof. Arminian readers stress that the fault lies in his own refusal: wisdom was offered and resisted. The confession shows how responsible he was.

Reformed

The lament puts the cause where Proverbs always puts it, in a heart that despised correction (compare 1:29–30; 12:1). Reformed readers, in agreement with Arminians, hold the man fully accountable for refusing instruction that was truly given.

Proverbs 5:13

13I haven’t obeyed the voice of my teachers, nor turned my ear to those who instructed me!

Arminian

He did not listen to his teachers. The verse echoes verse 1 (“turn your ear”), showing that the man heard the same call the son is now hearing. Arminian readers see a living choice set before the reader: listen now, or speak these words later.

Reformed

The echo of verse 1 makes the regret a mirror for the son. Reformed readers see the means of grace — parents, teachers, God’s word — despised. The warning itself is one of the means God uses to keep his people from that end.

Proverbs 5:14

14I have come to the brink of utter ruin, among the gathered assembly.”

Arminian

He was close to complete ruin in the assembly, meaning public disgrace before the community. Israel’s assembly was where judgment and reputation were settled. Arminian readers note that “brink” suggests he was spared total destruction, a mercy that should move the reader to heed the warning.

Reformed

“The gathered assembly” points to the public, covenant community, where his sin is exposed and judged. Reformed readers, with Arminians, see that private sin carries communal consequences, and that the people of God are wronged when marriage is betrayed.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
