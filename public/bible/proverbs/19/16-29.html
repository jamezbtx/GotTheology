Passage

Proverbs 19

Verses 16–29 · WEB

The section continues with themes of obedience, care for the poor, discipline, counsel, and the LORD’s purposes standing above human plans. Proverbs states general patterns of life under God rather than unconditional promises for every case. Arminian and Reformed readers share this wisdom teaching, while explaining God’s providence over human plans with somewhat different emphases.

Proverbs 19:16

16He who keeps the commandment keeps his soul, but he who is contemptuous in his ways shall die.

Arminian

Keeping the commandment preserves life, while despising one’s ways leads to death. Arminian readers see a real choice with real consequences. Obedience is the path of life that God sets before his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see obedience as the path of life, while emphasizing that such obedience flows from God’s grace. Both traditions affirm the moral seriousness of the choice the proverb presents.

Proverbs 19:17

17He who has pity on the poor lends to the LORD; he will reward him.

Arminian

Kindness to the poor is a loan to the LORD, who will repay. Arminian readers see God identifying himself with the needy. Generosity is never wasted, since God takes it as done to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God honoring mercy shown to the poor, a theme echoed in Matthew 25:40. Reward here is God’s gracious response, not earned merit. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 19:18

18Discipline your son, for there is hope; don’t be a willing party to his death.

Arminian

Discipline a son while there is hope, and do not become party to his ruin. Arminian readers see loving correction as urgent, since neglect can lead to destruction. Interpreters debate the second line’s exact sense, and the WEB offers one rendering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see discipline as an act of love that seeks the child’s good. The verse does not license harsh or abusive treatment. Christians in both traditions differ on methods of discipline; the shared point is hopeful, caring correction.

Proverbs 19:19

19A hot-tempered man must pay the penalty, for if you rescue him, you must do it again.

Arminian

A hot-tempered person must bear the consequences, since rescuing him only leads to more rescues. Arminian readers see wisdom in letting people face the results of their choices. Enabling can prevent change.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see that shielding someone from consequences may harden habits. Love sometimes allows correction through hardship. Both traditions share this practical wisdom. Wisdom distinguishes real help from enabling.

Proverbs 19:20

20Listen to counsel and receive instruction, that you may be wise in your latter end.

Arminian

Listening to counsel and receiving instruction leads to wisdom later in life. Arminian readers see teachability as a decision with long-term fruit. Wisdom grows over time through humble learning.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see humility before instruction as a mark of wisdom. The fruit appears in the latter end. Both traditions read the verse as shared ground.

Proverbs 19:21

21There are many plans in a man’s heart, but the LORD’s counsel will prevail.

Arminian

People make many plans, but the LORD’s counsel prevails. Arminian readers affirm that God’s purposes cannot be defeated and that he overrules human schemes. Many understand this as governing providence that does not cause every human choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the LORD’s counsel as sovereign over all human plans, including the plans themselves. Both traditions find comfort and humility here: human planning is legitimate, but God’s purpose is final. The verse is largely shared ground, with different accounts of providence in the background.

Proverbs 19:22

22That which makes a man to be desired is his kindness. A poor man is better than a liar.

Arminian

Kindness makes a person desirable, and a poor person is better than a liar. Arminian readers see character valued above wealth. Faithful love and honesty matter more than possessions.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see steadfast kindness and truthfulness as the true worth of a person. The line’s exact sense is debated, but the contrast between honesty and lying is clear. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 19:23

23The fear of the LORD leads to life, then contentment; he rests and will not be touched by trouble.

Arminian

The fear of the LORD leads to life, contentment, and rest. Arminian readers see reverent trust bringing security that circumstances cannot remove. The proverb states a general pattern of God’s care.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fear of the LORD as the root of true life and peace. Other Scripture shows the righteous still suffer, so the verse speaks of God’s overall protection rather than exemption from every trouble.

Proverbs 19:24

24The sluggard buries his hand in the dish; he will not so much as bring it to his mouth again.

Arminian

The sluggard buries his hand in the dish but will not lift it to his mouth. Arminian readers see humor exposing absurd laziness. Even the simplest effort becomes too much for the idle.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the comic image making a serious point about sloth. Both traditions read the proverb as shared wisdom about diligence. Laughter here serves moral instruction.

Proverbs 19:25

25Flog a scoffer, and the simple will learn prudence; rebuke one who has understanding, and he will gain knowledge.

Arminian

Punishing a scoffer teaches the simple, and rebuking the wise increases knowledge. Arminian readers see different people learning in different ways. Correction can benefit onlookers as well as the corrected.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see discipline serving the community’s instruction. The wise need only words, while scoffers resist. Both traditions read the verse within its legal and social setting without prescribing modern methods.

Proverbs 19:26

26He who robs his father and drives away his mother is a son who causes shame and brings reproach.

Arminian

A son who robs his father and drives away his mother brings shame. Arminian readers see grave betrayal of the family’s trust. Honoring parents includes caring for them, not exploiting them.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fifth commandment behind this proverb. Mistreating parents dishonors God’s order. Both traditions share this reading. The verse describes a deep breach of family loyalty and care for the vulnerable.

Proverbs 19:27

27If you stop listening to instruction, my son, you will stray from the words of knowledge.

Arminian

Ceasing to listen to instruction leads one away from knowledge. Arminian readers see the danger of drifting when one stops learning. Wisdom requires ongoing attention.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see continued teachability as necessary for remaining in the way of wisdom. Both traditions read the warning as shared ground. The address to my son recalls the parental voice of chapters 1–9.

Proverbs 19:28

28A corrupt witness mocks justice, and the mouth of the wicked gulps down iniquity.

Arminian

A corrupt witness mocks justice, and the wicked swallow iniquity eagerly. Arminian readers see how deceit corrupts courts and communities. Wickedness can become an appetite.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see sin as something the wicked consume with relish. Corruption of justice is serious before God. Both traditions share this reading. Corrupt testimony harms the innocent.

Proverbs 19:29

29Penalties are prepared for scoffers, and beatings for the backs of fools.

Arminian

Penalties and beatings await scoffers and fools. Arminian readers see consequences following persistent folly. The proverb reflects ancient forms of correction and should be read in that setting.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s moral order bringing consequences on mockers. Both traditions read the verse as a warning, not a prescription for modern punishment.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
