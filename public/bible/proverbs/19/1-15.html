Passage

Proverbs 19

Verses 1–15 · WEB

These sayings belong to the Solomonic collection that runs from 10:1 to 22:16. Most are freestanding two-line proverbs, yet clusters recur: honest speech and false witness, poverty and wealth, anger and patience, the household, and diligence. Both traditions read this as covenant wisdom grounded in the fear of the LORD, and nearly every verse is shared ground about how a godly life works out in ordinary relationships.

Proverbs 19:1

1Better is the poor who walks in his integrity than he who is perverse in his lips and is a fool.

Arminian

Integrity in poverty is better than crooked speech joined to folly. Arminian readers see character valued above status or wealth. The proverb challenges the assumption that prosperity signals God’s approval.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see integrity as true riches before God. The perverse fool may have more possessions, but his speech reveals a heart out of step with wisdom. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 19:2

2It isn’t good to have zeal without knowledge, nor to be hasty with one’s feet and miss the way.

Arminian

Zeal without knowledge and haste without care lead astray. Arminian readers see the need to pair enthusiasm with understanding. Good intentions do not replace wisdom; Paul makes a similar point in Romans 10:2.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see that sincerity alone cannot guide life rightly. Zeal must be informed by truth. Both traditions read the proverb as shared counsel against rash action, including in religious matters.

Proverbs 19:3

3The foolishness of man subverts his way; his heart rages against the LORD.

Arminian

A person’s own folly ruins his path, yet his heart rages against the LORD. Arminian readers stress human responsibility: people often blame God for consequences they brought on themselves. The proverb exposes self-deception.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fool blaming God for what his own sin produced. Fallen hearts shift responsibility rather than repent. Both traditions affirm that the fool is accountable, so the verse is shared ground rather than a point of contrast.

Proverbs 19:4

4Wealth adds many friends, but the poor is separated from his friend.

Arminian

Wealth attracts friends while the poor are left alone. Arminian readers see an honest observation about social life, not an endorsement. The proverb exposes how shallow friendships often follow money.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a realistic description of a fallen world. Wisdom names the pattern so that readers will not trust in such friendships or imitate them. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 19:5

5A false witness shall not be unpunished. He who pours out lies shall not go free.

Arminian

A false witness will not escape punishment. Arminian readers see God’s justice upholding truth in human courts and beyond. Lying may seem to succeed for a time, but accountability comes.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the ninth commandment behind the proverb. God defends truth and judges deceit. Both traditions read this as shared moral teaching. Courts depend on honest testimony.

Proverbs 19:6

6Many will entreat the favor of a ruler, and everyone is a friend to a man who gives gifts.

Arminian

Many seek the favor of the generous and powerful. Arminian readers see an observation about how gifts buy attention. The proverb invites readers to examine their own motives in friendship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the self-interest that often drives social relationships. Wisdom observes without approving. Both traditions read the verse as shared observation about human nature.

Proverbs 19:7

7All the relatives of the poor shun him; how much more do his friends avoid him! He pursues them with pleas, but they are gone.

Arminian

The poor are shunned even by relatives and friends, and pleas go unanswered. Arminian readers feel the pain of social abandonment the proverb describes. This observation calls God’s people to care for those others avoid.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the loneliness of poverty in a fallen world. Other proverbs, such as 19:17, call for compassion in response. Both traditions read this as shared ground. The source prints this verse in three lines.

Proverbs 19:8

8He who gets wisdom loves his own soul. He who keeps understanding shall find good.

Arminian

Getting wisdom is a way of loving one’s own life. Arminian readers see wisdom as genuinely good for the one who pursues it. Seeking understanding is not selfish but life-giving.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see wisdom bringing true flourishing. Self-love in this sense means seeking one’s real good under God. Both traditions share this reading. The gain is life itself, not merely advantage.

Proverbs 19:9

9A false witness shall not be unpunished. He who utters lies shall perish.

Arminian

The false witness will perish, repeating verse 5 with a stronger ending. Arminian readers see the repetition stressing how seriously God treats lying. Deceit leads toward destruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see emphasis through repetition. Perjury corrupts justice and invites judgment. Both traditions read the verse as shared teaching on truthfulness. The stronger verb sharpens the warning.

Proverbs 19:10

10Delicate living is not appropriate for a fool, much less for a servant to have rule over princes.

Arminian

Luxury does not suit a fool, nor does a servant ruling princes. Arminian readers see a saying about fitting order in ancient society. Its point is that power and privilege should match wisdom and character.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a concern for order and fitness rather than a timeless ranking of persons. The proverb reflects its social setting. Both traditions read it with attention to that context.

Proverbs 19:11

11The discretion of a man makes him slow to anger. It is his glory to overlook an offense.

Arminian

Discretion makes a person slow to anger, and overlooking an offense is glorious. Arminian readers see patience as a mark of wisdom and a reflection of God’s own forbearance. Restraint is strength, not weakness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s patience mirrored in the forgiving person. Overlooking offenses does not mean ignoring abuse or injustice. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 19:12

12The king’s wrath is like the roaring of a lion, but his favor is like dew on the grass.

Arminian

A king’s wrath is like a lion’s roar, but his favor is like dew. Arminian readers see practical counsel for living wisely under authority. Rulers’ responses carry great weight in people’s lives.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the power of rulers to harm or bless. Some readers also reflect on God as the greater King whose favor refreshes. Both traditions read the verse as shared wisdom.

Proverbs 19:13

13A foolish son is the calamity of his father. A wife’s quarrels are a continual dripping.

Arminian

A foolish son brings calamity, and a quarrelsome wife is like constant dripping. Arminian readers see the household as a place where folly and strife cause lasting harm. The proverb is addressed from a male perspective typical of its setting.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the home as a primary setting for wisdom or folly. Other proverbs speak similarly about quarrelsome husbands and foolish men. Both traditions read the verse as shared domestic wisdom.

Proverbs 19:14

14House and riches are an inheritance from fathers, but a prudent wife is from the LORD.

Arminian

Houses and wealth can be inherited, but a prudent wife is from the LORD. Arminian readers see a good marriage as a gift of God’s grace, not merely human arrangement. Gratitude is the fitting response.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God’s providence in the gift of a wise spouse. What cannot be secured by inheritance is given by the LORD. Both traditions share this reading.

Proverbs 19:15

15Slothfulness casts into a deep sleep. The idle soul shall suffer hunger.

Arminian

Laziness leads to deep sleep and hunger. Arminian readers see responsibility for one’s own diligence. The proverb warns against drifting into idleness and its consequences.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see work as part of God’s good order and sloth as a vice. The proverb states a general pattern, not a judgment on every person in need. Both traditions share this reading.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
