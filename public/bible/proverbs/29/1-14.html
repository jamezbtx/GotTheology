Proverbs 29

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Chapter 29 brings the Hezekiah collection (25:1–29:27) to its close. These sayings range across public and personal life: the fate of the one who stiffens his neck against rebuke, the effect of righteous and wicked rulers on a people, the danger of flattery, care for the poor, and the self-control of the wise. Both traditions read these as wisdom sayings about the ordinary course of life under God and share their practical sense.

Proverbs 29:1

1He who is often rebuked and stiffens his neck will be destroyed suddenly, with no remedy.

Arminian

The person rebuked again and again who “stiffens his neck” refuses correction like an ox that will not take the yoke. Ruin then comes “suddenly, with no remedy,” the same warning given of the worthless man in 6:15. Arminian readers hear the urgency of repeated warning: correction is a mercy that can be refused, and the warning is given so that the hearer will turn while there is time.

Reformed

“Stiffens his neck” is the language Israel’s history uses for a people who resist the LORD (compare Exod 32:9; Deut 9:6). The proverb warns that persistent refusal of correction ends in sudden and final ruin. Reformed readers, with Arminians, take it as a plain warning to the hearer: rebuke is God’s gift, and to harden oneself against it is deadly.

Proverbs 29:2

2When the righteous thrive, the people rejoice; but when the wicked rule, the people groan.

Arminian

The mood of a whole people follows the character of those in power. When the righteous flourish, people rejoice; when the wicked rule, they groan. Arminian readers note the public responsibility of leaders, whose choices bless or burden many others, and the call to pursue righteousness in public life.

Reformed

The proverb, echoing 28:12 and 28:28, links the moral character of rulers to the welfare of the nation. Reformed readers see here God’s ordinance of civil authority for the people’s good (compare Rom 13:3–4), and the misery that follows when that office is abused. Both traditions read it as practical wisdom about justice in public life.

Proverbs 29:3

3Whoever loves wisdom brings joy to his father; but a companion of prostitutes squanders his wealth.

Arminian

The son who loves wisdom gladdens his father, while the one who keeps company with prostitutes wastes his wealth. The verse recalls the warnings of chapters 5–7 and the prodigal son of Luke 15:13, 30. Arminian readers stress that what a person loves shapes the course of his life and that of his family.

Reformed

Love of wisdom and the squandering of wealth on prostitutes are set side by side as two loves with two outcomes. Reformed readers, sharing this reading, note that wisdom in Proverbs is bound up with the fear of the LORD (1:7), so the son’s love of wisdom is finally a matter of the heart turned toward God.

Proverbs 29:4

4The king by justice makes the land stable, but he who takes bribes tears it down.

Arminian

A king who rules by justice makes the land stable; one who takes bribes, or perhaps who levies heavy exactions (the Hebrew may be read either way), tears it down. Arminian readers see accountability built into authority: rulers answer to God for whether they build up or ruin the people entrusted to them.

Reformed

Justice is the foundation of a stable kingdom; bribery or greedy taxation tears it down. Reformed readers see in the verse God’s design for civil government as a servant of justice, and they share with Arminians the conviction that corruption in rulers is a real wrong against the people and against God.

Proverbs 29:5

5A man who flatters his neighbor spreads a net for his feet.

Arminian

The flatterer “spreads a net for his feet.” The feet may be the neighbor’s, trapped by false praise, or the flatterer’s own, caught in his deceit; the Hebrew allows either. Arminian readers take it as a warning about both receiving and giving flattery, since honest speech is part of loving one’s neighbor.

Reformed

Flattery is a trap. Whether the net catches the one flattered or the flatterer himself, the proverb exposes smooth speech as a form of deceit (compare 26:28; 28:23). Reformed readers, with Arminians, read it as a call to truthful speech that seeks the neighbor’s real good.

Proverbs 29:6

6An evil man is snared by his sin, but the righteous can sing and be glad.

Arminian

The evil man is caught by his own sin, while the righteous can sing and rejoice. Wrongdoing carries its own snare, as in 5:22. Arminian readers note the freedom and joy of the righteous life in contrast to the entanglement of the wicked.

Reformed

Sin traps the one who commits it; the righteous are free to sing. Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see in the pattern God’s moral government of the world, in which sin brings its own trouble while those who walk uprightly know a gladness the wicked lack. The proverb speaks of the usual course of life, not of every case.

Proverbs 29:7

7The righteous care about justice for the poor. The wicked aren’t concerned about knowledge.

Arminian

The righteous know and care about the cause of the poor; the wicked have no such concern. The Hebrew of the second line is compressed, and it is often read as “the wicked do not understand such concern.” Arminian readers see concern for the poor as a mark of true righteousness, rooted in God’s own care for the needy.

Reformed

Righteousness in Proverbs is not merely private; it shows itself in attention to the rights of the poor. Reformed readers, with Arminians, link this to the law and the prophets, which repeatedly call God’s people to defend the needy (compare Deut 15:7–11; Jer 22:16). Both traditions read the verse as a test of genuine godliness.

Proverbs 29:8

8Mockers stir up a city, but wise men turn away anger.

Arminian

Mockers set a city aflame with strife; the wise calm anger. Arminian readers see the real influence individuals have on a community, for good or ill, and the call to be peacemakers (compare Matt 5:9).

Reformed

The scoffer stirs up public unrest, while the wise turn away wrath. Reformed readers, sharing this reading, note that wisdom serves the common good and that peace in a city is a blessing worth guarding. Both traditions draw the same practical lesson.

Proverbs 29:9

9If a wise man goes to court with a foolish man, the fool rages or scoffs, and there is no peace.

Arminian

When a wise man takes a dispute with a fool to court, the fool only rages or laughs, and nothing is resolved. Arminian readers find here a realistic caution about when argument is fruitless (compare 26:4–5).

Reformed

Wisdom cannot force a fool to listen; the fool answers with anger or ridicule, and there is no peace. Reformed readers, with Arminians, read it as practical counsel about the limits of reasoning with those who reject wisdom.

Proverbs 29:10

10The bloodthirsty hate a man of integrity; and they seek the life of the upright.

Arminian

The bloodthirsty hate a person of integrity and seek the life of the upright. The Hebrew of the second line may also be read “the upright seek his life,” in the sense of caring for him; the WEB takes the more common sense. Arminian readers note that righteousness can provoke hostility, a theme carried through Scripture to the sufferings of Christ.

Reformed

Violent people hate integrity because it stands as a rebuke to them. Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the long enmity between the righteous and the wicked that runs through Scripture (compare Gen 4:8; 1 John 3:12–13). Both traditions read the verse as realism about the cost of uprightness.

Proverbs 29:11

11A fool vents all of his anger, but a wise man brings himself under control.

Arminian

The fool gives full vent to his anger; the wise man holds himself back. Arminian readers stress self-control as a virtue to be cultivated by grace and practice, a fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:23).

Reformed

Unrestrained anger marks the fool; restraint marks the wise. Reformed readers, with Arminians, see self-control as part of the renewed life the Spirit produces, and they read the proverb as practical guidance for speech and temper (compare 14:29; 16:32).

Proverbs 29:12

12If a ruler listens to lies, all of his officials are wicked.

Arminian

A ruler who listens to lies will soon have officials who lie to him. The character of a leader shapes those who serve him. Arminian readers note the responsibility of leaders to welcome truth, since their choices affect a whole circle of others.

Reformed

If a ruler rewards falsehood, his court fills with corrupt servants. Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the moral influence that authority exerts downward and the duty of rulers to love truth. Both traditions read it as wisdom about leadership.

Proverbs 29:13

13The poor man and the oppressor have this in common: The LORD gives sight to the eyes of both.

Arminian

The poor man and the oppressor meet on common ground: the LORD gives light to both their eyes, that is, life and sight. The verse is close to 22:2. Arminian readers see God’s goodness toward all people and the shared humanity that should restrain the oppressor and give dignity to the poor.

Reformed

The LORD gives life to rich and poor, oppressor and oppressed alike. Reformed readers often speak here of God’s common goodness to all, and they share with Arminians the lesson that every person stands before the same Creator, which humbles the powerful and honors the lowly (compare Matt 5:45).

Proverbs 29:14

14The king who fairly judges the poor, his throne shall be established forever.

Arminian

The king who judges the poor faithfully has a throne established forever. Arminian readers see justice for the weak as the true measure and security of authority (compare 16:12; 20:28).

Reformed

A throne endures through fair judgment of the poor. Reformed readers, with Arminians, note that the ideal king of Israel was to defend the needy (compare Ps 72:1–4, 12–14), a hope that Christian readers see fulfilled in the righteous reign of Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
