Passage

Proverbs 29

Verses 15–27 · WEB

The final sayings of the Hezekiah collection turn to the discipline of children and servants, the danger of hasty speech, anger, and pride, and the need for God’s revelation, for without it “the people cast off restraint” (29:18). The chapter closes with trust in the LORD over the fear of man, the LORD as the source of justice, and the righteous and the wicked as an abomination to each other. Both traditions read these as wisdom sayings and share their meaning.

Proverbs 29:15

15The rod of correction gives wisdom, but a child left to himself causes shame to his mother.

Arminian

Correction and reproof give wisdom, while a child left to himself brings shame to his mother. The “rod” stands for loving discipline, as elsewhere in Proverbs (13:24; 22:15). Arminian readers stress that children need guidance to grow in wisdom and that parents bear real responsibility for training them.

Reformed

The verse sets disciplined correction against neglect. Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the folly bound up in a child’s heart (22:15) and the parental duty to bring children up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord (Eph 6:4). Both traditions read the verse as a call to loving, consistent training, not harshness (compare Col 3:21).

Proverbs 29:16

16When the wicked increase, sin increases; but the righteous will see their downfall.

Arminian

When the wicked multiply, wrongdoing multiplies with them, but the righteous will see their fall. Arminian readers find encouragement to persevere in righteousness even when evil seems to be gaining ground.

Reformed

Wickedness spreads as the wicked grow in number, yet their downfall is certain. Reformed readers, with Arminians, see God’s justice as finally prevailing in history and at the last judgment (compare Ps 37:34–36). Both traditions read the verse as a word of hope for the righteous.

Proverbs 29:17

17Correct your son, and he will give you peace; yes, he will bring delight to your soul.

Arminian

Correcting one’s son brings peace and delight to the parent’s soul. Arminian readers note the long view of discipline: what is hard in the moment yields lasting joy for both parent and child.

Reformed

Discipline is not opposed to delight; it leads to it. Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see faithful correction as a form of love that mirrors the Father’s discipline of his children (compare Heb 12:10–11). Both traditions read the verse as encouragement to patient training.

Proverbs 29:18

18Where there is no revelation, the people cast off restraint; but one who keeps the law is blessed.

Arminian

Where there is no revelation (the Hebrew word is often rendered “vision,” prophetic revelation from God), the people cast off restraint and run wild. The second line blesses the one who keeps the law. Arminian readers stress that God’s word, made known and heeded, is what keeps a people in order and leads to blessing.

Reformed

The verse is often quoted about “vision” in the sense of planning, but in context it concerns revelation from God and keeping his law. Without God’s word a people becomes unrestrained; the one who keeps it is blessed. Reformed readers, sharing this reading with Arminians, stress the necessity of God’s revealed word for the life of his people (compare Amos 8:11–12; Ps 19:7–11).

Proverbs 29:19

19A servant can’t be corrected by words. Though he understands, yet he will not respond.

Arminian

A servant may understand words of correction yet not respond. Arminian readers take the proverb as an observation about the limits of mere words when the will is unwilling, and about the need for correction that reaches the heart.

Reformed

The servant understands but will not answer or obey. Reformed readers, with Arminians, read it as practical wisdom from household life: knowing what is right is not the same as doing it. The proverb describes a social setting of its time rather than endorsing every practice of it.

Proverbs 29:20

20Do you see a man who is hasty in his words? There is more hope for a fool than for him.

Arminian

There is more hope for a fool than for a person hasty in speech. The saying deliberately shocks, since the fool is usually the lowest figure in Proverbs (compare 26:12). Arminian readers hear a call to slow, careful speech (compare Jas 1:19).

Reformed

Rash words are worse than folly itself. Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see the tongue as an index of the heart and a frequent source of sin (compare Jas 3:2–8). Both traditions take the verse as a pointed warning against hasty speech.

Proverbs 29:21

21He who pampers his servant from youth will have him become a son in the end.

Arminian

The meaning of the last word is uncertain; the WEB reads “a son,” while other translations suggest “grief” or “insolence.” On any reading, the pampered servant does not turn out as the master intended. Arminian readers note the proverb’s concern with wise, balanced treatment in household life.

Reformed

Because the closing Hebrew word is rare, interpreters differ on whether the pampered servant becomes like a son or becomes a trouble. Reformed readers, with Arminians, read the verse modestly as practical counsel about indulgence and order in the household, without building doctrine on an uncertain term.

Proverbs 29:22

22An angry man stirs up strife, and a wrathful man abounds in sin.

Arminian

The angry man stirs up strife, and the hot-tempered man abounds in wrongdoing. Arminian readers see anger as a door through which many other sins enter, and the need to master it (compare Eph 4:26–27).

Reformed

Unchecked anger multiplies sin and conflict. Reformed readers, sharing this reading, note how often Proverbs links anger with strife (15:18; 30:33). Both traditions read the verse as a warning to guard one’s temper.

Proverbs 29:23

23A man’s pride brings him low, but one of lowly spirit gains honor.

Arminian

Pride brings a person low, but the humble in spirit gain honor. Arminian readers hear the pattern that Jesus himself taught: the one who exalts himself will be humbled (Luke 14:11).

Reformed

The reversal of pride and humility runs throughout Scripture (compare 16:18; 18:12; 1 Pet 5:5–6). Reformed readers, with Arminians, see humility as the posture that receives honor from God, and pride as the sin that brings its own fall.

Proverbs 29:24

24Whoever is an accomplice of a thief is an enemy of his own soul. He takes an oath, but dares not testify.

Arminian

The partner of a thief harms his own soul. When the public oath or curse is announced calling witnesses to come forward (compare Lev 5:1), he hears it but dares not speak, and so he adds guilt to guilt. Arminian readers stress that complicity is a real choice with real consequences.

Reformed

The accomplice is trapped: if he testifies he exposes himself, and if he keeps silent he falls under the curse of the oath. Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see how sin entangles and how fellowship in wrongdoing becomes self-destructive. Both traditions read the verse the same way.

Proverbs 29:25

25The fear of man proves to be a snare, but whoever puts his trust in the LORD is kept safe.

Arminian

Fear of people is a snare, but trust in the LORD brings safety; the Hebrew image is of being set securely on high. Arminian readers see a call to rely on God rather than on human approval, a trust that frees a person to do what is right.

Reformed

Fear of man traps the soul; trust in the LORD lifts it to safety. Reformed readers, sharing this reading, set this verse beside the call to fear God rather than people (compare Isa 51:12–13; Matt 10:28). Both traditions read it as a call to steady trust in God over the opinions and threats of others.

Proverbs 29:26

26Many seek the ruler’s favor, but a man’s justice comes from the LORD.

Arminian

Many seek the ruler’s favor, but true justice comes from the LORD. Arminian readers see a reminder that human courts and patrons are not the final judge; people should seek justice from God and act justly before him.

Reformed

Seeking influence with rulers is common, but the final verdict belongs to the LORD (compare 16:33; 21:1). Reformed readers, sharing this reading, see God’s sovereignty over human courts and the comfort this gives to those wronged. Both traditions read the verse as a call to look beyond human power to God.

Proverbs 29:27

27A dishonest man detests the righteous, and the upright in their ways detest the wicked.

Arminian

The collection closes with a mutual aversion: the unjust man is an abomination to the righteous, and the upright are an abomination to the wicked. Arminian readers see in it the deep moral divide between two ways of life that Proverbs has traced from the start.

Reformed

The word for “detest” is the strong word often rendered “abomination.” The righteous and the wicked find each other’s ways intolerable. Reformed readers, with Arminians, see here the two ways of Psalm 1 and Proverbs as a whole, and the hostility that holiness and wickedness have toward each other. The verse closes the Hezekiah collection that began at 25:1.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
