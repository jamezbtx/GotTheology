Passage

Genesis 30

Verses 25–43 · WEB · Voices

After Joseph's birth, Jacob asks Laban for leave to go home. Laban, having discovered by divination that the LORD has blessed him for Jacob’s sake, bargains over wages; Jacob proposes the speckled and spotted. Laban cheats by removing those animals; Jacob pastured the rest and uses peeled sticks. The man increases greatly—labor and cunning on the page, with chapter 31 soon crediting the increase to God.

Genesis 30:25

25When Rachel had borne Joseph, Jacob said to Laban, “Send me away, that I may go to my own place, and to my country.

Arminian

Joseph born, Jacob asks to go home. The years of service are enough; the land of his fathers calls. He does not steal away yet; he asks.

The request is responsible pilgrimage. Grace had promised return at Bethel; Jacob now seeks it. Timing after Joseph is a free choice to move, not a trance.

Reformed

Timing is tied to Joseph's birth. The heir-household is full enough to travel in Jacob's mind. The Bethel promise included homecoming.

Providence has brought him to the ask. Joseph as turning point is not only Rachel's joy; it is also the cue for leaving Haran. Laban's answer will test the road.

Genesis 30:26

26Give me my wives and my children for whom I have served you, and let me go; for you know my service with which I have served you.”

Arminian

Give me my wives and children for whom I have served—you know the service. He claims what labor earned: family and leave.

The passage carries honest accounting. Service was no illusion; so is the desire to provide at home. Agency here is a worker asking a kinsman for what is due. Labor is a moral act, not a footnote.

Reformed

Wives and children are named as the fruit of service. Jacob speaks as a laborer under Laban's roof.

The family is his, yet still on Laban's ground. God will have to extract the promised seed from Haran. Service remembered is true; the larger extraction is still the Lord's.

Genesis 30:27

27Laban said to him, “If now I have found favor in your eyes, stay here, for I have divined that the LORD has blessed me for your sake.”

Arminian

Laban has learned by divination that the LORD blessed him because of Jacob. Pagan means, true conclusion: blessing attached to Jacob's presence, sincerely given.

Arminian readers do not baptize divination. They do hear overflow from the promised man's presence. Laban wants that gift to stay. Superstition can notice a true mercy and still refuse to obey it.

Reformed

Laban is pagan-tinged; the LORD still blessed him because of Jacob—for the promised seed's sake.

Common-grace overflow on a shrewd uncle does not make Laban a worshiper. God can enrich Haran because the heir is there, then take him home. Divination is not the Lord's appointed way; the blessing it noticed is still the Lord's.

Genesis 30:28

28He said, “Appoint me your wages, and I will give it.”

Arminian

Name your wages. Laban will pay to keep the blessing nearby. The offer is a bargain, not repentance.

Jacob must answer without being owned. Responsible wage-setting is about to begin. Naming a wage is a moral act in a house that has already cheated him once.

Reformed

Wages-talk continues the old pattern of chapter 29. Laban names generosity while aiming to retain the blessed man.

Providence will use a wage-deal Laban means to control. The LORD's blessing is not Laban's to cage. What Laban offers, God can overrule.

Genesis 30:29

29Jacob said to him, “You know how I have served you, and how your livestock have fared with me.

Arminian

Jacob answers with known facts: you know my service; your livestock fared with me. Integrity appeals to shared memory.

Wesleyan Arminians honor truthful testimony. Jacob's labor was agency under God. He will not let Laban pretend the increase was random. Industry is a moral act; so is telling the truth about it.

Reformed

Service and faring are placed in the record before the sticks appear.

God has already been blessing through Jacob's work. The next verse will name the LORD. Labor is the means; the increase is not finally Jacob's craft.

Genesis 30:30

30For it was little which you had before I came, and it has increased to a multitude. The LORD has blessed you wherever I turned. Now when will I provide for my own house also?”

Arminian

You had little; it increased; the LORD blessed you wherever I turned. Now when will I provide for my own house?

Blessing on Laban was true and attached to Jacob's presence. Providing for one's household is a sincere duty, not greed. He asks for room to obey that duty. Industry and care for his own are responsible acts.

Reformed

The LORD is named as the source of Laban's plenty. Increase is not Jacob's magic; it is the Lord's overflow on the heir's labor.

Abrahamic blessing is already wetting Haran's grass. God can enrich a pagan household for the seed's sake—and Jacob is right to seek his own house too. Providence over wages begins before the sticks.

Key contrast Both name the LORD as the source of Laban's increase. Arminians press Jacob's industry and his duty to provide as responsible acts God may bless; Reformed interpreters typically underline that the plenty is covenant overflow on the promised man, not a wage Laban can keep by keeping Jacob.

Genesis 30:31

31Laban said, “What shall I give you?” Jacob said, “You shall not give me anything. If you will do this thing for me, I will again feed your flock and keep it.

Arminian

Laban: what shall I give you? Jacob: you shall not give me anything. He proposes terms instead of a gift that would bind him.

Refusing Laban's gift is wise agency. He will work again, but on a condition he names. Grace does not forbid shrewd, honest dealing. The coming proposal is a moral choice.

Reformed

Jacob will not be paid in Laban's chosen coin. The counter-proposal is coming.

Providence can work through a wage Jacob designs. The Lord is not absent from negotiation. Refusing a gift from a cheater can still sit inside God's care for the heir.

Genesis 30:32

32I will pass through all your flock today, removing from there every speckled and spotted one, and every black one among the sheep, and the spotted and speckled among the goats. This will be my hire.

Arminian

Speckled, spotted, black—those shall be my wages. He asks for the uncommon coloring, easy to distinguish later.

Arminian readers see prudence, not theft. Honesty will need a visible mark. He still must labor; the flock remains Laban's to agree. Naming a modest wage is responsible dealing.

Reformed

The rare animals become the wage. What looks like a modest ask will, under God, become increase.

Chapter 31 will say God showed him the speckled. Already the terms sit inside a larger purpose Jacob does not yet narrate. Providence over wages is underway before a stick is peeled.

Genesis 30:33

33So my righteousness will answer for me hereafter, when you come concerning my hire that is before you. Every one that is not speckled and spotted among the goats, and black among the sheep, that might be with me, will be considered stolen.”

Arminian

My honesty will answer later. Anything unspeckled with me shall be stolen. He invites inspection.

He stakes his name on visible integrity. In a house of cheating, that is a moral stand. Honesty is not optional piety. We do not yet know the sticks; we do know he offers an audit.

Reformed

The rule is public: wrong color equals theft. Jacob tries to make the future audit simple.

Integrity is even when Laban will immediately cheat. God can vindicate an honest wage Laban means to empty. Honesty here is not the last cause of increase; it is still a true word.

Genesis 30:34

34Laban said, “Behold, let it be according to your word.”

Arminian

Laban says Good—let it be as you said. Agreement is easy when he already plans to drain the wage-pool.

Good on a cheater's lips is not covenant. Jacob must still live with a man who consents in order to undercut. Laban's yes is a moral act—and it is already hollow.

Reformed

The deal is struck. Laban's yes is the setup for verse 35.

God has heard the terms. What Laban removes that day cannot cancel the Lord's intent to prosper Jacob. A cheater's Good does not bind heaven.

Genesis 30:35

35That day, he removed the male goats that were streaked and spotted, and all the female goats that were speckled and spotted, every one that had white in it, and all the black ones among the sheep, and gave them into the hand of his sons.

Arminian

That very day Laban strips out striped, speckled, spotted, white-marked, and black—and gives them to his sons. He empties the wage before work starts.

Arminian readers name it theft-by-preemption. Jacob's honesty is met with family conspiracy. Cheating is a moral act, not a tool God needed.

Reformed

Laban acts that day. The cheater uses the agreement as cover.

Providence allows the flock to be gutted so that later increase cannot be credited to Laban's generosity. God will still prosper His servant. Laban's sin is his; the Lord's care for Jacob is not denied.

Key contrast Both condemn Laban's same-day removal as cheating. Arminians stress it as a responsible, culpable fraud against an agreed wage; Reformed readers underline the same fraud inside providence—God permitting the emptying so that Jacob's later increase will be seen as the Lord's, not Laban's gift.

Genesis 30:36

36He set three days’ journey between himself and Jacob, and Jacob fed the rest of Laban’s flocks.

Arminian

Jacob is left with the rest—the unmarked colors—to pasture.

Distance is control. Jacob keeps working anyway. Faithful labor under unfair terms is still labor God can bless. Industry continues when the wage-pool has been stolen.

Reformed

Space is imposed so the marked animals cannot mix back. Laban thinks he has won.

The heir pastured the rest. The stage is set for God to bring speckled from the unmarked. Laban's hedge cannot bind the Lord. Distance is not stronger than providence.

Genesis 30:37

37Jacob took to himself rods of fresh poplar, almond, and plane tree, peeled white streaks in them, and made the white appear which was in the rods.

Arminian

Jacob peels poplar, almond, and plane sticks, exposing white. A stratagem, odd to us, begins. He uses the means he thinks will work.

Arminian readers can admit a man using imperfect means—without calling the sticks a sacrament. God may bless even flawed methods; that is not the same as baptizing folk craft.

Reformed

Chapter 31 will say God showed him the speckled; here we see the method.

God can overrule means, even odd ones, without making the peeled bark the true cause. The Lord of flocks is not a prisoner of folk breeding-craft. The increase will be God's blessing, not magic sticks as such.

Genesis 30:38

38He set the rods which he had peeled opposite the flocks in the watering troughs where the flocks came to drink. They conceived when they came to drink.

Arminian

Sticks in the troughs at watering, where they bred. He places his device at the ordinary meeting of thirst and mating.

This is human ingenuity at a God-governed watering place. We need not pretend the sticks forced heaven. We also need not mock a shepherd using the tools he has. Imperfect means remain his responsible act.

Reformed

Watering places become the theater. Breeding is timed to drink.

Providence already rules when flocks drink and conceive. Sticks may be Jacob's theory; conception remains the Lord's. Chapter 31 will not credit the wood.

Genesis 30:39

39The flocks conceived before the rods, and the flocks produced streaked, speckled, and spotted.

Arminian

They bred in front of the sticks and brought forth striped, speckled, spotted. The result matches the wage-class Laban had tried to drain.

God can prosper His servant even through odd methods. The outcome is mercy, not proof that magic works. Honesty still stands against Laban's cheating. We do not baptize the device; we do not deny God's care.

Reformed

The narrator reports the births. Readers who know chapter 31 hear God behind the speckled (31:7–12).

The Lord can bring the wage-colors from unmarked ewes. Means are not denied; they are subordinated. Jacob's increase is finally God's blessing, not magic sticks as such.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat peeled sticks as a sacrament and refuse to deny God's care for Jacob. Arminians typically allow that God may bless even imperfect means Jacob actually used; Calvin’s heirs generally read the births through 31:7–12—God, not the wood, giving the speckled as the true wage.

Genesis 30:40

40Jacob separated the lambs, and set the faces of the flocks toward the streaked and all the black in Laban’s flock. He put his own droves apart, and didn’t put them into Laban’s flock.

Arminian

He separates lambs, sets faces toward striped and black, keeps his droves apart. Husbandry continues after the sticks.

Responsible stewardship—he protects what God has begun to give. Separation is prudence, not theft. He will not mix his with Laban's to invite a new quarrel. Industry remains a moral act.

Reformed

Faces set, droves apart. Jacob works the flock with intent.

Providence uses a shepherd's separations. The promised man's wealth is being sorted in broad daylight. Skill is not imaginary; it is still under the God who later says He took the livestock and gave them to Jacob.

Genesis 30:41

41Whenever the stronger of the flock conceived, Jacob laid the rods in front of the eyes of the flock in the watering troughs, that they might conceive among the rods;

Arminian

Stronger animals get the sticks; he aims the method at vigor. He is not only pious; he is a selective breeder.

Arminians allow skill under God. Using means is not unbelief if the heart still knows the LORD gives increase. We still refuse to call the sticks a guaranteed charm. Selective breeding is a mixed act.

Reformed

Jacob sets the rods before the stronger animals. Jacob wants the robust offspring as his.

Even the selection of the strong sits under a God who can give or withhold. Chapter 31 will not credit the wood; it will credit the God who showed him the flock. Cunning is on the page; blessing is still the Lord's.

Genesis 30:42

42but when the flock were feeble, he didn’t put them in. So the feebler were Laban’s, and the stronger Jacob’s.

Arminian

Feebler without sticks: those for Laban, stronger for Jacob. The split is deliberate.

In practice: shrewdness toward a cheater differs from cheating. Still, the verse does not make Jacob a finished saint. God may honor the oppressed laborer without rubber-stamping every tactic. We do not baptize deception; we do not deny care.

Reformed

The feeble lambs go to Laban and the strong ones to Jacob. The narrator states the outcome of the selection.

God can let the stronger fall to the heir while Laban keeps the weak. Justice in a bent house often looks like this quiet reversal. The split serves the blessing promised to Jacob, not a theory of magic wood.

Genesis 30:43

43The man increased exceedingly, and had large flocks, female servants and male servants, and camels and donkeys.

Arminian

Thus the man increased greatly—flocks, servants, camels, donkeys. Abrahamic plenty shows up in Haran.

Arminians see providence advancing the blessing promised at Bethel. Increase is God's kindness through labor, odd means, and endurance under a cheat. Gratitude, not magic, is the right close. Industry was genuine; so was Laban's fraud; so is God's care.

Reformed

Great increase: the Abrahamic blessing advancing. The man who slept on a stone now holds caravans.

God prospered His servant (cf. 31:7–12). Laban's divination was half-right—the LORD blesses because of the promised seed—and that blessing now visibly clings to Jacob himself, ready for the road home. The sticks are not the last word; God is.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What older interpreters in each camp said about wages, spotted flocks, and God prospering Jacob.

Arminian Notable voices

“Laban was willing to consent to this bargain, because he thought if those few he had that were now speckled and spotted were separated from the rest, which was to be done immediately, the body of the flock which Jacob was to tend, being of one colour, either all black or all white, would produce few or none of mixt colours, and so he should have Jacob's service for nothing, or next to nothing. According to this bargain, those few that were party-coloured were separated, and put into the hands of Laban's sons, and sent three days journey off: so great was Laban's jealouly lest any of those should mix with the rest of the flock to the advantage of Jacob.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 30:34)

Reformed Notable voices

“Seeing that Jacob had been retained by a proposed reward for his services, it might appear that he was acting craftily in desiring his dismissal from his father-in-law. I cannot, however, doubt that the desire to return had already entered his mind, and that he ingenuously avowed his intention. First; having experienced, in many ways, how unjust, how perfidious, and even cruel, Laban had been, there is no wonder that he should wish to depart from him, as soon as ever the opportunity was afforded.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 30:25)
