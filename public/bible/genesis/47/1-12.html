Passage

Genesis 47

Verses 1–12 · ESV · Voices

After the reunion in chapter 46, Joseph presents his family to Pharaoh. Five brothers request Goshen as sojourners; Pharaoh grants the best of the land. Jacob blesses the king, names his life a 130-year sojourn, and Joseph settles and feeds the household in Rameses.

Genesis 47:1

1So Joseph went in and told Pharaoh, “My father and my brothers, with their flocks and herds and all that they possess, have come from the land of Canaan. They are now in the land of Goshen.”

Arminian

Joseph reports openly: father, brothers, flocks, and Goshen already. The family is not smuggled in; they stand as shepherds before the throne.

Classical Arminians hear responsible advocacy. Grace that saved the household now asks Joseph to tell the truth in a pagan court—honesty as the first act of settlement.

Reformed

The famine-story’s next move is speech to Pharaoh. Canaan’s remnant is named as already in Goshen.

Reformed accent: providence has parked them where Joseph said they would be (46:31–34). The report is not improvisation; it is the plan God has already used Joseph to lay.

Genesis 47:2

2And from among his brothers he took five men and presented them to Pharaoh.

Arminian

Five, not all twelve, are presented. Joseph shepherds the audience as well as the flock.

Arminian pastoral note: wisdom in public is still obedience. Grace enables a fitting presentation; the brothers must then speak as servants, not as princes of Canaan.

Reformed

Joseph selects and presents. The court sees a sample of the clan under his care.

Reformed reading: the man God raised still mediates. Presentation to Pharaoh is the means by which Goshen will be granted.

Genesis 47:3

3Pharaoh said to his brothers, “What is your occupation?” And they said to Pharaoh, “Your servants are shepherds, as our fathers were.”

Arminian

They answer as Joseph prepared them: shepherds, as our fathers were. Identity is vocation and lineage, spoken without shame.

Classical Arminians honor the truthful confession. They could have climbed toward Egyptian rank; they keep the fathers’ work. Faithfulness here is ordinary speech under a king’s question.

Reformed

Occupation is the gate to Goshen. “Your servants” and “as our fathers were” locate them in a calling Egypt despises (46:34).

Reformed note: God preserves a people by a despised trade. Shepherding will keep them distinct in the land that feeds them.

Genesis 47:4

4They said to Pharaoh, “We have come to sojourn in the land, for there is no pasture for your servants' flocks, for the famine is severe in the land of Canaan. And now, please let your servants dwell in the land of Goshen.”

Arminian

They ask to sojourn, not to seize. Famine, not ambition, is the plea; Goshen is requested as pasture.

Arminian emphasis: sojourning is honest about not owning Egypt. The request is grace-enabled humility—asking a place to live while remaining guests under God.

Reformed

“Sojourn” is the theological word (cf. 15:13). Severe famine in Canaan is the providence that moved them; Goshen is the asked-for shelter.

Reformed accent: the plea fulfills what God told Jacob: I will make you a great nation there (46:3). Even “please let” sits inside that oath.

Genesis 47:5

5Then Pharaoh said to Joseph, “Your father and your brothers have come to you.

Arminian

Pharaoh turns from the five to Joseph: they have come to you. The king recognizes the family as Joseph’s charge.

Classical Arminians see a door opened by prior faithfulness. Joseph’s years of service become cover for the household; gifts to one become mercy for many.

Reformed

The king states a fact Joseph already knows: they have come to you. Authority to settle them is about to be delegated.

Reformed comfort: God uses Pharaoh’s mouth to confirm the gathering He began in Joseph’s dreams.

Genesis 47:6

6The land of Egypt is before you. Settle your father and your brothers in the best of the land. Let them settle in the land of Goshen, and if you know any able men among them, put them in charge of my livestock.”

Arminian

Egypt is offered; Goshen is named; able men may keep Pharaoh’s livestock. The grant exceeds the request.

Arminian pastoral reading: unexpected favor is still to be received with integrity. Charge over royal flocks would be stewardship, not a chance to stop being sojourners.

Reformed

“Best of the land” and “Goshen” and royal livestock: Pharaoh’s generosity is real, and it is under a higher Hand.

Reformed note: the family is settled by command of the throne God has already bent (41:37–44). Able men in charge keep them near pasture and distinct.

Genesis 47:7

7Then Joseph brought in Jacob his father and stood him before Pharaoh, and Jacob blessed Pharaoh.

Arminian

The aged sojourner blesses the emperor. Hebrews will remember this as faith (11:21, with v. 31). The lesser, in the world’s eyes, speaks first in God’s economy.

Classical Arminians hear a priestly responsibility: Jacob does not merely bow. Grace enables him to bless a king who does not yet know the LORD, treating the moment as holy.

Reformed

Joseph stands Jacob before Pharaoh; Jacob blesses. The direction of blessing runs from the patriarch to the throne.

Reformed gospel: Melchizedek’s logic lingers—the greater blesses. God has put His man in a position to speak grace over Egypt’s king.

Genesis 47:8

8And Pharaoh said to Jacob, “How many are the days of the years of your life?”

Arminian

Pharaoh asks for a number. Court courtesy meets a pilgrim’s biography.

Arminian note: the question invites testimony. Jacob may now interpret his years before a pagan hearer—an opening grace gives and he must use.

Reformed

The king’s question is ordinary; the answer will be theology. Days and years are about to be named as sojourning.

Reformed accent: even small talk in this court is gathered into the story God is telling.

Genesis 47:9

9And Jacob said to Pharaoh, “The days of the years of my sojourning are 130 years. Few and evil have been the days of the years of my life, and they have not attained to the days of the years of the life of my fathers in the days of their sojourning.”

Arminian

Jacob will not call it a reign: sojourning, few and evil, shorter than the fathers. Honesty about hardship is not unbelief; it is pilgrim speech (Heb 11:13).

Classical Arminians hear a summons to the same confession. Life is gift and grief together. Grace does not require pretending the years were easy; it requires naming them as a sojourn toward God’s promise, not as a settled home.

Reformed

Twice he says “sojourning.” 130 years are dated as exile-on-the-way, few and evil beside Abraham and Isaac.

Reformed comfort: the covenant does not flatten pain. God still keeps the man who calls his days evil. The fathers’ longer sojourns underline that the land remains ahead—even in Goshen.

Key contrast Both hear pilgrim theology. Arminians typically stress Jacob’s responsible, grace-enabled confession of sojourning that hearers should share; Reformed readers typically stress that even “few and evil” years sit inside God’s sworn purpose to make a nation in Egypt.

Genesis 47:10

10And Jacob blessed Pharaoh and went out from the presence of Pharaoh.

Arminian

Blessing frames the audience: he blessed on the way in and on the way out. Departure is as liturgical as entrance.

Arminian pastoral word: leave the powerful with a blessing, not a scheme. Jacob’s last act in the court is still to speak good under God.

Reformed

The unit of the audience closes as it opened: blessing, then exit.

Reformed note: the patriarch does not linger to manage Egypt. He has done what faith does—blessed—and gone out.

Genesis 47:11

11Then Joseph settled his father and his brothers and gave them a possession in the land of Egypt, in the best of the land, in the land of Rameses, as Pharaoh had commanded.

Arminian

Settlement, possession, best of the land, Rameses—commanded by Pharaoh, executed by Joseph. The family now has a place to live the sojourn.

Classical Arminians see gift that still asks faithful use. Possession in Egypt is mercy for famine years; it must not become a substitute for the land promised to the fathers.

Reformed

“As Pharaoh had commanded” ties the grant to the throne God already rules. Rameses names the district of the best land.

Reformed accent: possession here is providence, not the end of pilgrimage. God parks the seed where it will multiply (v. 27).

Genesis 47:12

12And Joseph provided his father, his brothers, and all his father's household with food, according to the number of their dependents.

Arminian

Food is measured to dependents—care that counts mouths. Joseph’s love is logistics.

Arminian reading: those who have received bread become keepers of others. Grace that filled Joseph’s storehouses now feeds the household without favoritism of rank, according to need.

Reformed

Provision is named as Joseph’s act and as household-wide. The same administrator who fed nations now rations his own by number.

Reformed comfort: the dreams of sheaves end in bread for Jacob’s house. God keeps the family through the brother He sent ahead.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jacob blessing Pharaoh, and the family settled in the best of the land—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 47:1–12.

Arminian Notable voices

“Observe Jacob calls his life a pilgrimage, looking upon himself as a stranger in this world, and a traveller towards another. He reckoned himself not only a pilgrim now he was in Egypt, a strange country in which he never was before, but his life even in the land of his nativity was a pilgrimage. He reckoned his life by days; for even so it is soon reckoned, and we are not sure of the continuance of it for a day to an end, but may be turned out of this tabernacle at less than an hours warning.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 47:9)

Reformed Notable voices

“This confession was humiliating to the sons of Jacob, and especially to Joseph himself, whose high, and almost regal dignity, was thus marked with a spot of disgrace: for among the Egyptians (as we have said) this kind of life was disgraceful and infamous. Why, then, did not Joseph adopt the course, which he might easily have done, of describing his brethren as persons engaged in agriculture, or any other honest and creditable method of living?”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 47:3)
