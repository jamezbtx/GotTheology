Passage

Genesis 47

Verses 1–12 · WEB · Voices

After the reunion in chapter 46, Joseph presents his family to Pharaoh. Five brothers request Goshen as sojourners; Pharaoh grants the best of the land. Jacob blesses the king, names his life a 130-year sojourn, and Joseph settles and feeds the household in Rameses.

Genesis 47:1

1Then Joseph went in and told Pharaoh, and said, “My father and my brothers, with their flocks, their herds, and all that they own, have come out of the land of Canaan; and behold, they are in the land of Goshen.”

Arminian

Joseph reports openly: father, brothers, flocks, and Goshen already. The family is not smuggled in; they stand as shepherds before the throne.

Arminian interpreters hear responsible advocacy. Grace that saved the household now asks Joseph to tell the truth in a pagan court—honesty as the first act of settlement.

Reformed

The famine-story’s next move is speech to Pharaoh. Canaan’s remnant is named as already in Goshen.

Providence has parked them where Joseph said they would be (46:31–34). The report is not improvisation; it is the plan God has already used Joseph to lay.

Genesis 47:2

2From among his brothers he took five men, and presented them to Pharaoh.

Arminian

Five, not all twelve, are presented. Joseph shepherds the audience as well as the flock.

Pastorally: wisdom in public is still obedience. Grace enables a fitting presentation; the brothers must then speak as servants, not as princes of Canaan.

Reformed

Joseph chooses five brothers and presents them. The court sees a sample of the clan under his care.

The man God raised still mediates. Presentation to Pharaoh is the means by which Goshen will be granted.

Genesis 47:3

3Pharaoh said to his brothers, “What is your occupation?” They said to Pharaoh, “Your servants are shepherds, both we, and our fathers.”

Arminian

They answer as Joseph prepared them: shepherds, as our fathers were. Identity is vocation and lineage, spoken without shame.

Arminian readers honor the truthful confession. They could have climbed toward Egyptian rank; they keep the fathers’ work. Faithfulness here is ordinary speech under a king’s question.

Reformed

Occupation is the gate to Goshen. “Your servants” and “both we, and our fathers” locate them in a calling Egypt despises (46:34).

God preserves a people by a despised trade. Shepherding will keep them distinct in the land that feeds them.

Genesis 47:4

4They also said to Pharaoh, “We have come to live as foreigners in the land, for there is no pasture for your servants’ flocks. For the famine is severe in the land of Canaan. Now therefore, please let your servants dwell in the land of Goshen.”

Arminian

They ask to sojourn, not to seize. Famine, not ambition, is the plea; Goshen is requested as pasture.

Sojourning is honest about not owning Egypt. The request is grace-enabled humility—asking a place to live while remaining guests under God.

Reformed

Sojourn is the theological word (cf. 15:13). Severe famine in Canaan is the providence that moved them; Goshen is the asked-for shelter.

The plea fulfills what God told Jacob: I will make you a great nation there (46:3). Even “please let” sits inside that oath.

Genesis 47:5

5Pharaoh spoke to Joseph, saying, “Your father and your brothers have come to you.

Arminian

Pharaoh turns from the five to Joseph: they have come to you. The king recognizes the family as Joseph’s charge.

Arminians see a door opened by prior faithfulness. Joseph’s years of service become cover for the household; gifts to one become mercy for many.

Reformed

The king states a fact Joseph already knows: they have come to you. Authority to settle them is about to be delegated.

God uses Pharaoh’s mouth to confirm the gathering He began in Joseph’s dreams.

Genesis 47:6

6The land of Egypt is before you. Make your father and your brothers dwell in the best of the land. Let them dwell in the land of Goshen. If you know any able men among them, then put them in charge of my livestock.”

Arminian

Egypt is offered; Goshen is named; able men may keep Pharaoh’s livestock. The grant exceeds the request.

Application: unexpected favor is still to be received with integrity. Charge over royal flocks would be stewardship, not a chance to stop being sojourners.

Reformed

“Best of the land” and “Goshen” and royal livestock: Pharaoh’s generosity is no illusion, and it is under a higher Hand.

The family is settled by command of the throne God has already bent (41:37–44). Able men in charge keep them near pasture and distinct.

Genesis 47:7

7Joseph brought in Jacob, his father, and set him before Pharaoh; and Jacob blessed Pharaoh.

Arminian

The aged sojourner blesses the emperor. Hebrews will remember this as faith (11:21, with v. 31). The lesser, in the world’s eyes, speaks first in God’s economy.

The passage carries a priestly responsibility: Jacob does not merely bow. Grace enables him to bless a king who does not yet know the LORD, treating the moment as holy.

Reformed

Joseph stands Jacob before Pharaoh; Jacob blesses. The direction of blessing runs from the patriarch to the throne.

Melchizedek’s logic lingers—the greater blesses. God has put His man in a position to speak grace over Egypt’s king.

Genesis 47:8

8Pharaoh said to Jacob, “How old are you?”

Arminian

Pharaoh asks for a number. Court courtesy meets a pilgrim’s biography.

The question invites testimony. Jacob may now interpret his years before a pagan hearer—an opening grace gives and he must use.

Reformed

The king’s question is ordinary; the answer will be theology. Days and years are about to be named as sojourning.

Even small talk in this court is gathered into the story God is telling.

Genesis 47:9

9Jacob said to Pharaoh, “The years of my pilgrimage are one hundred thirty years. The days of the years of my life have been few and evil. They have not attained to the days of the years of the life of my fathers in the days of their pilgrimage.”

Arminian

Jacob will not call it a reign: sojourning, few and evil, shorter than the fathers. Honesty about hardship is not unbelief; it is pilgrim speech (Heb 11:13).

Wesleyans hear a summons to the same confession. Life is gift and grief together. Grace does not require pretending the years were easy; it requires naming them as a sojourn toward God’s promise, not as a settled home.

Reformed

Twice he says sojourning. 130 years are dated as exile-on-the-way, few and evil beside Abraham and Isaac.

The covenant does not flatten pain. God still keeps the man who calls his days evil. The fathers’ longer sojourns underline that the land remains ahead—even in Goshen.

Key contrast Both hear pilgrim theology. Arminians press Jacob’s responsible, grace-enabled confession of sojourning that hearers should share; Calvinists press that even “few and evil” years sit inside God’s sworn purpose to make a nation in Egypt.

Genesis 47:10

10Jacob blessed Pharaoh, and went out from the presence of Pharaoh.

Arminian

Blessing frames the audience: he blessed on the way in and on the way out. Departure is as liturgical as entrance.

Leave the powerful with a blessing, not a scheme. Jacob’s last act in the court is still to speak good under God.

Reformed

The unit of the audience closes as it opened: blessing, then exit.

The patriarch does not linger to manage Egypt. He has done what faith does—blessed—and gone out.

Genesis 47:11

11Joseph placed his father and his brothers, and gave them a possession in the land of Egypt, in the best of the land, in the land of Rameses, as Pharaoh had commanded.

Arminian

The family now has a place to live the sojourn.

Classical Arminians see gift that still asks faithful use. Possession in Egypt is mercy for famine years; it must not become a substitute for the land promised to the fathers.

Reformed

“As Pharaoh had commanded” ties the grant to the throne God already rules. Rameses names the district of the best land.

Possession here is providence, not the end of pilgrimage. God parks the seed where it will multiply (v. 27).

Genesis 47:12

12Joseph provided his father, his brothers, and all of his father’s household with bread, according to the sizes of their families.

Arminian

Food is measured to dependents—care that counts mouths. Joseph’s love is logistics.

Those who have received bread become keepers of others. Grace that filled Joseph’s storehouses now feeds the household without favoritism of rank, according to need.

Reformed

Provision is named as Joseph’s act and as household-wide. The same administrator who fed nations now rations his own by number.

The dreams of sheaves end in bread for Jacob’s house. God keeps the family through the brother He sent ahead.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on Jacob blessing Pharaoh, and the family settled in the best of the land, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Observe Jacob calls his life a pilgrimage, looking upon himself as a stranger in this world, and a traveller towards another. He reckoned himself not only a pilgrim now he was in Egypt, a strange country in which he never was before, but his life even in the land of his nativity was a pilgrimage. He reckoned his life by days; for even so it is soon reckoned, and we are not sure of the continuance of it for a day to an end, but may be turned out of this tabernacle at less than an hours warning.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 47:9)

Reformed Notable voices

“This confession was humiliating to the sons of Jacob, and especially to Joseph himself, whose high, and almost regal dignity, was thus marked with a spot of disgrace: for among the Egyptians (as we have said) this kind of life was disgraceful and infamous. Why, then, did not Joseph adopt the course, which he might easily have done, of describing his brethren as persons engaged in agriculture, or any other honest and creditable method of living?”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 47:3)
