Passage

Genesis 47

Verses 13–26 · ESV · Voices

While Israel eats in Goshen, the famine empties Egypt and Canaan of money, livestock, land, and finally personal freedom. Joseph buys all for Pharaoh, exempts priestly land, issues seed, and fixes a fifth as statute—the people calling it salvation even as they become servants.

Genesis 47:13

13Now there was no food in all the land, for the famine was very severe, so that the land of Egypt and the land of Canaan languished by reason of the famine.

Arminian

The camera leaves Goshen’s table: no food, very severe, both lands languishing. Mercy to Israel does not cancel the wider wound.

Classical Arminians refuse to skip this sentence. Neighbor-lands suffer; the God who feeds Jacob’s house still summons compassion for those who languish.

Reformed

Severity is stated twice. Egypt and Canaan together languish—the stage for Joseph’s next policy.

Reformed note: the famine God announced in 41 is still the governing fact. What follows is administration under that hard providence, not a separate economic parable.

Genesis 47:14

14And Joseph gathered up all the money that was found in the land of Egypt and in the land of Canaan, in exchange for the grain that they bought. And Joseph brought the money into Pharaoh's house.

Arminian

Money for grain, then into Pharaoh’s house. Joseph does not pocket the crisis.

Arminian pastoral caution: integrity in office matters. He feeds the hungry and still refuses private enrichment—an obedience open to imitation, and a warning against using scarcity as a personal net.

Reformed

All the money found is gathered and delivered to Pharaoh. The storehouses of 41 now reverse the flow of silver.

Reformed accent: Joseph remains Pharaoh’s servant. The concentration of money is the first step of a policy the narrator will not satirize, though later verses will show its cost.

Genesis 47:15

15And when the money was all spent in the land of Egypt and in the land of Canaan, all the Egyptians came to Joseph and said, “Give us food. Why should we die before your eyes? For our money is gone.”

Arminian

Silver ends; the plea is life: why should we die before your eyes? Need speaks when currency cannot.

Classical Arminians hear a real cry Joseph must answer. Grace that stored grain was for this hour. The people are not wrong to ask; dying in sight of bread would be a scandal.

Reformed

Egypt and Canaan spend out together. The question “before your eyes” puts death in Joseph’s presence.

Reformed reading: the cry fulfills why the dreams were given. God put a remnant of food behind one man so this sentence would not be the last.

Genesis 47:16

16And Joseph answered, “Give your livestock, and I will give you food in exchange for your livestock, if your money is gone.”

Arminian

Next tender is livestock. Exchange continues; charity here is still a market under a dying land.

Arminian honesty: the text is not a modern manifesto. Joseph keeps them alive by successive trades. Readers may debate the justice of the terms; they should not deny that food is actually given.

Reformed

If money is gone, herds become currency. Joseph names the next medium without delay.

Reformed note: administration is stepwise. The same wisdom that stored grain now rations it against remaining assets—severe, and still a means of life.

Genesis 47:17

17So they brought their livestock to Joseph, and Joseph gave them food in exchange for the horses, the flocks, the herds, and the donkeys. He supplied them with food in exchange for all their livestock that year.

Arminian

Horses, flocks, herds, donkeys—all that year, food for flesh. The narrator lists the animals so we feel the emptying.

Arminian pastoral word: survival can cost the farm. Those who still eat should remember how close a people can come to having nothing left but a body and a field (v. 18).

Reformed

The year is bought with every class of animal. “Supplied them with food” is the other half of the ledger.

Reformed accent: God keeps a nation alive through this hard barter. The livestock now Pharaoh’s will matter when seed is issued (v. 23).

Genesis 47:18

18And when that year was ended, they came to him the following year and said to him, “We will not hide from my lord that our money is all spent. The herds of livestock are my lord's. There is nothing left in the sight of my lord but our bodies and our land.

Arminian

They hide nothing: money gone, herds his, only bodies and land left. Confession of poverty is the last capital.

Classical Arminians hear people telling the truth before power. The verse asks whether those who govern will treat bodies as image-bearers, not as a final inventory item.

Reformed

The following year strips the remainder. They name Joseph “my lord” and inventory themselves.

Reformed reading: the famine’s length is God’s, announced long before. What they have left is what the next verse will offer for sale.

Genesis 47:19

19Why should we die before your eyes, both we and our land? Buy us and our land for food, and we with our land will be servants to Pharaoh. And give us seed that we may live and not die, and that the land may not be desolate.”

Arminian

They propose the sale of selves and soil for food and seed—live, not die; land not desolate. Servanthood is their asked-for shelter.

Arminian caution: desperation can volunteer bondage. The plea for seed is good; the church should still feel the weight of “buy us.” Life is the goal they name; persons remain more than Pharaoh’s purchase.

Reformed

The offer originates with the people: buy us, we will be servants, give seed. Survival and sowing are the twin asks.

Reformed note: the land’s desolation is averted by this grim covenant. Providence here is preservation through servitude Egypt itself requests—not a license for later oppression in Exodus, which God will judge.

Genesis 47:20

20So Joseph bought all the land of Egypt for Pharaoh, for all the Egyptians sold their fields, because the famine was severe on them. The land became Pharaoh's.

Arminian

All the land becomes Pharaoh’s because the famine was severe and they sold. Title concentrates at the top.

Classical Arminians will not baptize every concentration of property. The verse explains the mechanism without calling it Eden. Readers may thank God people ate, and still ask what such centralization does to neighbors when the rain returns.

Reformed

The land became Pharaoh’s—narrated as completed fact under severe famine.

Reformed accent: Joseph executes what the people offered. God is keeping Egypt (and Israel in Goshen) alive; the political shape of that keeping is a throne holding the fields.

Genesis 47:21

21As for the people, he made servants of them from one end of Egypt to the other.

Arminian

ESV follows the ancient versions: he made servants of them nationwide. (Hebrew can read as relocation to cities.) Either way, status changes from end to end.

Arminian pastoral grief: hunger rewrote freedom. Saving life is real; so is the new yoke. The later Exodus will show that Egyptian servitude is not the people’s lasting calling.

Reformed

Nationwide servanthood (ESV) is the social face of v. 20. The famine’s policy now covers persons, not only parcels.

Reformed reading: this is not Israel’s slavery in Exodus 1; it is Egypt selling itself to its own king. God still writes both stories, and He will later hear a different cry.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat the verse as a simple morality play. Arminians typically stress the real human cost of becoming servants and the call to just dealing; Reformed readers typically stress that this nationwide change unfolds under the famine God ordained, preserving life without confusing it with Israel’s later bondage.

Genesis 47:22

22Only the land of the priests he did not buy, for the priests had a fixed allowance from Pharaoh and lived on the allowance that Pharaoh gave them; therefore they did not sell their land.

Arminian

Priestly land is the exception: a fixed allowance meant they need not sell. Privilege in a famine is named, not hidden.

Arminian note: religious establishments can be insulated while fields change hands. The text lets us see it; wisdom asks whether our own “allowances” numb us to a neighbor’s sale.

Reformed

The exception proves the policy. Pharaoh’s own cult is already on his payroll, so their land stays theirs.

Reformed accent: Joseph does not seize what the throne has already assigned. Even this carve-out sits inside the same administration.

Genesis 47:23

23Then Joseph said to the people, “Behold, I have this day bought you and your land for Pharaoh. Now here is seed for you, and you shall sow the land.

Arminian

He names the purchase, then puts seed in their hands: sow. Ownership shifted; vocation to farm remains.

Classical Arminians hear both the hard “bought” and the mercy of seed. Grace in a famine often looks like another planting, not a speech. They are still called to work the soil they no longer hold as their own.

Reformed

Behold: bought, therefore seed. Joseph interprets the day before he issues grain for sowing.

Reformed comfort: the aim is that the land not be desolate (v. 19). God keeps Egypt productive through this sentence—purchase first, then seed.

Genesis 47:24

24And at the harvests you shall give a fifth to Pharaoh, and four fifths shall be your own, as seed for the field and as food for yourselves and your households, and as food for your little ones.”

Arminian

A fifth to Pharaoh, four fifths for seed, households, and little ones. The tax is stated; the children are named.

Arminian reading: policy that remembers little ones is nearer justice than policy that forgets them. Four fifths remaining is not nothing; it is a floor under which hunger would return.

Reformed

The 20 percent matches Joseph’s earlier advice in plenty (41:34). Harvest math is now statute-in-waiting (v. 26).

Reformed note: little ones are budgeted. The God who sent the dreams still feeds households through this fifth.

Genesis 47:25

25And they said, “You have saved our lives; may it please my lord, we will be servants to Pharaoh.”

Arminian

They call it salvation: you have saved our lives—and they consent again to be servants. Gratitude and yoke arrive in one sentence.

Classical Arminians let both clauses stand. Life-saving is praiseworthy; “we will be servants” is not the gospel’s last word about persons. Thanksgiving should not silence later cries when a king forgets Joseph (Ex 1).

Reformed

The people interpret Joseph’s work as life-saving and ratify servanthood. The narrator records their verdict, not a modern editorial.

Reformed accent: in this hour they are right that they live because of what God did through Joseph. Exodus will tell another chapter without erasing this one.

Genesis 47:26

26So Joseph made it a statute concerning the land of Egypt, and it stands to this day, that Pharaoh should have the fifth; the land of the priests alone did not become Pharaoh's.

Arminian

The fifth becomes statute “to this day,” with priestly land still excepted. Crisis hardens into standing law.

Arminian warning: emergency measures outlive emergencies. Faithful readers thank God for preserved life and remain wary of statutes that quietly keep a people small.

Reformed

Joseph legislates what the famine taught. The narrator’s “to this day” marks a lasting Egyptian arrangement, priests excepted.

Reformed close of the unit: God used this statute to keep land in seed and people in food. Israel, meanwhile, is about to multiply in Goshen (v. 27)—under the same God, on different terms.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on famine policy, land for Pharaoh, and bread for the people—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 47:13–26.

Arminian Notable voices

“He removed them to cities—He transplanted them, to shew Pharaoh's sovereign power over them, and that they might, in time, forget their titles to their lands, and be the easier reconciled to their new condition of servitude. How hard soever this seems to have been upon them, they themselves were sensible of it as a great kindness, and were thankful they were not worse used.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 47:21)

Reformed Notable voices

“It was a miserable spectacle, and one which might have softened hearts of iron, to see rich farmers, who previously had kept provision stored in their granaries for others, now begging food. Therefore, Joseph might be deemed cruel, because he does not give bread gratuitously to those who are poor and exhausted, but robs them of all their cattle, sheep, and asses.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 47:16)
