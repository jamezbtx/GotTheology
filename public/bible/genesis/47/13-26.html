Passage

Genesis 47

Verses 13–26 · WEB · Voices

While Israel eats in Goshen, the famine empties Egypt and Canaan of money, livestock, land, and finally personal freedom. Joseph buys all for Pharaoh, exempts priestly land, issues seed, and fixes a fifth as statute—the people calling it salvation even as they become servants.

Genesis 47:13

13There was no bread in all the land; for the famine was very severe, so that the land of Egypt and the land of Canaan fainted by reason of the famine.

Arminian

The camera leaves Goshen’s table: no food, very severe, both lands languishing. Mercy to Israel does not cancel the wider wound.

Arminian readers refuse to skip this sentence. Neighbor-lands suffer; the God who feeds Jacob’s house still summons compassion for those who languish.

Reformed

Severity is stated twice. Egypt and Canaan together languish—the stage for Joseph’s next policy.

The famine God announced in 41 is still the governing fact. What follows is administration under that hard providence, not a separate economic parable.

Genesis 47:14

14Joseph gathered up all the money that was found in the land of Egypt, and in the land of Canaan, for the grain which they bought: and Joseph brought the money into Pharaoh’s house.

Arminian

Money for grain, then into Pharaoh’s house. Joseph does not pocket the crisis.

Joseph’s integrity in office matters. He feeds the hungry and still refuses private enrichment—an obedience open to imitation, and a warning against using scarcity as a personal net.

Reformed

All the money found is gathered and delivered to Pharaoh. The storehouses of 41 now reverse the flow of silver.

Joseph remains Pharaoh’s servant. The concentration of money is the first step of a policy the narrator will not satirize, though later verses will show its cost.

Genesis 47:15

15When the money was all spent in the land of Egypt, and in the land of Canaan, all the Egyptians came to Joseph, and said, “Give us bread, for why should we die in your presence? For our money fails.”

Arminian

Silver ends; the plea is life: why should we die before your eyes? Need speaks when currency cannot.

Wesleyan Arminians find a cry Joseph must answer. Grace that stored grain was for this hour. The people are not wrong to ask; dying in sight of bread would be a scandal.

Reformed

Egypt and Canaan spend out together. The question “before your eyes” puts death in Joseph’s presence.

The cry fulfills why the dreams were given. God put a remnant of food behind one man so this sentence would not be the last.

Genesis 47:16

16Joseph said, “Give me your livestock; and I will give you food for your livestock, if your money is gone.”

Arminian

Next tender is livestock. Exchange continues; charity here is still a market under a dying land.

The text is not a modern manifesto. Joseph keeps them alive by successive trades. Readers may debate the justice of the terms; they should not deny that food is actually given.

Reformed

If money is gone, herds become currency. Joseph names the next medium without delay.

Administration is stepwise. The same wisdom that stored grain now rations it against remaining assets—severe, and still a means of life.

Genesis 47:17

17They brought their livestock to Joseph, and Joseph gave them bread in exchange for the horses, and for the flocks, and for the herds, and for the donkeys: and he fed them with bread in exchange for all their livestock for that year.

Arminian

The narrator lists the animals so we feel the emptying.

Survival can cost the farm. Those who still eat should remember how close a people can come to having nothing left but a body and a field (v. 18).

Reformed

The year is bought with every class of animal. “Fed them with bread” is the other half of the ledger.

God keeps a nation alive through this hard barter. The livestock now Pharaoh’s will matter when seed is issued (v. 23).

Genesis 47:18

18When that year was ended, they came to him the second year, and said to him, “We will not hide from my lord how our money is all spent, and the herds of livestock are my lord’s. There is nothing left in the sight of my lord, but our bodies, and our lands.

Arminian

They hide nothing: money gone, herds his, only bodies and land left. Confession of poverty is the last capital.

Classical Arminians hear people telling the truth before power. The verse asks whether those who govern will treat bodies as image-bearers, not as a final inventory item.

Reformed

The following year strips the remainder. They name Joseph “my lord” and inventory themselves.

The famine’s length is God’s, announced long before. What they have left is what the next verse will offer for sale.

Genesis 47:19

19Why should we die before your eyes, both we and our land? Buy us and our land for bread, and we and our land will be servants to Pharaoh. Give us seed, that we may live, and not die, and that the land won’t be desolate.”

Arminian

They propose the sale of selves and soil for food and seed—live, not die; land not desolate. Servanthood is their asked-for shelter.

Desperation can volunteer bondage. The plea for seed is good; the church should still feel the weight of “buy us.” Life is the goal they name; persons remain more than Pharaoh’s purchase.

Reformed

The offer originates with the people: buy us, we will be servants, give seed. Survival and sowing are the twin asks.

The land’s desolation is averted by this grim covenant. Providence here is preservation through servitude Egypt itself requests—not a license for later oppression in Exodus, which God will judge.

Genesis 47:20

20So Joseph bought all the land of Egypt for Pharaoh, for every man of the Egyptians sold his field, because the famine was severe on them, and the land became Pharaoh’s.

Arminian

All the land becomes Pharaoh’s because the famine was severe and they sold. Title concentrates at the top.

Classical Arminians will not baptize every concentration of property. The verse explains the mechanism without calling it Eden. Readers may thank God people ate, and still ask what such centralization does to neighbors when the rain returns.

Reformed

The land became Pharaoh’s—narrated as completed fact under severe famine.

Joseph executes what the people offered. God is keeping Egypt (and Israel in Goshen) alive; the political shape of that keeping is a throne holding the fields.

Genesis 47:21

21As for the people, he moved them to the cities from one end of the border of Egypt even to the other end of it.

Arminian

The WEB follows the Hebrew: he moved the people to the cities from end to end. (The ancient versions read that he made servants of them.) Either way, status changes from end to end.

Hunger has cost the Egyptians their freedom. Saving life is true; so is the new yoke. The later Exodus will show that Egyptian servitude is not the people’s lasting calling.

Reformed

Moving the people nationwide (or, in the ancient versions, making them servants) is the social face of v. 20. The famine’s policy now covers persons, not only parcels.

This is not Israel’s slavery in Exodus 1; it is Egypt selling itself to its own king. God still writes both stories, and He will later hear a different cry.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat the verse as a simple morality play. Arminians underline the human cost of becoming servants and the call to just dealing; Reformed interpreters usually emphasize that this nationwide change unfolds under the famine God ordained, preserving life without confusing it with Israel’s later bondage.

Genesis 47:22

22Only he didn’t buy the land of the priests, for the priests had a portion from Pharaoh, and ate their portion which Pharaoh gave them. That is why they didn’t sell their land.

Arminian

Priestly land is the exception: a fixed allowance meant they need not sell. Privilege in a famine is named, not hidden.

Religious establishments can be insulated while fields change hands. The text lets us see it; wisdom asks whether our own “allowances” numb us to a neighbor’s sale.

Reformed

The exception proves the policy. Pharaoh’s own cult is already on his payroll, so their land stays theirs.

Joseph does not seize what the throne has already assigned. Even this carve-out sits inside the same administration.

Genesis 47:23

23Then Joseph said to the people, “Behold, I have bought you and your land today for Pharaoh. Behold, here is seed for you, and you shall sow the land.

Arminian

He names the purchase, then puts seed in their hands: sow. Ownership shifted; vocation to farm remains.

Arminian readers hear both the hard “bought” and the mercy of seed. Grace in a famine often looks like another planting, not a speech. They are still called to work the soil they no longer hold as their own.

Reformed

Joseph interprets the day before he issues grain for sowing.

The aim is that the land not be desolate (v. 19). God keeps Egypt productive through this sentence—purchase first, then seed.

Genesis 47:24

24It will happen at the harvests, that you shall give a fifth to Pharaoh, and four parts will be your own, for seed of the field, for your food, for them of your households, and for food for your little ones.”

Arminian

The tax is stated; the children are named.

Policy that remembers little ones is nearer justice than policy that forgets them. Four fifths remaining is not nothing; it is a floor under which hunger would return.

Reformed

The 20 percent matches Joseph’s earlier advice in plenty (41:34). Harvest math is now statute-in-waiting (v. 26).

Little ones are budgeted. The God who sent the dreams still feeds households through this fifth.

Genesis 47:25

25They said, “You have saved our lives! Let us find favor in the sight of my lord, and we will be Pharaoh’s servants.”

Arminian

They call it salvation: you have saved our lives—and they consent again to be servants. Gratitude and yoke arrive in one sentence.

Classical Arminians let both clauses stand. Life-saving is praiseworthy; “we will be Pharaoh’s servants” is not the gospel’s last word about persons. Thanksgiving should not silence later cries when a king forgets Joseph (Ex 1).

Reformed

The people interpret Joseph’s work as life-saving and ratify servanthood. The narrator records their verdict, not a modern editorial.

In this hour they are right that they live because of what God did through Joseph. Exodus will tell another chapter without erasing this one.

Genesis 47:26

26Joseph made it a statute concerning the land of Egypt to this day, that Pharaoh should have the fifth. Only the land of the priests alone didn’t become Pharaoh’s.

Arminian

The fifth becomes statute “to this day,” with priestly land still excepted. Crisis hardens into standing law.

Emergency measures outlive emergencies. Faithful readers thank God for preserved life and remain wary of statutes that quietly keep a people small.

Reformed

Joseph legislates what the famine taught. The narrator’s “to this day” marks a lasting Egyptian arrangement, priests excepted.

God used this statute to keep land in seed and people in food. Israel, meanwhile, is about to multiply in Goshen (v. 27)—under the same God, on different terms.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on famine policy, land for Pharaoh, and bread for the people, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“He removed them to cities—He transplanted them, to shew Pharaoh's sovereign power over them, and that they might, in time, forget their titles to their lands, and be the easier reconciled to their new condition of servitude. How hard soever this seems to have been upon them, they themselves were sensible of it as a great kindness, and were thankful they were not worse used.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 47:21)

Reformed Notable voices

“It was a miserable spectacle, and one which might have softened hearts of iron, to see rich farmers, who previously had kept provision stored in their granaries for others, now begging food. Therefore, Joseph might be deemed cruel, because he does not give bread gratuitously to those who are poor and exhausted, but robs them of all their cattle, sheep, and asses.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 47:16)
