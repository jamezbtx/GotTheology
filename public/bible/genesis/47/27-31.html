Passage

Genesis 47

Verses 27–31 · ESV · Voices

The famine policy ends; the camera returns to Israel in Goshen: possessions, fruitfulness, and Jacob’s remaining seventeen years. Near death he binds Joseph under oath not to bury him in Egypt but with the fathers, then bows on the bed.

Genesis 47:27

27Thus Israel settled in the land of Egypt, in the land of Goshen. And they gained possessions in it, and were fruitful and multiplied greatly.

Arminian

Settled, possessed, fruitful, multiplied greatly: the creation/covenant verbs land in Goshen. God keeps the “great nation” word of 46:3 even in a foreign pasture.

Classical Arminians see gift that still calls for pilgrim memory. Fruitfulness in Egypt is real mercy; it must not erase Jacob’s sojourning speech (v. 9) or the promised land ahead.

Reformed

“Thus” contrasts Goshen with the Egyptians’ sales. Israel gains while Egypt yields land to Pharaoh.

Reformed gospel: fruitful-and-multiply is God’s oath in motion (12; 35; 46:3). The famine that emptied Canaan fills Jacob’s house in Egypt by promise.

Key contrast Both see covenant fruitfulness in Goshen. Arminians typically stress received mercy that must not replace pilgrim hope; Reformed readers typically stress God’s effectual word making a nation in the very land of sojourning.

Genesis 47:28

28And Jacob lived in the land of Egypt seventeen years. So the days of Jacob, the years of his life, were 147 years.

Arminian

Seventeen years in Egypt—matching, in number, the years Joseph had with him before Dothan. 147 closes the life-count he began at 130 (v. 9).

Arminian pastoral reading: added years are gift. The man who called his days few and evil is given a long quiet chapter. Grace can still write peace after a hard story.

Reformed

The narrator dates the sojourn: seventeen years, 147 total. Jacob’s evil days (v. 9) were not the last measure.

Reformed note: God numbered the extra years in the place He sent him. The clock runs to the oath of the next verse.

Genesis 47:29

29And when the time drew near that Israel must die, he called his son Joseph and said to him, “If now I have found favor in your sight, put your hand under my thigh and promise to deal kindly and truly with me. Do not bury me in Egypt,

Arminian

Dying, he binds Joseph with the old oath-gesture: kindly and truly—not buried in Egypt. The last request is geography as faith.

Classical Arminians hear a responsible last will. Finding favor with Joseph is not flattery; it is a father asking a son to keep him pointed at the promise, not at Goshen’s comfort.

Reformed

“Must die” and “do not bury me in Egypt” belong together. Kindness and truth mean a grave with the fathers, not a pyramid’s shadow.

Reformed accent: Israel’s end is timed by God. The oath will tether the clan to Canaan while their bodies still multiply in Egypt.

Genesis 47:30

30but let me lie with my fathers. Carry me out of Egypt and bury me in their burying place.” He answered, “I will do as you have said.”

Arminian

Lie with the fathers: carry me out. Joseph’s “I will” is prompt. Burial is confession that Egypt was never home.

Arminian hope: even a funeral can preach. Choosing Machpelah over the Nile is a last act of trust others can imitate—dying toward the promise, not toward the pantry.

Reformed

The burying place of 23 is still the family plot. Joseph consents without delay.

Reformed comfort: the oath of the son serves the oath of God. Carry-me-out is how the sojourner’s body will preach Hebrews 11 until the bones go up in the Exodus.

Genesis 47:31

31And he said, “Swear to me”; and he swore to him. Then Israel bowed himself upon the head of his bed.

Arminian

Swear—and he swore. Then Israel bows on the bed (Heb 11:21; some read “staff”). The last posture is worship.

Classical Arminians see faith finishing in reverence. The oath is not distrust of Joseph; it is how dying saints lean on God’s promise. Hearers are called to the same bow: not Egypt’s best land as the end, but the God of the fathers.

Reformed

The oath is doubled, then the bow. Hebrews hears worship in this weakness: dying Israel leaning and praising.

Reformed close: God gets the last gesture of the chapter. The man of few-and-evil days ends in homage, already pointing past Goshen to the land of the oath.

Key contrast Both read the bow as faith, with Hebrews 11. Arminians typically emphasize Jacob’s responsible last act of worship and the call to die toward the promise; Reformed readers typically emphasize that this dying homage is itself the Spirit’s fruit in a man God has kept to the end.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Israel’s fruitfulness, and the oath to bury him in Canaan—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 47:27–31.

Arminian Notable voices

“He died by degrees; his candle was not blown out, but gradually burnt down, so that he saw, at some distance, the time drawing nigh. He would be buried in Canaan, not because Canaan was the land of his nativity, but in faith, because it was the land of promise, which he desired thus, as it were to keep possession of 'till the time should come when his posterity should be masters of it: and because it was a type of heaven, that better country, which he was in expectation of.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 47:29)

Reformed Notable voices

“Hence we infer, not only the anxiety of Jacob, but his invincible magnanimity. It is a proof of great courage, that none of the wealth or the pleasures of Egypt could so allure him, as to prevent him from sighing for the land of Canaan, in which he had always passed a painful and laborious life. But the constancy of his faith appeared still more excellent, when he, commanding his dead body to be carried back to Canaan, encouraged his sons to hope for deliverance.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 47:29)
