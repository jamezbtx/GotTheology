Passage

Genesis 25

Verses 19–34 · ESV

Isaac’s generations open with barren Rebekah, prayer granted, struggling twins, and the LORD’s oracle that the older shall serve the younger. The boys grow into hunter and tent-dweller; Esau sells his birthright for stew. The passage sets the conflict Paul will cite in Romans 9, without turning either brother into a simple moral cartoon.

Genesis 25:19

19These are the generations of Isaac, Abraham’s son: Abraham fathered Isaac,

Arminian

The story now follows Isaac, named as Abraham’s son—the line the previous verses distinguished.

Arminian note: generations are history under a sincere promise. The heir’s story begins because God kept His word to Abraham.

Reformed

Toledoth of Isaac: Abraham fathered Isaac. The narrator restates the sworn line.

Reformed accent: the covenant story narrows by God’s purpose. This heading is election taking genealogical form.

Genesis 25:20

20and Isaac was forty years old when he took Rebekah, the daughter of Bethuel the Aramean of Paddan-aram, the sister of Laban the Aramean, to be his wife.

Arminian

Forty years old, Rebekah from Paddan-aram, Laban named. Marriage is particular and historical.

Classical Arminians see providence in the match (ch. 24) without erasing Isaac’s real taking of a wife. Family ties eastward will matter.

Reformed

Age and ancestry are precise. God has placed Rebekah in Isaac’s house as the next mother of promise.

Reformed reading: the Aramean household is not accident; it is the Lord’s provision for the seed.

Genesis 25:21

21And Isaac prayed to the Lord for his wife, because she was barren. And the Lord granted his prayer, and Rebekah his wife conceived.

Arminian

Barrenness again; Isaac prays; the LORD grants; she conceives. Prayer is a real means.

Arminian pastoral: God sincerely hears and answers. The same Lord who opened Sarah’s womb opens Rebekah’s—kindness that invites asking, not fatalism that skips prayer.

Reformed

Isaac prays because she is barren; conception is the LORD’s grant.

Reformed comfort: the promised line is closed until God opens it. Prayer is the appointed means; the Gift-giver remains the One who grants.

Genesis 25:22

22The children struggled together within her, and she said, “If it is thus, why is this happening to me?” So she went to inquire of the Lord.

Arminian

Struggle in the womb, a cry of distress, inquiry of the LORD. Rebekah does not pretend the pain is fine.

Arminian note: she seeks God with a real question. Grace allows lament and inquiry; she is not scolded for asking why.

Reformed

The twins wrestle before birth. Rebekah inquires of the LORD rather than of midwives’ lore alone.

Reformed reading: the conflict is already God’s theater. The next verse will interpret what she cannot yet see.

Genesis 25:23

23And the Lord said to her, “Two nations are in your womb, and two peoples from within you shall be divided; the one shall be stronger than the other, the older shall serve the younger.”

Arminian

Two nations, two peoples, divided; the older shall serve the younger. Arminians typically hear corporate and national destiny: Edom and Israel, and God’s right to order history.

That does not deny sincere mercy or turn either child into a cipher. When Paul later cites this story, Arminian readers usually refuse to make it a proof that God withholds mercy from persons arbitrarily.

Reformed

The LORD speaks before the boys have done good or bad (as Paul notes in Rom 9:10–13). “The older shall serve the younger” overturns primogeniture by purpose, not by later works.

Reformed readers typically hear God’s purpose of election already in the womb: nations, yes, and the free choice that will run through Jacob’s line—not based on the twins’ future striving.

Key contrast Both take the oracle as God’s word before birth. Arminians typically hear corporate/national destiny and God’s right to order history without denying sincere mercy to persons; Reformed readers typically hear God’s purpose of election, not based on works, which Paul later cites in Romans 9.

Genesis 25:24

24When her days to give birth were completed, behold, there were twins in her womb.

Arminian

Days completed; twins, as the oracle said.

Arminian pastoral: the word matches the birth. God told the truth about what was in her; promise and history meet in the delivery.

Reformed

Behold—twins, exactly as spoken.

Reformed note: fulfillment is already under way. The LORD’s sentence is not a metaphor waiting for later invention; two sons arrive.

Genesis 25:25

25The first came out red, all his body like a hairy cloak, so they called his name Esau.

Arminian

Firstborn: red, hairy, named Esau. Appearance writes the name.

Arminians refuse to make Esau a cartoon of “free-will failure” from verse 25. He is a real first son, named for what they saw, not yet the man of the stew.

Reformed

The first is Esau, marked in body. Primogeniture would be his.

Reformed reading: the oracle already said the older would serve. The name records the child, not a verdict that he was born a villain.

Genesis 25:26

26Afterward his brother came out with his hand holding Esau’s heel, so his name was called Jacob. Isaac was sixty years old when she bore them.

Arminian

Heel-holder, named Jacob; Isaac is sixty. Twenty years of marriage before this birth (v. 20).

Arminian note: Jacob’s name already hints at grasping. Do not baptize that grasp as piety. Both boys are named; both will be flawed.

Reformed

Jacob takes the heel; the name sticks. Twenty years after the wedding, the promised seed’s sons arrive.

Reformed accent: the younger’s posture is in the birth itself. God’s purpose does not wait for their adult résumés—and Jacob is not thereby made a hero.

Genesis 25:27

27When the boys grew up, Esau was a skillful hunter, a man of the field, while Jacob was a quiet man, dwelling in tents.

Arminian

Grown differences: hunter of the field, quiet man of tents. Vocations are not destinies of salvation.

Arminian pastoral: neither skill nor quietness is righteousness. Do not map “field” onto reprobation or “tents” onto election as if temperament saved.

Reformed

Two ways of life emerge. Providence includes ordinary callings.

Reformed note: the oracle was about nations and service, not about hunting being wicked. Character will show later; this verse is description, not a final judgment.

Genesis 25:28

28Isaac loved Esau because he ate of his game, but Rebekah loved Jacob.

Arminian

Parental partiality, with a reason named for Isaac. Love split down the middle is a wound, not a model.

Classical Arminians warn: grace-enabled love in a household should not copy this divide. God can use even flawed affection; we should not bless it.

Reformed

Isaac’s love is tied to his palate; Rebekah’s to Jacob. The family is already uneven.

Reformed reading: God’s purpose will stand through, not because of, parental favoritism. Neither parent is the electing God.

Genesis 25:29

29Once when Jacob was cooking stew, Esau came in from the field, and he was exhausted.

Arminian

Ordinary scene: stew, exhaustion. Esau’s weariness is real; Jacob is at the pot.

Arminian note: the test is coming. Neither brother is forced by the narrator into a morality play yet—but choices are about to be made.

Reformed

Exhausted from the field, Esau meets Jacob’s cooking. Means and moment are ordinary.

Reformed accent: a birthright can be despised in a kitchen. Providence does not need a battlefield to expose a heart.

Genesis 25:30

30And Esau said to Jacob, “Let me eat some of that red stew, for I am exhausted!” (Therefore his name was called Edom.)

Arminian

“Let me eat…for I am exhausted”—appetite and the name Edom (red).

Arminians hear a real cry of weakness, not a cartoon. Hunger is not yet the whole sin; it is the setting in which he will treat the birthright as useless.

Reformed

Red stew, Edom’s name. The narrator links craving and identity.

Reformed note: exhaustion explains the request; it will not excuse despising what God had placed on the firstborn. Appetite is about to outtalk inheritance.

Genesis 25:31

31Jacob said, “Sell me your birthright now.”

Arminian

Jacob names the price: the birthright, now. Grasping is not faith.

Arminian pastoral: do not make Jacob a hero of election. He exploits a hungry brother. The oracle did not license this bargain as virtue.

Reformed

“Sell me your birthright now” is opportunism. God’s purpose that the older serve the younger does not baptize Jacob’s method.

Reformed readers should say so plainly: election is God’s; this sentence is Jacob’s scheme.

Genesis 25:32

32Esau said, “I am about to die; of what use is a birthright to me?”

Arminian

Hyperbole of hunger: about to die; birthright useless. Arminians hear responsible speech. He devalues what was his.

This is not “free will’s necessary failure” as a type; it is one man treating holy privilege as worthless (cf. Heb 12:16).

Reformed

“Of what use” is the theology of the moment: present stew over future right.

Reformed reading: unbelief talks like this. Hebrews will call him unholy for it. God’s purpose stands; Esau’s words are still his.

Genesis 25:33

33Jacob said, “Swear to me now.” So he swore to him and sold his birthright to Jacob.

Arminian

Oath demanded, oath given, sale completed. Both act. Jacob presses; Esau swears.

Classical Arminians see two responsible agents. The oracle is not cancelled by the oath, and the oath is not made righteous by the oracle.

Reformed

Swear now—Jacob wants it locked. Esau sells.

Reformed note: a sworn transfer happens in history. God’s prior word is not Jacob’s excuse; Esau’s oath is not a mere accident. Both wills are on the page.

Genesis 25:34

34Then Jacob gave Esau bread and lentil stew, and he ate and drank and rose and went his way. Thus Esau despised his birthright.

Arminian

Bread and lentils; he ate, drank, rose, went. The narrator’s verdict: Esau despised his birthright.

Arminians typically stress this as a responsible, tragic choice (Heb 12:16–17)—not a cartoon of free-will failure, and not Jacob as the righteous contrast. Both are flawed; Esau’s contempt is still real and warned against.

Reformed

The verbs are casual: ate, drank, rose, went. Despising is the theological word. Reformed readers hear a heart that treats covenant privilege as cheap.

They should not turn Jacob into a Calvinist hero. Hear Hebrews’ warning and, with Romans 9, that God’s purpose does not rest on the twins’ works.

Key contrast Both refuse to baptize Jacob or to make Esau a mere type of “free will.” Arminians typically stress Esau’s responsible contempt of a real privilege God had given; Reformed readers typically join that contempt to God’s prior purpose, which does not depend on the sale and is not based on either brother’s works.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
