Passage

Genesis 25

Verses 19–34 · WEB · Voices

Isaac’s generations open with barren Rebekah, prayer granted, struggling twins, and the LORD’s oracle that the elder will serve the younger. The boys grow into hunter and tent-dweller; Esau sells his birthright for stew. The passage sets the conflict Paul will cite in Romans 9, without turning either brother into a simple moral cartoon.

Genesis 25:19

19This is the history of the generations of Isaac, Abraham’s son. Abraham became the father of Isaac.

Arminian

The story now follows Isaac, named as Abraham’s son—the line the previous verses distinguished.

Generations are history under a sincere promise. The heir’s story begins because God kept His word to Abraham.

Reformed

Toledoth of Isaac: Abraham fathered Isaac. The narrator restates the sworn line.

The covenant story narrows by God’s purpose. This heading is election taking genealogical form.

Genesis 25:20

20Isaac was forty years old when he took Rebekah, the daughter of Bethuel the Syrian of Paddan Aram, the sister of Laban the Syrian, to be his wife.

Arminian

Forty years old, Rebekah from Paddan-aram, Laban named. Marriage is particular and historical.

Arminian readers see providence in the match (ch. 24) without erasing Isaac’s taking of a wife. Family ties eastward will matter.

Reformed

Age and ancestry are precise. God has placed Rebekah in Isaac’s house as the next mother of promise.

The Aramean household is not accident; it is the Lord’s provision for the seed.

Genesis 25:21

21Isaac entreated the LORD for his wife, because she was barren. The LORD was entreated by him, and Rebekah his wife conceived.

Arminian

Barrenness again; Isaac prays; the LORD grants; she conceives. Prayer is a means.

God sincerely hears and answers. The Lord who opened Sarah’s womb opens Rebekah’s—kindness that invites asking, not fatalism that skips prayer.

Reformed

Isaac prays because she is barren; conception is the LORD’s grant.

Comfort: the promised line is closed until God opens it. Prayer is the appointed means; the Gift-giver remains the One who grants.

Genesis 25:22

22The children struggled together within her. She said, “If it is like this, why do I live?” She went to inquire of the LORD.

Arminian

Struggle in the womb, a cry of distress, inquiry of the LORD. Rebekah does not pretend the pain is fine.

She seeks God with a question. Grace allows lament and inquiry; she is not scolded for asking why.

Reformed

The twins wrestle before birth. Rebekah inquires of the LORD rather than of midwives’ lore alone.

The conflict is already God’s theater. The next verse will interpret what she cannot yet see.

Genesis 25:23

23The LORD said to her, “Two nations are in your womb. Two peoples will be separated from your body. The one people will be stronger than the other people. The elder will serve the younger.”

Arminian

Two nations, two peoples, divided; the elder will serve the younger. Arminians hear corporate and national destiny: Edom and Israel, and God’s right to order history.

That does not deny sincere mercy or turn either child into a cipher. When Paul later cites this story, Arminian readers usually refuse to make it a proof that God withholds mercy from persons arbitrarily.

Reformed

The LORD speaks before the boys have done good or bad (as Paul notes in Rom 9:10–13). “The elder will serve the younger” overturns primogeniture by purpose, not by later works.

Calvinists typically hear God’s purpose of election already in the womb: nations, yes, and the free choice that will run through Jacob’s line—not based on the twins’ future striving.

Key contrast Both take the oracle as God’s word before birth. Arminians hear corporate/national destiny and God’s right to order history without denying sincere mercy to persons; Calvinists hear God’s purpose of election, not based on works, which Paul later cites in Romans 9.

Genesis 25:24

24When her days to be delivered were fulfilled, behold, there were twins in her womb.

Arminian

Days completed; twins, as the oracle said.

The word matches the birth. God told the truth about what was in her; promise and history meet in the delivery.

Reformed

The twins arrive exactly as God had said.

Fulfillment is already under way. The LORD’s sentence is not a metaphor waiting for later invention; two sons arrive.

Genesis 25:25

25The first came out red all over, like a hairy garment. They named him Esau.

Arminian

Firstborn: red, hairy, named Esau. Appearance writes the name.

Arminians refuse to make Esau a cartoon of “free-will failure” from verse 25. He is a first son, named for what they saw, not yet the man of the stew.

Reformed

The first is Esau, marked in body. Primogeniture would be his.

The oracle already said the older would serve. The name records the child, not a verdict that he was born a villain.

Genesis 25:26

26After that, his brother came out, and his hand had hold on Esau’s heel. He was named Jacob. Isaac was sixty years old when she bore them.

Arminian

Heel-holder, named Jacob; Isaac is sixty. Twenty years of marriage before this birth (v. 20).

Jacob’s name already hints at grasping. Do not baptize that grasp as piety. Both boys are named; both will be flawed.

Reformed

Jacob takes the heel; the name sticks. Twenty years after the wedding, the promised seed’s sons arrive.

The younger’s posture is in the birth itself. God’s purpose does not wait for their adult résumés—and Jacob is not thereby made a hero.

Genesis 25:27

27The boys grew. Esau was a skillful hunter, a man of the field. Jacob was a quiet man, living in tents.

Arminian

Grown differences: hunter of the field, quiet man of tents. Vocations are not destinies of salvation.

For the hearer: neither skill nor quietness is righteousness. Do not map “field” onto reprobation or “tents” onto election as if temperament saved.

Reformed

Two ways of life emerge. Providence includes ordinary callings.

The oracle was about nations and service, not about hunting being wicked. Character will show later; this verse is description, not a final judgment.

Genesis 25:28

28Now Isaac loved Esau, because he ate his venison. Rebekah loved Jacob.

Arminian

Parental partiality, with a reason named for Isaac. Love split down the middle is a wound, not a model.

Arminians warn: grace-enabled love in a household should not copy this divide. God can use even flawed affection; we should not bless it.

Reformed

Isaac’s love is tied to his palate; Rebekah’s to Jacob. The family is already uneven.

God’s purpose will stand through, not because of, parental favoritism. Neither parent is the electing God.

Genesis 25:29

29Jacob boiled stew. Esau came in from the field, and he was famished.

Arminian

The scene is ordinary: a pot of stew and a famished hunter. Esau’s weariness is not imaginary; Jacob is at the pot.

The test is coming. Neither brother is forced by the narrator into a morality play yet—but choices are about to be made.

Reformed

Exhausted from the field, Esau meets Jacob’s cooking. Means and moment are ordinary.

A birthright can be despised in a kitchen. Providence does not need a battlefield to expose a heart.

Genesis 25:30

30Esau said to Jacob, “Please feed me with some of that red stew, for I am famished.” Therefore his name was called Edom.

Arminian

“Let me eat…for I am exhausted”—appetite and the name Edom (red).

Arminians find a cry of weakness, not a cartoon. Hunger is not yet the whole sin; it is the setting in which he will treat the birthright as useless.

Reformed

The red stew gives Esau the name Edom. The narrator links craving and identity.

Exhaustion explains the request; it will not excuse despising what God had placed on the firstborn. Appetite is about to outtalk inheritance.

Genesis 25:31

31Jacob said, “First, sell me your birthright.”

Arminian

Jacob names the price: the birthright, now. Grasping is not faith.

In practice: do not make Jacob a hero of election. He exploits a hungry brother. The oracle did not license this bargain as virtue.

Reformed

“Sell me your birthright” is opportunism. God’s purpose that the older serve the younger does not baptize Jacob’s method.

Reformed readers should say so plainly: election is God’s; this sentence is Jacob’s scheme.

Genesis 25:32

32Esau said, “Behold, I am about to die. What good is the birthright to me?”

Arminian

Hyperbole of hunger: about to die; birthright useless. Arminians hear responsible speech. He devalues what was his.

This is not “free will’s necessary failure” as a type; it is one man treating holy privilege as worthless (cf. Heb 12:16).

Reformed

“What good” is the theology of the moment: present stew over future right.

This is how unbelief talks when present appetite outweighs a future promise. Hebrews will call him unholy for it. God’s purpose stands; Esau’s words are still his.

Genesis 25:33

33Jacob said, “Swear to me first.” He swore to him. He sold his birthright to Jacob.

Arminian

Oath demanded, oath given, sale completed. Both act. Jacob presses; Esau swears.

Classical Arminians see two responsible agents. The oracle is not cancelled by the oath, and the oath is not made righteous by the oracle.

Reformed

Swear now—Jacob wants it locked. Esau sells.

A sworn transfer happens in history. God’s prior word is not Jacob’s excuse; Esau’s oath is not a mere accident. Both wills are on the page.

Genesis 25:34

34Jacob gave Esau bread and lentil stew. He ate and drank, rose up, and went his way. So Esau despised his birthright.

Arminian

Bread and lentils; he ate, drank, rose, went. The narrator’s verdict: Esau despised his birthright.

Arminians stress this as a responsible, tragic choice (Heb 12:16–17)—not a cartoon of free-will failure, and not Jacob as the righteous contrast. Both are flawed; Esau’s contempt is authentic and warned against.

Reformed

The verbs are casual: ate, drank, rose, went. Despising is the theological word. Reformed readers hear a heart that treats covenant privilege as cheap.

They should not turn Jacob into a Calvinist hero. Hear Hebrews’ warning and, with Romans 9, that God’s purpose does not rest on the twins’ works.

Key contrast Both refuse to baptize Jacob or to make Esau a mere type of “free will.” Arminians stress Esau’s responsible contempt of a privilege God had given; Reformed readers typically join that contempt to God’s prior purpose, which does not depend on the sale and is not based on either brother’s works.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

On the twins, the oracle, and Esau selling his birthright, a few classic voices from each tradition.

Arminian Notable voices

“The children struggled within her—The commotion was altogether extra-ordinary, and made her very uneasy: If it be so, or, since it is so, why am I thus? - Before the want of children was her trouble, now the struggle of the children is no less so. The word and prayer, by which we now enquire of the Lord, give great relief to those that are upon any account perplexed: it is a mighty ease to spread our case before the Lord, and ask council at his mouth.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 25:22)

Reformed Notable voices

“Because what Moses has said concerning the Ishmaelites was incidental, he now returns to the principal subject of the history, for the purpose of describing the progress of the Church. He expressly calls her the sister of Laban the Syrian, who was hereafter to become the father-in-law of Jacob, and concerning whom he had many things to relate. But it is chiefly worthy of observation that he declares Rebekah to have been barren during the early years of her marriage.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 25:19)
