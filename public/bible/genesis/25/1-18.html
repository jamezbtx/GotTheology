Passage

Genesis 25

Verses 1–18 · ESV · Voices

After Sarah’s death the narrator records Abraham’s later sons by Keturah, the gift of the estate to Isaac, Abraham’s death and burial by Isaac and Ishmael, God’s blessing on Isaac, and Ishmael’s twelve princes. The chapter closes Abraham’s story while distinguishing the covenant heir from other real offspring.

Genesis 25:1

1Abraham took another wife, whose name was Keturah.

Arminian

Keturah is named as wife. Abraham’s household is larger than Isaac; later verse 6 will group her sons with the sons of his concubines.

Classical Arminians honor this real marriage and real children. God’s particular covenant with Isaac does not make other sons fictional or unloved.

Reformed

After Sarah, Abraham takes Keturah. The genealogy is history, not a detour from the promise.

Reformed readers already know Isaac is the sworn seed (Gen 17; 21). Other sons will be listed as gift, not as rivals to the oath.

Genesis 25:2

2She bore him Zimran, Jokshan, Medan, Midian, Ishbak, and Shuah.

Arminian

Six named sons, including Midian, who will matter later in Israel’s story. These are Abraham’s offspring in the ordinary sense.

Arminian pastoral note: God can be kind to many children of Abraham without collapsing the covenant into a single household.

Reformed

Named sons, including Midian. The line of promise is not the only line that exists.

Reformed reading: God records them because they are real, then will distinguish Isaac’s inheritance. Election of the seed does not erase neighbors on the family tree.

Genesis 25:3

3Jokshan fathered Sheba and Dedan. The sons of Dedan were Asshurim, Letushim, and Leummim.

Arminian

Grandsons and tribal names appear. Abraham’s fruitfulness through Keturah spreads into peoples.

Arminians hear sincere kindness granted beyond the covenant heir—real nations, real names, not a polite fiction.

Reformed

Sheba, Dedan, and Dedan’s clans are mapped. Providence writes extra-covenant peoples into Abraham’s story.

Reformed note: the narrator can honor these tribes without making them the seed of promise.

Genesis 25:4

4The sons of Midian were Ephah, Epher, Hanoch, Abida, and Eldaah. All these were the children of Keturah.

Arminian

Midian’s sons are listed; “all these were the children of Keturah” closes the branch.

Classical Arminians refuse to treat them as a footnote of shame. They are Abraham’s children, even if they are not Isaac.

Reformed

The summary gathers Keturah’s children as a completed set.

Reformed accent: they belong to Abraham’s house historically; they will not inherit the sworn estate. The next verses make the distinction without contempt.

Genesis 25:5

5Abraham gave all he had to Isaac.

Arminian

The estate goes to Isaac. Covenant particularity is real: the heir is named, not guessed.

Arminians typically add that this choice does not cancel kindness to other sons. God’s particular gift of the promise-line is compatible with sincere generosity elsewhere.

Reformed

“All he had” to Isaac is the legal and theological point. The line of promise receives the inheritance by Abraham’s act under God’s purpose.

Reformed readers hear election of the seed taking household form: Isaac, not a committee of sons, is heir.

Key contrast Both take Isaac as the covenant heir, not a random favorite. Arminians typically stress that this particular choice sits beside real gifts and kept promises to other sons; Reformed readers typically stress God’s free purpose in electing the line of promise, so the estate follows the sworn seed.

Genesis 25:6

6But to the sons of his concubines Abraham gave gifts, and while he was still living he sent them away from his son Isaac, eastward to the east country.

Arminian

Gifts, not the estate; sent east while Abraham still lives, so Isaac is not crowded out. Kindness is real; distinction is real.

Classical Arminians hear a father who keeps the promise to Isaac without leaving the others empty-handed. Particular election of the heir does not erase provision.

Reformed

Gifts versus “all he had”: the sons of the concubines are provided for and sent away from Isaac.

Reformed reading: they are not nothing, and they are not the covenant seed. Eastward removal protects the heir God named.

Key contrast Shared ground: gifts are real and Isaac remains sole heir. Arminians typically emphasize sincere kindness that is not erased by the particular choice of Isaac; Reformed readers typically emphasize that provision is not the same as being the promised seed.

Genesis 25:7

7These are the days of the years of Abraham’s life, 175 years.

Arminian

A numbered life: 175 years. The pilgrim’s days are counted as gift.

Arminian pastoral: long years under promise invite gratitude and continued trust, not a claim that Abraham earned the span.

Reformed

One hundred seventy-five years are the Lord’s numbering.

Reformed comfort: the friend of God dies on a timetable God wrote. The days of his years are providence, not luck.

Genesis 25:8

8Abraham breathed his last and died in a good old age, an old man and full of years, and was gathered to his people.

Arminian

Good old age, full of years, gathered to his people—death as completion, not defeat.

Classical Arminians hear a kept word: God walked with him to the end. Fullness is gift received along a life of faith.

Reformed

“Gathered to his people” looks beyond the cave. The man of the covenant dies full, under the God who called him.

Reformed note: a good old age is the Lord’s finishing of a life He began (Gen 12).

Genesis 25:9

9Isaac and Ishmael his sons buried him in the cave of Machpelah, in the field of Ephron the son of Zohar the Hittite, east of Mamre,

Arminian

Both sons bury him. Rivalry is not the last word at the grave.

Arminian pastoral: Isaac the heir and Ishmael the sent-away son can still honor their father together. Grace does not require erasing Ishmael from the funeral.

Reformed

Isaac and Ishmael stand at Machpelah, the purchased cave. The heir and the other son share grief.

Reformed reading: providence can reunite them for burial without rewriting who carries the promise.

Genesis 25:10

10the field that Abraham purchased from the Hittites. There Abraham was buried, with Sarah his wife.

Arminian

Purchased field, buried with Sarah. The first plot in Canaan holds husband and wife.

Arminians hear faith’s foothold kept: what he bought in chapter 23 is where he now lies. Promise still waits for more land, but this grave is real.

Reformed

Title from the Hittites, burial with Sarah. The sworn couple rest in the only acre they legally held.

Reformed comfort: God kept a burying place. The covenant pair lie together under a deed, awaiting a larger possession.

Genesis 25:11

11After the death of Abraham, God blessed Isaac his son. And Isaac settled at Beer-lahai-roi.

Arminian

God blesses Isaac after Abraham’s death—the promise does not die with the patriarch. He settles at Beer-lahai-roi, already marked by God’s seeing (Gen 16).

Arminian note: blessing is God’s ongoing, sincere action toward the heir, to be received in faith.

Reformed

The narrator names the Giver: God blessed Isaac. The seed line continues by divine act, not by Abraham’s remaining strength.

Reformed accent: election of Isaac is confirmed in blessing after the father’s death. Beer-lahai-roi is where he dwells under that word.

Genesis 25:12

12These are the generations of Ishmael, Abraham’s son, whom Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah’s servant, bore to Abraham.

Arminian

Ishmael gets a toledoth. He is Abraham’s son, Hagar’s child, named without sneer.

Classical Arminians insist: the covenant heir is Isaac, and Ishmael is still Abraham’s son. God can keep a particular promise without deleting this heading.

Reformed

“Generations of Ishmael” is real history under God’s providence. Parentage is stated: Hagar the Egyptian, Sarah’s servant.

Reformed reading: the narrator honors the line, then will show twelve princes—fulfillment of Gen 17:20, not a rival to Isaac’s oath.

Genesis 25:13

13These are the names of the sons of Ishmael, named in the order of their birth: Nebaioth, the firstborn of Ishmael; and Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam,

Arminian

Birth-order names begin. Firstborn Nebaioth, then Kedar and the rest.

Arminian pastoral: God attends to Ishmael’s household with the same narrative care He gives other families. Kindness named in order is not a slip.

Reformed

Ordered names, firstborn marked. These sons exist because God said Ishmael would live and become a nation (Gen 17:20; 21:13).

Reformed note: particular election of Isaac never meant Ishmael would be childless.

Genesis 25:14

14Mishma, Dumah, Massa,

Arminian

Three more names. Even a short verse is a record of kept life.

Arminians hear God remembering people who will not carry the covenant seed. They are not invisible to Him.

Reformed

Mishma, Dumah, Massa stand in the list God wanted written.

Reformed comfort from another angle: providence is detailed. The God who elects Isaac still numbers Ishmael’s sons.

Genesis 25:15

15Hadad, Tema, Jetur, Naphish, and Kedemah.

Arminian

The list completes toward twelve.

Arminian reading: the count is heading toward the “twelve princes” of verse 16—a promise sincerely kept (Gen 17:20), not a consolation prize spoken in bad faith.

Reformed

Five more names finish the roster.

Reformed accent: God fulfills what He said about Ishmael in particular. The same Lord who chose Isaac keeps His word to Hagar’s son.

Genesis 25:16

16These are the sons of Ishmael and these are their names, by their villages and by their encampments, twelve princes according to their tribes.

Arminian

Twelve princes—the exact shape of Genesis 17:20. Villages and encampments: settled and nomad life.

Classical Arminians press the point: God’s particular choice of Isaac did not make this promise insincere. He said twelve princes; here they are.

Reformed

Twelve princes according to their tribes fulfill the word spoken over Ishmael.

Reformed reading: common and particular mercies can sit together. Ishmael is blessed as promised; he is still not the covenant seed through whom the nations are blessed.

Genesis 25:17

17(These are the years of the life of Ishmael: 137 years. He breathed his last and died, and was gathered to his people.)

Arminian

One hundred thirty-seven years, last breath, gathered to his people—the same death-formula used for Abraham.

Arminian pastoral: Ishmael’s life is honored as a completed span under God. Gathering is not reserved for Isaac alone.

Reformed

Ishmael’s years and gathering are recorded like a patriarch’s.

Reformed note: death under providence, a people of his own. The narrator can grant him this dignity without transferring Isaac’s blessing.

Genesis 25:18

18They settled from Havilah to Shur, which is opposite Egypt in the direction of Assyria. He settled over against all his kinsmen.

Arminian

Geography of Ishmael’s line: Havilah to Shur, toward Assyria, “over against” his kinsmen—near, not absorbed.

Arminians hear a kept place in the world beside Isaac’s house. Proximity without merger is still a kind of mercy.

Reformed

Settlement from Havilah to Shur fulfills the earlier word that Ishmael would dwell over against his kinsmen (cf. Gen 16:12).

Reformed reading: God assigns a range and a posture. The cousin-nations exist by His map, beside the heir, not instead of him.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Keturah’s sons, Abraham’s death, and God’s blessing on Isaac—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 25:1–18.

Arminian Notable voices

“Five and thirty years Abraham lived after the marriage of Isaac, and all that is recorded concerning him during that time lies here in a very few verses: we hear no more of God's extraordinary appearances to him, or trials of him; for all the days even of the greatest saints are not eminent days, some slide on silently, and neither come nor go with observation: such were these last days of Abraham. We have here an account of his children by Keturah, another wife, which be married after the death of Sarah.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 25:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“Then again Abraham took a wife It seems very absurd that Abraham, who is said to have been dead in his own body thirty-eight years before the decease of Sarah, should, after her death, marry another wife. such an act was, certainly, unworthy of his gravity. Besides, when Paul commends his faith, ( Romans 4:19 ,) he not only asserts that the womb of Sarah was dead, when Isaac was about to be born, but also that the body of the father himself was dead.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 25:1)
