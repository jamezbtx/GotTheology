Passage

Genesis 13

Verses 1–13 · ESV · Voices

After Egypt, Abram goes up into the Negeb with Sarai, Lot, and great wealth. Herds crowd the land, strife breaks out, and Abram yields first choice. Lot takes the Jordan valley toward Sodom; Abram remains in Canaan.

Genesis 13:1

1So Abram went up from Egypt, he and his wife and all that he had, and Lot with him, into the Negeb.

Arminian

After Egypt’s compromise, Abram goes up again—household, wife, and Lot with him. Return is a real, grace-enabled step: he does not settle in Pharaoh’s land but re-enters the country of promise.

Classical Arminians hear restoring kindness, not a closed circle. The Lord sincerely meets the pilgrim who stumbled; Abram’s going up is trust that can be offered or refused. Lot still walks with him, a kinsman whose later choices will also be his own.

Reformed

The Lord who preserved them in Egypt now brings them up into the Negeb. The promised line is not lost in Pharaoh’s house. Wife, goods, and nephew travel under the same hand that called Abram in chapter 12.

Reformed comfort: this ascent is providence before it is travelogue. God keeps the seed-bearer on the map of Canaan even after failure. Lot’s presence is already inside a story the Lord will soon split without aborting the promise.

Genesis 13:2

2Now Abram was very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold.

Arminian

Wealth after Egypt is mixed gift. Livestock, silver, and gold can serve worship or inflame rivalry. Abram remains a steward: prosperity is not itself holiness, and how he holds it will show whether faith or grasping rules the camp.

Arminian pastoral note: grace can enrich without baptizing every use of riches. The coming strife will be a moral test they are responsible to meet in peace, not a fate written into the metal.

Reformed

Very rich—and the narrator names the forms: flocks, silver, gold. God can supply His servant without making wealth the covenant. The same Lord who promised land also orders means.

Reformed reading: abundance is providence, not luck. The Lord already knows this plenty will press the household toward a separation that clarifies the heir. Gift and trial arrive in the same carts.

Genesis 13:3

3And he journeyed on from the Negeb as far as Bethel to the place where his tent had been at the beginning, between Bethel and Ai,

Arminian

He retraces first faith—back toward Bethel, the old tent-site between Bethel and Ai. Returning is remembrance with the feet: grace enables him to seek the place of earlier altar rather than a new Egypt.

Classical Arminians honor this as responsible pilgrimage. God does not drag an unwilling man north; He assists the traveler who comes again to the beginning.

Reformed

Geography is theological. Providence leads him to the first tent-country, “the beginning,” where the Lord had already met him. The route is not nostalgia only.

Reformed accent: the heir of promise is being parked again on promised soil. God returns Abram to the map of chapter 12 because the word spoken there still stands.

Genesis 13:4

4to the place where he had made an altar at the first. And there Abram called upon the name of the Lord.

Arminian

The first altar still locates him. Calling on the Lord’s name is living worship after failure—grace-enabled response, not a reflex. Prayer here is a real act: Abram actually calls.

Arminian warmth: the God who was named at the first still receives the pilgrim who returns. Worship is the fitting answer to restoring mercy, and it can be neglected; Abram does not neglect it.

Reformed

Worship is the first work of the returned pilgrim. The Name he invokes is the God who called him, not a local shrine Abram invents. Calling on the Lord is fruit of the faith God has wrought.

Reformed comfort: the altar “at the first” preaches continuity. The same Lord keeps Abram and is publicly confessed. Favor is not climbed into; it is answered in praise.

Genesis 13:5

5And Lot, who went with Abram, also had flocks and herds and tents,

Arminian

Lot is a real economic actor, not a shadow. Blessing has overflowed to the nephew who went with Abram—flocks, herds, tents of his own. Shared prosperity will require shared wisdom; he remains a responsible kinsman.

Arminian note: overflow is gift, not license. Lot will soon choose land for himself. Agency is already here in the herds that make him a household, not a dependent without decisions.

Reformed

Lot’s wealth sits inside Abram’s story. God has attached this nephew to the promised household for a time, flocks and all. Even Lot’s tents exist by providence that will soon separate the lines.

Reformed reading: the Lord can enrich a companion without making him the heir. Lot’s substance is real, and it is still subordinate to the promise that runs through Abram.

Genesis 13:6

6so that the land could not support both of them dwelling together; for their possessions were so great that they could not dwell together,

Arminian

The land’s limits are real. Abundance creates a genuine problem they must face. God does not magically erase the squeeze; they are responsible to seek peace rather than let crowding become a feud.

Classical Arminians hear ordinary providence that still leaves moral room. Possessions too great to share are a test of charity. How they answer the squeeze will be their own.

Reformed

Even scarcity-in-plenty is under the Lord’s hand. The land “could not support” them because God is moving the story toward a separation that will clarify the heir.

Reformed accent: providence uses the pressure of possessions without making greed the author of the promise. The squeeze is real, and it serves a purpose larger than pasture.

Genesis 13:7

7and there was strife between the herdsmen of Abram's livestock and the herdsmen of Lot's livestock. At that time the Canaanites and the Perizzites were dwelling in the land.

Arminian

Strife among herdsmen is a moral crisis, not a footnote—and Canaanites and Perizzites are dwelling in the land, watching. Family conflict in a pagan country is a testimony test. Grace enables peace; they can resist the drift toward quarrel.

Arminian pastoral: the watching nations make the fight public. Kinship still claims them. The quarrel is resistible; Abram will prove it by speaking peace.

Reformed

Strife is permitted in a land still occupied by Canaanites and Perizzites. God will not let the promise die in a herdsmen’s fight, yet He lets the pressure expose hearts.

Reformed note: the watching peoples underscore that this household’s peace is meant to display the Lord. Providence holds the land and the seed even while tents argue over grass.

Genesis 13:8

8Then Abram said to Lot, “Let there be no strife between you and me, and between your herdsmen and my herdsmen, for we are kinsmen.

Arminian

Abram speaks first for peace. Kinship is a moral claim: we must not quarrel as if we were rivals. Generosity here is a real, grace-enabled choice—he could have pulled rank as elder and did not.

Classical Arminians honor this as peaceable faith, not weakness. Assisting grace makes such speech possible; Abram actually yields his rights. Family conflict becomes a place where charity can still win.

Reformed

The peaceable word is fruit of faith God has wrought. Abram’s identity as kinsman outweighs his rights as senior. God is preserving the household’s witness while He prepares the land for the seed.

Reformed comfort: generosity is not Abram bargaining God into keeping the promise. It is the Spirit’s early harvest in the man the Lord has called—peace as evidence, not as purchase.

Genesis 13:9

9Is not the whole land before you? Separate yourself from me. If you take the left hand, then I will go to the right, or if you take the right hand, then I will go to the left.”

Arminian

Abram yields first choice—genuine moral agency. He trusts God enough to let Lot choose left or right. Grace enables this peaceable trust; it is not fatalism, and Lot’s upcoming decision will be a real, resistible use of that freedom.

Arminian pastoral: “the whole land before you” is an open offer between kinsmen. Abram does not manipulate the map. Faith here looks like refusing to clutch, while Lot remains responsible for how he takes the gift of first choice.

Reformed

The uncle who holds the promise can afford to take what is left, because the land is the Lord’s to give. Generosity is faith’s fruit; providence over the promised seed does not depend on who picks left or right.

Reformed accent: God will keep Abram even if Lot takes the greener ground. The offer is free on Abram’s lips because the title is already in heaven. Left and right cannot unmake the word of chapter 12.

Key contrast Both honor Abram’s peaceable offer as faith, not weakness. Arminians typically stress genuine, grace-enabled agency—Abram freely yields and Lot will freely choose. Reformed readers typically stress that the promised land is God’s to allot; Abram can surrender first choice because providence, not Lot’s eye, will keep the seed.

Genesis 13:10

10And Lot lifted up his eyes and saw that the Jordan Valley was well watered everywhere like the garden of the Lord, like the land of Egypt, in the direction of Zoar. (This was before the Lord destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah.)

Arminian

Lot looks and measures by sight—garden of the Lord, Egypt’s lushness—without asking what the men of the plain are like. Sight-based choice is a real moral act. The narrator’s aside about later ruin is a warning: appearances can be resistible bait.

Classical Arminians hear responsible looking. Lot is not forced toward the water. He lifts his eyes to what resembles Eden and Egypt, and that lift is already a drift he need not have taken.

Reformed

Lot’s eyes lift to water, not to the promise. The comparison with Eden and Egypt is irony under providence: what looks like the garden will become fire. God already knows Sodom’s end.

Reformed reading: Lot’s looking cannot overturn the Lord’s purpose for Abram, yet the looking is still his own. Providence frames the well-watered valley and the coming destruction in one sentence.

Genesis 13:11

11So Lot chose for himself all the Jordan Valley, and Lot journeyed east. Thus they separated from each other.

Arminian

“Chose for himself” is the verse’s center. Lot’s step east is genuine moral agency—a real, resistible drift toward the well-watered and, soon, toward Sodom. Separation is the fruit of that choice, not a script he could not decline.

Arminian grief: the land was offered in peace, and he took by sight. Journeying east (away from Abram, toward the valley) is still reversible in later chapters only if he will hear warning. Here the fork is his.

Reformed

Lot chooses, and God overrules the choice for the promise. Journeying east—away from the land’s heart, echoing earlier eastward exiles—does not steal Abram’s seed. Providence preserves the line even when the nephew chooses poorly.

Reformed weight: “for himself” unmasks the heart, and the separation still serves the Lord’s map. The heir remains in Canaan because God keeps him there, not because Lot’s eye was kind.

Key contrast Both take Lot’s choice as historically real and spiritually revealing. Arminians typically emphasize resistible, responsible agency—“chose for himself” as a live moral fork. Reformed readers typically emphasize that even a poor choice cannot unmake God’s purpose to keep Abram in the land of promise.

Genesis 13:12

12Abram settled in the land of Canaan, while Lot settled among the cities of the valley and moved his tent as far as Sodom.

Arminian

Two settlings, two directions. Abram remains in Canaan; Lot pitches toward Sodom by degrees—“as far as Sodom.” Drift is incremental and responsible. Grace had offered the whole land; Lot’s tent-moves are still steps he is taking.

Arminian warning: proximity is chosen, not merely happened-upon. Settling among the cities is a further yes to the valley he picked. The later rescue of Lot will not erase that he moved the tent himself.

Reformed

The geography of the covenant is becoming clear: Abram in Canaan, Lot among the valley cities. God parks the heir where the promise lives. Lot’s tent sliding toward Sodom is both his will and a contrast the Lord will use.

Reformed note: settlement is providence as well as choice. The promised land holds Abram; the cities of the plain hold Lot for a time. The seed-line is not in Sodom.

Genesis 13:13

13Now the men of Sodom were wicked, great sinners against the Lord.

Arminian

The narrator tells what Lot’s eyes omitted. Wickedness is “against the Lord”—real guilt, not mere social failure. Lot’s nearness is a warning: proximity to great sin is a resistible danger, not a fate.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere moral world. The Lord still names evil as evil. Lot is not yet Sodom, but he has placed his tent where great sinners live; that placement remains a responsible drift.

Reformed

God already knows the city Lot approaches. “Great sinners against the Lord” locates the coming judgment in divine holiness, not in chance disaster. Providence has not yet destroyed Sodom, but it has already marked the contrast.

Reformed sobriety: Abram in the land, Lot beside a people ripe for wrath. The Lord’s verdict on Sodom stands whether Lot saw it or not. Wickedness is measured against God, not against the valley’s water.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Abram yielding first choice, and Lot turning toward Sodom—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 13:1–13.

Arminian Notable voices

“He went on to Bethel—Thither he went, not only because he was willing to go among his old acquaintance; but because there he had formerly had his altar. and though the altar was gone, probably he himself having taking it down when he left the place, lest it should be polluted by the idolatrous Canaanites; yet he came to the place of the altar, either to revive the remembrance of the sweet communion he had had with God at that place, or, perhaps, to pay the vows he had there made to God when he undertook his journey into Egypt.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 13:3)

Reformed Notable voices

“In the commencement of the chapter, Moses commemorates the goodness of God in protecting Abram; whence it came to pass, that he not only returned in safety, but took with him great wealth. This circumstance is also to be noticed, that when he was leaving Egypt, abounding in cattle and treasures, he was allowed to pursue his journey in peace; for it is surprising that the Egyptians would suffer what Abram had acquired among them, to be transferred elsewhere.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 13:1)
