Passage

Genesis 13

Verses 14–18 · ESV

After Lot separates, the Lord tells Abram to lift his eyes: all the land in every direction, offspring like the dust, walk the length and breadth. Abram settles by the oaks of Mamre at Hebron and builds an altar to the Lord.

Genesis 13:14

14The Lord said to Abram, after Lot had separated from him, “Lift up your eyes and look from the place where you are, northward and southward and eastward and westward,

Arminian

After the split, the Lord speaks—sincere confirmation, not a consolation prize. “Lift up your eyes” answers Lot’s lifting of eyes: God invites Abram to look by promise, not by market value. The word is a genuine gift that calls for trust.

Arminian pastoral: grace meets the man who yielded first choice. The four directions are an open, honest grant. Abram must still receive the sight in faith; the Lord does not mock him with a view he is not meant to believe.

Reformed

Timing is theological: “after Lot had separated.” The Lord binds Himself to Abram when the nephew is gone. Unilateral speech—God initiates. The four directions are the map of a gift, not a negotiation.

Reformed comfort: election of the line becomes geographically explicit. Abram looks because God tells him to look. The view is as wide as the Giver’s purpose, not as wide as Lot’s leftover pasture.

Genesis 13:15

15for all the land that you see I will give to you and to your offspring forever.

Arminian

“I will give” is sheer grace—land and seed after separation. God sincerely confirms what He pledged. The forever gift is held out to Abram and his offspring as a covenant to be received in faith, not earned by outmaneuvering Lot.

Classical Arminians gladly confess that the promise is gift, not wages. Abram’s walking and later worship are grace-enabled response to a word already spoken in kindness. The land is not a prize for having been nicer than Lot.

Reformed

Unilateral grant: God binds Himself—“I will give… forever.” Offspring and land rest on His oath, not on Abram’s bargaining. The promise is as wide as the view and as durable as the Giver.

Reformed confession: this is covenant confirmation after the split, sheer grace. The Lord elects the line and keeps it. Forever is His word, not Abram’s hold on the ground.

Key contrast Both confess the land-and-seed word as sheer grace after Lot is gone. Arminians typically hear a sincere confirmation Abram must keep receiving in faith; Reformed readers typically hear a unilateral, electing grant in which God binds Himself to Abram and his offspring forever.

Genesis 13:16

16I will make your offspring as the dust of the earth, so that if one can count the dust of the earth, your offspring also can be counted.

Arminian

Dust-innumerable offspring is a promise meant to be believed. Childless Abram is sincerely given a future he cannot manufacture. Faith is the grace-enabled response: to count what cannot yet be counted because God said it.

Arminian note: “I will make” does not cancel Abram’s call to trust. The same Lord who promises a people also invites the barren man to receive the word. Hope here is responsible confidence in a sincere pledge.

Reformed

God “will make” the seed—His work, His math. Dust is a metaphor of sovereign increase. The barren household’s hope is the Lord’s creative word, the same God who made earth’s dust now pledging a people from Abram.

Reformed accent: offspring as dust is not a possibility left on the table. It is a promise the Maker will keep. The count that cannot be counted is already in His hand.

Genesis 13:17

17Arise, walk through the length and the breadth of the land, for I will give it to you.”

Arminian

Walking the land is grace-enabled response—embodied trust. God sincerely gives; Abram is summoned to inhabit the gift with his feet. Obedience here is not payment for the land but the fitting reception of a promise already spoken.

Classical Arminians hear command and gift together without confusing them. “Arise, walk” is a real call Abram can answer. The “for I will give” keeps the gift gracious even as the patriarch’s steps become worship.

Reformed

“Arise, walk… for I will give” joins command and gift. The tour of the land is a covenant sign the Lord Himself orders. Abram’s steps do not create the title; they trace a grant already decided.

Reformed comfort: the reason for walking is the gift, not the earning. God binds the land to Abram and then puts him on it. Length and breadth are His to measure and His to bestow.

Genesis 13:18

18So Abram moved his tent and came and settled by the oaks of Mamre, which are at Hebron, and there he built an altar to the Lord.

Arminian

Tent, oaks, Hebron, altar: Abram answers with worship. Moving and building are grace-enabled acts of faith. The altar is not a bargain; it is thanks to the God who confirmed the promise. Walking and worship belong together as living response.

Arminian close: the Lord sincerely spoke; Abram sincerely settles and calls on His name again. Hebron’s altar is the pilgrim’s yes—enabled by grace, still his own glad work.

Reformed

Hebron’s altar is worship of the God who elects and keeps. Abram settles where the Lord has spoken, not where the grass looked greener. The altar preaches that the land-gift is for communion with the Giver.

Reformed wonder: God binds Himself; the patriarch answers with sacrifice. Oaks of Mamre become a station of the promise because the Lord parked His man there and received the worship He had wrought.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
