Passage

Genesis 46

Verses 1–27 · WEB · Voices

Israel sets out with all he has, sacrifices at Beersheba to the God of his father, Isaac. God speaks in the visions of the night: do not be afraid to go down into Egypt; I will make you a great nation there; I will go down with you, bring you up again. Joseph’s hand will close your eyes. Then the household migrates, and the sons of Israel are listed by mothers—Leah, Zilpah, Rachel, and Bilhah—with Joseph’s two sons born in Egypt; all the persons of Jacob’s house who came into Egypt are seventy.

Genesis 46:1

1Israel traveled with all that he had, and came to Beersheba, and offered sacrifices to the God of his father, Isaac.

Arminian

Israel takes the journey with all he has and stops at Beersheba to sacrifice to Isaac’s God. The road to Egypt begins in worship, not panic.

Listen here for a responsible going-down: enabling grace meets him at the well of the fathers, and he still must come, offer, and trust. God will speak here because Jacob has actually arrived.

Reformed

The migration starts on covenant ground. Beersheba belongs to Isaac’s God before a wagon rolls into Egypt.

Providence is already steering the family toward preservation. The sacrifices mark whose God goes with them—not a gamble on Pharaoh’s invitation.

Genesis 46:2

2God spoke to Israel in the visions of the night, and said, “Jacob, Jacob!” He said, “Here I am.”

Arminian

God speaks in the visions of the night, and calls, “Jacob, Jacob.” The doubled name is personal; Jacob answers, “Here I am.”

Enabling grace meets him where he is afraid. The sincere address invites a response—presence, not coercion—at the edge of the descent.

Reformed

Night visions continue the God of the fathers. The Lord initiates speech; Jacob’s “Here I am” answers a call already sounded.

Covenant faithfulness: the same God who named him now locates him on the Egypt road and will not leave the conversation to chance.

Genesis 46:3

3He said, “I am God, the God of your father. Don’t be afraid to go down into Egypt, for there I will make of you a great nation.

Arminian

I am God, the God of your father. Don’t be afraid to go down into Egypt, for there I will make of you a great nation. Fear is named; the promise is sincere.

One hears an assurance Jacob must receive: going down is not abandonment. Enabling grace steadies a responsible yes to Egypt.

Reformed

The identity word—“I am God, the God of your father”—grounds the command not to fear. Great-nation fruit will ripen in Egypt, as God said.

Providence steers the family into Egypt for preservation and growth. The nation is God’s making, not Jacob’s nerve.

Key contrast Both hear a true “Don’t be afraid” tied to God’s identity. Arminians stress a sincere promise Jacob must trust as he chooses the road; Reformed readers underline that God Himself will make the nation in Egypt, so the descent is covenant purpose, not a risk left to Jacob.

Genesis 46:4

4I will go down with you into Egypt. I will also surely bring you up again. Joseph’s hand will close your eyes.”

Arminian

“I will go down with you into Egypt. I will also surely bring you up again. Joseph’s hand will close your eyes.” Presence, return, and a peaceful death are held out.

God meets Jacob at Beersheba with companionship, not mere permission. The promise is sincere; Jacob may go because God goes—and still he must go.

Reformed

God does not send Jacob into Egypt alone. He goes down, He brings up, and He appoints Joseph’s hand at the deathbed.

Covenant faithfulness: the descent is accompaniment. Providence will preserve the family in Egypt and still own the exodus and the burial.

Key contrast God Himself goes down to Egypt with Jacob. Arminians hear enabling presence that steadies a responsible journey; Reformed readers hear God’s effectual going-with as the guarantee that the family will be kept and brought up again.

Genesis 46:5

5Jacob rose up from Beersheba, and the sons of Israel carried Jacob, their father, their little ones, and their wives, in the wagons which Pharaoh had sent to carry him.

Arminian

Jacob sets out; sons carry father, little ones, and wives in Pharaoh’s wagons. The obedience is bodily—people on a road.

Responsible going-down continues after the vision. Grace has spoken; the household still has to leave Beersheba.

Reformed

Departure from Beersheba uses even Pharaoh’s wagons. Means from Egypt serve the God of Isaac.

Providence can hitch covenant travel to an empire’s carts. The family is being carried where God already said He would go.

Genesis 46:6

6They took their livestock, and their goods, which they had gotten in the land of Canaan, and came into Egypt—Jacob, and all his offspring with him,

Arminian

Livestock, goods, Canaan’s gain, and all offspring come into Egypt. Nothing of the household is left as an afterthought.

They bring what they have gained by responsible labor. Enabling grace does not erase the cost of relocating a whole camp.

Reformed

Jacob and all his offspring enter Egypt. The census is already in motion: the whole seed moves.

Preservation requires the family on Egyptian soil. God is gathering what He will keep through famine and, later, bring out.

Genesis 46:7

7his sons, and his sons’ sons with him, his daughters, and his sons’ daughters, and he brought all his offspring with him into Egypt.

Arminian

Sons, grandsons, daughters, granddaughters—all his offspring he brought. The narrator stresses Jacob’s agency: he brought them.

A father’s responsible care matches God’s invitation. Bringing everyone is faith expressed as logistics.

Reformed

Four generations named, then “all his offspring.” The line is not thinned by the move.

Covenant faithfulness counts the seed. God steers the entire house into Egypt so none of the promise is left behind in Canaan’s famine.

Genesis 46:8

8These are the names of the children of Israel, who came into Egypt, Jacob and his sons: Reuben, Jacob’s firstborn.

Arminian

Names of the descendants who came into Egypt begin with Reuben, Jacob’s firstborn. Genealogy is dignity, not filler.

Each son is a person under a sincere promise. The firstborn is listed without the old disgrace being the last word here.

Reformed

The list opens as “children of Israel”—covenant bookkeeping. Reuben is still firstborn in the record.

Providence preserves names. The God who goes down with Jacob also numbers the sons who will become tribes.

Genesis 46:9

9The sons of Reuben: Hanoch, Pallu, Hezron, and Carmi.

Arminian

Reuben’s sons: Hanoch, Pallu, Hezron, and Carmi. Four names, no sermon—yet each is kept.

God’s care includes ordinary grandsons. The promise to make a nation meets boys who must grow up in a foreign land.

Reformed

The firstborn’s line is itemized because God keeps families in detail.

Preservation is not abstract. These four will sit inside Israel’s later counts because the Lord brought them down.

Genesis 46:10

10The sons of Simeon: Jemuel, Jamin, Ohad, Jachin, Zohar, and Shaul the son of a Canaanite woman.

Arminian

Simeon’s sons include Shaul, “the son of a Canaanite woman.” The list tells the truth about mixed marriage without erasing the boy.

Grace does not hide family complexity. Responsible history names Shaul; the promise still holds him.

Reformed

Jemuel, Jamin, Ohad, Jachin, Zohar, and Shaul—Simeon’s house is not edited for piety.

Covenant faithfulness records even the Canaanite connection. God steers this mixed household into Egypt as surely as the rest.

Genesis 46:11

11The sons of Levi: Gershon, Kohath, and Merari.

Arminian

The priestly clans are still just three names on a migrant list.

They go down as sons, not yet as a tribe of ministers. Enabling grace will later call this line; today they must travel.

Reformed

Gershon, Kohath, and Merari are already in the book. Future tabernacle service sits in these three boys.

Providence is stocking Egypt with the very clans that will carry the holy things in the exodus.

Genesis 46:12

12The sons of Judah: Er, Onan, Shelah, Perez, and Zerah; but Er and Onan died in the land of Canaan. The sons of Perez were Hezron and Hamul.

Arminian

Judah’s sons: Er, Onan, Shelah, Perez, and Zerah—but Er and Onan died in Canaan; Perez’s sons Hezron and Hamul are named.

Death and scandal are not airbrushed. Responsible memory keeps the dead in the list and still traces the living line.

Reformed

Judah’s house includes the dead and the unexpected (Perez). The messianic line is already under God’s hand (ch. 38).

Providence can write Hezron and Hamul into Egypt after Er and Onan’s death. Covenant purpose is not stalled by Canaan’s graves.

Genesis 46:13

13The sons of Issachar: Tola, Puvah, Iob, and Shimron.

Arminian

Four more names the famine might have erased.

Each is worth recording. The sincere promise to make a nation includes these quieter sons.

Reformed

Issachar’s four are preserved in the migration roll. God numbers what the world would skip.

The nation in Egypt is built of such lists. Providence does not lose a Tola or a Yob on the road.

Genesis 46:14

14The sons of Zebulun: Sered, Elon, and Jahleel.

Arminian

Leah’s last sons’ sons complete her cluster before the tally.

Zebulun’s sons are ordinary names attached to responsible lives. They go down because Jacob brought them, under a God who invited the journey.

Reformed

Three sons of Zebulun close Leah’s male list. The catalog is complete because God is complete.

Covenant bookkeeping: even the least-narrated brother has a named seed God will keep in Goshen.

Genesis 46:15

15These are the sons of Leah, whom she bore to Jacob in Paddan Aram, with his daughter Dinah. All the souls of his sons and his daughters were thirty-three.

Arminian

Leah’s children, born in Paddan-aram, with Dinah; thirty-three sons and daughters in all. The daughter is counted, not forgotten.

The number is pastoral: God knows the household. A sincere census of grace, not a faceless mob going to Egypt.

Reformed

The narrator totals because the promise is numerical as well as personal.

Covenant faithfulness counts daughters with sons. This is how “great nation” begins: a numbered people under God’s hand.

Genesis 46:16

16The sons of Gad: Ziphion, Haggi, Shuni, Ezbon, Eri, Arodi, and Areli.

Arminian

Zilpah’s line is given the same care as Leah’s.

No concubine’s grandsons are surplus. Enabling mercy names them as persons who also must go down.

Reformed

Seven sons of Gad—full listing. God does not rank the census by Rachel’s love.

Providence preserves Zilpah’s offspring for tribal Israel. The steering into Egypt includes every branch.

Genesis 46:17

17The sons of Asher: Imnah, Ishvah, Ishvi, Beriah, and Serah their sister. The sons of Beriah: Heber and Malchiel.

Arminian

Asher: Imnah, Ishvah, Ishvi, Beriah, sister Serah; Beriah’s sons Heber and Malchiel. A sister is named among brothers.

The list makes room for Serah. God’s kindness records a daughter in a sons’ catalog; she belongs in the going-down.

Reformed

Asher’s house includes a named sister and two grandsons. The precision is theological: God knows the family.

Covenant seed is not only warriors. Serah’s name is kept because the Lord keeps the whole clan.

Genesis 46:18

18These are the sons of Zilpah, whom Laban gave to Leah, his daughter, and these she bore to Jacob, even sixteen souls.

Arminian

Zilpah’s sons, the maid Laban gave Leah—sixteen persons. The origin in Laban’s gift is told without contempt.

Sixteen lives from a servant’s body still share the sincere promise. Grace does not sort the camp by status.

Reformed

Sixteen from Zilpah, traced to Laban’s giving. Even old Aramean arrangements serve the census.

Providence used Laban’s household to fill Jacob’s. These sixteen enter Egypt as part of the house God will make a nation.

Genesis 46:19

19The sons of Rachel, Jacob’s wife: Joseph and Benjamin.

Arminian

The beloved’s two sons are named as a pair—one already in Egypt, one on the road.

Love’s preference is in the wording, yet both sons must live inside the same promise. Responsible family still includes the others just listed.

Reformed

Rachel is titled Jacob’s wife as the list turns. Joseph and Benjamin are the remaining sons of that marriage.

Covenant purpose has already placed Joseph in Egypt and now brings Benjamin down. The pair is God’s, not only Jacob’s treasure.

Genesis 46:20

20To Joseph in the land of Egypt were born Manasseh and Ephraim, whom Asenath, the daughter of Potiphera, priest of On, bore to him.

Arminian

Manasseh and Ephraim, born in Egypt to Asenath daughter of Potiphera priest of On. Egyptian marriage is stated plainly.

God can graft Egyptian-born sons into Israel’s house without pretending they were born in Canaan. The promise meets them where they are.

Reformed

Two sons born in Egypt are already the Lord’s before Jacob arrives. Asenath and On are not outside the story.

Providence planted Israel’s future tribes in Pharaoh’s land. Preservation includes children Joseph did not father in Canaan.

Genesis 46:21

21The sons of Benjamin: Bela, Becher, Ashbel, Gera, Naaman, Ehi, Rosh, Muppim, Huppim, and Ard.

Arminian

The “little one” already has a full quiver.

Jacob’s fear for Benjamin meets a list of sons. Grace has been fruitful; the youngest brother’s house is historical people, not a mascot.

Reformed

Ten names under Benjamin—surprising fullness for the youngest. God has already multiplied what Jacob clutched.

The census is providence on paper. These ten come into Egypt because the Lord intended a tribe, not a relic.

Genesis 46:22

22These are the sons of Rachel, who were born to Jacob: all the souls were fourteen.

Arminian

Rachel’s sons—fourteen persons in all. Joseph’s two in Egypt plus Benjamin’s house complete her count.

The number honors a dead wife’s line without eclipsing Leah’s thirty-three. Both tallies are sincere gifts to receive with thanks.

Reformed

The beloved’s smaller count is still exact, and it includes Egyptian-born sons.

Covenant math: God knows how the house adds up. Rachel’s fourteen are as kept as Leah’s thirty-three.

Genesis 46:23

23The son of Dan: Hushim.

Arminian

Dan’s son is Hushim. One name—Hebrew even says “sons”—yet the line is not omitted.

A thin list is still a person. God meets small households with the same promise that meets large ones.

Reformed

Hushim stands for Dan. The brevity does not mean neglect; Dan will be a tribe.

Providence can keep a people through a single named son on the Egypt roll. The Lord is not limited by a short verse.

Genesis 46:24

24The sons of Naphtali: Jahzeel, Guni, Jezer, and Shillem.

Arminian

Bilhah’s other son has four names to Dan’s one.

Fair listing, not favoritism in the narrator. These four also go down under a God who invited the journey.

Reformed

Four sons of Naphtali complete Bilhah’s male names. God numbers the maid’s grandchildren as carefully as Rachel’s.

The family God steers into Egypt is already tribal raw material. None of these four is an extra.

Genesis 46:25

25These are the sons of Bilhah, whom Laban gave to Rachel, his daughter, and these she bore to Jacob: all the souls were seven.

Arminian

Bilhah’s sons, the maid Laban gave Rachel—seven persons in all. Seven is a whole, not a leftover.

Servant-born children share the going-down. The sincere promise covers the whole camp, not only the beloved wife’s boys.

Reformed

Laban’s gift to Rachel becomes part of Israel’s numbered house.

Covenant faithfulness totals the maid’s offspring. God will make a nation from every side of this family He is moving.

Genesis 46:26

26All the souls who came with Jacob into Egypt, who were his direct offspring, in addition to Jacob’s sons’ wives, all the souls were sixty-six.

Arminian

Sixty-six of Jacob’s own descendants who came into Egypt, not counting sons’ wives. The narrator distinguishes seed from in-laws.

The count is careful because people matter. Responsible memory does not inflate or erase; it tells who actually traveled.

Reformed

Sixty-six descendants, wives excepted. The arithmetic is heading toward seventy.

Providence is already doing census work. The God who promised a nation now shows its first tallied size on the Egypt road.

Genesis 46:27

27The sons of Joseph, who were born to him in Egypt, were two souls. All the souls of the house of Jacob, who came into Egypt, were seventy.

Arminian

Joseph’s two Egyptian-born sons bring the house to seventy. The number is a completed household under promise.

Seventy is gift to receive, not a magic lock. God sincerely makes them a people; they still must live as that people in Egypt.

Reformed

Two sons plus sixty-six: seventy persons of Jacob’s house came into Egypt. The total is theological as well as numerical.

Covenant faithfulness: God has the whole house where He meant it—for preservation, for nationhood, for a later bringing-up. Seventy is the family He steers, not a coincidence.

Key contrast Both receive seventy as the house that entered Egypt. Arminians detect a sincere, completed household called to live the promise there; Reformed commentators hear God’s sovereign census—the exact people providence preserved and relocated for the nation’s making.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on visions at Beersheba, and “do not be afraid to go down to Egypt”, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“In his devotion he had an eye to God as the God of his father Isaac, that is, a God in covenant with him, for by Isaac the covenant was entailed upon him. He offered sacrifices, extraordinary sacrifices, besides those at his stated times. These sacrifices were offered, By way of thanksgiving for the late blessed change of the face of his family, for the good news he had received concerning Joseph, and the hopes he had of seeing him.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 46:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“The design of the repetition was to render him more attentive. For, by thus familiarly addressing him, God more gently insinuates himself into his mind: as, in the Scripture, he kindly allures us, that he may prepare us to become his disciples. The docility of the holy man appears hence, that as soon as he is persuaded that God speaks, he replies that he is ready to receive with reverence whatever may be spoken, to follow wheresoever he may be called, and to undertake whatever may be commanded.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 46:3)
