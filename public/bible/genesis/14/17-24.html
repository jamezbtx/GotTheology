Passage

Genesis 14

Verses 17–24 · WEB · Voices

Returning from victory, Abram meets two kings: Sodom’s ruler offering spoils, and Melchizedek of Salem, priest of God Most High, who brings bread and wine, blesses Abram, and receives a tenth. Abram swears he will take nothing of Sodom’s, lest a wicked city claim to have made him rich.

Genesis 14:17

17The king of Sodom went out to meet him after his return from the slaughter of Chedorlaomer and the kings who were with him, at the valley of Shaveh (that is, the King’s Valley).

Arminian

Two meetings wait in the King’s Valley. Sodom’s king goes out first in the narrative—gratitude mixed with a coming bargain. Abram must soon choose whose honor he will carry.

The accent falls on a moral fork at the gate of success. Victory can be spent on God Most High or cashed with Sodom. The king’s appearance is a temptation Abram will have to answer.

Reformed

Return from defeat of the eastern kings brings Sodom’s ruler into the King’s Valley. Providence stages the contrast: a doomed city’s king and, next, Salem’s priest-king.

God will not let the victory be interpreted as Sodom’s gift. The meeting place is already a theological test.

Genesis 14:18

18Melchizedek king of Salem brought out bread and wine. He was priest of God Most High.

Arminian

Salem’s king brings bread and wine—royal refreshment—and is named priest of God Most High. Office is double: table and altar, city and shrine. Abram receives a greater gift than spoil.

The blessing of a true priest is sincerely given and may be humbly received. Shared ground with the Reformed: this priest-king foreshadows Christ, our great Melchizedek, without turning the meal into a later controversy the text does not settle.

Reformed

King and priest meet in one man. Bread and wine are royal hospitality; priesthood is the office the Spirit later binds to Christ in Psalm 110 and Hebrews 7.

God sets a head over even Abram in this scene so that the father of the faithful bows to a priesthood older and other than Levi—an adumbration of the Son who is both King and Priest.

Key contrast Both read Melchizedek as historical priest-king and type of Christ. Arminians emphasize the sincere blessing offered and received in living faith; Calvinists typically emphasize God constituting this figure as a unique head who shadows Christ’s combined royal and sacerdotal office.

Genesis 14:19

19He blessed him, and said, “Blessed be Abram of God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth.

Arminian

Blessing runs downhill: priest to patriarch. Abram is blessed of God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth—title that will soon arm Abram’s oath against Sodom. The words are gift, not flattery.

Arminian readers treasure benediction as a means of grace. Abram can receive or shrug. He receives. The Possessor named here is the God who sincerely blesses those who trust Him.

Reformed

The priest’s blessing ratifies Abram’s calling. “Possessor of heaven and earth” names the true God against Canaan’s idols and against Sodom’s coming claim on the goods.

God uses Melchizedek’s mouth to seal what He has already begun. Abram is blessed because the Most High has taken him, not because the campaign earned a wage.

Genesis 14:20

20Blessed be God Most High, who has delivered your enemies into your hand.” Abram gave him a tenth of all.

Arminian

Blessing turns upward: God Most High delivered the enemy. Abram answers with a tenth of everything—voluntary honor to the priest of that God. Tithe here is worship, not a tax code.

Giving is a grace-enabled response. Abram could have pocketed the glory; he hands a tenth to Salem’s priest. Wesley’s later word fits the scene: our great Melchizedek is to have not the tithe only, but ourselves.

Reformed

Doxology names the true victor: God delivered. Abram’s tenth is firstfruits that subordinate even the father of the faithful to this priesthood—Hebrews will press the point toward Christ.

Tithe is confession that the spoils were never Abram’s to boast. Providence gave the kings into his hand; worship gives a tenth back through the priest God sent.

Genesis 14:21

21The king of Sodom said to Abram, “Give me the people, and take the goods for yourself.”

Arminian

Sodom’s counter-offer: keep the goods, return the people. It sounds generous and is a snare. Persons versus possessions is a false split when the city wants to write Abram’s story as its beneficiary.

What comes through is a resistible bargain. Abram can take pay for mercy. He will not. The test is moral and public: will victory be funded by Sodom’s ledger?

Reformed

The king of Sodom speaks after the blessing, as if to reframe the victory in civic terms. Goods for Abram, souls for Sodom—an offer that would yoke the promise to a doomed city.

God has already interpreted the battle through Melchizedek. Sodom’s sentence cannot be allowed to become the headline.

Genesis 14:22

22Abram said to the king of Sodom, “I have lifted up my hand to the LORD, God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth,

Arminian

Abram answers with an oath already lifted to the Lord—using Melchizedek’s titles. He has learned how to name God from the priest who blessed him. Speech about God can be copied in faith.

The raised hand is a responsible vow. Grace taught the Name; Abram binds himself. He will not let Sodom become his patron.

Reformed

The oath borrows Salem’s confession: Lord, God Most High, Possessor of heaven and earth. Abram’s worship shapes his ethics: the God he confesses decides what he will and will not take. The priest’s language becomes the patriarch’s shield.

God supplies both the blessing and the vocabulary that keeps Abram clean of Sodom’s credit.

Genesis 14:23

23that I will not take a thread nor a sandal strap nor anything that is yours, lest you should say, ‘I have made Abram rich.’

Arminian

Thread and sandal strap name total refusal. Motive is explicit: Sodom must not say it made Abram rich. Reputation for the Lord’s sake is a pastoral concern.

Wesleyan Arminians honor this as free, costly integrity. He could have taken a lawful share; he declines so that grace, not Sodom, gets the story. Shared ground: the Possessor of heaven and earth needs no city’s payroll.

Reformed

The smallest strap would be enough for Sodom’s boast. Abram cuts off even the appearance that the promise is underwritten by the plain.

God will enrich Abram by word and gift, not by a wicked king’s stipend. The oath protects the gospel of the call: the Lord, not Sodom, makes Abram’s name great.

Genesis 14:24

24I will accept nothing from you except that which the young men have eaten, and the portion of the men who went with me: Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre. Let them take their portion.”

Arminian

He will not bind allies to his vow. Young men have eaten; Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre may take their share. Holiness here is personal without becoming a law for neighbors.

Integrity can be strict for oneself and generous toward partners. Grace-enabled justice refuses both greed and controlling others’ wages. Shared confession: the chapter ends with worship paid to God Most High and distance kept from Sodom’s claim.

Reformed

The oath is not a snare for confederates. God-taught magnanimity lets Mamre’s house take a share while Abram takes none. Providence had given him allies; he will not impoverish them to look holy.

The priest-king’s blessing stands; Sodom’s ledger is refused; the household of promise remains free. Christ, antitype of Melchizedek, is King and Priest who feeds, blesses, and owns what no wicked city can buy.

Key contrast Both praise Abram’s refusal of Sodom’s pay as faith after Melchizedek’s blessing. Arminians typically underline a grace-enabled vow that others are not forced to copy; Reformed commentators typically underline that the Possessor of heaven and earth keeps the promised name from being underwritten by a doomed city.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on Melchizedek’s priest-king office and blessing, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“He brought forth bread and wine—For the refreshment of Abram and his soldiers, and in congratulation of their victory. This he did as a king. As priest of the most high God he blessed Abram, which we may suppose a greater refreshment to Abram than his bread and wine were.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 14:18)
“Jesus Christ, our great Melchizedek, is to be humbly acknowledged by every one of us as our king and priest, and not only the tithe of all, but all we have, must be given up to him.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 14:20)

Reformed Notable voices

“That he received Abram and his companions as guests belonged to his royalty; but the benediction pertained especially to his sacerdotal office… Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine; and seeing he was the priest of God, he blessed Abram; thus to each character is distinctly attributed what is its own.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 14:18)
“The same Person, therefore who was constituted the only and eternal Priest, in order that he might reconcile us to God, and who, having made expiation, might intercede for us, is also a King of infinite power to secure our salvation, and to protect us by his guardian care.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 14:18, Melchizedek as type of Christ)
