Passage

Genesis 26

Verses 1–16 · ESV · Voices

A second famine sends Isaac to Gerar, where the Lord appears, forbids Egypt, and reiterates Abraham’s oath of presence, land, and worldwide blessing. Isaac settles, repeats the “sister” lie over Rebekah, is exposed and protected by Abimelech, then prospers a hundredfold until Philistine envy sends him away.

Genesis 26:1

1Now there was a famine in the land, besides the former famine that was in the days of Abraham. And Isaac went to Gerar to Abimelech king of the Philistines.

Arminian

A second famine, after Abraham’s, presses Isaac toward Gerar and Abimelech’s court. Hunger locates a real choice of where to sojourn.

Classical Arminians hear accompanying grace already on the road. God will meet him here; Isaac remains responsible for the next step—Egypt or the land the Lord will name.

Reformed

History rhymes: famine again, Gerar again, a Philistine king again. The son’s path retraces the father’s under the same God.

Reformed framing: providence, not accident, places Isaac in Gerar. The field of the next word—and the next failure—is appointed.

Genesis 26:2

2And the Lord appeared to him and said, “Do not go down to Egypt; dwell in the land of which I shall tell you.

Arminian

The Lord appears and forbids Egypt. Isaac must dwell where God will specify—a genuine command, not a hint.

Arminian pastoral: grace initiates by appearing and speaking. The call to stay is sincere and can be obeyed.

Reformed

Appearance precedes strategy. God blocks the Egypt road Abraham once took and claims the right to name Isaac’s dwelling.

Reformed accent: vocation is God’s speech. Isaac will remain because the Lord has appeared, not because famine logic is wiser.

Genesis 26:3

3Sojourn in this land, and I will be with you and will bless you, for to you and to your offspring I will give all these lands, and I will establish the oath that I swore to Abraham your father.

Arminian

Sojourn, presence, blessing, land, and the oath sworn to Abraham are stacked as God’s “I will.” The promise is sincerely given to Isaac as to his father.

Classical Arminians hear a living covenant word meant to be trusted. Grace accompanies the sojourner; Isaac is invited to stay under a pledge that is not theater.

Reformed

God will be with him, bless him, give the lands, and establish the oath He already swore. The son’s future is the father’s oath kept.

Reformed comfort: God establishes what He swore. Presence and blessing are covenant initiative, not Isaac’s bargaining chip.

Key contrast Both hear Abraham’s oath now spoken to Isaac. Arminians typically stress a sincere pledge that accompanies and calls for trust; Reformed readers typically stress that God Himself establishes the oath He swore—sure mercy, not Isaac’s merit.

Genesis 26:4

4I will multiply your offspring as the stars of heaven and will give to your offspring all these lands. And in your offspring all the nations of the earth shall be blessed,

Arminian

Stars, lands, and worldwide blessing restated over Isaac. The nations are in view from the son’s generation, not only the father’s.

Arminian hope: the blessing aimed at the nations is sincerely held out. In the offspring—later, the Seed—God opens a gift the families of the earth are called to receive.

Reformed

Multiplication, land, and global blessing are God’s “I will” again. The Abrahamic gospel is restated as Isaac’s inheritance.

Reformed accent: all nations blessed in the offspring is promise God will keep. The scope is as sure as the Speaker.

Genesis 26:5

5because Abraham obeyed my voice and kept my charge, my commandments, my statutes, and my laws.”

Arminian

The “because” names Abraham’s obedience—voice, charge, commandments, statutes, laws—as the historical ground of this confirmation.

Arminian reading: covenant kindness is not wages, yet Abraham’s real, grace-enabled obedience is not a fiction. Isaac receives a word that honors a father’s kept trust.

Reformed

Abraham’s obedience is cited as covenant history, not as Isaac’s merit. The oath rests on what God swore and on the servant He formed.

Reformed note: “because Abraham obeyed” is God’s own accounting of the father, not a ladder Isaac must climb. Blessing flows from sworn mercy through a faithful servant.

Genesis 26:6

6So Isaac settled in Gerar.

Arminian

He settles as told. The appearance becomes an address.

Classical Arminians honor the obedience: grace enabled him to stay. Remaining in Gerar is a responsible yes to the word of verses 2–3.

Reformed

Settlement is the firstfruit of the appearance. Isaac is where God placed him.

Reformed note: the sojourn is obedience produced by the Lord’s speech. Gerar is now the appointed stage.

Genesis 26:7

7When the men of the place asked him about his wife, he said, “She is my sister,” for he feared to say, “My wife,” thinking, “lest the men of the place should kill me because of Rebekah,” because she was attractive in appearance.

Arminian

The father’s ruse returns: “She is my sister,” from fear of death over Rebekah’s beauty. The lie is named as fear, not as wisdom.

Arminian honesty: this is culpable unbelief under a still-fresh promise. “I will be with you” was just spoken; Isaac remains responsible for the deceit.

Reformed

Fear scripts the same half-truth Abraham used in Egypt and Gerar. Rebekah, like Sarah, is put at risk in a foreign court.

Reformed gravity: election does not make Isaac flawless. God will have to keep the marriage, because Isaac’s word has jeopardized it.

Genesis 26:8

8When he had been there a long time, Abimelech king of the Philistines looked out of a window and saw Isaac laughing with Rebekah his wife.

Arminian

Time exposes what fear hid. Abimelech sees marital intimacy—“laughing with” his wife—and the cover fails.

Arminian pastoral: hidden schemes do not stay hidden. Grace had given years in Gerar; Isaac’s pretense still needed truth.

Reformed

Providence uses a king’s window. The laughter that belongs to marriage becomes the means of uncovering.

Reformed reading: God can expose His own servant for the promise’s sake. The long stay is the stage of disclosure, not wasted time.

Genesis 26:9

9So Abimelech called Isaac and said, “Behold, she is your wife. How then could you say, ‘She is my sister’?” Isaac said to him, “Because I thought, ‘Lest I die because of her.’”

Arminian

The pagan king catechizes the heir: she is your wife—how then the sister-lie? Isaac answers with the thought of death.

Classical Arminians let the rebuke stand. Fear of dying is not an excuse; it is a confession of unbelief Isaac must own.

Reformed

Abimelech names the fact God already knew. Isaac’s defense is the same calculus Abraham used: I thought I would die.

Reformed irony: the man of the oath still reasoned from death, not from “I will be with you.” Providence unmasks him without discarding him.

Genesis 26:10

10Abimelech said, “What is this you have done to us? One of the people might easily have lain with your wife, and you would have brought guilt upon us.”

Arminian

“Done to us” and “guilt upon us”—the king sees communal harm. Adultery nearly imported by the sojourner’s ruse.

Arminian moral: neighbor-love was breached. A pagan court can still name guilt; Isaac’s scheme endangered people who did not share his covenant.

Reformed

Abimelech fears guilt more than Isaac feared God in verse 7. “One of the people” names a near-sin against marriage and against the Philistine house.

Reformed humility: common grace and conscience can shame the heir of promise. God uses a Gentile king to protect Rebekah and to name the wrong.

Genesis 26:11

11So Abimelech warned all the people, saying, “Whoever touches this man or his wife shall surely be put to death.”

Arminian

The king draws a death-line around Isaac and Rebekah. Pagan authority restrains the very harm Isaac feared.

Arminian note: God can check a court by a king’s edict. Preventing grace here runs through a Gentile warning Isaac did not deserve.

Reformed

Royal decree becomes the wall around the marriage. Touch will mean death—protection Abraham also needed in chapter 20.

Reformed accent: the Lord can govern Gerar by a Philistine mouth. Preservation of the line is God’s work, even when the instrument is a pagan ban.

Genesis 26:12

12And Isaac sowed in that land and reaped in the same year a hundredfold. The Lord blessed him,

Arminian

Same-year hundredfold is not climate luck; “the Lord blessed him.” Fruitfulness is gift in the land of sojourn.

Classical Arminians receive the harvest as sincere kindness. Blessing accompanies the man who stayed; it does not baptize the earlier lie.

Reformed

Sowing meets a hundredfold because the Lord blessed him—the “I will bless you” of verse 3 now in the barn.

Reformed comfort: covenant initiative shows in grain. The Giver, not Gerar’s soil alone, explains the yield.

Genesis 26:13

13and the man became rich, and gained more and more until he became very wealthy.

Arminian

Increase stacks: rich, then more, then very wealthy. Material gift is real and can test the heart as surely as famine did.

Arminian pastoral: wealth is not automatic holiness. Grace that enriches still calls Isaac to trust the Giver rather than the flocks.

Reformed

The blessing has a public shape: mounting wealth. God makes the promise visible in Isaac’s substance.

Reformed note: “gained more and more” is providence, not Isaac’s Midas touch. The Lord who swore presence is now openly prospering His man.

Genesis 26:14

14He had possessions of flocks and herds and many servants, so that the Philistines envied him.

Arminian

Flocks, herds, servants—and envy. Gift becomes a social wound. The neighbors’ jealousy is a real moral response Isaac must live with.

Arminian reading: prosperity under God does not cancel neighbor-tension. Envy is their choice; Isaac will need wisdom, not triumphalism.

Reformed

Possessions trigger Philistine envy. The blessing of verse 3 now draws hostility, as it will again at the wells.

Reformed framing: even envy sits under providence. God can use a neighbor’s jealousy to move the heir toward the next word and the next well.

Genesis 26:15

15(Now the Philistines had stopped and filled with earth all the wells that his father’s servants had dug in the days of Abraham his father.)

Arminian

Parenthesis of spite: Abraham’s wells filled with earth. Memory of the father is literally blocked.

Arminian note: people can resist a blessing they did not dig. The filled wells are responsible hostility; Isaac will have to reopen what envy buried.

Reformed

Stopping the wells is an attack on life in a dry land—and on Abraham’s footprint. The son’s story inherits the father’s conflicts.

Reformed accent: providence has allowed the filling. The next stage of the oath will include reclaiming water God already gave through Abraham.

Genesis 26:16

16And Abimelech said to Isaac, “Go away from us, for you are much mightier than we.”

Arminian

Expulsion: go away; you are mightier. Fear of Isaac’s strength ends the stay the Lord had blessed.

Classical Arminians hear a real political choice by Abimelech and a real departure Isaac must make. Grace will go with him; the sending-away is still a wound.

Reformed

Might that comes from blessing now unnerves Gerar. The king who protected the marriage cannot tolerate the wealth.

Reformed comfort: exile from Gerar does not void “I will be with you.” Even being sent away can serve the oath God is establishing.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on famine, the oath to Isaac, and the sister-ruse at Gerar—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 26:1–16.

Arminian Notable voices

“Sojourn in this land—There was a famine in Jacob's days, and God bid him go down into Egypt, Gen 46:3,4, a famine in Isaac's days, and God bid him not go down: a famine in Abraham's days, and God left him to his liberty, directing him neither way, which (considering that Egypt was always a place of trial to God's people) some ground upon the different characters of these three patriarchs. Abraham was a man of very intimate communion with God, and to him all places and conditions were alike;”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 26:2)

Reformed Notable voices

“I do not doubt but a reason is here given why Isaac rather went to the country of Gerar than to Egypt, which perhaps would have been more convenient for him; but Moses teaches that he was withheld by a heavenly oracle, so that a free choice was not left him. It may here be asked, why does the Lord prohibit Isaac from going to Egypt, whither he had suffered his father to go?”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 26:2)
