Passage

Genesis 26

Verses 17–35 · WEB · Voices

Isaac reopens Abraham’s wells, contends at Esek and Sitnah, and names Rehoboth when the Lord makes room. At Beersheba the Lord appears—“Don’t be afraid, for I am with you… for my servant Abraham’s sake”—Abimelech seeks a peace-pact, water is found, and Esau’s Hittite marriages bitter the household.

Genesis 26:17

17Isaac departed from there, encamped in the valley of Gerar, and lived there.

Arminian

He leaves and settles in the valley—another sojourn, not a surrendered calling. Departure is obedience under pressure, not unbelief.

Grace accompanies the moved tent. Isaac is still responsible to dwell where the Lord has him, even after a king’s dismissal.

Reformed

Encampment in the valley is the next appointed place. The heir of the oath is relocated, not abandoned.

Providence includes being sent away. The promise travels with Isaac into the wadi.

Genesis 26:18

18Isaac dug again the wells of water, which they had dug in the days of Abraham his father, for the Philistines had stopped them after the death of Abraham. He called their names after the names by which his father had called them.

Arminian

He reopens Abraham’s wells and restores the old names. Memory is piety: the son’s work honors the father’s gifts.

Arminian interpreters see responsible labor under grace. Digging and renaming are Isaac’s acts of trust—reclaiming inherited mercy rather than inventing a private religion.

Reformed

The wells Abraham dug, the names Abraham gave: covenant continuity in dirt and water. Philistine filling after the death does not erase the father’s grant.

God keeps the line by having the son recover what the father received. The names preach that the oath did not die with Abraham.

Genesis 26:19

19Isaac’s servants dug in the valley, and found there a well of flowing water.

Arminian

Servants dig; a spring is found—living water, not only a cistern. Work and gift meet in the valley.

Means matter. God often gives water through spades. Finding a spring is kindness to receive with thanks.

Reformed

Spring water in the valley is the Lord’s provision on the far side of eviction. Servants are the means; the find is gift.

The God of the oath can open a living well where Isaac was sent. Discovery is providence.

Genesis 26:20

20The herdsmen of Gerar argued with Isaac’s herdsmen, saying, “The water is ours.” So he called the name of the well Esek, because they contended with him.

Arminian

“The water is ours”—contention named Esek. Gift is immediately disputed.

Isaac names the wound rather than pretending peace. Conflict is authentic; he remains responsible for how he answers it.

Reformed

Gerar’s herdsmen claim the spring. The well of grace becomes a well of strife.

Even found water sits inside a contested land. Providence will move him on; the name Esek tells the truth about the fight.

Genesis 26:21

21They dug another well, and they argued over that, also. So he called its name Sitnah.

Arminian

A second well, a second quarrel, a name meaning enmity. Persistence is not naïveté; Isaac keeps digging under hostility.

Arminians honor the refusal to retaliate in kind. Grace enables another shaft; Sitnah records that neighbors can choose enmity again.

Reformed

Enmity follows the second find as it followed the first. The pattern is allowed, not accidental.

Sitnah is theology in a place-name. God can feed His man through wells he does not get to keep.

Genesis 26:22

22He left that place, and dug another well. They didn’t argue over that one. So he called it Rehoboth. He said, “For now the LORD has made room for us, and we will be fruitful in the land.”

Arminian

Movement, another well, no quarrel. Rehoboth: the Lord has made room; we shall be fruitful. Isaac credits God and receives the room with trust.

Room is gift, and fruitfulness is hoped under that gift. He does not seize Gerar; he names space the Lord gave and consents to dwell thankfully.

Reformed

“The Lord has made room” is the key clause. Fruitfulness in the land is God’s doing after contention.

God makes room. Rehoboth is not luck after two losses; it is the oath taking geographic shape.

Genesis 26:23

23He went up from there to Beersheba.

Arminian

He goes up to Beersheba—Abraham’s well-and-oath place. Geography is memory.

Returning to a father’s landmark can be faith. Isaac moves toward a place already marked by the Lord’s name.

Reformed

Ascent to Beersheba is not tourism. The map of promise pulls him to the site of Abraham’s treaty and well.

The son is being led to the place where God will speak that night. The road is providence.

Genesis 26:24

24The LORD appeared to him the same night, and said, “I am the God of Abraham your father. Don’t be afraid, for I am with you, and will bless you, and multiply your offspring for my servant Abraham’s sake.”

Arminian

Same-night appearance: I am Abraham’s God; fear not; I am with you; blessing and offspring for Abraham’s sake. Comfort is personal and sincere after the quarrels.

Arminians hear grace that accompanies a fearful man. The word invites trust; Isaac is addressed as a responsible hearer. “Abraham’s sake” honors covenant history without erasing Isaac’s call to believe.

Reformed

Identity first: God of Abraham. Then presence, blessing, multiplication—“for my servant Abraham’s sake.” The ground is sworn mercy, not Isaac’s recent wells or his Gerar failure.

“Don’t be afraid, for I am with you” is covenant initiative. Offspring multiplied for Abraham’s sake means the oath God establishes, not wages Isaac earned at Esek and Sitnah.

Key contrast Both hear covenant comfort in “Fear not… I am with you.” Arminians stress a sincere, accompanying word Isaac must trust; Reformed Christians stress presence and offspring given for Abraham’s sake as sworn mercy, not Isaac’s merit.

Genesis 26:25

25He built an altar there, and called on the LORD’s name, and pitched his tent there. There Isaac’s servants dug a well.

Arminian

Altar, invocation, tent, well: worship first, then dwelling and water. Calling on the name is Isaac’s responsible answer to the night word.

In practice: grace that appeared is meant to be answered. The altar is not payment; it is faith’s yes—enabled, still his.

Reformed

The sequence is theological: God appears; Isaac worships; tent and well follow. Means of life sit under means of praise.

Calling on the name is the fitting fruit of “I am with you.” The well at Beersheba will confirm what the altar already confessed.

Genesis 26:26

26Then Abimelech went to him from Gerar with Ahuzzath his friend, and Phicol the captain of his army.

Arminian

The king returns with adviser and army commander—a formal embassy, not a casual visit. Power comes seeking the man it expelled.

Former hostility can turn. Isaac will have to interpret the visit and answer it; grace does not skip discernment.

Reformed

Gerar’s court arrives in force. The God who was with Isaac is now publicly interesting to the powers that sent him away.

Providence draws the expellers back. The next pact will be their idea because they have seen what verse 28 will name.

Genesis 26:27

27Isaac said to them, “Why have you come to me, since you hate me, and have sent me away from you?”

Arminian

He tells the truth: you hated me and sent me away. Peace is not amnesia.

Wesleyan Arminians honor the honest question. Reconciliation, if it comes, must face the wound; Isaac is responsible for candor as well as for later hospitality.

Reformed

Memory of expulsion is spoken aloud. The heir does not pretend Gerar’s envy was kindness.

God can bring a hated man to the place where his enemies request a covenant. The question clears the ground for the oath they will ask.

Genesis 26:28

28They said, “We saw plainly that the LORD was with you. We said, ‘Let there now be an oath between us, even between us and you, and let’s make a covenant with you,

Arminian

Pagans confess: we see plainly the LORD was with you. They want a sworn pact.

The sincere presence promised in verses 3 and 24 is now visible even to Gerar. Neighbor-nations can recognize blessing and seek peace; Isaac must still decide how to answer.

Reformed

“The LORD was with you” is the nations’ theology of Isaac. The “I am with you” of the night word is now public fact.

God makes His accompaniment plain enough that a Philistine court proposes covenant. The oath-God is advertising His man.

Genesis 26:29

29that you will do us no harm, as we have not touched you, and as we have done to you nothing but good, and have sent you away in peace.’ You are now the blessed of the LORD.”

Arminian

They want no harm, claim they did only good, and name him “the blessed of the Lord.” The claim of “nothing but good” is generous toward themselves after wells and exile.

Pastorally: even mixed motives and airbrushed history can still speak a true title. Isaac is the Lord’s blessed; he can grant peace without endorsing every Gerar spin.

Reformed

“Blessed of the Lord” is the right name, whatever their memory of the sending-away. They seek safety from the man God has marked.

Common-grace diplomacy serves the promise. The title they speak is truer than their version of the past.

Genesis 26:30

30He made them a feast, and they ate and drank.

Arminian

Feast, eating, drinking—hospitality where there had been hate. Table is a chosen means of peace.

Classical Arminians see grace-enabled generosity. Isaac is not required by fate to host them; he freely makes a feast under the God who made room.

Reformed

The meal is covenant prelude. God can turn envy into shared bread.

The blessed of the Lord becomes host. Providence is not only wells and wealth; it is a table in the presence of former adversaries.

Genesis 26:31

31They rose up some time in the morning, and swore an oath to one another. Isaac sent them away, and they departed from him in peace.

Arminian

Early oaths, then a peaceful sending. The pact is sworn and kept in the morning light.

Oaths are responsible speech. Isaac binds himself in truth; peace is a moral outcome, not a mood.

Reformed

Exchanged oaths echo Abraham’s earlier treaty at this place. Peace is granted as the Lord had been with him.

The God of the oath oversees lesser oaths. Departure in peace comes from presence they had “seen plainly.”

Genesis 26:32

32The same day, Isaac’s servants came, and told him concerning the well which they had dug, and said to him, “We have found water.”

Arminian

Same day: we have found water. Peace-pact and provision arrive together.

Servants dig; God gives the find. Receiving the news is an invitation to recognize the Giver, not only the crew.

Reformed

Timing is loud: the day of oaths is the day of water. The Lord who made room now opens a well at Beersheba.

Providence synchronizes treaty and spring. “We have found” is true as means; the Giver is the Lord who was with Isaac.

Genesis 26:33

33He called it “Shibah”. Therefore the name of the city is “Beersheba” to this day.

Arminian

Shibah—oath-sounding name—locks the well to the sworn pact. Beersheba’s name is retold in Isaac’s generation.

Wesley’s heirs hear memorial: faith names places so memory can obey. The city-name is gift and witness, to be received as the Lord’s doing.

Reformed

Well and oath fuse in the name. Abraham’s Beersheba is confirmed in the son.

“to this day” is narrator’s seal that God kept this marker in Israel’s map. The oath-God writes geography.

Genesis 26:34

34When Esau was forty years old, he took as wife Judith, the daughter of Beeri the Hittite, and Basemath, the daughter of Elon the Hittite.

Arminian

At forty, Esau marries two Hittite women. The choice is narrated as his taking, not as fate.

Marriages are moral acts. Grace had marked this household; Esau’s choosing still wounds the line’s peace, as the next verse will say.

Reformed

Hittite wives at forty sit in the same chapter as Beersheba’s oath. The heir of blessing is not Esau’s instinct.

Providence records the marriages that will embitter the tent. The promise line will not run by Esau’s preference.

Genesis 26:35

35They grieved Isaac’s and Rebekah’s spirits.

Arminian

The marriages make life bitter for both parents. Household pain is not dismissed as “just a type.”

Classical Arminians treat the bitterness as the fruit of choices. Esau’s taking wounds people who must still pray, parent, and wait on the God of Abraham.

Reformed

Bitterness in Isaac’s tent is the chapter’s last word—after wells, feast, and peace with Gerar. Covenant comfort does not void family grief.

Even this ache sits under the God who appeared at Beersheba. The oath stands; the household still needs mercy the Lord alone can keep.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on wells reopened, Rehoboth, and the LORD’s “Fear not, I am with you”, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“He digged a well, and for that they strove not—Those that follow peace, sooner or later, shall find peace: those that study to be quiet seldom fail of being so. This well they called Rehoboth—Enlargements, room enough.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 26:22)

Reformed Notable voices

“First, we see that the holy man was so hated by his neighbors, as to be under the necessity of seeking a retreat for himself which was destitute of water; and no habitation is so troublesome and inconvenient for the ordinary purposes of life as that which suffers from scarcity of water. Besides, the abundance of his cattle and the multitude of his servants -- who were like a little army -- rendered a supply of water very necessary; whence we learn that he was brought into severe straits.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 26:18)
