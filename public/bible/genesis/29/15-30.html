Passage

Genesis 29

Verses 15–30 · ESV · Voices

After a month, Laban opens wage-talk. Jacob loves Rachel and serves seven years that feel like days; Laban substitutes Leah at the feast, appeals to local custom, and binds Jacob to another seven years. The deceiver is deceived, and the household that will bear Israel’s tribes is formed in pain.

Genesis 29:15

15Then Laban said to Jacob, "Because you are my kinsman, should you therefore serve me for nothing? Tell me, what shall your wages be?"

Arminian

Kinship, Laban says, should not mean unpaid labor. He invites Jacob to name wages—fair on the surface.

Classical Arminians hear a real negotiation. Grace does not erase contracts; it will expose whether Laban’s offer is honest.

Reformed

Wages enter after a month of unpriced work. Laban frames generosity while steering the deal.

Reformed reading: God lets the bargain be spoken. The next years are already in His hand, including Laban’s later fraud.

Genesis 29:16

16Now Laban had two daughters. The name of the older was Leah, and the name of the younger was Rachel.

Arminian

Two daughters, named by birth order: Leah older, Rachel younger. The household’s future is already a pair, not a single beloved.

Arminian note: Scripture will not let Rachel’s beauty erase Leah. Both women are about to be treated as pieces in a father’s game—and both will be seen by God.

Reformed

The narrator pauses to rank the sisters. Older and younger will become the hinge of the trick.

Reformed accent: God names both. The line of promise will run through this whole house, not only through the sister Jacob prefers.

Genesis 29:17

17Leah's eyes were weak, but Rachel was beautiful in form and appearance.

Arminian

Leah’s eyes are weak (or soft); Rachel is beautiful in form and appearance. The text reports attraction without baptizing it as wisdom.

Classical Arminians warn against making a person’s worth the score of a face. Grace will later honor the unloved sister; readers should not copy Jacob’s ranking.

Reformed

Beauty and “weak eyes” are stated as fact, not as God’s last word on either woman.

Reformed pastoral edge: providence is not identical with Jacob’s preference. The LORD’s seeing (v. 31) will not match the man’s eye.

Genesis 29:18

18Jacob loved Rachel. And he said, "I will serve you seven years for your younger daughter Rachel."

Arminian

Love names the wage: seven years for Rachel. Jacob chooses and offers costly service; he does not seize.

Arminian reading: affection is real and the vow of service is a free, responsible pledge. Seven years will test whether love is more than sight.

Reformed

He loves Rachel and prices the bride in years of labor. The number is his to offer; the outcome is not his to guarantee.

Reformed note: God will use the seven years—and the fraud that follows them—to school the man who once bought a birthright cheap.

Genesis 29:19

19Laban said, "It is better that I give her to you than that I should give her to any other man; stay with me."

Arminian

Laban agrees it is better to give Rachel to Jacob than to another, and says “stay.” Consent sounds warm and leaves the terms soft.

Classical Arminians mark the missing oath. A vague “better” is not yet a clear covenant; Jacob should have pressed for Leah’s place to be named.

Reformed

“Stay with me” keeps labor in the house. Laban praises the match without swearing the name Rachel as the wedding’s only bride.

Reformed sobriety: God permits the ambiguous yes. What Laban leaves unsaid will become the night’s cruelty.

Genesis 29:20

20So Jacob served seven years for Rachel, and they seemed to him but a few days because of the love he had for her.

Arminian

Seven years feel like a few days because of love. Time bends under affection; labor becomes almost light.

Arminian pastoral warmth: human love can be a true gift and still be naïve. Grace does not mock the years; it will also refuse to let love become an excuse for what Laban does next.

Reformed

Love shortens the wait in Jacob’s heart. The narrator honors the devotion even as the trap is set.

Reformed comfort and warning: God can use long service; He is not bound to make the beloved the first wife. The few-days feeling does not cancel the coming morning.

Key contrast Both honor Jacob’s real love. Arminians typically stress responsible affection that still needed clearer terms; Reformed readers typically stress the same years as providence preparing a harder lesson than Jacob priced.

Genesis 29:21

21Then Jacob said to Laban, "Give me my wife that I may go in to her, for my time is completed."

Arminian

Time completed: give me my wife. Jacob claims the agreed woman as already “my wife” in right, not yet in the tent.

Classical Arminians hear a just demand. A completed term should be paid; delaying or swapping the bride is theft of labor and of name.

Reformed

He asks for what the years have earned. “My wife” is covenant language Laban has not yet honored.

Reformed note: the request is righteous. God will still allow the night to unfold against it—justice delayed is not justice denied forever.

Genesis 29:22

22So Laban gathered together all the people of the place and made a feast.

Arminian

A public feast gathers the place. Celebration becomes cover. Community joy can hide a private fraud.

Arminian caution: shared ritual does not cleanse a lie. The feast should have sealed Rachel’s marriage; Laban uses it to dim the lights.

Reformed

The feast is Laban’s stage. Neighbors become unwitting witnesses to a switched bride.

Reformed reading: God is not fooled by the party. The public joy will make the morning’s “behold” sharper.

Genesis 29:23

23But in the evening he took his daughter Leah and brought her to Jacob, and he went in to her.

Arminian

Evening, Leah, and Jacob goes in to her—unwitting. The wrong sister is given; the act is still a real union he did not consent to as Leah’s husband.

Classical Arminians name Laban’s sin without blaming Leah as the plot’s author. A father’s deception violates both daughters and the groom; agency here is Laban’s.

Reformed

Darkness covers the switch. Jacob lies with the wife he did not choose.

Reformed gravity: God will still father tribes from this night. Providence never excuses Laban; it can overrule him for a people not yet born.

Genesis 29:24

24(Laban gave his female servant Zilpah to his daughter Leah to be her servant.)

Arminian

Zilpah is given to Leah as servant—status gift that will later mean more children and more rivalry. The parenthesis is not small.

Arminian pastoral note: servants are not props. Zilpah’s life is bound into a marriage she did not choose; later chapters will show the cost.

Reformed

The narrator inserts Zilpah now. Dowry and future conflict arrive together.

Reformed note: even the servant-gift sits inside God’s larger household story. What Laban means as wedding custom, Scripture will later name as a source of sons.

Genesis 29:25

25And in the morning, behold, it was Leah! And Jacob said to Laban, "What is this you have done to me? Did I not serve with you for Rachel? Why then have you deceived me?"

Arminian

Morning light: Leah. Jacob’s cry is righteous—“Why have you deceived me?” The man who deceived Isaac now tastes the same verb.

Classical Arminians refuse two straw men: this is not karma as impersonal fate, nor is Jacob innocent. Laban’s guilt is full; Jacob’s past deceit is also real. Grace lets a sinner learn by being sinned against without calling the lesson permission to copy Laban.

Reformed

“Behold, it was Leah” is the chapter’s shock. The deceiver is deceived, measure for measure, under God’s holy government.

Reformed confession: Laban is fully culpable, and God is not absent. Providence can use a wicked uncle to humble Jacob without authoring the lie or canceling Jacob’s earlier guilt.

Key contrast Both condemn Laban’s fraud and refuse to excuse Jacob’s past. Arminians typically stress real, responsible deceit meeting a just protest; Reformed readers typically stress the same guilt inside God’s retributive providence that schools the patriarch.

Genesis 29:26

26Laban said, "It is not so done in our country, to give the younger before the firstborn."

Arminian

Local custom, cited after the fact: the younger must not precede the firstborn. Laban hides theft under tradition he never named in verse 19.

Arminian edge: custom is not covenant. A rule unspoken at the bargain cannot rewrite seven years of labor. Firstborn privilege here is a father’s convenient ethic.

Reformed

“Our country” becomes the alibi. The firstborn card is played only after the night.

Reformed irony: Jacob once took a firstborn’s blessing in the dark; now firstborn custom is used against him. God writes a bitter rhyme without making Laban righteous.

Genesis 29:27

27Complete the week of this one, and we will give you the other also in return for serving me another seven years."

Arminian

Finish Leah’s week, then Rachel—plus seven more years. Laban stacks bride on bride and labor on labor.

Classical Arminians hear coercion dressed as offer. Jacob may consent under pressure; that does not make the terms just. Polygamy here is described, not blessed as God’s ideal.

Reformed

Another seven years is the new price. Two wives are Laban’s lever.

Reformed reading: God will permit the double marriage that Scripture later regulates and the New Testament will not hold up as Eden. The tribes will still come through this tangled house.

Genesis 29:28

28Jacob did so, and completed her week. Then Laban gave him his daughter Rachel to be his wife.

Arminian

He completes Leah’s week; then Rachel is given. Jacob yields to the terms rather than walking away.

Arminian pastoral realism: remaining can be weary faithfulness, not applause for Laban. He keeps his word to the week even when Laban did not keep his.

Reformed

Jacob “did so.” Rachel is finally given as wife.

Reformed note: the second marriage arrives on Laban’s calendar, not Jacob’s. God still hands him Rachel, and the household of Israel takes its painful shape.

Genesis 29:29

29(Laban gave his female servant Bilhah to his daughter Rachel to be her servant.)

Arminian

Bilhah is given to Rachel as Zilpah was to Leah. Matching gifts, matching future contests.

Classical Arminians again refuse to skip the servant. Her body and children will be drafted into sister-rivalry; the text will not treat her as furniture.

Reformed

Bilhah parallels Zilpah. The narrator is stocking the next chapter’s births.

Reformed providence: even the servants’ assignment is on the page because sons of Israel will come from these women too.

Genesis 29:30

30So Jacob went in to Rachel also, and he loved Rachel more than Leah, and served Laban for another seven years.

Arminian

He goes in to Rachel, loves her more than Leah, and serves seven more years. Unequal love is named without approval.

Arminian pastoral grief: preference in the tent will wound Leah and warp the sons. Grace does not deny the affection for Rachel; it will also hear the unloved sister in the next verses.

Reformed

More love for Rachel, more years for Laban. The household is now two wives and a ranking of hearts.

Reformed gravity: God records the imbalance. The next line will show that the LORD’s seeing does not copy Jacob’s love.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on seven years for Rachel, and Leah given in her place—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 29:15–30.

Arminian Notable voices

“Should thou therefore serve me for nought? - No, what reason for that? If Jacob be so respectful as to give him his service without demanding any consideration for it, yet Laban will not be so unjust as to take advantage either of his necessity, or of his good nature. It appears by computation that Jacob was now seventy years old when he bound himself apprentice for a wife; probably Rachel was young and scarce marriageable when Jacob came first, which made him the more willing to stay for her till his seven years were expired.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 29:15)

Reformed Notable voices

“The iniquity of Laban betrays itself in a moment; for it is a shameful barbarity to give his daughter, by way of reward, in exchange for Jacob's services, making her the subject of a kind of barter. He ought, on the other hand, not only to have assigned a portion to his daughter, but also to have acted more liberally towards his future son-in-law. But under the pretext of affinity, he defrauds him of the reward of his labor, the very thing which he had before acknowledged to be unjust.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 29:18)
