Passage

Genesis 29

Verses 31–35 · ESV · Voices

The LORD sees that Leah is hated, opens her womb, and leaves Rachel barren for a time. Leah bears Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and Judah—naming each from affliction toward praise. The unloved wife becomes mother of priesthood and of the royal, messianic line.

Genesis 29:31

31When the LORD saw that Leah was hated, he opened her womb, but Rachel was barren.

Arminian

The LORD sees Leah hated and opens her womb; Rachel is barren. Divine sight is compassion for the overlooked, not a contest scoreboard.

Classical Arminians stress that God truly regards affliction and acts. Seeing is not bare knowledge; it is kindness that gives life. Barrenness here is a chapter, not a final verdict on Rachel.

Reformed

“The LORD saw” governs the births. He opens and withholds wombs according to mercy and purpose.

Reformed confession: God is not a spectator of the tent’s ranking. He counters Jacob’s “more than Leah” with children, and He remains Lord of the closed womb as well.

Key contrast Both preach God’s kindness to the unloved. Arminians typically emphasize sincere, responsive compassion that regards Leah’s pain; Reformed readers typically emphasize the same mercy as the LORD’s sovereign opening and shutting of the womb.

Genesis 29:32

32And Leah conceived and bore a son, and she called his name Reuben, for she said, "Because the LORD has looked upon my affliction; for now my husband will love me."

Arminian

Reuben—“see, a son.” Leah credits the LORD for looking on her affliction, then hopes Jacob will now love her. Faith and ache share a sentence.

Arminian pastoral reading: she prays her theology aloud. Grace has seen her; she still longs for a husband’s heart. God can answer the first without immediately fixing the second.

Reformed

She names the boy from the LORD’s looking. Affliction is not invisible in heaven.

Reformed note: the hoped-for husband-love may not arrive as she wants. God’s seeing is sure; Jacob’s heart remains a slower story. Reuben is gift, not leverage that binds a man.

Genesis 29:33

33She conceived again and bore a son, and said, "Because the LORD has heard that I am hated, he has given me this son also." And she called his name Simeon.

Arminian

Simeon—heard. The LORD has heard that she is hated and given another son. She keeps talking to God about the same wound.

Classical Arminians honor persevering prayer. Being heard is real; the gift of a second son does not mean she was wrong to name the hatred.

Reformed

Heard, then given. The name preaches that God attends the unloved wife’s cry.

Reformed comfort: “this son also” is surplus mercy. Hatred in the house does not veto the LORD’s giving.

Genesis 29:34

34Again she conceived and bore a son, and said, "Now this time my husband will be attached to me, because I have borne him three sons." Therefore his name was called Levi.

Arminian

Levi—attached. She hopes three sons will bind Jacob to her. The longing for covenant closeness in marriage is not silly; it is still unmet.

Arminian caution: children are not glue. Grace invites Jacob to honor Leah as wife, not only as a mother of sons. The later priesthood will come from this boy, beyond her immediate hope.

Reformed

She interprets the third birth as coming attachment. The name Levi outlives the strategy.

Reformed irony and hope: Jacob’s heart still leans Rachel-ward, yet God attaches Levi to Israel’s worship. Providence writes a priestly tribe from a wife still bargaining for love.

Genesis 29:35

35And she conceived again and bore a son, and said, "This time I will praise the LORD." Therefore she called his name Judah. Then she ceased bearing.

Arminian

Judah—praise. This time she praises the LORD rather than pricing a husband’s love. The turn is worship: the Giver Himself becomes the sentence.

Classical Arminians linger here with Leah. Grace can move a wounded heart from “now he will love me” to “I will praise the LORD.” From this son comes the royal and messianic line—praise that reaches David and Christ.

Reformed

Praise of the LORD names Judah. Then she ceases bearing for a time. The climax of the four is doxology, not a new scheme.

Reformed gospel already in seed: the tribe of the scepter (Gen 49:8–10) and Matthew’s Christ descend from the unloved wife’s fourth son. God chooses the praised LORD, not Jacob’s favorite, as the royal road.

Key contrast Both read Judah as Leah’s turn to praise and as the messianic line’s door. Arminians typically stress her grace-enabled shift from husband-hope to worship; Reformed readers typically stress God’s sovereign choice of this son for kingship and Christ.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the LORD seeing Leah hated, and Judah born from praise—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 29:31–35.

Arminian Notable voices

“She appears very ambitious of her husband's love; she reckoned the want of it her affliction, not upbraiding him with it as his fault, nor reproaching him for it; but laying it to heart as her grief, which she had reason to bear, because she was consenting to the fraud by which she became his wife. She called her first-born Reuben, see a son, with this pleasant thought, Now will my husband love me.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 29:32)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses relates that Leah was not ungrateful to God. For a profane stupor so occupies the mind of nearly all men, that, like cattle, they swallow up whatever benefits God, in his kindness, bestows upon them. Further, Leah not only acknowledges God as the author of her fruitfulness; but also assigns as a reason, that her affliction had been looked upon by the Lord, and a son had been given her who should draw the affection of her husband to herself.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 29:32)
