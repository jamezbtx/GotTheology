Passage

Genesis 8

Verses 20–22 · ESV · Voices

Noah’s first act on dry ground is worship: he builds an altar and offers burnt offerings; the LORD smells the pleasing aroma and promises never again to curse the ground or strike down every living creature as in the flood, while earth’s seasons endure.

Genesis 8:20

20Then Noah built an altar to the LORD and took some of every clean animal and some of every clean bird and offered burnt offerings on the altar.

Arminian

Noah’s first act on dry ground is worship, not city-building. He offers from the clean animals God had him take. Gratitude is a real response to mercy received—the heart answering the God who remembered.

Arminian pastoral reading: the altar is not a bribe to buy safety already given; it is thanksgiving. Grace restores a world, and the fitting first work is to give it back in offering. Both camps agree: worship is the right first response.

Reformed

Altar before agriculture. Burnt offerings rise to the LORD who saved them. Sacrifice already hints at atonement God will accept. The remnant’s first vocation is worship because grace produced a people who give thanks.

Reformed accent: Noah truly builds and truly offers—agency is not denied—yet the story’s weight falls on the God who preserved a worshiper through water. The clean gift points beyond Noah to a mercy God Himself will smell with pleasure.

Genesis 8:21

21And when the LORD smelled the pleasing aroma, the LORD said in his heart, “I will never again curse the ground because of man, for the intention of man’s heart is evil from his youth. Neither will I ever again strike down every living creature as I have done.”

Arminian

The LORD is pleased with the offering—not because Noah purchased safety, but because worship met mercy. The promise never again to curse the ground or strike every creature as in the flood is sincere common grace toward the world. “The intention of man’s heart is evil from his youth” is honest: sin is deep.

Classical Arminians refuse Pelagian optimism, yet they also refuse to say evil erases every genuine response to grace. The God who names our corruption still pledges kindness while the earth remains. Mercy is real; the invitation to trust and worship remains real too.

Reformed

God smells the pleasing aroma and speaks “in his heart”—a unilateral resolve. He will not again curse the ground because of man or repeat this total stroke, even though the human heart remains evil from youth. Anthropology is sober: the flood did not fix native corruption.

Reformed comfort: preservation of the world is therefore grace, not a wager on improved humanity. The promise stands because God swore it, not because the heart grew good. Sacrifice accepted and oath spoken—both come from Him, for a race that cannot save itself by becoming better.

Key contrast Both take the human heart’s evil as real and the promise as God’s own; Arminians typically hear sincere common grace that still leaves room for genuine response, while Reformed readers typically hear a sober anthropology paired with a unilateral oath of preservation that does not wait on improved human nature.

Genesis 8:22

22While the earth remains, seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night, shall not cease.

Arminian

Seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night—ordinary time becomes a gift. The world is a stable theater for repentance, faith, work, and fruitfulness. Common grace is real kindness, holding history open.

Arminian hope: while the earth remains, God’s patience is not empty. Seasons preach that judgment was not the last word. The regularity of days is meant to lead hearts toward the God who remembered Noah and still remembers the world.

Reformed

“While the earth remains” is God’s decree over nature’s clock. Stability is not the universe’s inertia; it is the Lord’s word. Providence makes mission and ordinary life possible until the appointed end.

Reformed confession: both camps agree that the earth is restabilized by God’s speech, not by human virtue. Seasons will hold because He said they shall not cease. The God of the flood is the God of harvest—holy in judgment, faithful in mercy.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Noah’s altar and the promise of seedtime and harvest—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 8:20–22.

Arminian Notable voices

“He was well pleased with Noah's pious zeal, and these hopeful beginnings of the new world, as men are with fragrant and agreeable smells. I will not again curse the ground, Heb. I will not add to curse the ground any more—God had cursed the ground upon the first entrance of sin, Ge 3:17, when he drowned it he added to that curse: but now he determines not to add to it any more.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 8:21)

Reformed Notable voices

“And the Lord smelled a sweet savor Moses calls that by which God was appeased, an odour of rest; as if he had said, the sacrifice had been rightly offered. Yet nothing can be more absurd than to suppose that God should have been appeased by the filthy smoke of entrails, and of flesh. But Moses here, according to his manner, invests God with a human character for the purpose of accommodating himself to the capacity of an ignorant people.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 8:21)
