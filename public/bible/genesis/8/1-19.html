Passage

Genesis 8

Verses 1–19 · WEB · Voices

After the flood, God remembers Noah and the creatures in the ark; the waters recede, the ark rests on Ararat, and Noah waits with raven and dove until God commands the family and the animals to go out and fill the earth again.

Genesis 8:1

1God remembered Noah, all the animals, and all the livestock that were with him in the ship; and God made a wind to pass over the earth. The waters subsided.

Arminian

“God remembered” is covenant language, not a reminder slipped to a forgetful Lord. The God who shut Noah in now turns toward him in mercy—wind over the waters, a world beginning to be mended. The care is personal: Noah, the beasts, the livestock with him in the ship.

Wesleyan Arminians hear covenant kindness that invites trust. Mercy is not a fiction, not theatrical; it calls Noah (and us) to keep hoping in the God who remembers. Grace here is not a closed circle. The very Lord who judged still moves toward those who wait on Him.

Reformed

“God remembered” is the narrative’s hinge—covenant faithfulness, not recovered memory. He had pledged Himself to Noah; now He acts to preserve the remnant He placed in the ark. The wind is providence: the Judge of the flood is also the Lord who commands the waters down.

Preservation is God’s work before it is Noah’s endurance. The family and the creatures survive because the Lord keeps covenant. He does not deny Noah’s waiting; He is the reason that waiting is not in vain.

Key contrast Both reject any hint that God had forgotten Noah; Arminians hear covenant mercy that invites ongoing trust, while Reformed interpreters hear the Lord’s effectual faithfulness to preserve the remnant He had placed in the ark.

Genesis 8:2

2The deep’s fountains and the sky’s windows were also stopped, and the rain from the sky was restrained.

Arminian

The God who opened the deep and the heavens now closes them. Judgment is not a machine left running; mercy restrains the rain. Hope has a source: the Lord who wound the storm up is able to wind it down.

This restraint is sincere kindness toward a ruined world. God is not locked into wrath. Those who wait in the ark are invited to read the quieting skies as a call to keep trusting, not as fate grinding on.

Reformed

Closing the fountains and the windows is as sovereign as opening them. The flood does not exhaust itself; God restrains it. Providence governs the end of judgment as surely as its beginning.

The instruments of wrath answer to the Lord’s voice. Waters subside because He says so. The remnant is safer in His timing than in any theory of nature left to itself.

Genesis 8:3

3The waters continually receded from the earth. After the end of one hundred fifty days the waters receded.

Arminian

The waters recede “continually”—slow mercy, measured in 150 days. Noah must live inside a deliverance that is already underway but not yet finished. Faith here looks like patience more than a dramatic leap.

Arminians honor that waiting as genuine. Grace enables endurance; it does not erase the call to wait. The timetable is God’s, and the family in the ark is responsible to trust Him through the long abatement.

Reformed

“Continually” marks God’s ongoing work, dated to the day. Abatement is not luck or a tired ocean. The Creator who sent the flood now steadily takes it back.

History’s clock is in God’s hand. The 150 days preach providence, not delay-as-neglect. He knows the hour the waters will have abated, and He brings it.

Genesis 8:4

4The ship rested in the seventh month, on the seventeenth day of the month, on Ararat’s mountains.

Arminian

The ark comes to rest—gift, not a landing Noah steers. Ararat is mercy under the keel. Grounded, they are still not sent out; rest is a station of hope, not yet a commission to scatter.

Receiving safety is itself obedience-shaped faith. Noah does not seize the mountain; he is given it. The God who remembered now lets the vessel sit still so trust can deepen rather than scramble.

Reformed

Seventh month, seventeenth day, mountains of Ararat—providence with a date stamp. God docks the vessel He designed. The remnant rests where He appoints, not where human navigation guessed.

The ark’s rest is the Lord’s doing. He judged by water; He now parks salvation’s boat on a mountain. Dates in Genesis are pastoral: your times are in His hand.

Genesis 8:5

5The waters receded continually until the tenth month. In the tenth month, on the first day of the month, the tops of the mountains were visible.

Arminian

Mountain tops appear—hope becomes visible before the door opens. God often lets His people see signs while they still wait. The sight is meant to nourish patience, not to baptize impatience as courage.

Visible mercy invites a living response of trust. The family can take heart without rewriting God’s schedule. Grace shows enough to sustain them until the later word to go out.

Reformed

Waters keep abating until the tenth month; on the first day the peaks are seen. What appears is what God unveils. The world is being restored on His calendar.

Sight itself is gift. Mountain tops do not surface by accident. Faith looks at a landscape God is drying, and learns to tell time by His months rather than by panic.

Genesis 8:6

6At the end of forty days, Noah opened the window of the ship which he had made,

Arminian

After forty more days Noah opens the window he had made. Waiting is not idleness; it includes wise looking. He uses the means God allowed him to build.

Classical Arminians gladly confess human agency under grace. Noah is not a spectator of his own rescue. He looks, tests, and hopes—without snatching the timeline from the Lord who remembered him.

Reformed

Forty days after the peaks, Noah opens a window. The act is not in doubt, yet it sits inside God’s larger timing. Means matter; they do not replace the Lord who dries the earth.

We need not deny Noah’s initiative to honor God’s sovereignty. The window is an instrument in a story God is writing. Providence includes the looking of the man He preserved.

Genesis 8:7

7and he sent out a raven. It went back and forth, until the waters were dried up from the earth.

Arminian

A raven goes back and forth, until the waters dry—an early probe, not yet a green signal. Noah employs created means. Wisdom and waiting can live in the same heart.

Grace does not make Noah passive. He seeks knowledge of a world God is healing, and he remains responsible to wait for more than a restless bird before he walks out.

Reformed

Even the raven’s circuit runs until God has dried the earth. An unclean scavenger can serve as a sign in a world still being reset. Noah sends; the drying remains the Lord’s work.

Reformed readers see providence in ordinary creatures. The bird’s going and returning do not dethrone God; they display a world still answering to His purpose for the remnant in the ark.

Genesis 8:8

8He himself sent out a dove to see if the waters were abated from the surface of the ground,

Arminian

Then a dove, sent “to see” whether the waters had subsided. Noah wants knowledge so he may obey well. Faith is allowed to ask questions of the ground God is restoring.

Seeking a sign is not unbelief when it serves patient obedience. The dove is a means of discernment. Grace invites this careful hope rather than a reckless leap onto still-flooded earth.

Reformed

Noah sends the dove after the raven, testing the waters step by step. The patriarch does not rush the door. Prudence here is fruit of a heart God has kept through judgment.

God is not threatened by Noah’s testing of the waters. The remnant lives by wisdom as well as by miracle. Providence trains the saved to wait for a habitable world.

Genesis 8:9

9but the dove found no place to rest her foot, and she returned into the ship to him, for the waters were on the surface of the whole earth. He put out his hand, and took her, and brought her to him into the ship.

Arminian

No place for her foot—the earth is not ready. Noah’s hand is tender: he takes the weary bird back in. Mercy inside the ark mirrors the God who remembered them. Waiting is not failure; it is love that will not force an unfinished deliverance.

What comes through is a holy refusal to pretend the ground is dry. Grace that saved them also teaches them not to outrun God’s work. The open hand that receives the dove is itself a response to mercy.

Reformed

The waters are still on the surface of the whole earth. Noah’s gentle reception of the dove displays kindness in a man preserved by grace. Timing remains the Lord’s; the ark is still the appointed refuge.

Shelter continues until God makes a place to stand. The remnant is not safer for leaving early. Preservation includes this homing of the dove and the still-closed door of the ark.

Genesis 8:10

10He waited yet another seven days; and again he sent the dove out of the ship.

Arminian

Noah’s waiting has a rhythm—chosen delay, not frozen panic. Obedience includes not going yet.

Grace enables patience; it does not annul the duty to wait. Seven more days preach that hope can be punctual without being hasty. The promise is trusted by staying as much as by sending.

Reformed

Another week under God’s hand. The remnant lives by His clock. Waiting is not wasted time; it is how the preserved family learns that exit will be gift, not grab.

The seven days are as much providence as the wind of verse 1. God schools the ark’s household in dependence. He who remembered them now teaches them the pace of mercy.

Genesis 8:11

11The dove came back to him at evening and, behold, in her mouth was a freshly plucked olive leaf. So Noah knew that the waters were abated from the earth.

Arminian

A freshly plucked olive leaf—life returning. Noah “knew.” God allows evidence that feeds confident hope. The sign does not replace the later command to go out, but it nourishes trust that mercy is already at work.

Knowledge here is a gift that still asks for a living response. Noah is not guessing in the dark. Grace gives enough light to keep waiting well until the Lord speaks.

Reformed

Knowledge arrives through a sign God provided. The earth’s healing is His doing; Noah reads what providence places in the bird’s beak.

“Knew” is not autonomous insight. God supplies the token, and faith interprets it. Olive life after judgment is a sermon: the Lord who kills and makes alive is already mending the ground.

Genesis 8:12

12He waited yet another seven days, and sent out the dove; and she didn’t return to him any more.

Arminian

A third sending, and the dove does not return. Silence now means the bird has found a home. Noah still does not leap; he has learned to wait for more than a good sign.

Classical Arminians honor this restraint as holiness, not timidity. Faith can read the world and still refuse to baptize impatience as courage. The God who invites trust also invites us not to run ahead of His word.

Reformed

Non-return is itself providence. The dove finds a home because God has made one. Noah remains until the Lord speaks—signs serve; they do not authorize self-discharge from God’s appointed shelter.

The ark is still holy confinement until the command of verses 15–17. Effectual preservation includes staying put. The remnant exits by a word, not by a bird’s successful relocation.

Genesis 8:13

13In the six hundred first year, in the first month, the first day of the month, the waters were dried up from the earth. Noah removed the covering of the ship, and looked. He saw that the surface of the ground was dry.

Arminian

New year’s day in Noah’s six hundred first year: waters dried, covering removed, he looks. Human looking is no illusion; so is the dryness God has given. Hope now has a view—and the family still stays until God speaks.

Using your eyes is not unbelief. Noah inspects a mercy already accomplished in part. Grace that remembered him also trains him to wait for the Voice after the view.

Reformed

The date is recorded to the day. God writes history with precision. Removing the covering is permitted sight; remaining until the command shows that preservation includes waiting on the Word.

The calendar belongs to the Lord. “Behold” is gift. The remnant’s safety is still the ark and the God of the ark, even when the ground looks dry.

Genesis 8:14

14In the second month, on the twenty-seventh day of the month, the earth was dry.

Arminian

Nearly two months after he saw dry ground, the earth is fully dried out. God’s thoroughness blesses patience. Mercy is not hurried; it finishes the work so that going out will be life, not a second drowning.

The extra weeks honor both divine care and human waiting. Noah could have argued with the first glimpse; instead the story lets dryness become complete. Trusting grace means not rushing a half-ready world.

Reformed

Another exact date—second month, twenty-seventh day. Creation’s recovery is complete on God’s schedule, not on Noah’s first glimpse. The Lord who judged by water now brings the earth to a finished dryness.

God does not fumble the end of judgment. Dates preach certainty. The world the remnant will inherit is the world He has actually prepared.

Genesis 8:15

15God spoke to Noah, saying,

Arminian

Then God said to Noah. Signs, dates, and dove-flights prepared him; they did not replace the Voice. Going out will be obedience, not guesswork. Grace that remembered now speaks.

Wesleyan Arminians find a sincere word meant to be believed and done. The Lord addresses a man who can truly respond. Command is kindness: Noah is not left to invent his own exodus from the ark.

Reformed

Command precedes exit. The remnant does not vote itself onto dry ground. The God who shut them in now addresses the man He kept. Effectual preservation includes this speech.

Salvation’s story is word-driven to the end. Sight of dry ground was not sufficient warrant. They leave because God speaks—initiative still His, agency in Noah still true under that word.

Genesis 8:16

16“Go out of the ship, you, your wife, your sons, and your sons’ wives with you.

Arminian

The summons is particular and familial—Noah, wife, sons, sons’ wives. Deliverance is for a household called to walk out in trust. The command is sincere; their going will be a response, not puppetry.

Grace enables this exit and still treats it as obedience. God does not save a nameless cargo. He calls persons by their bonds of marriage and family to step onto a world He has made ready.

Reformed

God specifies who may leave the ark He appointed. The family He preserved is the family He now sends. Obedience will follow because the Lord who saved them also summons them.

Election’s care in the flood story is not cold. The names and households are counted. The same sovereignty that kept them through water now orders their first steps on dry ground—agency included, not erased.

Genesis 8:17

17Bring out with you every living thing that is with you of all flesh, including birds, livestock, and every creeping thing that creeps on the earth, that they may breed abundantly on the earth, and be fruitful, and multiply on the earth.”

Arminian

Blessing language returns—swarm, be fruitful, multiply. Judgment is not God’s last word for the earth. The command is generous: life is to fill the world again under a sincere word of common kindness.

This is commission, not theater. Creatures are sent to flourish. The God who remembered Noah remembers the earth’s filling, and He invites creaturely fruitfulness as response to His gift of a washed world.

Reformed

The creation mandate is restated by the God who held every kind through the flood. Fruitfulness is gift and charge from the same mouth that judged. Providence that preserved “all flesh” in the ark now orders their spreading.

The world begins again by decree. Swarming and multiplying are not human self-start; they answer a command. Yet the beasts truly go, and the earth is truly to be filled—God’s purpose working through creaturely life.

Genesis 8:18

18Noah went out, with his sons, his wife, and his sons’ wives with him.

Arminian

“Noah went out.” Word then walk. The family follows the voice that remembered them. This is true obedience enabled by grace—Noah is not a spectator of his own story.

Classical Arminians refuse both Pelagian swagger and a picture of Noah as a puppet. He truly goes out, because God truly spoke and because grace made a believing response possible. Mercy received becomes mercy obeyed.

Reformed

Command then compliance. Reformed readers do not deny Noah’s agency; they locate it under a prior word. The family exits because God spoke and because He had kept them able to hear and to obey.

This “so” is the right shape of a saved life. Grace is first; footsteps follow. The remnant’s freedom is no pretense, and it is freedom on a path God marked—from ark to earth by His summons.

Genesis 8:19

19Every animal, every creeping thing, and every bird, whatever moves on the earth, after their families, went out of the ship.

Arminian

Every living thing goes out by families—ordered life after catastrophe, not a stampede. God’s mercy restores kind and kinship. The earth is invited to fill again under blessing, not under leftover chaos.

Judgment was in earnest, and so is this merciful restart. Worship will be the family’s first act (v. 20), but already the world is being restabilized by God’s word. Creaturely flourishing is a gift meant to be received and lived.

Reformed

Families of creatures file out as God intended. The ark’s cargo was never random. Preservation of kinds is covenant care for a world He will not annihilate again in this way.

Both camps agree here in the main—mercy is as true as the flood. The Lord who judged now restabilizes the earth by command. What He preserved, He now plants back into the world He still rules.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

On God remembering Noah and the waters receding, a few classic voices from each tradition.

Arminian Notable voices

“But the whole race of mankind, except Noah and his family, was now extinguished, and gone into the land of forgetfulness, so that God's remembering Noah was the return of his mercy to mankind, of whom he would not make a full end. Noah himself, tho' one that had found grace in the eyes of the Lord, yet seemed to be forgotten in the ark; but at length God returned in mercy to him, and that is expressed by his remembering him.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 8:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“The remembrance of which Moses speaks, ought to be referred not only to the external aspect of things, (so to speak,) but also to the inward feeling of the holy man. Indeed it is certain, that Gods from the time in which he had once received Noah into his protection, was never unmindful of him; for, truly, it was by as great a miracle, that he did not perish through suffocation in the ark, as if he had lived without breath, submerged in the waters.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 8:1)
