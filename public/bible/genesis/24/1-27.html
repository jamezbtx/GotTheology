Passage

Genesis 24

Verses 1–27 · ESV · Voices

Abraham is old and richly blessed. He binds his senior servant by oath: no Canaanite wife for Isaac, and Isaac must not be taken back to Mesopotamia. The servant prays at Nahor’s well; before he finishes, Rebekah appears, waters the camels, and proves to be kin. He worships the LORD who has led him in the way.

Genesis 24:1

1Now Abraham was old, well advanced in years. And the LORD had blessed Abraham in all things.

Arminian

Old age and full blessing sit together. Abraham’s years have not emptied the promise; they press the next act of faith—a wife for Isaac, the son of his old age.

Classical Arminians hear vocation still open: grace has blessed him in all things, and that gift now calls for a responsible step. The Lord who enriched the household still invites Abraham to seek the seed’s future by means, not by sitting still.

Reformed

The narrator sums a life under God’s hand: old, advanced, blessed in all things. Prosperity is not luck; it is the Lord’s pledged kindness to the man of the covenant.

Reformed comfort: the God who blessed Abraham in all things will not leave the heir unmarried and the line hanging. Age is the hour of providence, not the end of the oath.

Genesis 24:2

2And Abraham said to his servant, the oldest of his household, who had charge of all that he had, “Put your hand under my thigh,

Arminian

He turns to the oldest servant, the man already trusted with everything. The thigh-oath is solemn, bodily, and public between master and steward.

Arminian pastoral note: God often works through a faithful household. The servant’s agency is real; he will pray, travel, and discern. Grace uses a willing steward rather than bypassing him.

Reformed

The charge of all Abraham has now includes the future of the seed. A servant’s hand under the thigh binds the errand to the patriarch’s body and hope.

Reformed note: providence appoints means. The Lord who holds the promise also holds this unnamed steward in office for such a day.

Genesis 24:3

3that I may make you swear by the LORD, the God of heaven and God of the earth, that you will not take a wife for my son from the daughters of the Canaanites, among whom I dwell,

Arminian

The oath is by the LORD, God of heaven and earth—no local idol. Isaac must not marry among the Canaanites with whom they dwell. Holiness of the household is a moral charge, not a mood.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere boundary: the family of promise must not melt into the land’s daughters by casual choice. Abraham uses his authority so that Isaac’s marriage will serve the Lord’s purpose, and the servant must own that charge.

Reformed

Heaven and earth’s God is invoked so the seed will not be absorbed by Canaan. Dwelling among them does not mean becoming them.

Reformed accent: the covenant line is guarded by oath. God has given this land to Abraham’s offspring; He will not have the heir yoked to the peoples whose iniquity the Lord has already named.

Genesis 24:4

4but will go to my country and to my kindred, and take a wife for my son Isaac.”

Arminian

The alternative is a journey to kindred: a wife from the old household, for Isaac by name. Marriage is sought as a means of the promise, not as a private romance only.

Arminian emphasis: going and taking are real tasks. The servant must travel and ask. Enabling grace does not cancel the road; it sends a man down it.

Reformed

Country and kindred become the map of the errand. Isaac is named because the whole covenant rides on this son.

Reformed reading: God can fetch a wife from Mesopotamia without fetching Isaac back. The heir stays in the land; the bride will be brought to the promise.

Genesis 24:5

5The servant said to him, “Perhaps the woman may not be willing to follow me to this land. Must I then take your son back to the land from which you came?”

Arminian

The servant names a real possibility: she may not be willing. He will not kidnap a bride, and he asks what happens if she refuses the land.

Classical Arminians linger here with gratitude. Human willingness is treated as morally weighty. The errand assumes a woman who can say no, and a servant who must not force the story.

Reformed

Prudence speaks. The steward foresees a fork: her will, or Isaac’s return. Abraham will close one door and leave the other in God’s hands.

Reformed note: even this “perhaps” sits inside providence. The question protects the land-oath. Isaac must not be extracted from Canaan to solve a marriage.

Genesis 24:6

6Abraham said to him, “See to it that you do not take my son back there.

Arminian

The land is non-negotiable. Isaac is not to be walked backward out of the promise, even for a wife.

Arminian pastoral: Abraham’s charge is a responsible fence. Faith stays where God has planted the heir. Marriage must come to Canaan; Canaan must not be abandoned for marriage.

Reformed

“See to it” is sharp. The son of the oath stays in the land sworn to offspring.

Reformed comfort: geography is theology. God took Abraham from there; He will not have Isaac resettled in the old country as if the call were reversible.

Genesis 24:7

7The LORD, the God of heaven, who took me from my father’s house and from the land of my kindred, and who spoke to me and swore to me, ‘To your offspring I will give this land,’ he will send his angel before you, and you shall take a wife for my son from there.

Arminian

Abraham rests the errand on God’s past: He took, He spoke, He swore the land. On that basis the servant may expect an angel sent before him, and a wife taken from there.

Classical Arminians hear living confidence, not fatalism. Prayer and travel still matter; the angel “before you” assists a servant who must go. The God who called Abraham sincerely pledges help for the next obedience.

Reformed

The oath to give this land is the engine of the mission. The same Lord who extracted Abraham will send His angel ahead of the steward.

Reformed accent: this is covenant certainty. God will prosper the way because He has sworn offspring in this land. The angel is not a maybe; he is the Lord’s escort for a decreed mercy.

Genesis 24:8

8But if the woman is not willing to follow you, then you will be free from this oath of mine; only you must not take my son back there.”

Arminian

If she is not willing, the servant is free—the oath does not license coercion. Isaac still must not go back. Two freedoms stand: hers to refuse, and the land’s claim on the son.

Arminian reading: willingness is a real moral act. God is not staging a puppet. The woman will be asked; her no would count. Grace seeks a bride who will follow, not a captive.

Reformed

Her possible refusal does not reopen Mesopotamia for Isaac. The servant may be released; the heir may not be relocated.

Reformed weight: God can provide another means without undoing the land-oath. Human unwillingness, if it came, would not cancel the promise—only this particular path. Isaac stays because God has spoken.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat Rebekah as cargo. Arminians typically stress her genuine, resistible willingness as a condition Abraham himself honors. Reformed readers typically stress that even a refusal could not send Isaac back; the land-oath and God’s purpose for the heir stand above the errand.

Genesis 24:9

9So the servant put his hand under the thigh of Abraham his master and swore to him concerning this matter.

Arminian

He does what was asked. The oath is taken, not merely discussed. A servant’s word becomes a holy bond.

Classical Arminians honor this as responsible fidelity. He could have stalled; he swears. Means of grace include promises kept under God.

Reformed

The hand goes under the thigh; the matter is sealed. God will work through a sworn man.

Reformed note: the steward’s oath is itself providence. The Lord binds a household servant into the story of the seed.

Genesis 24:10

10Then the servant took ten of his master’s camels and departed, taking all sorts of choice gifts from his master; and he arose and went to Mesopotamia to the city of Nahor.

Arminian

Ten camels and choice gifts: the household’s wealth travels as a bride-price and a testimony. He arises and goes to Nahor’s city.

Arminian pastoral: faith packs the camels. Prayer will come at the well, but first he actually departs. Enabling grace looks like a road taken.

Reformed

Mesopotamia, the city of Nahor: providence returns to the kindred Abraham left, now to fetch a wife for the son of promise.

Reformed reading: gifts and camels are Abraham’s blessed store (v. 1) put in motion. The Lord funds the errand He has ordained.

Genesis 24:11

11And he made the camels kneel down outside the city by the well of water at the time of evening, the time when women go out to draw water.

Arminian

He stops at the evening well, when women draw water. The place and hour are wise: he puts himself where a conversation can happen.

Classical Arminians see sanctified prudence. God is not honored by aimless waiting. The servant chooses a well and an hour; grace will meet him there without erasing his good sense.

Reformed

Camels kneel at the hour women come. Ordinary timing becomes the stage of a covenant meeting.

Reformed accent: the Lord appoints wells as surely as wars. Evening water-drawing is not chance; it is the clock of providence.

Genesis 24:12

12And he said, “O LORD, God of my master Abraham, please grant me success today and show steadfast love to my master Abraham.

Arminian

He prays in Abraham’s God: success today, steadfast love shown to his master. The errand is framed as mercy to Abraham, not as the servant’s career.

Arminian warmth: “please grant” is a real ask. Prayer here engages God’s will and the man’s need. Success is requested, not presumed; the servant believes the Lord truly hears and may answer this day.

Reformed

Steadfast love (hesed) is covenant language. He asks the God of Abraham to be Abraham’s God still, today, at this well.

Reformed comfort: the servant appeals to pledged kindness, not to luck. Success, if it comes, will be the Lord’s hesed made visible.

Genesis 24:13

13Behold, I am standing by the spring of water, and the daughters of the men of the city are coming out to draw water.

Arminian

He tells God where he is and who is coming. Prayer names the scene: a spring, daughters of the city, a man on an oath.

Arminian note: he does not wait in abstraction. He stands, he sees, he prays over the actual road. Means and petition belong together.

Reformed

The spring and the approaching daughters are laid before the Lord. Providence is about to walk up in a particular woman.

Reformed reading: “behold” is faith watching the stage God has set. The servant’s location is already an answer beginning.

Genesis 24:14

14Let the young woman to whom I shall say, ‘Please let down your jar that I may drink,’ and who shall say, ‘Drink, and I will water your camels’—let her be the one whom you have appointed for your servant Isaac. By this I shall know that you have shown steadfast love to my master.”

Arminian

He asks for a sign that is also a character test: not mere chance, but costly kindness—water for a stranger and for ten camels. He wants to know God’s hesed by a woman appointed for Isaac.

Classical Arminians hear a servant who will still speak and still look. The request does not cancel her agency; it asks God to mark a willing, generous woman. Prayer seeks knowledge so he may act without forcing.

Reformed

“Appointed” is the verb to linger on. He is not shopping; he is asking the Lord to identify the one already chosen for Isaac.

Reformed accent: the sign reveals a decree. Steadfast love will be knowable because God has appointed a wife for the heir. Guidance here is God’s showing what He has willed.

Key contrast Both take the prayer as faith, not superstition. Arminians typically stress a request for guidance that still honors the woman’s free, costly kindness. Reformed readers typically stress “appointed”: the sign uncovers God’s prior choice of a wife for Isaac.

Genesis 24:15

15Before he had finished speaking, behold, Rebekah, who was born to Bethuel the son of Milcah, the wife of Nahor, Abraham’s brother, came out with her water jar on her shoulder.

Arminian

Before the prayer is done, Rebekah is there—named, genealogied, jar on her shoulder. God answers while the servant is still speaking, and she is also simply doing her evening work.

Arminian reading: swift answer encourages prayer. Rebekah comes on her own errand; she is not dragged. The Lord can meet a praying man with a woman who freely walks to the well.

Reformed

The genealogy is the tell: Bethuel, Milcah, Nahor, Abraham’s brother. The appointed wife is kin, arriving on time.

Reformed comfort: “before he had finished” is too exact for chance. God brought her while the words were still in the servant’s mouth. Election of this woman for the seed wears a water jar.

Key contrast Both see a prayer answered in the same instant. Arminians typically emphasize that God answers a seeking servant while Rebekah also comes freely to her work. Reformed readers typically emphasize the timing and lineage as evidence that God had already appointed her.

Genesis 24:16

16The young woman was very attractive in appearance, a maiden whom no man had known. She went down to the spring and filled her jar and came up.

Arminian

Beauty and chastity are noted without turning her into a prize only. She goes down, fills, comes up—she is at work before she is asked.

Arminian pastoral: character will show in what she does next, not in the narrator’s first glance. Grace can inhabit ordinary industry. She remains a responsible young woman on an errand.

Reformed

The heir’s wife is a maiden unknown to man, attractive, and busy at the spring. God has not sent a cipher.

Reformed note: providence includes her purity and her steps down to the water. The Lord prepares a fit mother for the next generation of promise.

Genesis 24:17

17Then the servant ran to meet her and said, “Please give me a little water to drink from your jar.”

Arminian

He runs and asks for a little water—the first half of his own sign. He does not wait for her to notice him; he engages.

Classical Arminians like the run. Faith uses legs. The prayer does not replace the request; it sends him toward her with courtesy.

Reformed

The steward moves. Providence has placed her; the sworn man must still speak the sentence he told God he would speak.

Reformed reading: means and decree meet in a polite ask. God answers prayer through a servant who runs.

Genesis 24:18

18She said, “Drink, my lord.” And she quickly let down her jar upon her hand and gave him a drink.

Arminian

“Drink, my lord”—quick, courteous, hands-on. She lets the jar down and gives. Hospitality begins in a small cup.

Arminian warmth: this kindness is hers. No trance is required. Enabling grace (if we speak of it here) would be God’s quiet help to a generous heart, not the erasure of Rebekah.

Reformed

Speed and honor mark her. The first half of the sign is already true.

Reformed note: the Lord has matched the servant’s prayer with a woman whose instinct is gift. Ordinary courtesy is here a thread of the covenant.

Genesis 24:19

19When she had finished giving him a drink, she said, “I will draw water for your camels also, until they have finished drinking.”

Arminian

She volunteers the rest: camels also, until they have finished. Ten thirsty camels are a long night’s work. She offers what the servant asked God to hear.

Classical Arminians hear a free overflow. She was not quoted a script. She proposes costly service because that is who she is—and the servant will recognize the Lord’s kindness in it.

Reformed

The exact kindness named in prayer now sits on her lips. “Until they have finished” is thorough mercy.

Reformed accent: God has put the sign-words in a willing mouth. The appointed wife is identifiable by a generosity the Lord Himself specified.

Genesis 24:20

20So she quickly emptied her jar into the trough and ran again to the well to draw water, and she drew for all his camels.

Arminian

Quickly, emptied, ran, drew for all. The narrator slows us with labor: this is sweat, not a slogan. Kindness that costs is the family resemblance of Abraham’s house.

Arminian pastoral praise: she finishes the work. Grace-enabled love looks like running with a jar. Rebekah is not a symbol only; she is a woman who serves strangers well.

Reformed

All his camels drink because she runs. The sign is not a trick of timing only; it is endurance God has planted in her.

Reformed reading: the Lord who appointed her also fitted her for the hour. Costly hospitality becomes the seal on the errand.

Genesis 24:21

21The man gazed at her in silence to learn whether the LORD had prospered his journey or not.

Arminian

He does not pounce. He gazes in silence, still learning whether the Lord has prospered the journey. Discernment has a pause in it.

Arminian note: even a matching sign is tested. He waits to see the whole—the camels finished, the lineage asked. Prayerful people may watch before they conclude. The Lord is not honored by hasty claims.

Reformed

Silence is faith’s ear. Prosperity of the journey is the Lord’s to grant; the servant watches for it rather than announcing it.

Reformed comfort: he treats success as God’s act. The question “or not” keeps him humble until the Lord makes the kinship plain.

Genesis 24:22

22When the camels had finished drinking, the man took a gold ring weighing a half shekel, and two bracelets for her arms weighing ten gold shekels,

Arminian

After the camels finish, gifts appear: a gold ring, two heavy bracelets. Generosity answers generosity; Abraham’s wealth speaks before the family does.

Arminian reading: gifts are means, not magic. They honor her labor and open a conversation. The servant still must ask whose daughter she is.

Reformed

Gold by weight is the blessed household made portable. Providence funds the next words.

Reformed note: the Lord who prospered Abraham (v. 1) now hangs that prosperity on Rebekah’s arms as a sign of serious intent.

Genesis 24:23

23and said, “Please tell me whose daughter you are. Is there room in your father’s house for us to spend the night?”

Arminian

Identity and lodging: whose daughter, and is there room? He has not yet assumed the answer. Courtesy keeps asking.

Classical Arminians see responsible caution. Signs do not skip questions. He seeks her house by consent, not by claim.

Reformed

The lineage question is the hinge. If she is not kin, the gold is still a gift, not a marriage.

Reformed accent: God will answer through her mouth. The father’s house is about to be named as the house the angel was sent to find.

Genesis 24:24

24She said to him, “I am the daughter of Bethuel the son of Milcah, whom she bore to Nahor.”

Arminian

Bethuel, Milcah, Nahor—the very kindred Abraham named. She tells the truth simply.

Arminian pastoral: her word is a real disclosure. The servant’s prayer and her identity meet without either being fake. God answers by a woman who knows her family and says so.

Reformed

The genealogy of verse 15 is now on her lips. Nahor’s line has walked to the well.

Reformed comfort: the oath’s map is confirmed. The Lord has led to Abraham’s brother’s house, as spoken.

Genesis 24:25

25She added, “We have plenty of both straw and fodder, and room to spend the night.”

Arminian

Plenty of straw, fodder, and room: she matches camel-kindness with household welcome. Hospitality is a family store, not a moment’s impulse.

Arminian note: she offers what she can actually give. Grace-shaped homes make space. The yes to lodging is another free kindness.

Reformed

Provision for beasts and men is ready. God has prepared a house as well as a woman.

Reformed reading: plenty here is the overflow of a providence that intended this night all along.

Genesis 24:26

26The man bowed his head and worshiped the LORD

Arminian

Before he bargains or eats, he bows and worships. The first fruit of a prospered journey is praise.

Classical Arminians love this order. Answered prayer should stop a man. Worship is a chosen act under grace—he could have rushed to the house; he adores.

Reformed

The Name receives the credit on the spot. Bowed head is theology in a body.

Reformed instinct: when God leads, the right reflex is worship. The servant will speak next, but first the Lord is honored as the One who did this.

Genesis 24:27

27and said, “Blessed be the LORD, the God of my master Abraham, who has not forsaken his steadfast love and his faithfulness toward my master. As for me, the LORD has led me in the way to the house of my master’s kinsmen.”

Arminian

He blesses the God of Abraham: hesed and faithfulness not forsaken. Then he interprets his own path: the Lord has led me in the way to the kinsmen’s house.

Arminian emphasis: leading is real and knowable in the event. The servant traces kindness and names it. Prayer, running, asking, and waiting were not theater; they were the way God led a man who actually walked it.

Reformed

Steadfast love and faithfulness are the covenant pair. God has not forsaken Abraham; therefore this well is not an accident.

Reformed confession: “the LORD has led me” is the doctrine of the chapter. Guidance is God’s faithful hand, not the servant’s luck. The house of kinsmen is where the oath intended him to stand.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the servant’s oath and the prayer answered at the well—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 24:1–27.

Arminian Notable voices

“Send me good speed this day—We have leave to be particular in recommending our affairs to the care of Divine providence. Those that would have good speed must pray for it this day, in this affair. Thus we must, in all our ways acknowledge God.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 24:12)

Reformed Notable voices

“The servant, being destitute of counsel, retakes himself to prayers. Yet he does not simply ask counsel of the Lord; but he also prays that the maid appointed to be the wife of Isaac should be brought to him with a certain sign, from which he might gather that she was divinely presented to him. It is an evidence of his piety and faith, that in a matter of such perplexity he is not bewildered, as one astonished; but breaks forth into prayer with a collected mind.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 24:12)
