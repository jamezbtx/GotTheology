Passage

Genesis 24

Verses 1–27 · WEB · Voices

Abraham is old and richly blessed. He binds his senior servant by oath: no Canaanite wife for Isaac, and Isaac must not be taken back to Mesopotamia. The servant prays at Nahor’s well; before he finishes, Rebekah appears, waters the camels, and proves to be kin. He worships the LORD who has led him in the way.

Genesis 24:1

1Abraham was old, and well advanced in age. The LORD had blessed Abraham in all things.

Arminian

Old age and full blessing sit together. Abraham’s years have not emptied the promise; they press the next act of faith—a wife for Isaac, the son of his old age.

The accent falls on vocation still open: grace has blessed him in all things, and that gift now calls for a responsible step. The Lord who enriched the household still invites Abraham to seek the seed’s future by means, not by sitting still.

Reformed

The narrator sums a life under God’s hand: old, advanced, blessed in all things. Prosperity is not luck; it is the Lord’s pledged kindness to the man of the covenant.

The God who blessed Abraham in all things will not leave the heir unmarried and the line hanging. Age is the hour of providence, not the end of the oath.

Genesis 24:2

2Abraham said to his servant, the elder of his house, who ruled over all that he had, “Please put your hand under my thigh.

Arminian

He turns to the oldest servant, the man already trusted with everything. The thigh-oath is solemn, bodily, and public between master and steward.

God often works through a faithful household. The servant’s agency is not in doubt; he will pray, travel, and discern. Grace uses a willing steward rather than bypassing him.

Reformed

The charge of all Abraham has now includes the future of the seed. A servant’s hand under the thigh binds the errand to the patriarch’s body and hope.

Providence appoints means. The Lord who holds the promise also holds this unnamed steward in office for such a day.

Genesis 24:3

3I will make you swear by the LORD, the God of heaven and the God of the earth, that you shall not take a wife for my son of the daughters of the Canaanites, among whom I live.

Arminian

The oath is by the LORD, God of heaven and earth—no local idol. Isaac must not marry among the Canaanites with whom they dwell. Holiness of the household is a moral charge, not a mood.

Arminian interpreters hear a sincere boundary: the family of promise must not melt into the land’s daughters by casual choice. Abraham uses his authority so that Isaac’s marriage will serve the Lord’s purpose, and the servant must own that charge.

Reformed

Heaven and earth’s God is invoked so the seed will not be absorbed by Canaan. Dwelling among them does not mean becoming them.

The covenant line is guarded by oath. God has given this land to Abraham’s offspring; He will not have the heir yoked to the peoples whose iniquity the Lord has already named.

Genesis 24:4

4But you shall go to my country, and to my relatives, and take a wife for my son Isaac.”

Arminian

The alternative is a journey to kindred: a wife from the old household, for Isaac by name. Marriage is sought as a means of the promise, not as a private romance only.

Going and taking are tasks. The servant must travel and ask. Enabling grace does not annul the road; it sends a man down it.

Reformed

Country and kindred become the map of the errand. Isaac is named because the whole covenant rides on this son.

God can fetch a wife from Mesopotamia without fetching Isaac back. The heir stays in the land; the bride will be brought to the promise.

Genesis 24:5

5The servant said to him, “What if the woman isn’t willing to follow me to this land? Must I bring your son again to the land you came from?”

Arminian

The servant names a possibility: she may not be willing. He will not kidnap a bride, and he asks what happens if she refuses the land.

Arminian interpreters linger here with gratitude. Human willingness is treated as morally weighty. The errand assumes a woman who can say no, and a servant who must not force the story.

Reformed

Prudence speaks. The steward foresees a fork: her will, or Isaac’s return. Abraham will close one door and leave the other in God’s hands.

Even this perhaps sits inside providence. The question protects the land-oath. Isaac must not be extracted from Canaan to solve a marriage.

Genesis 24:6

6Abraham said to him, “Beware that you don’t bring my son there again.

Arminian

The land is non-negotiable. Isaac is not to be walked backward out of the promise, even for a wife.

Abraham’s charge is a responsible fence. Faith stays where God has planted the heir. Marriage must come to Canaan; Canaan must not be abandoned for marriage.

Reformed

See to it is sharp. The son of the oath stays in the land sworn to offspring.

Geography is theology. God took Abraham from there; He will not have Isaac resettled in the old country as if the call were reversible.

Genesis 24:7

7The LORD, the God of heaven—who took me from my father’s house, and from the land of my birth, who spoke to me, and who swore to me, saying, ‘I will give this land to your offspring—he will send his angel before you, and you shall take a wife for my son from there.

Arminian

Abraham rests the errand on God’s past: He took, He spoke, He swore the land. On that basis the servant may expect an angel sent before him, and a wife taken from there.

Arminian interpreters hear living confidence, not fatalism. Prayer and travel still matter; the angel “before you” assists a servant who must go. The God who called Abraham sincerely pledges help for the next obedience.

Reformed

The oath to give this land is the engine of the mission. The very Lord who extracted Abraham will send His angel ahead of the steward.

This is covenant certainty. God will prosper the way because He has sworn offspring in this land. The angel is not a maybe; he is the Lord’s escort for a decreed mercy.

Genesis 24:8

8If the woman isn’t willing to follow you, then you shall be clear from this oath to me. Only you shall not bring my son there again.”

Arminian

If she is not willing, the servant is free—the oath does not license coercion. Isaac still must not go back. Two freedoms stand: hers to refuse, and the land’s claim on the son.

Willingness is a moral act. God is not staging a puppet. The woman will be asked; her no would count. Grace seeks a bride who will follow, not a captive.

Reformed

Her possible refusal does not reopen Mesopotamia for Isaac. The servant may be released; the heir may not be relocated.

God can provide another means without undoing the land-oath. Human unwillingness, if it came, would not cancel the promise—only this particular path. Isaac stays because God has spoken.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat Rebekah as cargo. Arminians emphasize her genuine, resistible willingness as a condition Abraham himself honors. Calvinists underline that even a refusal could not send Isaac back; the land-oath and God’s purpose for the heir stand above the errand.

Genesis 24:9

9The servant put his hand under the thigh of Abraham his master, and swore to him concerning this matter.

Arminian

He does what was asked. The oath is taken, not merely discussed. A servant’s word becomes a holy bond.

Classical Arminians honor this as responsible fidelity. He could have stalled; he swears. Means of grace include promises kept under God.

Reformed

The hand goes under the thigh; the matter is sealed. God will work through a sworn man.

The steward’s oath is itself providence. The Lord binds a household servant into the story of the seed.

Genesis 24:10

10The servant took ten of his master’s camels, and departed, having a variety of good things of his master’s with him. He arose, and went to Mesopotamia, to the city of Nahor.

Arminian

Ten camels and choice gifts: the household’s wealth travels as a bride-price and a testimony. He arises and goes to Nahor’s city.

For the hearer: faith packs the camels. Prayer will come at the well, but first he actually departs. Enabling grace looks like a road taken.

Reformed

Mesopotamia, the city of Nahor: providence returns to the kindred Abraham left, now to fetch a wife for the son of promise.

Gifts and camels are Abraham’s blessed store (v. 1) put in motion. The Lord funds the errand He has ordained.

Genesis 24:11

11He made the camels kneel down outside the city by the well of water at the time of evening, the time that women go out to draw water.

Arminian

He stops at the evening well, when women draw water. The place and hour are wise: he puts himself where a conversation can happen.

Classical Arminians see sanctified prudence. God is not honored by aimless waiting. The servant chooses a well and an hour; grace will meet him there without erasing his good sense.

Reformed

Camels kneel at the hour women come. Ordinary timing becomes the stage of a covenant meeting.

The Lord appoints wells as surely as wars. Evening water-drawing is not chance; it is the clock of providence.

Genesis 24:12

12He said, “LORD, the God of my master Abraham, please give me success today, and show kindness to my master Abraham.

Arminian

He prays in Abraham’s God: success today, steadfast love shown to his master. The errand is framed as mercy to Abraham, not as the servant’s career.

“Please give” is an ask. Prayer here engages God’s will and the man’s need. Success is requested, not presumed; the servant believes the Lord truly hears and may answer this day.

Reformed

Steadfast love (hesed) is covenant language. He asks the God of Abraham to be Abraham’s God still, today, at this well.

The servant appeals to pledged kindness, not to luck. Success, if it comes, will be the Lord’s hesed made visible.

Genesis 24:13

13Behold, I am standing by the spring of water. The daughters of the men of the city are coming out to draw water.

Arminian

He tells God where he is and who is coming. Prayer names the scene: a spring, daughters of the city, a man on an oath.

He does not wait in abstraction. He stands, he sees, he prays over the actual road. Means and petition belong together.

Reformed

The spring and the approaching daughters are laid before the Lord. Providence is about to walk up in a particular woman.

“Behold” is faith watching the stage God has set. The servant’s location is already an answer beginning.

Genesis 24:14

14Let it happen, that the young lady to whom I will say, ‘Please let down your pitcher, that I may drink,’ then she says, ‘Drink, and I will also give your camels a drink,’—let her be the one you have appointed for your servant Isaac. By this I will know that you have shown kindness to my master.”

Arminian

He asks for a sign that is also a character test: not mere chance, but costly kindness—water for a stranger and for ten camels. He wants to know God’s hesed by a woman appointed for Isaac.

What comes through is a servant who will still speak and still look. The request does not nullify her agency; it asks God to mark a willing, generous woman. Prayer seeks knowledge so he may act without forcing.

Reformed

“Appointed” is the verb to linger on. He is not shopping; he is asking the Lord to identify the one already chosen for Isaac.

The sign reveals a decree. Steadfast love will be knowable because God has appointed a wife for the heir. Guidance here is God’s showing what He has willed.

Key contrast Both take the prayer as faith, not superstition. Arminians emphasize a request for guidance that still honors the woman’s free, costly kindness. Reformed commentators emphasize “appointed”: the sign uncovers God’s prior choice of a wife for Isaac.

Genesis 24:15

15Before he had finished speaking, behold, Rebekah came out, who was born to Bethuel the son of Milcah, the wife of Nahor, Abraham’s brother, with her pitcher on her shoulder.

Arminian

Before the prayer is done, Rebekah is there—named, genealogied, jar on her shoulder. God answers while the servant is still speaking, and she is also simply doing her evening work.

Swift answer encourages prayer. Rebekah comes on her own errand; she is not dragged. The Lord can meet a praying man with a woman who freely walks to the well.

Reformed

The genealogy is the tell: Bethuel, Milcah, Nahor, Abraham’s brother. The appointed wife is kin, arriving on time.

“Before he had finished” is too exact for chance. God brought her while the words were still in the servant’s mouth. Election of this woman for the seed wears a water jar.

Key contrast Both see a prayer answered in the same instant. Arminians stress that God answers a seeking servant while Rebekah also comes freely to her work. Calvinists typically stress the timing and lineage as evidence that God had already appointed her.

Genesis 24:16

16The young lady was very beautiful to look at, a virgin. No man had known her. She went down to the spring, filled her pitcher, and came up.

Arminian

Beauty and chastity are noted without turning her into a prize only. She goes down, fills, comes up—she is at work before she is asked.

Character will show in what she does next, not in the narrator’s first glance. Grace can inhabit ordinary industry. She remains a responsible young woman on an errand.

Reformed

The heir’s wife is a maiden unknown to man, attractive, and busy at the spring. God has not sent a cipher.

Providence includes her purity and her steps down to the water. The Lord prepares a fit mother for the next generation of promise.

Genesis 24:17

17The servant ran to meet her, and said, “Please give me a drink, a little water from your pitcher.”

Arminian

He runs and asks for a little water—the first half of his own sign. He does not wait for her to notice him; he engages.

Arminian readers like the run. Faith uses legs. The prayer does not replace the request; it sends him toward her with courtesy.

Reformed

The steward moves. Providence has placed her; the sworn man must still speak the sentence he told God he would speak.

Means and decree meet in a polite ask. God answers prayer through a servant who runs.

Genesis 24:18

18She said, “Drink, my lord.” She hurried, and let down her pitcher on her hand, and gave him a drink.

Arminian

“Drink, my lord”—quick, courteous, hands-on. She lets the jar down and gives. Hospitality begins in a small cup.

This kindness is hers. No trance is required. Enabling grace (if we speak of it here) would be God’s quiet help to a generous heart, not the erasure of Rebekah.

Reformed

Speed and honor mark her. The first half of the sign is already true.

The Lord has matched the servant’s prayer with a woman whose instinct is gift. Ordinary courtesy is here a thread of the covenant.

Genesis 24:19

19When she had finished giving him a drink, she said, “I will also draw for your camels, until they have finished drinking.”

Arminian

She volunteers the rest: camels also, until they have finished. Ten thirsty camels are a long night’s work. She offers what the servant asked God to hear.

Wesley’s heirs hear a free overflow. She was not quoted a script. She proposes costly service because that is who she is—and the servant will recognize the Lord’s kindness in it.

Reformed

The exact kindness named in prayer now sits on her lips. “Until they have finished” is thorough mercy.

God has put the sign-words in a willing mouth. The appointed wife is identifiable by a generosity the Lord Himself specified.

Genesis 24:20

20She hurried, and emptied her pitcher into the trough, and ran again to the well to draw, and drew for all his camels.

Arminian

Quickly, emptied, ran, drew for all. The narrator slows us with labor: this is sweat, not a slogan. Kindness that costs is the family resemblance of Abraham’s house.

She finishes the work. Grace-enabled love looks like running with a jar. Rebekah is not a symbol only; she is a woman who serves strangers well.

Reformed

All his camels drink because she runs. The sign is not a trick of timing only; it is endurance God has planted in her.

The Lord who appointed her also fitted her for the hour. Costly hospitality becomes the seal on the errand.

Genesis 24:21

21The man looked steadfastly at her, remaining silent, to know whether the LORD had made his journey prosperous or not.

Arminian

He does not pounce. He gazes in silence, still learning whether the Lord has prospered the journey. Discernment has a pause in it.

Even a matching sign is tested. He waits to see the whole—the camels finished, the lineage asked. Prayerful people may watch before they conclude. The Lord is not honored by hasty claims.

Reformed

Silence is faith’s ear. Prosperity of the journey is the Lord’s to grant; the servant watches for it rather than announcing it.

Comfort: he treats success as God’s act. The question “or not” keeps him humble until the Lord makes the kinship plain.

Genesis 24:22

22As the camels had finished drinking, the man took a golden ring of half a shekel weight, and two bracelets for her hands of ten shekels weight of gold,

Arminian

After the camels finish, gifts appear: a gold ring, two heavy bracelets. Generosity answers generosity; Abraham’s wealth speaks before the family does.

Gifts are means, not magic. They honor her labor and open a conversation. The servant still must ask whose daughter she is.

Reformed

Gold by weight is the blessed household made portable. Providence funds the next words.

The Lord who prospered Abraham (v. 1) now hangs that prosperity on Rebekah’s arms as a sign of serious intent.

Genesis 24:23

23and said, “Whose daughter are you? Please tell me. Is there room in your father’s house for us to stay?”

Arminian

Identity and lodging: whose daughter, and is there room? He has not yet assumed the answer. Courtesy keeps asking.

Arminians see responsible caution. Signs do not skip questions. He seeks her house by consent, not by claim.

Reformed

The lineage question is the hinge. If she is not kin, the gold is still a gift, not a marriage.

God will answer through her mouth. The father’s house is about to be named as the house the angel was sent to find.

Genesis 24:24

24She said to him, “I am the daughter of Bethuel the son of Milcah, whom she bore to Nahor.”

Arminian

Bethuel, Milcah, Nahor—the very kindred Abraham named. She tells the truth simply.

Her word is a disclosure. The servant’s prayer and her identity meet without either being fake. God answers by a woman who knows her family and says so.

Reformed

The genealogy of verse 15 is now on her lips. Nahor’s line has walked to the well.

The oath’s map is confirmed. The Lord has led to Abraham’s brother’s house, as spoken.

Genesis 24:25

25She said moreover to him, “We have both straw and feed enough, and room to lodge in.”

Arminian

Plenty of straw, fodder, and room: she matches camel-kindness with household welcome. Hospitality is a family store, not a moment’s impulse.

She offers what she can actually give. Grace-shaped homes make space. The yes to lodging is another free kindness.

Reformed

Provision for beasts and men is ready. God has prepared a house as well as a woman.

Plenty here is the overflow of a providence that intended this night all along.

Genesis 24:26

26The man bowed his head, and worshiped the LORD.

Arminian

Before he bargains or eats, he bows and worships. The first fruit of a prospered journey is praise.

Wesleyan Arminians love this order. Answered prayer should stop a man. Worship is a chosen act under grace—he could have rushed to the house; he adores.

Reformed

The Name receives the credit on the spot. Bowed head is theology in a body.

When God leads, the right reflex is worship. The servant will speak next, but first the Lord is honored as the One who did this.

Genesis 24:27

27He said, “Blessed be the LORD, the God of my master Abraham, who has not forsaken his loving kindness and his truth toward my master. As for me, the LORD has led me on the way to the house of my master’s relatives.”

Arminian

He blesses the God of Abraham: hesed and faithfulness not forsaken. Then he interprets his own path: the LORD has led me on the way to the kinsmen’s house.

Leading is authentic and knowable in the event. The servant traces kindness and names it. Prayer, running, asking, and waiting were not theater; they were the way God led a man who actually walked it.

Reformed

Steadfast love and faithfulness are the covenant pair. God has not forsaken Abraham; therefore this well is not an accident.

“The LORD has led me” is the doctrine of the chapter. Guidance is God’s faithful hand, not the servant’s luck. The house of kinsmen is where the oath intended him to stand.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on the servant’s oath and the prayer answered at the well, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Send me good speed this day—We have leave to be particular in recommending our affairs to the care of Divine providence. Those that would have good speed must pray for it this day, in this affair. Thus we must, in all our ways acknowledge God.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 24:12)

Reformed Notable voices

“The servant, being destitute of counsel, retakes himself to prayers. Yet he does not simply ask counsel of the Lord; but he also prays that the maid appointed to be the wife of Isaac should be brought to him with a certain sign, from which he might gather that she was divinely presented to him. It is an evidence of his piety and faith, that in a matter of such perplexity he is not bewildered, as one astonished; but breaks forth into prayer with a collected mind.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 24:12)
