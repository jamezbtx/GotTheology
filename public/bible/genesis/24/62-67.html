Passage

Genesis 24

Verses 62–67 · ESV

Isaac is dwelling in the Negeb, having returned from Beer-lahai-roi. He goes out to meditate in the field at evening and sees camels coming. Rebekah veils herself; the servant reports all he has done. Isaac brings her into Sarah’s tent; she becomes his wife; he loves her, and is comforted after his mother’s death.

Genesis 24:62

62Now Isaac had returned from Beer-lahai-roi and was dwelling in the Negeb.

Arminian

The heir comes into view: back from Beer-lahai-roi—the well of the Living One who sees me—dwelling in the Negeb. Isaac is in the land, not in Mesopotamia, just as Abraham required.

Arminian pastoral: he lives where he was meant to live. The bride must come to him. Staying in Canaan was already an obedience; meeting her will be another.

Reformed

Beer-lahai-roi recalls the God who sees (ch. 16). The heir dwells in the south under that Name.

Reformed note: Isaac never left the map of promise. Providence kept the son in the land while the servant fetched the wife.

Genesis 24:63

63And Isaac went out to meditate in the field toward evening. And he lifted up his eyes and saw, and behold, there were camels coming.

Arminian

Evening field, meditation, lifted eyes, camels. He is seeking God when the answer to a prayer he may not have fully known comes over the horizon.

Classical Arminians honor the meditation. Isaac is not idle; he is a man at prayer in a field. Lifting the eyes is a human act that meets a divine arrival. Means and gift share the dusk.

Reformed

Evening at the well and evening in the field now meet. Camels that knelt in Mesopotamia come into Isaac’s sight.

Reformed comfort: God times a son’s meditation to a caravan’s arrival. The heir looks up because the Lord has brought the appointed wife within view.

Genesis 24:64

64And Rebekah lifted up her eyes, and when she saw Isaac, she dismounted from the camel

Arminian

She lifts her eyes as he lifted his. Seeing him, she dismounts—respect, eagerness, the end of the long ride made bodily.

Arminian note: she acts on what she sees. Dismounting is her motion, not the servant’s command yet. The woman who said “I will go” now meets the man she went toward.

Reformed

Two liftings of eyes, one meeting. Providence arranges a mutual seeing in the field.

Reformed reading: the appointed pair look at each other under God’s dusk. The long road has reached the heir.

Genesis 24:65

65and said to the servant, “Who is that man, walking in the field to meet us?” The servant said, “It is my master.” So she took her veil and covered herself.

Arminian

Who is that man? My master. She veils. Modesty and betrothal custom mark the moment; she will meet Isaac as a bride, not as a casual traveler.

Classical Arminians see a last free gesture of honor. Covering herself is her act of reverence toward the man she has chosen to meet. Agency continues into the field.

Reformed

“My master” transfers the servant’s loyalty from Abraham’s errand to Isaac’s presence. The veil is the threshold of marriage.

Reformed note: God brings her to the master of the household of promise. Even the covering is fitting order in the story He has led.

Genesis 24:66

66And the servant told Isaac all the things that he had done.

Arminian

The steward reports everything. Isaac receives not only a woman but a testimony: oath, prayer, well, household, her “I will go.”

Arminian pastoral: marriage here is built on a rehearsed work of God and of people. Isaac should know how she came. Faith lives by hearing the story as well as by seeing the camels.

Reformed

All the things he had done are also all the things the Lord had done. The heir is catechized before he is comforted.

Reformed accent: Isaac’s household begins with a sermon of providence. The servant’s report is how the decree arrives in the son’s ear.

Genesis 24:67

67Then Isaac brought her into the tent of Sarah his mother and took Rebekah, and she became his wife, and he loved her. So Isaac was comforted after his mother’s death.

Arminian

Sarah’s tent receives a new mother of the line. He takes her; she becomes his wife; he loved her; he is comforted after Sarah’s death. Love and comfort are not extras—they are the human warmth of a kept promise.

Classical Arminians linger on “he loved her.” The marriage is covenant and affection. Rebekah’s willing going meets Isaac’s taking and loving. God used means—oath, prayer, hospitality, a yes—to heal a son’s grief and to carry the seed.

Reformed

The mother’s tent, the wife, the love, the comfort: God restocks the household after Machpelah. The line does not end in mourning.

Reformed close: this is why the angel was sent, why the well was timed, why she said go. The Lord appointed a wife for Isaac, and in her He comforts the heir. Promise has a future in a loved woman under Sarah’s canvas.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
