Passage

Genesis 24

Verses 28–61 · WEB · Voices

Rebekah runs to her mother’s household; Laban comes out to the man at the spring. The servant will not eat until he retells the oath, the prayer, and the answered sign. Laban and Bethuel confess that the thing is from the LORD. Gifts are given; a delay is refused; Rebekah herself says, “I will go,” and they bless her as she rides toward Isaac.

Genesis 24:28

28The young lady ran, and told her mother’s house about these words.

Arminian

She runs home with the news—to her mother’s household. The story now belongs to a family who must hear and answer.

Rebekah is a messenger, not a package. She tells “these words.” Consent later will rest on a household that has actually been informed.

Reformed

The errand moves from well to house by her feet. Providence uses her voice to open the door.

“Mother’s house” is the particular room God has chosen for the next yes. The Lord who led to the well now leads the report indoors.

Genesis 24:29

29Rebekah had a brother, and his name was Laban. Laban ran out to the man, to the spring.

Arminian

Laban is introduced by a run toward the man. Later chapters will complicate him; here he is simply the brother who comes out.

Classical Arminians take the run at face value first: a household moving quickly. Brothers have free agency in this world of betrothal. What he does with that agency will tell.

Reformed

A named brother now stands in the path of the promise. God will deal with Laban as surely as with Rebekah.

Even Laban’s haste is inside the story the Lord is writing for Isaac’s wife. The spring remains the meeting place.

Genesis 24:30

30When he saw the ring, and the bracelets on his sister’s hands, and when he heard the words of Rebekah his sister, saying, “This is what the man said to me,” he came to the man. Behold, he was standing by the camels at the spring.

Arminian

He sees gold and hears her report, then goes. The narrator lets us notice both: gifts on her arms, and “saying, This is what the man said.” Mixed motives are possible; so is ordinary family excitement.

Laban is not yet a villain, though greed is not to be excused either. Laban is responsible for why he runs. The man, for his part, is still standing by the camels—waiting, not barging in.

Reformed

Ring, bracelets, and a sister’s words fetch Laban to the spring. The servant has not moved from the place of prayer.

God can use a brother’s eye for gold without making gold the covenant. The man by the camels is still the Lord’s sent steward.

Genesis 24:31

31He said, “Come in, you blessed of the LORD. Why do you stand outside? For I have prepared the house, and room for the camels.”

Arminian

“You blessed of the LORD”—hospitality in the Name, house and camels prepared. Guest-right is offered before the speech is heard in full.

Welcome is a moral act. Whether Laban’s heart is simple or mixed, the words call the servant in. A household can still practice kindness that God will overrule for good.

Reformed

Blessed-of-the-LORD language puts the visit under Abraham’s God, even on Laban’s lips.

God has prepared a house through a brother’s mouth. The camels have a place because the errand has a place.

Genesis 24:32

32The man came into the house, and he unloaded the camels. He gave straw and feed for the camels, and water to wash his feet and the feet of the men who were with him.

Arminian

Camels unharnessed, straw, fodder, water for feet—the men with him are finally mentioned. Mercy is practical: beasts and dusty feet.

Classical Arminians honor ordinary hospitality as true obedience in its sphere. The mission will wait one washing. Grace does not despise a basin.

Reformed

The caravan is received. God grants rest to the instruments of the oath.

Even fodder is providence. The Lord who led to the house now lets the camels kneel inside it.

Genesis 24:33

33Food was set before him to eat, but he said, “I will not eat until I have told my message.” Laban said, “Speak on.”

Arminian

Food is set; he will not eat until he speaks. The errand outranks the meal. Laban (or the host) says, “Speak on.”

He keeps his word under pressure of courtesy. Faithfulness can decline a plate. The household must hear before they feast, so their yes, if it comes, is informed.

Reformed

The sworn man will not bury the message under bread. First the story, then the supper.

God has given him a single task; appetite can wait. Providence is served by a steward who knows the order of loves.

Genesis 24:34

34He said, “I am Abraham’s servant.

Arminian

He begins with identity: Abraham’s servant, not a self-made suitor. The marriage, if it happens, will be for his master’s son.

Humility is a chosen stance. He could have led with gold; he leads with belonging. Grace-shaped speech serves another’s house.

Reformed

The sentence is small and large. He is a man under authority, and that is his credential.

God advances the covenant through a servant who will not steal the name. Abraham’s God is still the subject.

Genesis 24:35

35The LORD has blessed my master greatly. He has become great. The LORD has given him flocks and herds, silver and gold, male servants and female servants, and camels and donkeys.

Arminian

The inventory is theological: the LORD has greatly blessed; therefore Abraham has become great. Flocks, metals, servants, camels, donkeys—the well-known wealth is credited to God.

This is testimony meant to be believed. Blessing is gift, recited so a family can trust the God who gave it. The servant evangelizes a household with Abraham’s story.

Reformed

Greatness is God’s doing. The list matches chapter 13 and verse 1: providence made visible in herds and gold.

This grows from the oath, not of chance. The God who promised to bless Abraham has done it, and the bride’s family should know whose house she would enter.

Genesis 24:36

36Sarah, my master’s wife, bore a son to my master when she was old. He has given all that he has to him.

Arminian

A son in old age, and all things given to him: Isaac is miracle and heir. The wife sought is for that son, not for a lesser branch.

The story of Sarah’s late fruit is told to ground hope. God has already done the harder thing. A marriage now is a fitting next trust, still requiring this family’s willing part.

Reformed

The aged womb and the total inheritance sit in one verse. The seed is identified; the estate is his.

God has concentrated the promise in Isaac. Rebekah, if she comes, comes to the heir of everything the Lord gave Abraham.

Genesis 24:37

37My master made me swear, saying, ‘You shall not take a wife for my son from the daughters of the Canaanites, in whose land I live,

Arminian

He retells the oath: no wife from Canaan’s daughters, though they dwell in that land. The family must hear the fence as well as the blessing.

Repetition is care. They deserve the terms. Holiness of marriage is not smuggled in after gifts.

Reformed

The sworn prohibition is public now in Nahor’s house. Canaan is named as the land of dwelling, not of intermarriage for the heir.

God keeps the line distinct by an oath the servant will not hide.

Genesis 24:38

38but you shall go to my father’s house, and to my relatives, and take a wife for my son.’

Arminian

Father’s house and clan: the positive map. A wife from this very circle, for the son.

Those in Wesley’s line hear an honor offered to this household. They are being asked, not raided. The going and taking still wait on their word.

Reformed

The clan of origin is the appointed field. Providence has walked the servant into the sentence he now quotes.

“My father’s house” was Abraham’s charge; it is now this room. The decree wears a family name.

Genesis 24:39

39I asked my master, ‘What if the woman will not follow me?’

Arminian

He includes his own doubt: perhaps she will not follow. Honesty about her freedom is part of the pitch.

The retelling keeps her agency on the table. They should know Abraham’s house will not coerce their daughter. A sincere offer can be refused.

Reformed

The perhaps is not edited out. God is not afraid of the question of her following.

Even the servant’s fear is now testimony. The story will show that the woman does follow—because the Lord prospers the way.

Genesis 24:40

40He said to me, ‘The LORD, before whom I walk, will send his angel with you, and prosper your way. You shall take a wife for my son from my relatives, and of my father’s house.

Arminian

Abraham’s walk before the Lord is the ground of the angel and the prospered way. The wife shall be from this clan and house.

Walking before God and being sent with an angel belong together. Means (a life of faith) and help (an angel) meet. The servant still had to come and speak.

Reformed

Angel with you, way prospered, wife from this house: Abraham’s confidence is now Laban’s information.

The Lord before whom Abraham walked binds Himself to this errand. Prosperity of the way is God’s pledged action, not a wish.

Genesis 24:41

41Then you will be clear from my oath, when you come to my relatives. If they don’t give her to you, you shall be clear from my oath.’

Arminian

Coming to the clan fulfills the servant’s duty; if they don’t give her, he is free. Their giving is treated as a hinge.

Arminians take “if they don’t give” as genuine. Households can refuse. The oath does not turn kin into machinery. Love and faithfulness (v. 49) will have to be chosen.

Reformed

The oath has a limit: come, ask, and if they don’t give, release. God does not need a forced bride to keep Isaac in the land.

Human giving is the appointed means. If it failed, the promise would not fail—only this path. Tonight, the path is this room’s decision.

Genesis 24:42

42I came today to the spring, and said, ‘The LORD, the God of my master Abraham, if now you do prosper my way which I go—

Arminian

Today, this spring, this prayer: if You do prosper my way I go. He puts the family at the well with him.

“If now you do prosper” is humble petition, not presumption. He asked to be shown. Prayer still seeks a living yes from God.

Reformed

The condition is the Lord’s prospering. The servant interprets the day as God’s possible guidance, now proven.

He came because God was already prospering the way. The prayer asked to see what the oath had pledged.

Genesis 24:43

43behold, I am standing by this spring of water. Let it happen, that the maiden who comes out to draw, to whom I will say, “Please give me a little water from your pitcher to drink,”

Arminian

He restates the standing and the first sentence of the sign. They can test his story against their sister’s memory.

Pastorally: testimony should be checkable. He invites a household to compare notes. Truthful speech honors their agency.

Reformed

The spring again, the virgin drawing water. Providence is retold so that this house may confess it.

The God who scripted the well now has the well recited in the receiving room.

Genesis 24:44

44then she tells me, “Drink, and I will also draw for your camels,”—let her be the woman whom the LORD has appointed for my master’s son.’

Arminian

Camels also—then “appointed for my master’s son.” He does not claim a lucky match; he claims the Lord’s appointment.

Classical Arminians can say both: she freely offered, and he asked God to mark such a woman for Isaac. Appointment need not erase her kindness; it names God’s hand in finding her.

Reformed

Appointed: the word returns. The family hears that Rebekah’s camel-work was the Lord’s identifier for Isaac’s wife.

God had appointed her. The sign was how a steward might know what heaven had already willed.

Genesis 24:45

45Before I had finished speaking in my heart, behold, Rebekah came out with her pitcher on her shoulder. She went down to the spring, and drew. I said to her, ‘Please let me drink.’

Arminian

He names heart-prayer: speaking in my heart, not only with lips. Before he finished, she came, jar on shoulder, and he asked her to drink.

God hears the inward ask. Prayer in the heart is engagement, not a formality. Rebekah’s coming and his speaking still happen as human acts under that hearing.

Reformed

Rebekah appears before the servant has finished praying in his heart. The Lord answers what was not yet audible to anyone else.

Providence runs faster than the Amen. God brought her while the petition was still interior.

Genesis 24:46

46She hurried and let down her pitcher from her shoulder, and said, ‘Drink, and I will also give your camels a drink.’ So I drank, and she also gave the camels a drink.

Arminian

Quick jar, double offer, then the facts: I drank; she watered the camels. The family hears their sister’s exact generosity.

This is Rebekah as they know her, or as they should. Costly kindness is not a trick the servant invented. She did it.

Reformed

The sign-words are quoted in her voice. God has given this household evidence they can recognize.

The Lord fitted her speech to the prayer. What she did at the well is now the ground of a marriage in the promise.

Genesis 24:47

47I asked her, and said, ‘Whose daughter are you?’ She said, ‘The daughter of Bethuel, Nahor’s son, whom Milcah bore to him.’ I put the ring on her nose, and the bracelets on her hands.

Arminian

Lineage first in the telling, then the ring on her nose and bracelets. They see the gold they already noticed, now explained as response to her name.

He asked, she answered, then gifts. The family is not being bought in the dark. Identity preceded the weight of gold in the meaning of the act.

Reformed

Bethuel, Nahor, Milcah: the clan-map closes. Gifts mark the recognition.

When God names the house, the steward clothes the recognition in Abraham’s gold. Providence completes its own sign.

Genesis 24:48

48I bowed my head, and worshiped the LORD, and blessed the LORD, the God of my master Abraham, who had led me in the right way to take my master’s brother’s daughter for his son.

Arminian

Worship again: bowed, blessed the LORD who had led him in the right way to take this kinsman’s daughter for Isaac.

“The right way” is guidance known after walking it—prayer, wait, ask, receive. He credits God without denying the steps. Leading is personal and moral, not a shove in the dark.

Reformed

The right way is the Lord’s leading to the right woman. Worship interprets the whole road.

God led; the daughter of the kinsman is for the son. The servant’s praise is a doctrine of particular providence.

Genesis 24:49

49Now if you will deal kindly and truly with my master, tell me. If not, tell me, that I may turn to the right hand, or to the left.”

Arminian

Now the fork is theirs: show hesed and faithfulness to Abraham, or not—and tell him. He will turn right or left if they refuse. The offer is sincere and urgent.

Wesleyan Arminians hear an alternative. This household must answer. Steadfast love toward Abraham is a choice they can make or withhold. Prayer has not canceled the need for their word.

Reformed

Hesed and faithfulness, the same pair he blessed in God (v. 27), are now asked of Laban and Bethuel. Align with what the Lord has done, or say no.

He does not threaten; he waits on a human yes that God has been pleased to require. Right and left are open to him; the promise is not.

Genesis 24:50

50Then Laban and Bethuel answered, “The thing proceeds from the LORD. We can’t speak to you bad or good.

Arminian

Father and brother speak together: the thing proceeds from the LORD. They cannot improve or veto it with “bad or good.” Recognition precedes release.

This is a yielded yes, not a silenced mind. They judge that God has acted and therefore they will not resist. Consent here is authentic—they could have spoken against it (v. 41); they refuse to.

Reformed

“From the LORD” is the chapter’s verdict in their mouths. When God has made the matter plain, human contradiction is out of place.

They confess a deed already done in heaven’s court. Their inability to speak bad or good is the right humility before an evident appointment.

Key contrast Both hear a household bowing to God’s hand. Arminians typically stress a genuine, grace-enabled consent that refuses to resist what they see God has done. Calvin’s heirs generally underline that the thing is from the Lord first—their words acknowledge a purpose they did not originate.

Genesis 24:51

51Behold, Rebekah is before you. Take her, and go, and let her be your master’s son’s wife, as the LORD has spoken.”

Arminian

Rebekah is before you: take, go, let her be the wife—as the LORD has spoken. They match the servant’s theology with a sending.

In practice: they release her under the Name. The marriage is framed as obedience to what God has said, and she will still be asked in verse 58. Means keep including her voice.

Reformed

“As the LORD has spoken” ties the betrothal to revelation, not to a clever bargain.

The wife of the master’s son is granted because God has spoken. The seed’s marriage is a kept word.

Genesis 24:52

52When Abraham’s servant heard their words, he bowed himself down to the earth to the LORD.

Arminian

He hits the earth to the LORD. Third worship of the chapter: well, retelling, now their yes.

Arminians see gratitude as the fit response to answered prayer and human consent together. He does not thank luck, and he does not skip God to thank only Laban.

Reformed

Their words drive him down before God, not up into self-congratulation.

Worship is the servant’s liturgy of providence. The Lord who led now receives the household’s agreement as His due praise.

Genesis 24:53

53The servant brought out jewels of silver, and jewels of gold, and clothing, and gave them to Rebekah. He also gave precious things to her brother and her mother.

Arminian

Jewelry, garments for Rebekah; costly ornaments for brother and mother. The gifts now function as betrothal honor in the open.

Family economics of the age are not beneath the story. Fair dealing with her people is part of taking a wife. Grace does not despise custom that protects a daughter’s house.

Reformed

Abraham’s blessed store (v. 35) is laid on the family that yielded her.

God supplies what the errand requires. Silver and gold serve the covenant marriage, not a purchase of the promise.

Genesis 24:54

54They ate and drank, he and the men who were with him, and stayed all night. They rose up in the morning, and he said, “Send me away to my master.”

Arminian

Now he eats. Night passes; morning brings the next obedience: send me away to my master. The mission has a homeward clock.

Rest is allowed; delay is not the plan. He received hospitality and still keeps the errand. Faith can sleep and then rise to finish.

Reformed

Bread after the word, then dawn and dismissal. The servant’s master is still Abraham, still in Canaan.

Providence has a pace. The Lord who prospered the way now presses the return.

Genesis 24:55

55Her brother and her mother said, “Let the young lady stay with us a few days, at least ten. After that she will go.”

Arminian

Affection and custom may sit in the request; so may stalling. They do not refuse the marriage—they slow it.

Wesleyan Arminians need not villainize a goodbye. Families feel loss. The question is whether love of home will now resist a clear leading they have already confessed.

Reformed

Ten days would feel small and is, in this story, a brake on a prospered way.

Even kin-love can cut across God’s timing. The household that said “from the LORD” is tempted to hold her a little longer.

Genesis 24:56

56He said to them, “Don’t hinder me, since the LORD has prospered my way. Send me away that I may go to my master.”

Arminian

Do not delay me: the Lord has prospered my way. When God has made the road straight, lingering becomes a kind of unbelief.

They are asked not to resist the light they have. Delay can be a soft no. The servant’s urgency is moral, not rude—send me, that I may go.

Reformed

Prospered way is the argument. Providence, once clear, should not be stalled by sentiment.

The Lord’s success of the journey carries authority to leave in the morning. The heir in Canaan is waiting under the same God.

Genesis 24:57

57They said, “We will call the young lady, and ask her.”

Arminian

They will call her and ask her. Her voice is required before the camels move. The household that spoke for her now lets her speak.

Asking her is right. Marriage in this chapter honors a woman’s yes. Enabling grace does not skip Rebekah’s mouth.

Reformed

Calling her is itself a means. God is pleased to have the appointed wife say the word.

Decree and question are not enemies here. The Lord who appointed her will have her own sentence in the record.

Genesis 24:58

58They called Rebekah, and said to her, “Will you go with this man?” She said, “I will go.”

Arminian

Will you go with this man? I will go. Short, costly, clear. She consents to leave mother, brother, and land for a husband she has not yet seen, on the strength of what God has done.

Classical Arminians hear a genuine, grace-enabled yes. Verse 8 had imagined her unwillingness; she is not unwilling. The promise sought a follower, and she freely follows. This is not coercion; it is faith’s venture.

Reformed

Her “I will go” is the human sound of the appointment. The Lord who led the servant now looses the bride’s feet.

God inclined her to go. The yes is hers, and it is the yes He appointed. Isaac’s wife is not dragged; she is drawn, and she speaks.

Key contrast Both traditions treasure Rebekah’s “I will go.” Arminians stress a resistible consent—she could have stayed, and she goes. Reformed readers stress that her going fulfills what God appointed; the Lord who prospered the way also gave her this yes.

Genesis 24:59

59They sent away Rebekah, their sister, with her nurse, Abraham’s servant, and his men.

Arminian

They send her with her nurse—companionship for a long ride—and the servant’s men. The household actually lets go.

For the hearer: sending is an act. Love finishes in release. The nurse’s presence is ordinary mercy for a young woman leaving home.

Reformed

Sister, nurse, servant, men: the caravan of the promise forms.

God extracts Rebekah from Mesopotamia as He once extracted Abraham—toward Canaan, toward the heir.

Genesis 24:60

60They blessed Rebekah, and said to her, “Our sister, may you be the mother of thousands of ten thousands, and let your offspring possess the gate of those who hate them.”

Arminian

The farewell blessing echoes the Abrahamic word: countless offspring, a gate possessed (cf. 22:17). Family lips speak more than they may know.

They pray a blessing over her. Words of fruitfulness and victory are asked of God as she goes. The household’s last gift is intercession, not only gold.

Reformed

Thousands of ten thousands and the possessed gate are covenant language. God had already sworn this shape of future to Abraham.

They unwittingly bless what the Lord has decreed. Rebekah rides out under a word bigger than a wedding toast.

Genesis 24:61

61Rebekah arose with her ladies. They rode on the camels, and followed the man. The servant took Rebekah, and went his way.

Arminian

She arises, rides, follows; he takes her and goes his way. Two agencies in one departure: she follows, he takes.

Arminian readers like the pairing. She is not luggage, and he is not a bystander. Grace-enabled following meets faithful stewardship on the road to Isaac.

Reformed

Camels again, now carrying the appointed wife toward the land of promise.

Reformed close of the scene: the servant’s way is still the Lord’s way. Rebekah is in motion because God prospered the journey from the first kneeling at the well.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

On Laban’s house, retold providence, and Rebekah’s consent, a few classic voices from each tradition.

Arminian Notable voices

“We have here the making up of the marriage between Isaac and Rebekah, related largely and particularly. Thus we are directed to take notice of God's providence in the little common occurrences of human life, and in them also to exercise our own prudence, and other graces: for the scripture was not intended only for the use of philosophers and statesmen, but to make us all wise and virtuous in the conduct of ourselves and families.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 24:29)

Reformed Notable voices

“I will not eat until I have told my errand Moses begins to show by what means the parents of Rebekah were induced to give her in marriage to their nephew. That the servant, when food was set before him, should refuse to eat till he had completed his work is a proof of his diligence and fidelity; and it may with propriety be regarded as one of the benefits which God had vouchsafed to Abraham, that he should have a servant so faithful, and so intent upon his duty.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 24:33)
