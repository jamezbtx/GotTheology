Passage

Genesis 18

Verses 16–33 · ESV · Voices

The men look down toward Sodom, and the LORD will not hide from Abraham what He is about to do—Abraham is chosen to command his household in righteousness and justice. Abraham still stands before the LORD and bargains from fifty righteous down to ten, pleading that the Judge of all the earth do what is just.

Genesis 18:16

16Then the men set out from there, and they looked down toward Sodom. And Abraham went with them to set them on their way.

Arminian

Hospitality is not finished at the table. The visitors look down toward Sodom, and Abraham walks them out—still the friend who will not let them go without company.

Classical Arminians hear love of neighbor already in the escort. The doomed cities are in view; grace has made Abraham a man who walks toward the crisis rather than turning back to the tent in private comfort.

Reformed

The men set out; Sodom is now the destination. Abraham’s escort is real courtesy, and it is also the hinge into the LORD’s counsel about judgment.

Reformed framing: providence has turned the meal toward the plain. What Abraham does not yet know, God is about to disclose. The walk is the threshold of intercession.

Genesis 18:17

17The LORD said, “Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do,

Arminian

The LORD speaks as a friend: Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do? Communion means disclosure. Those who live with God are let in on His mind more than others.

Arminian pastoral reading: God sincerely takes Abraham into confidence. The question is not theater. Grace treats the patriarch as a partner who may hear, grieve, and pray—not as a spectator locked out of the Judge’s purpose.

Reformed

“Shall I hide?” is how Scripture lets us overhear God’s resolve to reveal. The secret of the LORD is with those He has befriended (Ps 25:14; Amos 3:7).

Reformed accent: disclosure is gift, not Abraham’s right to audit heaven. God will not hide because He has already bound this man into the story He is writing.

Genesis 18:18

18seeing that Abraham shall surely become a great and mighty nation, and all the nations of the earth shall be blessed in him?

Arminian

Why tell Abraham? Because he will be a great nation, and all nations will be blessed in him. The friend of God must know that blessing and judgment both belong to the LORD of the whole earth.

Classical Arminians hear the nations already in the reason. Sodom is one of those peoples. If blessing is sincerely meant for the families of the earth, Abraham may yet plead that this city not be cut off without remaining righteous ones.

Reformed

The promise of chapter 12 is the reason for this confidence. A mighty nation and worldwide blessing are God’s work; therefore Abraham must understand what God does when a city fills with outcry.

Reformed note: the covenant line is taught by judgment as well as by gift. What God is about to do will catechize Abraham’s seed in the fear of the LORD.

Genesis 18:19

19For I have chosen him, that he may command his children and his household after him to keep the way of the LORD by doing righteousness and justice, so that the LORD may bring to Abraham what he has promised him.”

Arminian

“I have chosen him” (Hebrew: I have known him) is unto a purpose: that he may command children and household in the way of the LORD—righteousness and justice. Election language here is vocational and moral, not a bare destiny chart.

Classical Arminians stress the “that he may.” Grace chooses Abraham for a charge he must actually fulfill. Family catechesis is why God opens His counsel. The promise advances as the household keeps the way; known-and-chosen does not cancel responsible command.

Reformed

ESV’s “chosen” names covenant knowing: God has set His love on Abraham. Household piety is the fruit of that choice, the means “so that the LORD may bring” what He has promised.

Reformed confession: the cause of the blessing is God’s fountain, not Abraham’s merit. He is admitted to the counsel because God has elected him to be a householder of the way—righteousness and justice as the family mark of a people God Himself will bring through.

Key contrast Both honor Abraham’s charge to command his household in righteousness. Arminians typically hear “chosen/known” as God’s knowing a man for a vocation he must fulfill; Reformed readers typically hear electing love, with household piety as the appointed fruit through which God brings His own promise to pass.

Genesis 18:20

20Then the LORD said, “Because the outcry against Sodom and Gomorrah is great and their sin is very grave,

Arminian

The outcry is great; the sin is very grave. Someone is crying—and God hears. Justice is not optional when victims and wickedness fill a city’s name.

Arminian preaching leans into the cry as real. The Judge of all the earth listens to the oppressed. Abraham will soon plead for mercy, but he does not deny the gravity. Love of neighbor includes naming sin as God names it.

Reformed

God states the case before the sentence. Outcry and grave sin are the grounds, not rumor and caprice.

Reformed gravity: the LORD is not indifferent. What reaches His ears will reach His hand. The disclosure to Abraham is already a verdict in motion—holy, public, and just.

Genesis 18:21

21I will go down to see whether they have done altogether according to the outcry that has come to me. And if not, I will know.”

Arminian

“I will go down to see” is God speaking after the manner of men—not as one in doubt, but as one who will not strike on hearsay. Procedure itself preaches justice.

Classical Arminians hear sincere forbearance. The window of investigation is real in the story’s terms: God is slow to wrath, thorough before He destroys. That patience is why an intercessor still has room to stand and speak.

Reformed

The LORD needs no information. He condescends to the language of inquiry so that His equity will be unmistakable when judgment falls.

Reformed reading: “I will know” is not a gap in omniscience; it is judicial display. He never breaks forth in punishment except upon a people ripe in crime—and He will have Abraham see that it is so.

Genesis 18:22

22So the men turned from there and went toward Sodom, but Abraham still stood before the LORD.

Arminian

Two go toward Sodom; Abraham still stands before the LORD. The friend remains. Intercession begins as simply not leaving.

Arminian pastoral image: prayer is staying when others move on. Grace has given Abraham a place in front of God; he uses it for cities that cannot pray for themselves.

Reformed

The messengers of inspection proceed; Abraham is left standing before the LORD. The scene splits into going and remaining—execution and audience.

Reformed note: God has so arranged the moment that an intercessor is still in the room. Standing before the LORD is itself a vocation He has opened, not a human seizure of the bench.

Genesis 18:23

23Then Abraham drew near and said, “Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked?

Arminian

He draws near—Wesley heard holy concern and holy confidence, an assurance of faith. The first plea is justice: will You sweep away righteous with wicked?

Classical Arminians honor this as real intercession, not play-acting. Abraham asks a question God invites. Drawing near is how grace-taught people treat the Judge: close enough to plead, humble enough to ask rather than accuse.

Reformed

Drawing near is reverence, not a lawsuit. Calvin is careful: Abraham does not, in a contentious spirit, oppose God as if he had a right to intercede; he suppliantly entreats.

Reformed comfort: the question is permitted because persuasion of God’s justice is already fixed. He pours the knot into the LORD’s bosom rather than charging Him with wrong.

Genesis 18:24

24Suppose there are fifty righteous within the city. Will you then sweep away the place and not spare it for the fifty righteous who are in it?

Arminian

He starts at fifty: spare the whole place for their sake. The righteous become a reason for mercy on the wicked who share their streets.

Arminian hope: God may truly spare a city for the sake of a remnant. The supposition is offered in faith that such mercy would not stain the Judge. Intercession asks for more than private escape; it asks that neighbors live.

Reformed

Fifty is a large number, and God will not mock the request. The principle is stated: the presence of the righteous may be a means of sparing the place.

Reformed accent: Abraham does not demand that the wicked go unjudged as a class; he asks that public destruction wait on the finding of the just. The LORD can spare a whole for the sake of a few—and remains free as Judge.

Genesis 18:25

25Far be it from you to do such a thing, to put the righteous to death with the wicked, so that the righteous fare as the wicked! Far be that from you! Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?”

Arminian

The famous cry: far be it from You that righteous and wicked fare alike. Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just? Abraham treats God’s justice as a real standard the LORD Himself will honor.

Arminius called justice that virtue by which God administers all things correctly and consistently renders to every one his own. Classical Arminians hear no lecture to a weak deity, but confidence that the Judge never confuses the godly with the guilty—and that this confidence is a fit ground of prayer.

Reformed

Calvin: Abraham does not teach God His duty; he reasons from God’s nature that it is impossible for Him to intend anything unjust. The Judge’s will is the law of justice and rectitude; He cannot swerve.

Reformed prayer stands here: keep persuasion of His justice fixed, then pour the tormenting difficulty into His bosom. The question is worship. God will loosen the knot—whether by sparing, by extracting the righteous, or by proving that the remnant was not there.

Key contrast Both refuse the thought that God would treat righteous and wicked as the same. Arminians typically stress justice as a real standard God honors and a fit ground for pleading; Reformed readers typically stress that God’s own nature and will are the law of right—so the intercessor reasons from who God is, not from a rule above Him.

Genesis 18:26

26And the LORD said, “If I find at Sodom fifty righteous in the city, I will spare the whole place for their sake.”

Arminian

The LORD answers: if fifty, I will spare the whole for their sake. Mercy is not wrung from a reluctant God; He agrees at once.

Arminian pastoral weight: the pledge is sincere. God binds His own speech to a condition Abraham may keep testing. The Judge is willing to let a handful of righteous become the city’s shield.

Reformed

God freely matches the request. “I will spare” is His word, not Abraham’s victory over an unwilling Lord.

Reformed reading: the conversation is itself within providence. The LORD who disclosed the outcry now discloses His willingness to spare for fifty—means of teaching justice and of drawing out the intercession He intended.

Genesis 18:27

27Abraham answered and said, “Behold, I have undertaken to speak to the Lord, I who am but dust and ashes.

Arminian

Dust and ashes before he asks again. Wesley: he is amazed at his own boldness and the liberty God graciously allowed—God’s greatness, his own meanness.

Classical Arminians keep both notes: draw near, and know the distance. Accepted prayer is humble. The man who bargains still confesses he has no claim but mercy’s leave to speak.

Reformed

Westminster cites this verse among the marks of accepted prayer: understanding, reverence, humility. The nearer Abraham comes, the smaller he knows himself to be.

Reformed instinct: dust and ashes is not self-hatred; it is true theology. Familiarity with God never becomes casual. Intercession that forgets creatureliness stops being prayer and becomes presumption.

Genesis 18:28

28Suppose five of the fifty righteous are lacking. Will you destroy the whole city for lack of five?” And he said, “I will not destroy it if I find forty-five there.”

Arminian

Five fewer: will You destroy the whole for lack of five? The LORD will not. Each step is answered without rebuke.

Arminian reading: God is not brittle. Grace allows the next question. The intercessor learns that the Judge’s mercy is not exhausted by the first concession.

Reformed

Forty-five is granted as fifty was. The LORD remains the finder—“if I find.” Abraham proposes; God decides.

Reformed note: persistence is welcome, and the condition stays God’s. The city will live only if the righteous are actually there, not if the prayer is merely eloquent.

Genesis 18:29

29Again he spoke to him and said, “Suppose forty are found there.” He answered, “For the sake of forty I will not do it.”

Arminian

Again he spoke—forty. For the sake of forty, I will not do it. The refrain is mercy’s patience with a pleading man.

Classical Arminians hear a God who can be asked again. Intercession is not a single shot; it is returning to the same throne with a smaller number and the same hope.

Reformed

“For the sake of forty” keeps the logic of representation: a remnant may hold back the blow.

Reformed accent: God is not bargaining as an equal. He is teaching Abraham—and us—how far He is willing to let the righteous count, while the finding remains His.

Genesis 18:30

30Then he said, “Oh let not the Lord be angry, and I will speak. Suppose thirty are found there.” He answered, “I will not do it, if I find thirty there.”

Arminian

Fear of anger, then thirty. Wesley: if we were dealing with a man, we might well fear his patience; but He with whom we have to do is God and not man, and He is pleased when He is wrestled with.

Arminian piety takes that seriously. Importunity is not rudeness when it is dust-and-ashes faith. The LORD keeps saying He will not do it if the righteous are found.

Reformed

“Let not the Lord be angry” is the right instinct even in bold prayer. Fervency does not forget whom we address.

Reformed balance: God commands perseverance, and He is not worn down like a pagan deity. He answers because it pleases Him to hear; the “if I find” still fences the request with His search and His will.

Genesis 18:31

31He said, “Behold, I have undertaken to speak to the Lord. Suppose twenty are found there.” He answered, “For the sake of twenty I will not destroy it.”

Arminian

Twenty now. He names the undertaking again: I have taken it upon myself to speak. Courage and unworthiness travel together.

Arminian pastoral note: as the number drops, the plea grows more desperate and more neighborly. God does not shame the shrinking request. He meets it: for twenty I will not destroy it.

Reformed

For twenty He will not destroy it. The same word of spare-for-the-sake-of holds at a number no city should fail to meet if it were not deep in ruin.

Reformed gravity: each concession also tightens the noose of the evidence. If Sodom cannot produce twenty, the outcry will have been proven true.

Genesis 18:32

32Then he said, “Oh let not the Lord be angry, and I will speak again but this once. Suppose ten are found there.” He answered, “For the sake of ten I will not destroy it.”

Arminian

Once more—ten. He stops. Wesley asked why: either Abraham owned that a place so empty of the righteous deserved to perish, or God restrained his spirit from asking farther. Either way, the LORD’s last word here is still mercy: for ten I will not destroy it.

Classical Arminians linger over the persistence. Prayer is an efficacious means God instituted for asking and obtaining. The Judge kept saying yes down the ladder. That the city will fall is not because God would not spare for ten; it is because ten will not be found. Lot’s house will show how thin the remnant was.

Reformed

“This once” and ten: Abraham’s fervency meets a boundary. Westminster’s marks are all here—humility, faith, love, perseverance—and prayer according to God’s will. When God has determined a ruin, He may close the asking even as He has honored every request made.

Reformed consolation: the conversation is not Abraham extracting an unwilling pardon. It is God demonstrating that He would have spared for ten, then going His way. Intercession is real; the outcome remains the Judge’s. Chapter 19 will rescue the righteous without acquitting the city.

Key contrast Both prize persistent, humble prayer and God’s willingness to spare for a handful of righteous. Arminians typically stress that importunity truly prevails with a God pleased to be wrestled with, and that the city falls only because the condition is unmet; Reformed readers typically stress prayer according to God’s will—fervent and effectual as a means, yet bounded by the counsel in which He both invites the plea and appoints the end.

Genesis 18:33

33And the LORD went his way, when he had finished speaking to Abraham, and Abraham returned to his place.

Arminian

The LORD goes when He has finished speaking; Abraham returns to his place to wait. Wesley: his prayer was heard, and yet Sodom not spared, because there were not ten righteous in it.

Arminian close: love has done what love can do before the throne. The friend goes home without a cynical heart. He has met the Judge who does what is just, and he leaves the event with Him.

Reformed

God finishes. The audience ends at His word, not when Abraham has no more numbers. Abraham returns—not as one dismissed in shame, but as a servant whose intercession has been received.

Reformed rest: the LORD went His way. Judgment will proceed in righteousness; the righteous will not be forgotten. Prayer has had its hour; providence now keeps the next.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Abraham’s intercession, the justice of God, and prayer that a city be spared for the sake of the righteous—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 18:16–33.

Arminian Notable voices

“Abraham drew near—This expression intimates, A holy concern. A holy confidence; he drew near with an assurance of faith, drew near as a prince… The importunity which believers use in their addresses to God is such, that if they were dealing with a man like themselves, they could not but fear that he would be angry with them. But he with whom we have to do is God and not man, and he is pleased when he is wrestled with.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 18:23, 30)
“The justice of God, considered universally, is a virtue of God, according to which he administers all things correctly and in a suitable manner, according to that which his wisdom dictates as befitting himself. In conjunction with wisdom, it presides over all his acts, decrees and deeds; and according to it, God is said to be “just and right,” his way “equal,” and himself to be “just in all his ways.” The particular justice of God is that by which he consistently renders to every one his own.”
Jacobus Arminius · Public Disputation XXI, “On Those Attributes of God Which Have Some Analogy to the Moral Virtues”

Reformed Notable voices

“He does not here teach God His duty, as if any one should say to a judge, ‘See what thy office requires’; but he reasons from the nature of God, that it is impossible for Him to intend anything unjust. For although he wonders how God should think of destroying Sodom, in which he was persuaded there was a number of good men; he yet retains this principle, that it was impossible for God, who is the Judge of the world, and by nature loves equity, yea, whose will is the law of justice and rectitude, should in the least degree swerve from righteousness… only let our persuasion of His justice remain fixed, and we shall be permitted to pour into His bosom the difficulties which torment us, in order that He may loosen the knots which we cannot untie.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 18:25)
“Prayer, with thanksgiving, being one special part of religious worship, is by God required of all men: and that it may be accepted, it is to be made in the name of the Son, by the help of His Spirit, according to His will, with understanding, reverence, humility, fervency, faith, love, and perseverance; and, if vocal, in a known tongue.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · 21.3 (1647)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
