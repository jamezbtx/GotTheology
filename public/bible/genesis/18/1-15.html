Passage

Genesis 18

Verses 1–15 · WEB · Voices

The LORD appears to Abraham by the oaks of Mamre in the heat of the day; three men receive lavish hospitality while Sarah remains in the tent. The promise of a son within the year is restated; Sarah laughs at her worn-out body, and the LORD asks whether anything is too hard for Him—then names the laugh she denies.

Genesis 18:1

1The LORD appeared to him by the oaks of Mamre, as he sat in the tent door in the heat of the day.

Arminian

The narrator tells us what the tent does not yet announce: the LORD appeared—by ordinary oaks, in ordinary heat, at an ordinary door. Theophany comes as a visit, not a storm.

Classical Arminians receive this as self-disclosure. God draws near to be welcomed. Appearance is grace that invites recognition and hospitality, not a mere backdrop for a decree already finished offstage.

Reformed

The LORD appeared—initiative is His. Mamre, tent door, heat of day: providence locates the meeting in the patriarch’s ordinary noon, not a shrine of Abraham’s choosing.

The God of the covenant comes to Abraham. Theophany is gift before a meal is offered. What follows is the Lord’s visit, whatever Abraham first perceives of the three who stand there.

Genesis 18:2

2He lifted up his eyes and looked, and saw that three men stood near him. When he saw them, he ran to meet them from the tent door, and bowed himself to the earth,

Arminian

Three men are already standing; Abraham runs and bows. Haste honors the stranger. Hebrews will later say some have entertained angels unawares.

Eagerness to welcome is a praiseworthy response. Grace has formed a host who treats the unknown guest as sacred rather than as interruption.

Reformed

“Behold”: three men, already there. Abraham’s running and bowing sit under a visit God has begun.

The Lord comes in human appearance. The scene is not Abraham hunting God; it is God standing in front of him, and the patriarch’s reverence is the right first motion.

Genesis 18:3

3and said, “My lord, if now I have found favor in your sight, please don’t go away from your servant.

Arminian

“My lord, if now I have found favor… do not pass by your servant.” Whether he yet knows the fullness of the visit, he asks the Guest to stay.

Arminian interpreters hear petition that assumes God can be asked. Favor is sought; the house is offered. Address is personal—a servant speaking to One he hopes will not pass by.

Reformed

My lord, if I have found favor, don’t pass by your servant. Servant-language frames the meal as audience with a superior.

Even if Abraham’s “Lord” begins as courteous address, the narrative has already named the Visitor. The request not to pass by is answered because the Lord intended to stay.

Genesis 18:4

4Now let a little water be fetched, wash your feet, and rest yourselves under the tree.

Arminian

Water, washed feet, rest under the tree—ordinary mercy made holy by the Guest. Hospitality starts with the body’s need.

Love of neighbor is concrete. The God who will announce a son first receives a basin and shade. Kindness is not beneath theology.

Reformed

The tree becomes a resting place by Abraham’s offer. Feet and water belong to the Lord’s condescension as truly as the later oracle.

God allows Himself to be served in the form of men. Providence includes this pause under the oak.

Genesis 18:5

5I will get a piece of bread so you can refresh your heart. After that you may go your way, now that you have come to your servant.” They said, “Very well, do as you have said.”

Arminian

A morsel of bread to refresh them, then they may pass on—since they have come to their servant. The guests consent: “Do as you have said.”

Arminian readers notice dialogue. The visitors accept; Abraham’s offer is not ignored. Relationship here is give-and-receive, not puppetry.

Reformed

“You have come to your servant” is truer than Abraham knows. They said, “Do as you have said”—permission that lets the feast proceed under the Lord’s will.

Even the consent to eat is the Visitor’s. Hospitality unfolds because God grants it.

Genesis 18:6

6Abraham hurried into the tent to Sarah, and said, “Quickly prepare three seahs of fine meal, knead it, and make cakes.”

Arminian

Quick! Three seahs of fine meal—lavish, not a crumb. Sarah is enlisted at once. The household works fast for guests.

Sarah is not a prop. She kneads the cakes that will sit before the Lord. Shared labor is part of receiving the visit.

Reformed

Abraham goes quickly; the measure is generous. The tent becomes a kitchen of promise before any child is named this afternoon.

Haste in the tent is the human side of a meeting God appointed. Sarah’s work is caught up into the Lord’s visit.

Genesis 18:7

7Abraham ran to the herd, and fetched a tender and good calf, and gave it to the servant. He hurried to dress it.

Arminian

He runs to the herd, chooses a tender and good calf, and a young man prepares it quickly. Abraham serves his guests his best, not what is left over.

Costly welcome is a free, responsible act. Grace has made Abraham generous; the calf is gift, not tax.

Reformed

Tender and good: the narrator lingers on quality. A young man is drawn into the same hurry.

The feast is prepared under providence. What Abraham selects, God will receive as Guest—condescension that will then speak a son.

Genesis 18:8

8He took butter, milk, and the calf which he had dressed, and set it before them. He stood by them under the tree, and they ate.

Arminian

Curds, milk, and calf set before them; Abraham stands by under the tree, and they eat. The host serves; he does not recline as equal.

Arminian interpreters see table fellowship seriously. God is not too spiritual to eat. Presence is hospitable before it is oracular.

Reformed

They ate. The Lord of heaven takes a meal in Mamre’s shade while Abraham attends.

Covenant nearness includes this eating. The God who will open Sarah’s womb first sits at Abraham’s table.

Genesis 18:9

9They asked him, “Where is Sarah, your wife?” He said, “There, in the tent.”

Arminian

“Where is Sarah your wife?” They know her name. Abraham answers: she is in the tent. The question draws the hidden wife into the promise.

God inquires so Sarah may be included. The conversation will not proceed as a men’s aside; the mother of the child must be in earshot.

Reformed

They know her name and her place. “Where is Sarah?” is not ignorance; it is the Lord’s way of bringing her into the word that follows.

Omniscience still asks. The tent’s occupant is addressed through the husband so the promise will land on both.

Genesis 18:10

10He said, “I will certainly return to you at about this time next year; and behold, Sarah your wife will have a son.” Sarah heard in the tent door, which was behind him.

Arminian

The LORD speaks a dated promise: about this time next year, Sarah will have a son. She listens at the tent door—grace puts the word within her hearing.

Wesleyan Arminians find a sincere pledge. The date is specific so faith has something to hold. She is not excluded; she is invited to believe what she overhears.

Reformed

“I will certainly return” is the Lord’s oath-like certainty. A son is announced as fact, not as a maybe if Sarah feels able.

The promise does not wait on the listener’s inner yes. Sarah overhears a word God will perform; Isaac’s year is already on the Lord’s calendar.

Key contrast Both hear a serious promise of a son within the year. Arminians underline a sincere word Sarah is invited to trust as she listens; Reformed commentators press the LORD’s sure “I will” that will bring the child whether or not the hidden hearer yet believes.

Genesis 18:11

11Now Abraham and Sarah were old, well advanced in age. Sarah had passed the age of childbearing.

Arminian

The narrator explains the laugh before it comes: both are old; the way of women has ceased. Nature has closed the door.

The obstacle is not mood but the body. Faith will have to receive a gift beyond fertility’s ordinary course—without pretending the closed door is imaginary.

Reformed

Sarah had passed the age of women ceased. The narrator stacks impossibilities so the next verse’s laugh has a body.

Providence has let the promise wait until nature is finished. The son will be from deadness, as Paul will say, so that power is clearly God’s.

Genesis 18:12

12Sarah laughed within herself, saying, “After I have grown old will I have pleasure, my lord being old also?”

Arminian

She laughs to herself: worn out, my lord old will I have pleasure, my lord being old also? The laugh is private unbelief, not public mockery in the courtyard.

Classical Arminians refuse to baptize the laugh. Doubt here is a responsible failure to receive the word. Grace still speaks; she is not yet believing what she heard.

Reformed

Sarah laughs inwardly and passes an inward verdict on the promise. She measures the promise by her flesh and Abraham’s age.

God will answer a laugh He heard without being shown. Unbelief is not in doubt, and it cannot rewrite the appointed year. The promise is stronger than her private verdict.

Key contrast Both name the laugh as unbelief, not holy amusement. Arminians stress Sarah’s resistible, responsible doubt under a still-sincere promise; Reformed Christians underline that this hidden unbelief cannot void the LORD’s purpose to give the son.

Genesis 18:13

13The LORD said to Abraham, “Why did Sarah laugh, saying, ‘Will I really bear a child when I am old?’

Arminian

The LORD asks Abraham why Sarah laughed, and quotes her thought: shall I bear a child when I am old? The hidden is open to Him.

God exposes so He may correct. The question is not cruel theater; it is a call to drop the laugh and take the promise. Even a thought in the tent is addressable.

Reformed

God speaks to Abraham about Sarah’s unspoken laugh. Omniscience narrates her heart more accurately than her mouth will.

The Lord hears what is said “within herself.” Nothing in the tent is offstage. The same God who promised the son now names the doubt.

Genesis 18:14

14Is anything too hard for the LORD? At the set time I will return to you, when the season comes around, and Sarah will have a son.”

Arminian

“Is anything too hard for the LORD?” The question rebukes the laugh by preaching God’s power. The appointed time and the son are restated so she may yet believe.

The passage carries a sincere invitation to trust omnipotence. Nothing is too hard—therefore Sarah may receive the word. The dated return is held out to be believed, not merely announced over her head.

Reformed

The rhetorical question is itself the answer: nothing is too hard. Appointed time, return, son—the same promise, now underlined by power.

“Too hard” cannot apply to the God who creates from nothing and opens a dead womb. The appointed time will arrive because He has appointed it; Isaac is not suspended on Sarah’s mood.

Key contrast Both preach that nothing is too hard for the LORD. Arminians typically stress the question as a genuine call to trust a power sincerely offered to Sarah’s faith; Reformed commentators typically stress that the same omnipotence will effect the son at the appointed time, not merely make him possible.

Genesis 18:15

15Then Sarah denied it, saying, “I didn’t laugh,” for she was afraid. He said, “No, but you did laugh.”

Arminian

Fear makes her lie: “I didn’t laugh.” The LORD answers with gentle firmness: “No, but you did laugh.” Truth is named without the Guest storming out.

Denial does not erase the word. She is caught, not crushed. Grace tells the truth so that later joy (21:6) can replace this laugh. The call is to drop the falsehood and trust the Promiser.

Reformed

Afraid, she denies what God already quoted. He will not let the record stand false: you did laugh.

The Lord is neither mocked nor brittle. He names the laugh and still keeps the promise of verse 14. Kindness and omniscience hold together; Isaac will still come.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Voices from both camps

On hospitality at Mamre and the promise that Sarah will have a son, a few classic voices from each tradition.

Arminian Notable voices

“This appearance of God to Abraham seems to have had in it more of freedom and familiarity, and less of grandeur and majesty, than those we have hitherto read of, and therefore more resembles that great visit which in the fulness of time the Son of God was to make to the world. He sat in the tent-door in the heat of the day—Not so much to repose himself, as to seek an opportunity of doing good, by giving entertainment to strangers.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 18:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“Before Moses prceeds to his principal subject, he describes to us, the hospitality of the holy man; and he calls the angels men, because, being clothed with human bodies, they appeared to be nothing else than men. For angels do not need those services of ours, which are the true evidences of charity. Moreover, hospitality holds the chief place among these services; because it is no common virtue to assist strangers, from whom there is no hope of reward.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 18:2)
