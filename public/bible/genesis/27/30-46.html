Passage

Genesis 27

Verses 30–46 · WEB · Voices

Esau returns, weeps, and hears Isaac confirm Jacob’s blessing while granting him a lesser word. Hatred and a murder plot follow; Rebekah sends Jacob to Laban and pleads Isaac with the distress of Esau’s Hittite wives.

Genesis 27:30

30As soon as Isaac had finished blessing Jacob, and Jacob had just gone out from the presence of Isaac his father, Esau his brother came in from his hunting.

Arminian

Timing is razor-thin: Jacob scarcely gone, Esau coming in. Jacob and Esau pass through the same tent within moments of each other.

Arminians still treat Esau’s return as his own hunt completed—he did what his father asked. The collision is tragic and human, not a puppet show.

Reformed

“As soon as” and scarcely preach providence without naming God.

Reformed readers hear a door held until the blessing was spoken. The Lord can time a hunt’s end without authoring the deceit that filled the interval.

Genesis 27:31

31He also made savory food, and brought it to his father. He said to his father, “Let my father arise, and eat of his son’s venison, that your soul may bless me.”

Arminian

Esau also prepares delicious food and asks to be blessed—mirroring Jacob, but in earnest.

He is a responsible son doing the errand. His later rage will be sin; this request is not yet murder.

Reformed

The older son brings what Isaac actually wanted.

Sincerity in the kitchen cannot rewrite a blessing already given. Providence has let the firstborn arrive second.

Genesis 27:32

32Isaac his father said to him, “Who are you?” He said, “I am your son, your firstborn, Esau.”

Arminian

Isaac asks “Who are you?” a second time. “I am your son, your firstborn, Esau.” This time the name is true.

Wesleyan Arminians hear the pain of a man telling the truth after a brother has used the same sentence as a lie.

Reformed

Isaac must ask identity a third time. The true firstborn’s voice exposes the fraud.

God’s choice of the younger does not turn Esau into a non-son; it does mean this claim will not retrieve the blessing.

Genesis 27:33

33Isaac trembled violently, and said, “Who, then, is he who has taken venison, and brought it to me, and I have eaten of all before you came, and have blessed him? Yes, he will be blessed.”

Arminian

Isaac trembles “violently,” then says, “Yes, he will be blessed.” Horror and confirmation sit together.

He recognizes both the deceit and the weight of his own word. He is responsible for standing by a blessing he now knows was taken.

Reformed

Violent trembling, then “he will be blessed.”

Isaac yields to a purpose bigger than his preference. Hebrews 11:20 hears faith here—not faith in the trick, but faith that the spoken blessing will stand as God’s.

Genesis 27:34

34When Esau heard the words of his father, he cried with an exceedingly great and bitter cry, and said to his father, “Bless me, even me also, my father.”

Arminian

An exceedingly great and bitter cry: “Bless me, even me also.” The grief is not softened.

Classical Arminians do not sneer at tears. Hebrews 12:17 will warn that tears after the fact are not the same as repentance; this cry is still a human soul in pain.

Reformed

Esau’s cry is recorded at full volume. Pity is permitted.

Reformed readers can grieve without rewriting the purpose of God. The blessing was never a wage Esau was owed by hunting; the bitterness is not in doubt, and it does not unmake the oracle.

Genesis 27:35

35He said, “Your brother came with deceit, and has taken away your blessing.”

Arminian

Isaac names it: “Your brother came with deceit.” The narrator agrees.

The Bible does not baptize the method. Calling it deceit keeps election from becoming a cover story.

Reformed

Deceit is the father’s verdict.

Providence overrules, and the text still says “with deceit.” God is not credited as the liar; Jacob is.

Genesis 27:36

36He said, “Isn’t he rightly named Jacob? For he has supplanted me these two times. He took away my birthright. See, now he has taken away my blessing.” He said, “Haven’t you reserved a blessing for me?”

Arminian

Esau etches the name: Jacob, heel-grabber, cheat—twice. Birthright and blessing both feel stolen.

Genesis 25:33 still stands: Esau despised his birthright by his own oath. Both brothers have sinned; blaming only Jacob is too neat, and excusing Jacob is too cheap.

Reformed

The pun on “Jacob” is bitter and partly true.

Esau’s accounting skips his own sale of the birthright. God’s purpose stands through two sinners, not one villain and one victim.

Genesis 27:37

37Isaac answered Esau, “Behold, I have made him your lord, and all his brothers I have given to him for servants. I have sustained him with grain and new wine. What then will I do for you, my son?”

Arminian

Isaac admits the structure: lord, servants, grain and wine already given. There is little left to redistribute.

Words spoken have moral weight. Isaac will not pretend he can rewind; he remains responsible for what he has bound.

Reformed

The blessing’s content is treated as done.

This is more than a father’s mood. Once given in the Lord’s name, the portion holds because God holds it—not because Jacob’s fraud created a magic Isaac cannot break.

Genesis 27:38

38Esau said to his father, “Do you have just one blessing, my father? Bless me, even me also, my father.” Esau lifted up his voice, and wept.

Arminian

“Have just one blessing?” He weeps aloud. The question is pastoral and raw.

The note struck is a man begging for leftover mercy. Weeping is agency too—grief that can turn to God or to a grudge.

Reformed

One blessing of this kind, yes. Hebrews 12:17 sits beside the tears: he sought it with weeping and did not reverse it.

Reformed readers hold pity and purpose together. God’s choice stands; Esau’s tears are not mocked, and they are not a second decree.

Genesis 27:39

39Isaac his father answered him, “Behold, your dwelling will be of the fatness of the earth, and of the dew of the sky from above.

Arminian

Isaac answers with a lesser word: dwelling away from fatness and dew. This is not verse 28.

Esau is not cursed into nothing; he is blessed secondarily. God still deals with him as a historical man, not a discarded prop.

Reformed

The poem rhymes with Jacob’s and then diverges.

Secondary blessing is still providence. Esau will have a dwelling and a future; he will not have Jacob’s portion. Election of the younger does not require Esau’s annihilation.

Genesis 27:40

40You will live by your sword, and you will serve your brother. It will happen, when you will break loose, that you will shake his yoke from off your neck.”

Arminian

Sword, service, then a restless breaking of the yoke. History will bear this out.

This is a future Esau’s line will inhabit by their own wars and choices. The word is not fatalism that erases later responsibility.

Reformed

“You will serve” recalls 25:23; “shake his yoke” leaves room in history.

God maps Edom’s path without making every later revolt a holy act. Providence overrules; sword-life is described, not praised.

Genesis 27:41

41Esau hated Jacob because of the blessing with which his father blessed him. Esau said in his heart, “The days of mourning for my father are at hand. Then I will kill my brother Jacob.”

Arminian

Esau hated Jacob and planned murder after Isaac’s funeral. Rage is a responsible sin.

Being wronged does not license blood. The birthright he once sold now becomes a grievance he will kill for.

Reformed

Hatred settles in “because of the blessing.” The elect son is now in danger from his brother.

God had chosen Jacob; He did not author Esau’s hate. Providence will have to preserve the line from a murderer-in-waiting.

Genesis 27:42

42The words of Esau, her elder son, were told to Rebekah. She sent and called Jacob, her younger son, and said to him, “Behold, your brother Esau comforts himself about you by planning to kill you.

Arminian

The plot is told to Rebekah; she calls Jacob. Information becomes another maternal intervention.

Classical Arminians see her again as agent—protecting a son she helped endanger. Hearing a murder plan obliges action; she remains responsible for how she acts.

Reformed

Words leak; the mother learns.

God can expose a secret hate to spare Jacob. Means of preservation (a report, a warning) are mercy; they do not erase her earlier deceit.

Genesis 27:43

43Now therefore, my son, obey my voice. Arise, flee to Laban, my brother, in Haran.

Arminian

“Obey my voice. Arise, flee to Laban.” Same refrain as verse 8, now for flight.

Fleeing murder is wisdom; treating Laban as a simple fix is naïve. Jacob remains responsible to go, and later to become more than his mother’s errand.

Reformed

Haran is both refuge and furnace.

The Lord will meet Jacob at Bethel on this road (ch. 28). Flight is Rebekah’s idea; providence will turn the exile into the next chapter of promise.

Genesis 27:44

44Stay with him a few days, until your brother’s fury turns away—

Arminian

“Stay with him a few days, until your brother’s fury turns away.” She expects a short storm.

Arminians hear misjudged timing as a human error. Hope that fury simply cools can be sincere and still too small.

Reformed

“A few days” will become twenty years.

Reformed readers hear a mother’s underestimate under a God’s long calendar. The delay will serve Jacob’s formation, not Rebekah’s schedule.

Genesis 27:45

45until your brother’s anger turns away from you, and he forgets what you have done to him. Then I will send, and get you from there. Why should I be bereaved of you both in one day?”

Arminian

She wants anger forgotten and both sons alive: “Why should I be bereaved of you both in one day?” Maternal fear of double loss is intelligible.

She still scripts the reunion. Love that plots can also plead; neither cancels responsibility for the original fraud.

Reformed

Bereavement of both—Jacob slain, Esau the killer—would empty her house.

God will keep both alive for now, on terms she does not control. Her question is true; His purpose for the line is deeper than her fear.

Genesis 27:46

46Rebekah said to Isaac, “I am weary of my life because of the daughters of Heth. If Jacob takes a wife of the daughters of Heth, such as these, of the daughters of the land, what good will my life do me?”

Arminian

She loathes life because of Esau’s Hittite wives and uses that distress to steer Isaac toward sending Jacob. The feeling may be honest (26:34–35); the use is tactical.

Grief at Canaanite marriages, and a responsible choice to argue by that grief rather than confess the flight’s true cause.

Reformed

Hittite women become the public reason.The narrator has already said that they were a grief of mind to Isaac and Rebekah. Providence can fold a true marital grief into Jacob’s departure without making Rebekah’s partial story a model of candor.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Voices from both camps

How earlier teachers on both sides handled Esau’s cry, hatred, and Jacob sent away, quoted directly.

Arminian Notable voices

“Isaac trembled exceedingly—Those that follow the choice of their own affections rather than the dictates of the Divine will, involve themselves in such perplexities as these. But he soon recovers himself, and ratifies the blessing he had given to Jacob, I have blessed him, and he shall be blessed—He might have recalled it, but now at last he is sensible he was in an error when he designed it for Esau.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 27:33)

Reformed Notable voices

“Besides, it is no common fear which Moses describes, but that which utterly confounds the holy man: for, whereas he was perfectly conscious of his own vocation, and therefore was persuaded that the duty of naming the heir with whom he should deposit the covenant of eternal life was divinely enjoined upon him, he no sooner discovered his error than he was filled with fear, that in an affair so great and so serious God had suffered him to err; for unless he had thought that God was the director of this act, what should have hindered him from alleging his ignorance as an excuse, and from becoming enraged against Jacob, who had stolen in upon him by fraud and by unjustifiable arts?”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 27:33)
