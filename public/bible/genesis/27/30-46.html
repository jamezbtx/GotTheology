Passage

Genesis 27

Verses 30–46 · ESV · Voices

Esau returns, weeps, and hears Isaac confirm Jacob’s blessing while granting him a lesser word. Hatred and a murder plot follow; Rebekah sends Jacob to Laban and pleads Isaac with the distress of Esau’s Hittite wives.

Genesis 27:30

30As soon as Isaac had finished blessing Jacob, when Jacob had scarcely gone out from the presence of Isaac his father, Esau his brother came in from his hunting.

Arminian

Timing is razor-thin: Jacob scarcely gone, Esau coming in. Two sons, one tent.

Classical Arminians still treat Esau’s return as his own hunt completed—he did what his father asked. The collision is tragic and human, not a puppet show.

Reformed

“As soon as” and “scarcely” preach providence without naming God.

Reformed readers hear a door held until the blessing was spoken. The Lord can time a hunt’s end without authoring the deceit that filled the interval.

Genesis 27:31

31He also prepared delicious food and brought it to his father. And he said to his father, “Let my father arise and eat of his son’s game, that you may bless me.”

Arminian

Esau also prepares delicious food and asks to be blessed—mirroring Jacob, but in earnest.

Arminian pastoral: he is a responsible son doing the errand. His later rage will be sin; this request is not yet murder.

Reformed

The older son brings what Isaac actually wanted.

Reformed note: sincerity in the kitchen cannot rewrite a blessing already given. Providence has let the firstborn arrive second.

Genesis 27:32

32His father Isaac said to him, “Who are you?” He answered, “I am your son, your firstborn, Esau.”

Arminian

“Who are you?” again. “I am your son, your firstborn, Esau.” This time the name is true.

Classical Arminians hear the pain of a man telling the truth after a brother has used the same sentence as a lie.

Reformed

Isaac must ask identity a third time. The true firstborn’s voice exposes the fraud.

Reformed accent: God’s choice of the younger does not turn Esau into a non-son; it does mean this claim will not retrieve the blessing.

Genesis 27:33

33Then Isaac trembled very violently and said, “Who was it then that hunted game and brought it to me, and I ate it all before you came, and I have blessed him? Yes, and he shall be blessed.”

Arminian

Isaac trembles “very violently,” then says, “Yes, and he shall be blessed.” Horror and confirmation sit together.

Arminian reading: he recognizes both the deceit and the weight of his own word. He is responsible for standing by a blessing he now knows was taken.

Reformed

Violent trembling, then “he shall be blessed.”

Reformed comfort: Isaac yields to a purpose bigger than his preference. Hebrews 11:20 hears faith here—not faith in the trick, but faith that the spoken blessing will stand as God’s.

Genesis 27:34

34As soon as Esau heard the words of his father, he cried out with an exceedingly great and bitter cry and said to his father, “Bless me, even me also, O my father!”

Arminian

An exceedingly great and bitter cry: “Bless me, even me also.” The grief is real.

Classical Arminians do not sneer at tears. Hebrews 12:17 will warn that tears after the fact are not the same as repentance; this cry is still a human soul in pain.

Reformed

Esau’s cry is recorded at full volume. Pity is permitted.

Reformed readers can grieve without rewriting the purpose of God. The blessing was never a wage Esau was owed by hunting; the bitterness is real, and it does not unmake the oracle.

Genesis 27:35

35But he said, “Your brother came deceitfully, and he has taken away your blessing.”

Arminian

Isaac names it: “Your brother came deceitfully.” The narrator agrees.

Arminian pastoral: the Bible does not baptize the method. Calling it deceit keeps election from becoming a cover story.

Reformed

Deceit is the father’s verdict.

Reformed honesty: providence overrules, and the text still says “deceitfully.” God is not credited as the liar; Jacob is.

Genesis 27:36

36Esau said, “Is he not rightly named Jacob? For he has cheated me these two times. He took away my birthright, and behold, now he has taken away my blessing.” Then he said, “Have you not reserved a blessing for me?”

Arminian

Esau etches the name: Jacob, heel-grabber, cheat—twice. Birthright and blessing both feel stolen.

Classical Arminians remember 25:33: Esau despised his birthright by his own oath. Both brothers have sinned; blaming only Jacob is too neat, and excusing Jacob is too cheap.

Reformed

The pun on “Jacob” is bitter and partly true.

Reformed note: Esau’s accounting skips his own sale of the birthright. God’s purpose stands through two sinners, not one villain and one victim.

Genesis 27:37

37Isaac answered and said to Esau, “Behold, I have made him lord over you, and all his brothers I have given to him for servants, and with grain and wine I have sustained him. What then can I do for you, my son?”

Arminian

Isaac admits the structure: lord, servants, grain and wine already given. There is little left to redistribute.

Arminian reading: words spoken have moral weight. Isaac will not pretend he can rewind; he remains responsible for what he has bound.

Reformed

The blessing’s content is treated as done.

Reformed accent: this is more than a father’s mood. Once given in the Lord’s name, the portion holds because God holds it—not because Jacob’s fraud created a magic Isaac cannot break.

Genesis 27:38

38Esau said to his father, “Have you but one blessing, my father? Bless me, even me also, O my father.” And Esau lifted up his voice and wept.

Arminian

“Have you but one blessing?” He weeps aloud. The question is pastoral and raw.

Classical Arminians hear a man begging for leftover mercy. Weeping is real agency too—grief that can turn to God or to a grudge.

Reformed

One blessing of this kind, yes. Hebrews 12:17 sits beside the tears: he sought it with weeping and did not reverse it.

Reformed readers hold pity and purpose together. God’s choice stands; Esau’s tears are not mocked, and they are not a second decree.

Genesis 27:39

39Then Isaac his father answered and said to him: “Behold, away from the fatness of the earth shall your dwelling be, and away from the dew of heaven on high.

Arminian

Isaac answers with a lesser word: dwelling away from fatness and dew. This is not verse 28.

Arminian note: Esau is not cursed into nothing; he is blessed secondarily. God still deals with him as a real man, not a discarded prop.

Reformed

The poem rhymes with Jacob’s and then diverges.

Reformed accent: secondary blessing is still providence. Esau will have a dwelling and a future; he will not have Jacob’s portion. Election of the younger does not require Esau’s annihilation.

Genesis 27:40

40By your sword you shall live, and you shall serve your brother; but when you grow restless you shall break his yoke from your neck.”

Arminian

Sword, service, then a restless breaking of the yoke. History will bear this out.

Classical Arminians hear a real future Esau’s line will inhabit by their own wars and choices. The word is not fatalism that erases later responsibility.

Reformed

“You shall serve” recalls 25:23; “break his yoke” leaves room in history.

Reformed reading: God maps Edom’s path without making every later revolt a holy act. Providence overrules; sword-life is described, not praised.

Genesis 27:41

41Now Esau hated Jacob because of the blessing with which his father had blessed him, and Esau said to himself, “The days of mourning for my father are approaching; then I will kill my brother Jacob.”

Arminian

Esau hated Jacob and planned murder after Isaac’s funeral. Rage is a responsible sin.

Arminian warning: being wronged does not license blood. The birthright he once sold now becomes a grievance he will kill for.

Reformed

Hatred settles in “because of the blessing.” The elect son is now in danger from his brother.

Reformed sobriety: God had chosen Jacob; He did not author Esau’s hate. Providence will have to preserve the line from a real murderer-in-waiting.

Genesis 27:42

42But the words of Esau her older son were told to Rebekah. So she sent and called Jacob her younger son and said to him, “Behold, your brother Esau comforts himself about you by planning to kill you.

Arminian

The plot is told to Rebekah; she calls Jacob. Information becomes another maternal intervention.

Classical Arminians see her again as agent—protecting a son she helped endanger. Hearing a murder plan obliges action; she remains responsible for how she acts.

Reformed

Words leak; the mother learns.

Reformed note: God can expose a secret hate to spare Jacob. Means of preservation (a report, a warning) are mercy; they do not erase her earlier deceit.

Genesis 27:43

43Now therefore, my son, obey my voice. Arise, flee to Laban my brother in Haran

Arminian

“Obey my voice. Arise, flee to Laban.” Same refrain as verse 8, now for flight.

Arminian pastoral: fleeing murder is wisdom; treating Laban as a simple fix is naïve. Jacob remains responsible to go, and later to become more than his mother’s errand.

Reformed

Haran is both refuge and furnace.

Reformed accent: the Lord will meet Jacob at Bethel on this road (ch. 28). Flight is Rebekah’s idea; providence will turn the exile into the next chapter of promise.

Genesis 27:44

44and stay with him a while, until your brother’s fury turns away—

Arminian

“Stay with him a while, until your brother’s fury turns away.” She expects a short storm.

Classical Arminians hear misjudged timing as a human error. Hope that fury simply cools can be sincere and still too small.

Reformed

“A while” will become twenty years.

Reformed readers hear a mother’s underestimate under a God’s long calendar. The delay will serve Jacob’s formation, not Rebekah’s schedule.

Genesis 27:45

45until your brother’s anger turns away from you, and he forgets what you have done to him. Then I will send and bring you from there. Why should I be bereft of you both in one day?”

Arminian

She wants anger forgotten and both sons alive: “Why should I be bereft of you both in one day?” Maternal fear of double loss is intelligible.

Arminian reading: she still scripts the reunion. Love that plots can also plead; neither cancels responsibility for the original fraud.

Reformed

Bereavement of both—Jacob slain, Esau the killer—would empty her house.

Reformed comfort: God will keep both alive for now, on terms she does not control. Her question is real; His purpose for the line is deeper than her fear.

Genesis 27:46

46Then Rebekah said to Isaac, “I loathe my life because of the Hittite women. If Jacob marries one of the Hittite women like these, one of the women of the land, what good will my life be to me?”

Arminian

She loathes life because of Esau’s Hittite wives and uses that distress to steer Isaac toward sending Jacob. The feeling may be honest (26:34–35); the use is tactical.

Classical Arminians name both: real grief at Canaanite marriages, and a responsible choice to argue by that grief rather than confess the flight’s true cause.

Reformed

Hittite women become the public reason.

Reformed note: the narrator has already said they made life bitter for Isaac and Rebekah. Providence can fold a true marital grief into Jacob’s departure without making Rebekah’s partial story a model of candor.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Esau’s cry, hatred, and Jacob sent away—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 27:30–46.

Arminian Notable voices

“Isaac trembled exceedingly—Those that follow the choice of their own affections rather than the dictates of the Divine will, involve themselves in such perplexities as these. But he soon recovers himself, and ratifies the blessing he had given to Jacob, I have blessed him, and he shall be blessed—He might have recalled it, but now at last he is sensible he was in an error when he designed it for Esau.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 27:33)

Reformed Notable voices

“Besides, it is no common fear which Moses describes, but that which utterly confounds the holy man: for, whereas he was perfectly conscious of his own vocation, and therefore was persuaded that the duty of naming the heir with whom he should deposit the covenant of eternal life was divinely enjoined upon him, he no sooner discovered his error than he was filled with fear, that in an affair so great and so serious God had suffered him to err; for unless he had thought that God was the director of this act, what should have hindered him from alleging his ignorance as an excuse, and from becoming enraged against Jacob, who had stolen in upon him by fraud and by unjustifiable arts?”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 27:33)
