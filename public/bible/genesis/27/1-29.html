Passage

Genesis 27

Verses 1–29 · WEB · Voices

Isaac, old and nearly blind, means to bless Esau. Rebekah and Jacob deceive him, and Jacob receives the blessing the Lord had already assigned—through sinful means God does not author as a lie.

Genesis 27:1

1When Isaac was old, and his eyes were dim, so that he could not see, he called Esau his elder son, and said to him, “My son?” He said to him, “Here I am.”

Arminian

Isaac is old, eyes dim, and he calls Esau “his elder son.” Favoritism chooses the audience before the blessing is spoken.

What comes through is a responsible preference. Blindness does not annul agency: the father summons the son he loves, not the son the oracle named (25:23).

Reformed

Age and dim sight set the scene, yet the first move is Isaac’s call to Esau. The household already leans (25:28).

Reformed readers remember that God’s purpose according to election stands behind this mess. Providence will overrule the father’s favoring without making that favoring holy.

Genesis 27:2

2He said, “See now, I am old. I don’t know the day of my death.

Arminian

“I don’t know the day of my death” is honest frailty, then used to hurry a blessing. Mortality can sober a man or scramble him.

Isaac still chooses whom to bless. Uncertainty about the day is not an excuse to ignore the Lord’s earlier word.

Reformed

The patriarch feels death near and moves to settle the blessing on his own terms.

Even a fading saint can act against the oracle. God’s purpose for Jacob does not wait on Isaac’s accurate timetable.

Genesis 27:3

3Now therefore, please take your weapons, your quiver and your bow, and go out to the field, and get me venison.

Arminian

Quiver, bow, field, game—Isaac sends Esau into the work he loves. The errand is an assignment Esau can fulfill.

Arminians see ordinary vocation recruited into a biased plan. Hunting is not the sin; using it to lock in favor is.

Reformed

The hunt is means. Isaac will eat what Esau brings and then bless.

Providence can let the older son go to the field so the younger can enter the tent. God is not the author of the scheme that will fill the gap.

Genesis 27:4

4Make me savory food, such as I love, and bring it to me, that I may eat, and that my soul may bless you before I die.”

Arminian

“Savory food, such as I love” joins appetite to blessing. Isaac’s love of savory game (25:28) still steers his soul.

Holy speech can be yoked to a favorite’s plate. The blessing he intends is an act he is responsible to aim at the right son.

Reformed

Soul-blessing is covenantal, not a mood. Isaac tries to channel the promise toward Esau by a meal.

Reformed readers hear a father’s will leaning against 25:23. The Lord will let the words fall on Jacob without endorsing the appetite as theology.

Genesis 27:5

5Rebekah heard when Isaac spoke to Esau his son. Esau went to the field to hunt for venison, and to bring it.

Arminian

Rebekah is listening. Overhearing is not yet the crime, but it becomes fuel for a plot.

Arminian interpreters name her as a moral agent: she hears, she will command. Election of Jacob does not license turning news into fraud.

Reformed

The narrator lets us see what Isaac did not: Rebekah hears the plan.

God is not surprised. What she overhears will be used in a lie He does not condone, while His purpose according to election still stands.

Genesis 27:6

6Rebekah spoke to Jacob her son, saying, “Behold, I heard your father speak to Esau your brother, saying,

Arminian

She reports to “her son Jacob,” matching Isaac’s “his elder son.” The split household now speaks in sides.

Rebekah chooses a confidant and will recruit him. Grace does not erase her responsibility for what she does with the news.

Reformed

The mother’s address already picks a side.

The oracle named Jacob, yet this verse shows a woman taking matters into her own hands. Providence can employ a report without baptizing the coming deceit.

Genesis 27:7

7‘Bring me venison, and make me savory food, that I may eat, and bless you before the LORD before my death.’

Arminian

She quotes Isaac, adding “before the LORD.” The blessing is treated as worship even while she plans to steal it.

Arminian interpreters hear the danger: naming the LORD over a scheme does not make the scheme holy.

Reformed

“Before the LORD” tells the truth about the blessing’s weight.

The Name Isaac invoked is bigger than Isaac’s intent. God will own His promise without owning Rebekah’s method.

Genesis 27:8

8Now therefore, my son, obey my voice according to that which I command you.

Arminian

“Obey my voice according to that which I command you.” Maternal authority is pressed into fraud.

A child can refuse a sinful command. Jacob will still have to choose; Rebekah is responsible for issuing the order.

Reformed

Command language fills the tent. This is not God’s voice.

Reformed readers distinguish 25:23 from a mother’s plot. Providence overrules; it does not speak this command.

Genesis 27:9

9Go now to the flock and get me two good young goats from there. I will make them savory food for your father, such as he loves.

Arminian

Two goats from the flock will impersonate wild game. The plan is concrete, skilled, and hers.

Arminians refuse to call this faith. Trust in the oracle would wait on God; this is cooking a lie.

Reformed

She will manufacture the meal Isaac tied to the blessing.

The goats are not the Lord’s appointed means. He can still give Jacob the blessing without having required a counterfeit stew.

Genesis 27:10

10You shall bring it to your father, that he may eat, so that he may bless you before his death.”

Arminian

The aim is stated: that he may bless you before his death. Ends are named; means are fraud.

A true promise must not be seized by deceit. Election is not cover for getting there first.

Reformed

She aims Jacob at the blessing Isaac meant for Esau.

God’s purpose will land on Jacob, yet this sentence is still a human plot. The Lord does not need her timetable to keep 25:23.

Genesis 27:11

11Jacob said to Rebekah his mother, “Behold, Esau my brother is a hairy man, and I am a smooth man.

Arminian

Jacob’s first objection is tactical: Esau is hairy, I am smooth. He fears detection more than deceit.

Arminian readers hear a responsible pause that does not yet become repentance. He could have refused the whole scheme.

Reformed

The younger son names the risk, not the sin.

Even the elect line can think like a trickster. God’s choice of Jacob is not Jacob’s virtue on display here.

Genesis 27:12

12What if my father touches me? I will seem to him as a deceiver, and I would bring a curse on myself, and not a blessing.”

Arminian

He dreads a curse if his father feels him. Conscience flickers, then calculates.

Pastorally: fear of being caught is not love of truth. He still stands at a live fork—honesty that trusts God, or blessing by fraud.

Reformed

Curse versus blessing is covenant language, now treated as a gamble.

Jacob cannot earn what God promised, and he also cannot steal it as if the Lord were boxed in. The fear is not denied; so is the unbelief inside it.

Genesis 27:13

13His mother said to him, “Let your curse be on me, my son. Only obey my voice, and go get them for me.”

Arminian

“Let your curse be on me”—Rebekah absorbs the risk to keep the plot moving.

Classical Arminians do not praise this as sacrifice. Taking a curse to enable a lie is still sin; a mother cannot transfer guilt by fiat.

Reformed

She offers to bear the curse so Jacob will obey.

Reformed readers hear reckless love, not atonement. God will neither need her oath nor treat the lie as her gift to providence.

Genesis 27:14

14He went, and got them, and brought them to his mother. His mother made savory food, such as his father loved.

Arminian

He went, took, brought; she prepared. The verbs are ordinary and guilty.

Jacob’s feet ratify the scheme. Agency is not erased by her cooking.

Reformed

The meal is prepared “such as his father loved.”

Providence can let savory food be made without condoning the hands that make it. The blessing’s certainty does not cleanse the kitchen.

Genesis 27:15

15Rebekah took the good clothes of Esau, her elder son, which were with her in the house, and put them on Jacob, her younger son.

Arminian

Esau’s best garments go on Jacob. Identity is borrowed, not received.

Arminians name the theft of a brother’s honor as a free choice, not a necessary step in election.

Reformed

Clothes become a mask.

God had already named Jacob; the wardrobe cannot improve the oracle. Providence will overrule Isaac’s eyes without endorsing the costume.

Genesis 27:16

16She put the skins of the young goats on his hands, and on the smooth of his neck.

Arminian

Goatskins on hands and neck complete the impersonation. The body is falsified.

Classical Arminians refuse the excuse that God wanted Jacob blessed, so the skins were required. They were not.

Reformed

Touch will be deceived because skin was faked.

The Lord can use a blind father’s hands without authoring the goatskins. Means He hates can still fail to overturn a purpose He loves.

Genesis 27:17

17She gave the savory food and the bread, which she had prepared, into the hand of her son Jacob.

Arminian

Food and bread are placed in Jacob’s hand—he must carry the lie in.

For the hearer: the last step is still his. Grace that chose Jacob does not force this tray into Isaac’s room.

Reformed

The younger son is now equipped.

Reformed readers watch providence permit the entrance. Permission is not approval; the God of 25:23 will keep His word without signing this menu.

Genesis 27:18

18He came to his father, and said, “My father?” He said, “Here I am. Who are you, my son?”

Arminian

“My father.” “Who are you, my son?” The question is a mercy—an opening to tell the truth.

One hears a live chance Jacob can still take. He is not a puppet walking a script.

Reformed

Isaac’s uncertainty is already a crack in the plan.

God can unsettle the deceiver with a simple question. The father’s “Who are you?” does not make God the author of the answer that follows.

Genesis 27:19

19Jacob said to his father, “I am Esau your firstborn. I have done what you asked me to do. Please arise, sit and eat of my venison, that your soul may bless me.”

Arminian

“I am Esau your firstborn.” The lie is plain, plus a fake obedience. He claims the blessing under a stolen name.

This is fraud, not faith. Grace does not erase the responsibility of that sentence.

Reformed

He takes Esau’s name and Esau’s errand in one breath.

Jacob does not earn the blessing here; he sins toward it. God’s purpose stands in spite of the lie, not because the lie was needed.

Genesis 27:20

20Isaac said to his son, “How is it that you have found it so quickly, my son?” He said, “Because the LORD your God gave me success.”

Arminian

Pressed on speed, Jacob drags the LORD’s name into the lie. Pious speech now covers deceit.

Wesley’s heirs hear the worst turn yet. Using the Lord’s name as camouflage is the abuse this chapter warns against: election as cover for fraud.

Reformed

He attributes the fraud’s timing to YHWH.

Providence is true, and taking God’s name in vain is still sin. The Lord may have allowed a quick return; He did not authorize this testimony.

Genesis 27:21

21Isaac said to Jacob, “Please come near, that I may feel you, my son, whether you are really my son Esau or not.”

Arminian

Isaac asks to feel him—doubt seeking certainty. The father is not merely a dupe; he tests.

Both men remain agents. Isaac’s caution is an attempt at discernment; Jacob can still confess.

Reformed

Touch is summoned because the voice has already raised suspicion.

God does not owe Isaac a miracle of recognition. He may let dim eyes fail while still judging the lie.

Genesis 27:22

22Jacob went near to Isaac his father. He felt him, and said, “The voice is Jacob’s voice, but the hands are the hands of Esau.”

Arminian

“The voice is Jacob’s… the hands are Esau’s.” Isaac almost names the truth.

Classical Arminians honor this flicker of discernment. He could have stopped; he will talk himself past his own ears.

Reformed

Voice and hands disagree.

Reformed readers hear a man torn between senses, not a God who needed confusion. Providence can withhold clarity without authoring the costume that confused him.

Genesis 27:23

23He didn’t recognize him, because his hands were hairy, like his brother Esau’s hands. So he blessed him.

Arminian

He does not recognize him, “so he blessed him.” Recognition fails; blessing proceeds.

A mistaken blessing is still Isaac’s act, and Jacob’s disguise is still Jacob’s guilt. God is not the one who put hair on those hands.

Reformed

Hebrews 11:20 will later say Isaac blessed by faith—faith in God’s future, not faith in a trick.

The blessing lands on the elect son through a father’s erring senses. Purpose stands; the deceit is not thereby made righteous.

Genesis 27:24

24He said, “Are you really my son Esau?” He said, “I am.”

Arminian

“Are you my son Esau?” A second chance, a second lie.

Arminians refuse to soften the word. Direct denial is a responsible choice, not a slip of nerves.

Reformed

Isaac asks again; Jacob doubles down.

Confirmation by a lie does not create the blessing’s validity. God had already chosen; this “I am” is Jacob’s sin, not Jacob’s title-deed.

Genesis 27:25

25He said, “Bring it near to me, and I will eat of my son’s venison, that my soul may bless you.” He brought it near to him, and he ate. He brought him wine, and he drank.

Arminian

Eat, drink, then bless—Isaac binds the meal to the word. Jacob serves wine as part of the ruse.

Table fellowship is being counterfeited. Sharing food under a false name is still his act.

Reformed

The patriarch eats “his son’s game” that is not Esau’s.

The meal proceeds under a misnomer, yet the God who owns the blessing is not fooled. He will honor His purpose without honoring the menu’s pretense.

Genesis 27:26

26His father Isaac said to him, “Come near now, and kiss me, my son.”

Arminian

“Come near now, and kiss me.” Affection is requested and will be used as cover.

Classical Arminians see how love-tokens can be recruited to a lie. Jacob can still refuse the kiss.

Reformed

Nearness and a kiss precede the poem of blessing.

Reformed readers do not treat the kiss as God’s idea. Providence may let a father embrace the wrong face; He does not command the fraud that made the face feel right.

Genesis 27:27

27He came near, and kissed him. He smelled the smell of his clothing, and blessed him, and said, “Behold, the smell of my son is as the smell of a field which the LORD has blessed.

Arminian

Smell of garments, then blessing: “a field which the LORD has blessed.” Isaac reads Esau’s clothes as the Lord’s field.

Sensory piety can misread. The LORD does bless the land; He did not perfume this disguise.

Reformed

The blessing begins with smell and ascribes it to the LORD.

The field-language is bigger than Esau’s cloak. God can speak true plenty over Jacob even when the father’s nose is mistaken.

Genesis 27:28

28God give you of the dew of the sky, of the fatness of the earth, and plenty of grain and new wine.

Arminian

Dew, fatness, grain, and wine—ordinary gifts named as God’s giving. The content is generous and public.

Arminian interpreters hear earnest petition: “God give.” Isaac is asking, not magically creating; the words still pass through a deceived mouth.

Reformed

The blessing’s substance is God-given abundance.

These goods belong to the promise-line. The Lord can grant dew and fatness to Jacob because He had already chosen him—not because Jacob earned grain by a costume.

Genesis 27:29

29Let peoples serve you, and nations bow down to you. Be lord over your brothers. Let your mother’s sons bow down to you. Cursed be everyone who curses you. Blessed be everyone who blesses you.”

Arminian

Peoples, nations, lordship over brothers, curse and blessing echoing 12:3. The words are Abraham-sized.

Jacob has not merited this by deceit. The echo of Abram’s call is gift; using fraud to wear it is still sin.

Reformed

Nations bowing and the 12:3 formula show this is the covenant blessing, not a private wish.

God’s purpose according to election stands. The Lord overrules Isaac’s intended target without making the lie the engine of the promise.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on the stolen blessing and the word that still stands, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“Those that are blessed of God are kissed with the kisses of his mouth, and they do by love and loyalty kiss the son, Psa 2:12. 2. Upon occasion of the sweet smell of his garments he said, See the smell of my son is as the smell of a field which the Lord hath blessed—That is, like that of the most fragrant flowers and spices. Three things Jacob is here blessed with, Plenty, Ge 27:28.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 27:27)

Reformed Notable voices

“The allegory of Ambrose on this passage is not displeasing to me. Jacob, the younger brother, is blessed under the person of the elder; the garments which were borrowed from his brother breathe an odour grateful and pleasant to his father. In the same manner we are blessed, as Ambrose teaches, when, in the name of Christ, we enter the presence of our Heavenly Father: we receive from him the robe of righteousness, which, by its odour, procures his favor; in short, we are thus blessed when we are put in his place.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 27:27)
