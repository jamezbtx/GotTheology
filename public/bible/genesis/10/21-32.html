Passage

Genesis 10

Verses 21–32 · ESV · Voices

The table turns to Shem, father of Eber, then Peleg and Joktan, and closes: from these clans the nations spread abroad after the flood.

Genesis 10:21

21To Shem also, the father of all the children of Eber, the elder brother of Japheth, children were born.

Arminian

Shem is introduced with honor: father of all the children of Eber, and Japheth’s elder brother. The line that will include Abraham is a family among families, not a denial of the others already listed.

Classical Arminians gladly trace the Messiah’s ancestors here, while insisting God’s mercy remains wide: Shem’s privilege serves the blessing of the nations, not a closed circle of grace.

Reformed

“Father of all the children of Eber” flags the line of promise. Eber yields the family from which Abraham comes; Shem is placed to carry the story toward Christ.

Reformed accent: God sovereignly marks this branch. Among all the Table’s names, providence highlights Shem and Eber because the election of a people for the world’s good is already in view.

Key contrast Both honor Shem and Eber. Arminians typically read this line as the appointed means of a sincere, wide blessing for the nations; Reformed readers typically read it as God’s sovereign choice of the promise-bearing family that will run to Abraham and Christ.

Genesis 10:22

22The sons of Shem: Elam, Asshur, Arpachshad, Lud, and Aram.

Arminian

Shem’s sons include Elam, Asshur, Arpachshad, Lud, and Aram—eastern and northern peoples as well as the ancestor of the Hebrews’ nearer kin.

Arminian pastoral: even the promise line sits among brothers. Asshur and Aram will later be empires and neighbors; they remain kin in this list. God’s blessing of Shem is larger than one grandson.

Reformed

Five sons, one of whom (Arpachshad) will carry the recorded line to Eber and Abraham. God names the others because they too are ordered under His rule.

Reformed reading: the promise narrows without pretending the other sons do not exist. Sovereignty can distinguish a line while still governing Elam and Asshur.

Genesis 10:23

23The sons of Aram: Uz, Hul, Gether, and Mash.

Arminian

Aram’s sons include Uz—later associated with Job’s land. Wisdom and suffering will arise from this branch too.

Arminians hear that God attends to Aramean families with the same naming care. Cultures north and east of Canaan are gift, not narrative leftovers.

Reformed

Aram’s grandsons are catalogued because the Lord numbers Israel’s neighbors in advance.

Reformed accent: Uz and his brothers exist by providence. The God of the Table is already the God of the east.

Genesis 10:24

24Arpachshad fathered Shelah; and Shelah fathered Eber.

Arminian

The chain tightens: Arpachshad, Shelah, Eber. Ordinary fathering is how God keeps a line in the world.

Classical Arminians see responsible history in begetting and naming—human families as the means of blessing—while tracing with the church the road that will reach Abraham.

Reformed

Three links, no gaps: God is preserving the spine of the genealogy that will later extend to Abraham and, in the fullness of time, to Christ.

Reformed comfort: providence over genealogies is meticulous. The line toward Abraham is not a lucky survival of names; it is God’s keeping.

Genesis 10:25

25To Eber were born two sons: the name of the one was Peleg, for in his days the earth was divided, and his brother's name was Joktan.

Arminian

Peleg is named for a division of the earth in his days—often linked to the scattering of Babel in the next chapter, or to a splitting of territories. Joktan is named beside him without that etymology.

Classical Arminians read the division as a historical event under providence that still involves human pride (the tower) and human spreading. The earth is divided “in his days,” a responsible moment in the nations’ story, not a denial of God’s care for every resulting people.

Reformed

The narrator pauses to interpret a name: Peleg because the earth was divided. God timestamps a global ordering of peoples.

Reformed confession: division of the earth is by God’s act, not demographic accident. In Peleg’s days the Lord apportioned the world—setting the stage on which He will call Abraham from among the nations.

Key contrast Both tie Peleg’s name to a real division of the earth. Arminians typically emphasize a historical spreading (often linked to Babel’s pride) in which human responsibility and God’s wide care for resulting nations meet; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s sovereign dividing of peoples and lands as decree, preparing the line of Eber toward Abraham.

Genesis 10:26

26Joktan fathered Almodad, Sheleph, Hazarmaveth, Jerah,

Arminian

Joktan’s many sons begin here—names associated with southern and eastern clans. The brother not named for division still fathers a full household.

Arminian pastoral: blessing is not only on Peleg’s side. Joktan’s children display the gift of fruitfulness among Shem’s other branches.

Reformed

The list of Joktanites is long because God records more than the promise spine. He is Lord of the many as well as of the few.

Reformed accent: providence over genealogies includes the populous side of Eber. These names are in Scripture because the Lord willed their memory.

Genesis 10:27

27Hadoram, Uzal, Diklah,

Arminian

Three more sons, briefly named. The Table refuses to rush past them.

Arminians hear dignity in the briefest list: God knows Hadoram, Uzal, and Diklah. Wideness of mercy begins with being named before Him.

Reformed

The genealogy continues without ornamental comment. God is counting.

Reformed reading: stories unknown to us still sit inside named decree. These sons exist because the Lord ordered Eber’s house.

Genesis 10:28

28Obal, Abimael, Sheba,

Arminian

Sheba appears again (compare Ham’s line)—trade names can overlap in the ancient record, or related peoples can share a name. Either way, southern wealth is on the map.

Arminian note: God is not confused by overlapping names. Cultures of incense and gold are still families first, gifts under providence.

Reformed

Obal, Abimael, and Sheba take their place in Joktan’s roster. The Lord of nations is precise even when later readers must sort shared names.

Reformed accent: overlapping names do not threaten providence. God knows which Sheba He means in each text.

Genesis 10:29

29Ophir, Havilah, and Jobab; all these were the sons of Joktan.

Arminian

Ophir and Havilah close the list with lands later famed for gold. “All these were the sons of Joktan” gathers a large family as one household.

Classical Arminians give thanks for material gifts and for many sons: prosperity and people are from God’s hand, to be received without pride.

Reformed

The summary line is bookkeeping under God: all these belong to Joktan in His record.

Reformed confession: gold-lands and ordinary names alike exist by the Lord’s appointment. The Table’s “all these” is a completeness claim—God has not missed a son He meant to list.

Genesis 10:30

30The territory in which they lived extended from Mesha in the direction of Sephar to the hill country of the east.

Arminian

Joktan’s sons receive a territory description, as Canaan’s sons did. Eastward hills and named bounds mean these clans had a home.

Arminian pastoral: land is gift. God gives peoples room to live; cultures take shape in real geography under a providence that is kind, not stingy.

Reformed

Borders for Joktan match the pattern used for Canaan: God assigns space as well as ancestry.

Reformed accent: territories exist by decree. Mesha toward Sephar is not a random range; the Lord set the eastern hill country as their dwelling.

Genesis 10:31

31These are the sons of Shem, by their clans, their languages, their lands, and their nations.

Arminian

Shem’s section closes with the same fourfold refrain as Japheth’s coastlands and Ham’s sons. Shem is one nation-family among the three, even as his line carries special promise.

Arminians hold both: gratitude for the Eber–Abraham road, and refusal to treat other Shemite nations as extras. Languages and lands among Shem’s children are gifts of a God whose mercy is sincerely wide.

Reformed

The refrain again: clans, languages, lands, nations. Shem’s diversity is structured by God.

Reformed reading: the promise line does not dissolve the Table into one clan. God ordered many Shemite nations while keeping Eber’s thread for Abraham and Christ.

Genesis 10:32

32These are the clans of the sons of Noah, according to their genealogies, in their nations, and from these the nations spread abroad on the earth after the flood.

Arminian

The chapter’s seal: clans, genealogies, nations, then spreading abroad after the flood. From one family, a world of peoples—exactly the filling of the earth God intended.

Classical Arminians read this as blessing: God wants the nations, not a single empire. Spreading is gift and calling; the next chapter will show pride trying to halt it. Mercy remains wide to every people that came from these sons.

Reformed

The closing sentence is decree-shaped: from these, nations spread. The Table is not anthropology alone; it is theology of providence.

Reformed confession: the post-flood world of peoples exists because God willed it. Genealogies, nations, and their spreading prepare the stage for Abraham—and, in the fullness of time, for the Christ who gathers the nations without erasing what God has made.

Key contrast Both see the nations’ spread after the flood as God’s purpose, not accident. Arminians typically emphasize blessing, responsible history, and sincere wideness of mercy toward every people from Noah; Reformed readers typically emphasize sovereign ordering of genealogies and territories, the earth filled by decree, and the Table as the set stage for Abraham and Christ.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Shem, Eber, and the spreading of the clans after the flood—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 10:21–32.

Arminian Notable voices

“The description of Shem, Ge 10:21, we have not only his name, Shem, which signifies a name; but two titles to distinguish him by. He was the father of all the children of Eber. Eber was his great grandson, but why should he be called the father of all his children, rather than of all Arphaxad's or Salah's?”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 10:21)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses, being about to speak of the sons of Shem, makes a brief introduction, which he had not done in reference to the others. Nor was it without reason; for since this was the race chosen by God, he wished to sever it from other nations by some special mark. This also is the reason why he expressly styles him the father of the sons of Eber,' and the elder brother of Japheth.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 10:21)
