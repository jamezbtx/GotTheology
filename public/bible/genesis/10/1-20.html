Passage

Genesis 10

Verses 1–20 · WEB · Voices

After the flood, Genesis lists Noah’s sons and traces Japheth and Ham—coastlands, Nimrod’s kingdom, Canaan’s territory—as nations take shape under God’s care.

Genesis 10:1

1Now this is the history of the generations of the sons of Noah and of Shem, Ham, and Japheth. Sons were born to them after the flood.

Arminian

The Table of Nations opens as a generations account: after judgment, God lets life continue through Noah’s three sons. History is not a closed circle of one tribe; families multiply under God’s blessing.

Listen here for the wideness of mercy after the flood. God’s kindness toward all peoples begins with this family tree. Cultures will diversify; the story is not only about Israel yet, but about humanity given a fresh start.

Reformed

“Now this is the history of the generations” is covenant bookkeeping. The God who judged the world now orders the post-flood family, naming Shem, Ham, and Japheth as the root of every later nation.

Reformed readers see providence already: sons born after the flood are not accident. God is mapping peoples toward His purposes, including the line that will run through Shem to Abraham and Christ.

Genesis 10:2

2The sons of Japheth were: Gomer, Magog, Madai, Javan, Tubal, Meshech, and Tiras.

Arminian

Japheth’s sons are listed without scorn—peoples who will fill coasts and inland regions. Each name is a historical family, not a theological afterthought.

Arminians gladly confess that God’s blessing of the nations includes these far peoples. Mercy is not tribal; the God of the flood is already the God of many languages and lands.

Reformed

The catalog is precise because God rules history in particulars. Japheth’s line is named, numbered, and placed under providence before later prophets ever mention Magog or Javan.

Nations do not wander into being. Even peoples far from Canaan exist by the Lord’s ordering, not by chance.

Genesis 10:3

3The sons of Gomer were: Ashkenaz, Riphath, and Togarmah.

Arminian

Grandsons of Japheth receive the same dignity of naming. Human cultures branch as gift; families become clans without losing their place in God’s care.

God attends to lesser-known names. Wideness of mercy means no people is too small to be recorded in the story of blessing after the flood.

Reformed

Genealogies are God’s map, not filler. Ashkenaz, Riphath, and Togarmah stand because the Lord numbers the families of the earth.

Providence over genealogies is as true as providence over kings. The God who will choose Abraham already knows every branch of Japheth.

Genesis 10:4

4The sons of Javan were: Elishah, Tarshish, Kittim, and Dodanim.

Arminian

Javan’s sons point toward maritime peoples—Tarshish and Kittim later known in Israel’s world. Trade, ships, and distant coasts are not outside God’s interest.

Arminians hear cultures as gift under providence: seafaring nations too belong to the God who blessed Noah’s sons. The later gospel will sail those same seas.

Reformed

Names that will echo in prophetic oracles already sit in this list. God is writing geography before Israel exists.

The Lord orders even the coast-bound lines of Javan. History’s later maps were first His.

Genesis 10:5

5Of these were the islands of the nations divided in their lands, everyone after his language, after their families, in their nations.

Arminian

Spreading, lands, language, clans, nations—diversity is described as fact, not as a curse in this verse. Peoples take shape with their own speech and soil.

Classical Arminians see human cultures as gift under providence: language and clan can be received with gratitude. The wideness of mercy will later meet these very nations with the gospel; they are not accidents waiting to be erased.

Reformed

Fourfold order—lands, language, clans, nations—reads as divine arrangement. Peoples spread because God allots; they do not merely drift.

Nations exist by decree, not accident. The God who will divide tongues in the next chapter already frames here a world of distinct peoples under His rule.

Key contrast Both honor the spread of coastland peoples as God’s doing. Arminians press cultures as gifts under a sincere, wide mercy toward all nations; Calvinists stress that lands, languages, and nations exist by God’s sovereign allotment, not by historical accident.

Genesis 10:6

6The sons of Ham were: Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan.

Arminian

Ham’s sons include great civilizations—Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan. The Table does not flatten them into villains at the first naming.

Even peoples later in conflict with Israel begin as families under God’s post-flood blessing. Responsible history will show both gift and later rebellion; the names themselves are still human kin.

Reformed

Ham’s line is as carefully listed as Japheth’s. Egypt and Canaan will dominate later chapters, yet they first appear here under the same generations formula.

God sovereignly orders even the peoples who will oppress or entice Israel. Their existence is not a surprise to the Lord who will redeem a people from Egypt and give them Canaan.

Genesis 10:7

7The sons of Cush were: Seba, Havilah, Sabtah, Raamah, and Sabteca. The sons of Raamah were: Sheba and Dedan.

Arminian

Arabian and African names cluster under Cush—Sheba and Dedan among them. Distant traders and desert clans belong in Scripture’s family album.

Arminians hear the blessing of the nations: God records southern peoples with the same care as nearer ones. Mercy is geographically wide before any missionary map is drawn.

Reformed

The nested genealogy (Cush, then Raamah, then Sheba and Dedan) shows ordered descent, not chaos.

Providence over genealogies includes the south. These names will surface again in Israel’s story because God already placed them in His book.

Genesis 10:8

8Cush became the father of Nimrod. He began to be a mighty one on the earth.

Arminian

Nimrod is singled out: first to be a mighty man. Strength and founding power are human capacities after the flood.

Classical Arminians read responsible history here: might can serve or swell into pride. God’s blessing of skill is distinct from God’s approval of every use of power. Nimrod’s name will soon attach to Babel.

Reformed

“The first… mighty man” marks a turning in post-flood society—concentrated power under one figure.

Even the rise of a hunter-king sits under providence. God is not surprised by empires; He orders the stage on which pride and later judgment will appear.

Key contrast Both take Nimrod’s might as historically weighty. Arminians emphasize responsible use of God-given strength that can become pride; Reformed commentators typically highlight God’s sovereign permission and ordering of such power within His larger purpose for the nations.

Genesis 10:9

9He was a mighty hunter before the LORD. Therefore it is said, “like Nimrod, a mighty hunter before the LORD”.

Arminian

The proverb remembers Nimrod “before the Lord”—his fame is public, even proverbial. Hunting and prowess are not condemned in the wording itself.

Living “before the Lord” can mean under God’s eye, not necessarily in God’s favor. Might that becomes a byword invites humility: human glory is accountable to the Lord who sees.

Reformed

The proverb is preserved because God superintends even cultural memory. “Before the Lord” locates Nimrod’s career in the divine presence, whether as under God’s gaze or as a defiant counterpart.

The Lord remains the measure of every mighty man. Empires and hunters do not operate offstage from providence.

Key contrast Nimrod’s fame stands “before the Lord.” Arminians hear moral accountability—might must not curdle into pride; Reformed readers hear that even proverbial power unfolds under God’s sovereign gaze and purpose.

Genesis 10:10

10The beginning of his kingdom was Babel, Erech, Accad, and Calneh, in the land of Shinar.

Arminian

Kingdom begins at Babel in Shinar—the same region soon associated with the tower. Civic founding is a human act: cities, names, a start of empire.

Arminians see culture-making as gift that can be bent. Building cities is not evil in itself; gathering power at Babel will soon display pride that resists God’s command to fill the earth. Responsible history treats Nimrod’s kingdom as chosen concentration, not fate.

Reformed

The first kingdom listed in Scripture begins at Babel. God records the geography of Shinar because later judgment and scattering will fall on this very land.

Kingdoms exist by God’s allotment even when they become stages of rebellion. Babel’s beginning is not outside the decree that will humble it and, in time, call Abraham from that same world.

Key contrast Both connect Nimrod’s kingdom with Babel and Shinar. Arminians stress human responsibility in concentrating power that can become proud resistance; Reformed commentators press God’s sovereign ordering of this first kingdom within the history that leads to scattering, Abraham, and Christ.

Genesis 10:11

11Out of that land he went into Assyria, and built Nineveh, Rehoboth Ir, Calah,

Arminian

Expansion from Shinar into Assyria: more cities, including Nineveh. Human ambition maps itself in brick and name.

City-building can be industry under providence, yet Nineveh’s later violence shows how founding power, unhumbled, becomes a threat to neighbors. History remains responsible at every new foundation.

Reformed

The move into Assyria is narrated as fact under God’s eye. Nineveh, later the rod of judgment and the object of Jonah’s mission, is already on the map.

God knew the great city before it terrified Israel. Providence over territories includes the cities that will both oppress and, in His time, hear mercy.

Genesis 10:12

12and Resen between Nineveh and the great city Calah.

Arminian

Resen completes the cluster; “the great city” may name the urban complex. Greatness is noted without applause.

Arminians catch a caution: “great” is a human measure. Cultures and capitals are gifts that must not become idols. The Lord of the nations is not impressed by size the way we are.

Reformed

“The great city” is placed on the biblical map by God’s narrator, not by Assyrian propaganda alone.

Urban greatness sits inside providence. The Lord who numbers villages also numbers the great city—and will later address it as He wills.

Genesis 10:13

13Mizraim became the father of Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, Naphtuhim,

Arminian

Egypt’s descendants are listed as peoples, not as a monolith. North African and related groups branch from a named father.

Even Egypt—later house of bondage—begins as family. God’s blessing of nations includes those who will become both gift and trial to Israel; mercy’s wideness does not erase later moral history.

Reformed

Egypt fathered peoples: genealogy explains geopolitics. The Lord is tracing how a superpower’s related clans come to be.

The God who will judge Egypt’s gods already wrote Egypt’s family into the Table. Sovereignty over nations is older than the Exodus.

Genesis 10:14

14Pathrusim, Casluhim (which the Philistines descended from), and Caphtorim.

Arminian

A parenthetical note: Philistines come from Casluhim. Future enemies of Israel are still human relatives in this list.

Arminian readers refuse to treat neighboring peoples as less than kin. They exist by God’s blessing of families; conflict later will be moral and historical, not a denial that they too are under the wideness of God’s care.

Reformed

The narrator inserts the Philistine origin because God is preparing Israel’s later story. Nothing about these coastal peoples will catch the Lord off guard.

Even the people who will seize the coast and harass the judges exist by decree. Providence over genealogies includes Israel’s future rivals.

Genesis 10:15

15Canaan became the father of Sidon (his firstborn), Heth,

Arminian

Canaan’s firstborn is Sidon; Heth (associated with the Hittites) follows. Coastal and inland peoples of the land are named as sons.

These are the peoples among whom Abraham will sojourn and whose land Israel will later receive. They are families first—cultures as gift—before they are “the Canaanites” of later war narratives.

Reformed

Sidon and Heth stand at the head of Canaan’s line because God is identifying the inhabitants of the land He will promise.

The future inheritance is not random displacement. The Lord who lists Canaan’s sons already knows the territory He will allot to Abraham’s seed.

Genesis 10:16

16the Jebusites, the Amorites, the Girgashites,

Arminian

Clan names become people names—Jebusites, Amorites, Girgashites. Jerusalem’s later occupants already have a place in the family record.

Arminians hear shared humanity: the peoples Israel will meet are cousins in the Table of Nations. God’s sincere concern for nations does not vanish when Israel is chosen; election of one people is unto blessing that still takes other peoples seriously.

Reformed

The list is theological as well as ethnological: these are the named occupants of the land in later promise texts.

God specifies the peoples whose territory He will give. The Amorites’ iniquity and Israel’s inheritance both sit under the same sovereign timeline.

Genesis 10:17

17the Hivites, the Arkites, the Sinites,

Arminian

Lesser-known names continue the Canaanite family. Obscurity in our memory is not obscurity to God.

For the hearer: every clan has a history God knows. The wideness of mercy includes peoples we skip in reading; they too were given land and language under providence.

Reformed

Hivites, Arkites, Sinites fill out the ordered list. God does not generalize “Canaan” without remainder.

Particularity is a mark of decree. The Lord who numbers hairs numbers clans.

Genesis 10:18

18the Arvadites, the Zemarites, and the Hamathites. Afterward the families of the Canaanites were spread abroad.

Arminian

The list ends with Arvad, Zemar, Hamath, then dispersal. Clans spread after being named—movement, not freeze-frame.

Arminian interpreters see dispersal as history under providence that still involves human choices to migrate and settle. Cultures spread as gift; the command to fill the earth is being lived, even by Canaan’s sons.

Reformed

“Afterward… dispersed” is the narrator’s signal that geography follows genealogy by God’s arrangement.

Canaanite scattering is not demographic accident. The Lord who will later define Israel’s borders already watches these clans take their places.

Key contrast Both see Canaanite dispersal as ordered, not random. Arminians typically emphasize responsible migration and cultures as gift under a wide providence; Reformed Christians stress God’s sovereign placement of clans in the land He will later allot.

Genesis 10:19

19The border of the Canaanites was from Sidon—as you go toward Gerar—to Gaza—as you go toward Sodom, Gomorrah, Admah, and Zeboiim—to Lasha.

Arminian

Borders are given: Sidon to Gaza, and toward the cities of the plain. Geography is pastoral here—the land has shape, and notorious cities already sit inside it.

Naming Sodom and Gomorrah in a boundary notice is sober. Territory is gift; what people do in that territory remains accountable. Mercy toward nations does not void later judgment on cities that fill up wickedness.

Reformed

The boundary sketch is theological cartography. God records the extent of Canaan because this is the stage of promise, sojourn, and later judgment on the cities of the plain.

Territories exist by God’s assignment. Sidon, Gaza, and the cities toward Lasha are not self-made maps; they lie where the Lord set them in the Table.

Genesis 10:20

20These are the sons of Ham, after their families, according to their languages, in their lands and their nations.

Arminian

The Hamite section closes with the same fourfold pattern: clans, languages, lands, nations. Identity is social, linguistic, geographic, and political—human culture in full.

Arminians give thanks: God blesses the nations as nations. The Table is not a prelude to erasing Ham’s children but a witness that they too received ordered life after the flood. The gospel’s later wideness already has a map.

Reformed

The refrain matches verse 5: God structures peoples along clan, tongue, land, and nation.

Ham’s nations exist by decree. The closing formula insists that this diversity is the Lord’s work, not a historical accident awaiting meaning.

Key contrast Both close Ham’s line as nations under God. Arminians typically emphasize the blessing and sincere wideness of mercy toward these cultures; Reformed interpreters underline that clans, languages, lands, and nations stand by God’s sovereign ordering.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on the table of nations from Japheth and Ham, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Moses begins with Japhet's family, either because he was the eldest, or because that lay remotest from Israel, and had least concern with them, at that time when Moses wrote; and therefore he mentions that race very briefly; hastening to give account of the posterity of Ham, who were Israel's enemies, and of Shem, who were Israel's ancestors.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 10:2)

Reformed Notable voices

“First, in these bare names we have still some fragment of the history of the world. This second commencement of mankind is especially worthy to be known; and detestable is the ingratitude of those, who, when they had heard, from their fathers and grandfathers of the wonderful restoration of the world in so short a time, yet voluntarily became forgetful of the grace and the salvation of God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 10:1)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
