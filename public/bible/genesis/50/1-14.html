Passage

Genesis 50

Verses 1–14 · WEB · Voices

Joseph weeps over Jacob, has Israel embalmed, and Egypt mourns seventy days. With Pharaoh’s leave a great company goes up to Canaan, laments at Atad, and buries him in Machpelah—Abraham’s purchased cave—then Joseph and his brothers return to Egypt.

Genesis 50:1

1Joseph fell on his father’s face, wept on him, and kissed him.

Arminian

Joseph falls on his father’s face, weeps, and kisses him. Rank in Egypt does not void a son’s tears. Faith that trusts the promises still honors the body of the dead.

Arminians receive this as responsible love. Grace does not make mourners stoic; it frees a son to weep without despair. Tenderness here is holy duty, not a delay of hope.

Reformed

The son God raised in Egypt weeps on Jacob’s face. Providence that kept him at Pharaoh’s side does not freeze his heart at death.

God is not embarrassed by these tears. The Lord who promised to go down with Jacob and bring him up again now meets a household in honest mourning.

Genesis 50:2

2Joseph commanded his servants, the physicians, to embalm his father; and the physicians embalmed Israel.

Arminian

Joseph commands the physicians to embalm—Egyptian skill used to honor “Israel,” not to paganize him. The body is treated as worthy of care for the long road to Canaan.

Care of the dead is stewardship. Embalming here serves the oath to bury in the land, not a denial that dust returns to dust. Wisdom uses a foreign craft for a covenant purpose.

Reformed

Physicians embalmed Israel: the covenant name on an Egyptian table. God can commandeer a court’s arts to keep a patriarch intact for Machpelah.

Even Egypt’s physicians serve the promise. The means are medical and royal; the end is the plot God already named. Egyptian technique carries Jacob toward the sworn grave.

Genesis 50:3

3Forty days were used for him, for that is how many days it takes to embalm. The Egyptians wept for Israel for seventy days.

Arminian

Forty days to embalm; seventy days Egypt weeps—near royal honor for a Hebrew shepherd. Neighbors can truly mourn; their tears are not theater. Grief is given time, not hurried off the stage.

Classical Arminians receive a nation’s grief as kindness. Egypt weeps because Joseph’s father mattered. Honor given can be received with thanks.

Reformed

Seventy days of Egyptian weeping is extraordinary favor. The God who gave Joseph to Pharaoh now clothes Jacob with a near-royal lament.

Hearts of a people sit in the Lord’s hand. State mourning becomes providence honoring the patriarch on the way to the land of promise.

Genesis 50:4

4When the days of weeping for him were past, Joseph spoke to Pharaoh’s staff, saying, “If now I have found favor in your eyes, please speak in the ears of Pharaoh, saying,

Arminian

When weeping has its time, Joseph speaks through Pharaoh’s household. He asks favor; he does not seize a caravan. Grief still walks the path of petition and courtesy.

Oath-keeping requires courage before power. Joseph uses access grace has given; he remains a servant who must request, not a man who overrides the king.

Reformed

Days of weeping end; petition begins. Joseph’s favor in the court is the door God already opened.

The household of Pharaoh becomes the mouthpiece. God has placed Joseph where an oath to a dying father can be heard in a king’s ear. The viceroy still petitions because the throne is under the Lord.

Genesis 50:5

5‘My father made me swear, saying, “Behold, I am dying. Bury me in my grave which I have dug for myself in the land of Canaan.” Now therefore, please let me go up and bury my father, and I will come again.’”

Arminian

Jacob’s oath: a tomb hewed in Canaan—there shall you bury me. Joseph adds, “And I will come.” Promise of the land and integrity toward Pharaoh stand together.

Arminian readers honor a vow freely sworn and now kept. Hope in the land is trusted, not coerced; burying there is obedient love, and he keeps faith with Egypt by pledging to come back.

Reformed

Jacob’s hewn tomb in Canaan is covenant geography. There shall you bury me reaches back to Abraham’s purchased cave.

God keeps the burial oath inside empire. Joseph’s “I will come” sits under the same providence—the sojourn in Egypt is not yet finished. The land claims the body while Egypt still holds the family.

Genesis 50:6

6Pharaoh said, “Go up, and bury your father, just like he made you swear.”

Arminian

Pharaoh’s word matches the oath: go up, bury, as he made you swear. A pagan king can speak the truth about a vow and release a son to keep it.

Rulers remain moral agents under providence. Pharaoh’s permission is a yes; God’s people may receive it as kindness and still go up to keep a father’s charge.

Reformed

“Just like he made you swear”—Pharaoh himself honors the vow. The king’s yes is the Lord’s provision for Jacob’s burial.

Even Pharaoh serves the promise. What God swore about the land now moves through a Gentile mouth. The oath is kept because this king said yes.

Genesis 50:7

7Joseph went up to bury his father; and with him went up all the servants of Pharaoh, the elders of his house, all the elders of the land of Egypt,

Arminian

Joseph goes up, and Egypt’s elders go with him. Honor of the dead becomes a public procession, not a private errand hidden in Goshen.

Arminian readers see a family keeping faith while a nation chooses to escort them. The elders’ going is respect; it does not make Egypt heir of the promise. They honor Joseph’s father without inheriting Abraham’s oath.

Reformed

Servants, household elders, and elders of the land: Egypt’s height accompanies Israel’s father toward Canaan.

God grants a state escort for a sojourner. The God of Abraham is publicly honored in the company, whether every elder names Him or not. Empire walks behind a shepherd’s coffin because the Lord willed this honor.

Genesis 50:8

8all the house of Joseph, his brothers, and his father’s house. Only their little ones, their flocks, and their herds, they left in the land of Goshen.

Arminian

The whole household goes up; children, flocks, and herds stay in Goshen. They honor the dead without abandoning the living or the place of sojourn.

Wisdom holds two callings—bury in Canaan, keep the little ones safe. What they leave behind already implies the return Joseph pledged. They will come back; Goshen is not yet home, and not yet empty.

Reformed

Brothers and father’s house go; Goshen keeps the children and herds. The family is not relocating the promise yet.

God holds the next generation in Egypt while the bones go home. The sojourn continues by design, even as Machpelah is about to receive Jacob. Little ones remain because the going-out is not yet.

Genesis 50:9

9Both chariots and horsemen went up with him. It was a very great company.

Arminian

Chariots and horsemen make a very great company. Egypt’s strength escorts a Hebrew burial. Power is here used to honor, not to enslave.

Wesleyan Arminians receive the spectacle as gift. The company is great because many went; the oath, not the cavalry, is the point. Honor is genuine; Egypt is still not the land of the oath.

Reformed

A military escort for a shepherd’s funeral: God has reversed Joseph’s old humiliation.

Comfort: the great company is providence made visible. What brothers once sent down in a pit, God now sends up with chariots toward the land He swore. Honor is public; the promise is still a burial, not an Exodus.

Genesis 50:10

10They came to the threshing floor of Atad, which is beyond the Jordan, and there they lamented with a very great and severe lamentation. He mourned for his father seven days.

Arminian

At Atad, beyond the Jordan, the lament is very great and grievous; Joseph adds seven days. Grief is given full measure on the threshold of the land.

Seven days is chosen honor, not rushed disposal. Mourning is responsible love; faith can linger in tears at the edge of promise without unbelief. They stop because love requires it, not because hope has failed.

Reformed

Threshing floor, Jordan, seven days: God appoints a place and a week for grievous lament.

The land is near, and mourning is still holy. Covenant hope does not skip Atad; it walks through a lament God is not ashamed to record. Seven days on Canaan’s doorstep is not delay; it is grief under promise.

Genesis 50:11

11When the inhabitants of the land, the Canaanites, saw the mourning in the floor of Atad, they said, “This is a grievous mourning by the Egyptians.” Therefore its name was called Abel Mizraim, which is beyond the Jordan.

Arminian

Canaanites read the scene as “grievous mourning by the Egyptians,” and the place is named Abel-mizraim. Outsiders can recognize honor even when they miss the covenant plot.

Arminian interpreters hear a public witness. Egypt’s tears tell something true about Jacob’s worth; neighbors remain responsible for what they do with that sight.

Reformed

The land’s inhabitants name the floor from Egypt’s grief. God writes a memorial—“mourning of Egypt”—onto Canaan’s map.

Even misread, the lament marks what God is doing. A Gentile name now sits on the path of a patriarch toward Machpelah. Abel-mizraim will remember Egypt’s tears on the way to Abraham’s cave.

Genesis 50:12

12His sons did to him just as he commanded them,

Arminian

“Thus his sons did… as he had commanded.” Obedience to a dying father is the headline. The grand escort serves a simple fidelity, not a display for its own sake.

The brothers act together. Grace that reconciled them now shows in keeping Jacob’s word—responsible sons who obey a command they could have left in Egypt.

Reformed

The narrator underlines command and fulfillment. What Israel ordered, his sons performed.

God keeps covenant through a family’s kept word. The dying patriarch’s command is honored because the Lord who promised the land is faithful. Kept command is how the sworn geography receives its next heir.

Genesis 50:13

13for his sons carried him into the land of Canaan, and buried him in the cave of the field of Machpelah, which Abraham bought with the field, as a possession for a burial site, from Ephron the Hittite, near Mamre.

Arminian

They carry him to Canaan and bury him in Machpelah, east of Mamre—the field Abraham bought from Ephron. Honor of the dead is tied to a deed, not a borrowed grave.

Classical Arminians see promise and purchase together. Burying here is trust that the oath still stands; hope in the land is trusted, not coerced. The sons treat the deed as holy, not as nostalgia.

Reformed

Machpelah, Mamre, Ephron: the first owned plot in the sworn land receives another heir.

Machpelah is the down payment of the land. God keeps the burial word of chapter 49 and the purchase of chapter 23—covenant faithfulness with an address east of Mamre. Paid silver in Abraham’s day still locates Jacob’s bones.

Genesis 50:14

14Joseph returned into Egypt—he, and his brothers, and all that went up with him to bury his father, after he had buried his father.

Arminian

After the burial, Joseph returns into Egypt—he, and his brothers, and the whole company. The land is honored; the sojourn is not abandoned. They go back as they pledged to Pharaoh.

Classical Arminians hear integrity toward the king and patience toward the promise. Returning is obedience in the meantime; a later generation will come up again.

Reformed

The funeral ends in Egypt again. God still has a people in Goshen, and the Exodus is not yet.

Comfort: the sojourn continues until God visits—as 15–26 will say. Burial in Canaan does not annul the appointed years; the Lord who claimed Machpelah also holds Israel in Egypt until He brings them up.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What older interpreters in each camp said about Egypt’s mourning, and Jacob buried in Canaan.

Arminian Notable voices

“Forty days were taken up in embalming the body, which the Egyptians had an art of doing so curiously, as to preserve the very features of the face unchanged. All this time, and thirty days more, seventy in all, they either confined themselves and sat solitary, or when they went out, appeared in the habit of close mourners, according to the decent custom of the country.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 50:3)

Reformed Notable voices

“Although formerly more labor was expended on funerals, and that even without superstition, than has been deemed right subsequently to the proof given of the resurrection exhibited by Christ: yet we know that among the Egyptians there was greater expense and pomp than among the Jews. Even the ancient historians record this among the most memorable customs of that nation.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 50:2)
