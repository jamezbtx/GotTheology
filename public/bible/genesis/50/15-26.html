Passage

Genesis 50

Verses 15–26 · ESV · Voices

After Jacob’s burial, Joseph’s brothers fear that with their father gone he will finally repay the evil they did. They send a plea for forgiveness; Joseph weeps, refuses to take God’s place, and reads the sale through providence: they meant evil, God meant it for good, that many people should live. He provides for them, lives 110 years, and dies in Egypt under oath that God will visit them and that his bones will go up—hope aimed at the Exodus.

Genesis 50:15

15When Joseph’s brothers saw that their father was dead, they said, “It may be that Joseph will hate us and pay us back for all the evil that we did to him.”

Arminian

Father buried, fear speaks: maybe Joseph will hate us and pay us back. They themselves name the deed “evil.” Grief has not erased conscience.

Classical Arminians hear a real moral memory. The brothers are not puppets who only now “discover” guilt; they freely sinned and now freely dread. Fear of payback can still become a door to asking forgiveness.

Reformed

With Israel dead, they expect hatred. The old crime lives in their imagination more loudly than Joseph’s tears at the grave.

Reformed pastoral: providence did not delete their guilt. That they still call it “evil” is true. God will answer their fear with a word about His meaning—not by pretending they never meant harm.

Genesis 50:16

16So they sent a message to Joseph, saying, “Your father gave this command before he died:

Arminian

They send a message rather than come first—distance born of shame—and appeal to a father’s dying command.

Arminian note: mediation is a human choice under guilt. Whether Jacob actually said it or they borrow his name, they are seeking pardon, not plotting a new harm. Grace can meet even a nervous embassy and turn it toward peace.

Reformed

A message, a claimed paternal command. They still cannot walk straight into Joseph’s face.

Reformed reading: God can use even this anxious embassy. The sentence they put in Jacob’s mouth will become the occasion of Joseph’s tears and of the chapter’s great word about what God meant—not what they meant alone.

Genesis 50:17

17‘Say to Joseph, “Please forgive the transgression of your brothers and their sin, because they did evil to you.”’ And now, please forgive the transgression of the servants of the God of your father.” Joseph wept when they spoke to him.

Arminian

Forgive transgression, sin, evil—triple confession. Then they name themselves servants of “the God of your father.” Joseph weeps.

Classical Arminians: repentance here is verbal and costly. Forgiveness is asked, not assumed. Joseph’s tears are a free, holy response—grace in a brother’s heart, not a cold mechanism that cannot still be received.

Reformed

The vocabulary is unflinching: transgression, sin, evil. They plead the family God. Joseph weeps.

Reformed comfort: the weeping is not denial of justice but the heart God has been forming through years. Tears prepare the word that will both name their guilt and locate it under God’s good purpose.

Genesis 50:18

18His brothers also came and fell down before him and said, “Behold, we are your servants.”

Arminian

After the message, they come and fall down: “we are your servants.” The dream of sheaves is fulfilled in fear, not yet in peace.

Arminian pastoral: bowing can be panic or surrender. Joseph will treat it as a chance to lift them, not to enslave them. Human humility is real; so is the need for a word that heals rather than a new yoke.

Reformed

They fall down—Genesis 37 completed in a new key. Servants before the brother they sold.

Reformed note: God has kept the dream and the family. Their posture is of their own fear; their preservation is of the Lord’s design. Joseph will not take the throne they offer; he will speak as a brother under God.

Genesis 50:19

19But Joseph said to them, “Do not fear, for am I in the place of God?

Arminian

“Do not fear”—and the question is pastoral: am I in God’s place? Vengeance is not Joseph’s office.

Classical Arminians gladly hear a man refuse to play deity. He will not seize a final verdict on their souls; he leaves judgment with God and chooses mercy. Forgiveness here is a free, responsible act, not a forced amnesia.

Reformed

Do not fear—because he is not God. The question locates vengeance where it belongs.

Reformed accent: Joseph’s restraint is itself under God’s hand. He refuses a role that is not his, which is how a man lives inside sovereignty: not seizing the bench, not pretending evil was nothing.

Genesis 50:20

20As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today.

Arminian

Two “meant”s: they meant evil; God meant it for good—to keep many people alive. Same events, two intentions. Human malice is not rewritten as a secret virtue.

Arminian emphasis: God weaves free, even wicked, choices without coercing them. The brothers remain guilty; God remains good. Providence is not puppetry. It is the Father’s wise ordering of what agents truly willed, unto life.

Reformed

You meant evil; God meant it for good. Concurrent sovereignty: one deed, two agents, two intents. Guilt is not diluted; the saving purpose is not accidental.

Reformed confession: God did not merely salvage leftovers after the sale. He meant the whole for good—many kept alive—without making the brothers innocent. Joseph’s sentence holds both.

Key contrast Both refuse to deny either the brothers’ guilt or God’s good purpose. Arminians typically stress God weaving free (even evil) choices without coercing them; Reformed readers typically stress concurrent sovereignty—God meant it for good without making the brothers innocent.

Genesis 50:21

21So do not fear; I will provide for you and your little ones.” Thus he comforted them and spoke kindly to them.

Arminian

Fear is answered with provision and kind speech. Theology becomes groceries and tone of voice for “your little ones.”

Arminian pastoral: forgiven people still need to hear “do not fear.” Joseph’s kindness is a chosen ministry. Grace that interpreted the past now sustains households in the present; mercy has a table, not only a thesis.

Reformed

The same mouth that preached providence now pledges bread. Comfort and kind speech are the pastoral fruit of verse 20.

Reformed note: he provides because God meant life. The viceroy’s kindness is how the “good” lands on frightened households—covenant care, not a lecture only. Little ones eat because God meant it for good.

Genesis 50:22

22So Joseph remained in Egypt, he and his father’s house. Joseph lived 110 years.

Arminian

He remains in Egypt with his father’s house and lives 110 years—fullness in a foreign land. Remaining is not unbelief; it is the present assignment.

Classical Arminians: long life in Egypt is gift, not a command to settle forever. The years are time to trust the promise while still sojourning, keeping hope awake until God visits.

Reformed

Remaining and 110 years: God keeps the family where He planted them for famine and for a future going-out.

Reformed framing: Egypt is not the land of the oath, yet it is under oath. Longevity is providence storing Israel until “God will visit.” The calendar itself serves the promise.

Genesis 50:23

23And Joseph saw Ephraim’s children of the third generation. The children also of Machir the son of Manasseh were counted as Joseph’s own.

Arminian

Third generation of Ephraim; Machir’s children counted as Joseph’s own. Fruitfulness answers the pit and the prison.

Arminian hope: seeing grandchildren is ordinary mercy. God keeps a family line by births as well as by grand sentences. The promise to Abraham is already multiplying in Goshen, inviting trust for the generations still to come.

Reformed

Eyes on the third generation; Manasseh’s line gathered to Joseph (the Hebrew picture is birth at his knees). Blessing is counted.

Reformed comfort: the man sold without offspring now claims a household. God meant fruit, not only survival. Ephraim and Machir are the good made visible in flesh.

Genesis 50:24

24And Joseph said to his brothers, “I am about to die, but God will visit you and bring you up out of this land to the land that he swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob.”

Arminian

Death is near; the accent falls on God who will visit and bring them up to the sworn land. Joseph’s faith looks past his coffin to the promise.

Classical Arminians: “God will visit you” is a sincere pledge they must keep believing. The oath to the fathers is living hope; Exodus will answer those who hold it, not a land they can seize by settling in Egypt.

Reformed

I die; God will visit. The visit is the Lord’s own act—bring you up to the land He swore.

Reformed confession: the chain Abraham–Isaac–Jacob is unbreakable because God swore. Joseph’s dying word is election’s geography: not Egypt forever, but the land of the oath. Hebrews 11:22 will remember this faith.

Genesis 50:25

25Then Joseph made the sons of Israel swear, saying, “God will surely visit you, and you shall carry up my bones from here.”

Arminian

He binds them by oath: God will surely visit, and you shall carry my bones. Faith wants a body in the promised land, not a monument in Egypt.

Arminian pastoral: the oath is a means of keeping hope alive across generations. They must choose, when the visit comes, to lift what he left. Bones preach that Egypt is not home.

Reformed

“Surely visit”—certainty stacked on the verb. Bones as a down payment on Exodus.

Reformed note: Joseph locates his remains in the promise. God will act; Israel will carry. Exodus 13:19 and Joshua 24:32 keep this oath. Sovereignty does not cancel the command to pick up the coffin.

Genesis 50:26

26So Joseph died, being 110 years old. They embalmed him, and he was put in a coffin in Egypt.

Arminian

Death, embalming, a coffin in Egypt—the book ends in a box, not yet in Canaan. The last word of Genesis is unfinished on purpose.

Classical Arminians: hope is real and still future. The coffin invites a people to wait for the God who visits. Faith can die in a foreign land without surrendering the promise—or making Egypt home.

Reformed

One hundred ten years, Egyptian burial, coffin in Egypt. Genesis closes under Gentile care, with a Hebrew hope inside the wood.

Reformed comfort: the story is not stuck. “In Egypt” is the last geography so that “God will visit” can be the next. Providence that meant good in the sale will mean an exodus for these bones.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on “you meant evil, God meant it for good,” and Joseph’s bones—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 50:15–26.

Arminian Notable voices

“Ye thought evil, but God meant it unto good—In order to the making Joseph a greater blessing to his family than otherwise he could have been. Fear not, I will nourish you—See what an excellent spirit Joseph was of, and learn of him to render good for evil. He comforted them, and, to banish all their fears, he spake kindly to them.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 50:20–21)

Reformed Notable voices

“Joseph well considers the providence of God; so that he imposes it on himself as a compulsory law, not only to grant pardon, but also to exercise beneficence. So that whatever poison Satan produces, God turns it into medicine for his elect. And although in this place God is said to have “meant it unto good,” because contrary to expectation, he had educed a joyful issue out of beginnings fraught with death.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 50:20)
