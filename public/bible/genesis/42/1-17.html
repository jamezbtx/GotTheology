Passage

Genesis 42

Verses 1–17 · WEB · Voices

Famine in Canaan sends ten of Joseph’s brothers to Egypt for grain. They bow before the governor they sold, who recognizes them, remembers his dreams, speaks roughly, and holds them as alleged spies until Benjamin is named.

Genesis 42:1

1Now Jacob saw that there was grain in Egypt, and Jacob said to his sons, “Why do you look at one another?”

Arminian

Jacob breaks the stare: Why do you look at one another? Hunger has frozen them. A father’s question pushes toward action, not blame.

Grace often arrives as a summons to move. Looking at one another can be fear; going for grain is responsible love of the household.

Reformed

News of Egyptian grain reaches Jacob. Providence has already stored what Canaan lacks (ch. 41).

The family of promise stares while God has prepared bread in a foreign court. The question exposes paralysis more than information.

Genesis 42:2

2He said, “Behold, I have heard that there is grain in Egypt. Go down there, and buy for us from there, so that we may live, and not die.”

Arminian

Go down… that we may live and not die. Survival is named without shame. Seeking bread is obedience to life, not unbelief by itself.

Arminian interpreters hear a charge: sons must act. Enabling providence does not annul the duty to go and buy.

Reformed

Life and death sit in a father’s mouth. Egypt, once a threat in the Joseph story, is now the granary.

God can feed the covenant house from the very place He sent the betrayed son. “Live and not die” is His larger intent (50:20) already at work.

Genesis 42:3

3Joseph’s ten brothers went down to buy grain from Egypt.

Arminian

Ten go down. The number quietly marks who is missing—Joseph sold, Benjamin held back.

They choose the road. Buying grain is honest work; what they will meet is the brother their earlier choice put on this path.

Reformed

Ten brothers on the Egypt road: the old sin’s geography reversed.

They go to buy, but God is bringing them to Joseph. Commerce is the means; reunion under test is the purpose.

Genesis 42:4

4But Jacob didn’t send Benjamin, Joseph’s brother, with his brothers; for he said, “Lest perhaps harm happen to him.”

Arminian

Benjamin stays because Jacob fears harm—Rachel’s remaining son guarded. Fear is understandable after Joseph’s “death.”

Love can clutch. Jacob’s caution is fatherly and still short-sighted; grace will later ask him to unclench the boy.

Reformed

Joseph’s full brother is withheld. Jacob’s fear names harm he cannot see God governing.

The same God who kept Joseph in Egypt keeps Benjamin in Canaan for now. Parental fear is deeply felt; it is not the last word.

Genesis 42:5

5The sons of Israel came to buy among those who came, for the famine was in the land of Canaan.

Arminian

They come as one party among many. Famine does not single them out in the crowd—yet their story is unique.

Pastorally: need is shared; guilt is personal. Standing in a bread line does not erase the pit of chapter 37.

Reformed

Sons of Israel among the others: covenant family reduced to hungry buyers.

Canaan’s famine is the Lord’s pressure to move them toward the son they rejected. The famine is a common disaster with a particular purpose.

Genesis 42:6

6Joseph was the governor over the land. It was he who sold to all the people of the land. Joseph’s brothers came, and bowed themselves down to him with their faces to the earth.

Arminian

They bow, faces to the ground—the scene of Joseph’s dreams (37:7–10) without their knowing. Need teaches knees.

Bowing here is not yet repentance. Grace can use hunger to bring bodies low so consciences may later follow.

Reformed

Governor Joseph sells to all; his brothers bow. Dreams remembered by heaven are now acted on earth.

What God showed in youth He fulfills in famine. Their faces to the earth are providence, not accident.

Key contrast Both read the bowing as the dreams coming true. Arminians typically stress their responsible coming-to-buy that grace then uses; Reformed Christians typically emphasize God’s sure word bringing them down whether they meant worship or not.

Genesis 42:7

7Joseph saw his brothers, and he recognized them, but acted like a stranger to them, and spoke roughly with them. He said to them, “Where did you come from?” They said, “From the land of Canaan, to buy food.”

Arminian

He knows them; they get a stranger’s roughness. The question is simple; the treatment is a test, not a tantrum only.

Joseph remains a moral agent. Rough speech can serve discernment; it can also wound. Grace will show his tears soon enough (v. 24).

Reformed

Recognition without reunion: Joseph holds the knowledge they lack.

The governor’s roughness sits inside God’s larger mercy. He is not finished with these brothers; concealment is part of the proving.

Genesis 42:8

8Joseph recognized his brothers, but they didn’t recognize him.

Arminian

One-way recognition. Twenty years, Egyptian dress, and a throne hide the boy from the pit.

We often fail to see those we have wronged, even when they feed us. Grace still offers a later unveiling if they will face the truth.

Reformed

He knows; they do not. Asymmetry of knowledge is the engine of the chapter.

God can hide a brother in plain sight until the hour of testing is ripe. Revelation is timed.

Genesis 42:9

9Joseph remembered the dreams which he dreamed about them, and said to them, “You are spies! You have come to see the nakedness of the land.”

Arminian

He remembers the dreams, then names them spies. Memory of God’s word and a hard charge arrive together.

Classical Arminians: remembering providence should not become cruelty. The spy-charge is a probe of honesty they once lacked. They remain free to tell the truth about the family.

Reformed

Dreams remembered: Joseph interprets the bowing. The spy accusation presses them into speech about home and brothers.

God brings His word to Joseph’s mind at the right moment. The test is severe; the purpose is the family, not random harm.

Genesis 42:10

10They said to him, “No, my lord, but your servants have come to buy food.

Arminian

No, my lord—deference and a true errand: food. They speak as servants to the man they once sold as a slave.

Honest hunger sits beside an older dishonesty. Grace can start with a true sentence (“we came to buy”) on the way to a deeper confession.

Reformed

Servants buying food: the stated mission is not imaginary.

God uses a true need as the cover under which He will surface the true past.

Genesis 42:11

11We are all one man’s sons; we are honest men. Your servants are not spies.”

Arminian

Sons of one man—true. Honest men—contested. They have never been spies, but they have been brothers who lied to a father.

“Honest” is a claim they must yet live. Grace exposes the gap between family identity and family history so they can repent, not merely protest.

Reformed

One father, honest men, never spies. Two claims are solid; “honest” is the wound.

God will test the word they volunteer. Covenant sons can still be self-deceived about honesty.

Genesis 42:12

12He said to them, “No, but you have come to see the nakedness of the land!”

Arminian

He repeats the charge. Pressure continues until more of the family story comes out.

Repeated questioning can be mercy if it aims at truth. They are not forced to lie; they can speak of the twelve.

Reformed

Nakedness of the land: a governor’s security language. Joseph keeps the role that hides the brother.

The extra pressure is the means by which Benjamin and “one is no more” will be named.

Genesis 42:13

13They said, “We, your servants, are twelve brothers, the sons of one man in the land of Canaan; and behold, the youngest is today with our father, and one is no more.”

Arminian

Twelve, youngest at home, one is no more. They tell the shape of the family and bury Joseph in a clause.

“No more” is the story they sold Jacob. Conscience has not yet said “we sold him.” Grace still waits for a fuller truth (v. 21).

Reformed

The missing one is mentioned as gone. Benjamin is located with the father.

God draws the whole household onto the table. Even their half-confession serves the path to reunion.

Key contrast Both hear the cost of “one is no more.” Arminians emphasize their responsible, still-partial truth-telling; Reformed readers press providence forcing the family facts into the open.

Genesis 42:14

14Joseph said to them, “It is like I told you, saying, ‘You are spies!’

Arminian

He does not yet drop the charge. The test holds.

Joseph chooses not to unveil. That choice is human and weighty; it will include tears. Testing must not become payback for its own sake.

Reformed

Spies still: the role continues. Knowledge stays with Joseph.

Delay is not denial of mercy. The governor’s word keeps them in the proving God is using.

Genesis 42:15

15By this you shall be tested. By the life of Pharaoh, you shall not go out from here, unless your youngest brother comes here.

Arminian

The test: Benjamin must come. Joseph will see whether they treat Rachel’s other son as they treated him.

Joseph sets the brothers a moral test. Will they sacrifice the youngest again, or keep faith with father and boy? Grace invites a different brotherhood.

Reformed

By Pharaoh’s life, Benjamin is required. The test is crafted around the one Jacob fears to send.

God aims the proving at the old wound—the favored remaining son. What they do with Benjamin will show what the years have done.

Genesis 42:16

16Send one of you, and let him get your brother, and you shall be bound, that your words may be tested, whether there is truth in you, or else by the life of Pharaoh surely you are spies.”

Arminian

One goes, the rest confined—truth in you is the issue. Their words about family are put on trial.

Classical Arminians: truth is a responsible act. They can send, stay, speak, or scheme. The test assumes they are answerable, not puppets.

Reformed

Confinement until a brother appears: words weighed by deed.

“Whether there is truth in you” is the spiritual question under the civil one. God is after truth in the inward parts, using a governor’s terms.

Genesis 42:17

17He put them all together into custody for three days.

Arminian

Time to think in the kind of confinement they once gave him.

Pastorally: pauses can be mercy. Grace often gives space to remember a brother’s distress before the next choice.

Reformed

The narrator lets the number echo other biblical threes of turning.

Comfort: even custody is timed. God limits the cell as surely as He limited the pit and the prison Joseph knew.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on the brothers bowing, and Joseph remembering his dreams, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“The laying up of God's oracles in our hearts will be of excellent use to us in all our conduct. Joseph had an eye to his dreams, which he knew to be divine, in his carriage towards his brethren, and aimed at the accomplishment of them, and the bringing his brethren to repentance; and both those points were gained. He shewed himself harsh with them: the very manner of his speaking, considering the post he was in, was enough to frighten them, for he spake roughly to them—He charged them with ill designs against the government, treated them as dangerous persons, ye are spies, protesting by the life of Pharaoh that they were so.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 42:9)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses begins, in this chapter, to treat of the occasion which drew Jacob with his whole family into Egypt; and thus leaves it to us to consider by what hidden and unexpected methods God may perform whatever he has decreed. Though, therefore, the providence of God is in itself a labyrinth; yet when we connect the issue of things with their beginnings, that admirable method of operation shines clearly in our view, which is not generally acknowledged, only because it is far removed from our observation.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 42:1)
