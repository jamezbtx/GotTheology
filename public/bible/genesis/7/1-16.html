Passage

Genesis 7

Verses 1–16 · WEB · Voices

The LORD commands Noah to go into the ark with his household, for He has seen him righteous in this generation, and to take clean and unclean animals and birds so that offspring will live. In seven days rain will fall forty days and nights and blot out every living thing. Noah obeys. At six hundred years he and his family enter; after seven days the flood comes. On a dated day the fountains of the deep burst and the windows of heaven open; they enter two by two of all flesh, and the LORD shuts him in.

Genesis 7:1

1The LORD said to Noah, “Come with all of your household into the ship, for I have seen your righteousness before me in this generation.

Arminian

The LORD speaks a saving command: come into the ship, you and all your household. The reason is personal and moral—He has seen that Noah is righteous before Him in this generation.

Arminian interpreters hear God recognizing Noah’s faith-righteousness, not a self-made résumé. Grace that can be received meets a man who has walked with God; the invitation into the ark is sincere, and the household is summoned to enter by trust.

Reformed

The LORD’s word opens the door of the ark: Noah and his household are commanded in because God has seen him righteous in this generation. Judgment is coming; preservation is already spoken.

Reformed readers hear decree as well as command. God has determined both to judge the wicked world and to save this household; the word that sends Noah in is not a bare suggestion but the effectual summons of the God who preserves a remnant.

Genesis 7:2

2You shall take seven pairs of every clean animal with you, the male and his female. Of the animals that are not clean, take two, the male and his female.

Arminian

Clean animals come in seven pairs, unclean in a pair—male and mate. Preservation is detailed, not vague. God cares that life continue beyond the flood.

The same God who judges also provides means of life. Noah must take what God names; obedience is the appointed way to receive preserving grace, not a substitute for it.

Reformed

Seven pairs of the clean, a pair of the unclean: the LORD specifies what will live. Remnant language is already animal as well as human.

Preservation is as particular as judgment. God decrees which life will pass through the waters and commands the means—pairs, mates, an ark—by which His purpose stands.

Genesis 7:3

3Also of the birds of the sky, seven and seven, male and female, to keep seed alive on the surface of all the earth.

Arminian

Birds too, seven pairs, male and female, so that offspring remain alive on the surface of all the earth. The aim is life after judgment, not mere survival of Noah’s family.

Arminians gladly confess God’s will to keep a living world. The command invites Noah’s cooperation: take them, keep them, so that grace received becomes life multiplied on the earth again.

Reformed

The birds of the heavens are numbered into the remnant so their offspring will live on the earth. Creation’s future is in God’s hands, not chance.

God will not let His made world end in total blot. He appoints the pairs and the purpose; what He intends to keep alive He commands into the ark.

Genesis 7:4

4In seven days, I will cause it to rain on the earth for forty days and forty nights. I will destroy every living thing that I have made from the surface of the ground.”

Arminian

Seven days remain, then rain forty days and nights. I will destroy every living thing God has made will be blotted from the ground. The warning is timed, public in its delay, and terrible in its scope.

Those seven days are sincere space to take the warning. God is patient without being indecisive; judgment of wickedness is no empty threat, and the window to enter is genuine, not theatrical.

Reformed

The LORD announces both the timetable and the outcome: rain, forty days and nights, blotting out of every living thing He has made. Wickedness will not go unjudged.

God decrees the flood as surely as He decrees the remnant. The seven days do not make the sentence uncertain; they display a sovereign schedule in which patience and judgment both belong to Him.

Genesis 7:5

5Noah did everything that the LORD commanded him.

Arminian

Noah did everything that the LORD commanded him. The narrator pauses on obedience—not a hint, not a partial gesture, but all.

Noah’s obedience is not illusory. Faith-righteousness shows itself in doing what God said. Grace that can be received is not opposed to works of trust; the man who believes goes into the ark as commanded.

Reformed

Noah did everything that the LORD commanded. The flood story is never only about water; it is about a word that produces the obedience it requires.

The command is effectual. God who called Noah righteous also brought forth complete obedience—not to erase Noah’s agency, but to show that preserving grace actually moves the remnant to do what He said.

Genesis 7:6

6Noah was six hundred years old when the flood of waters came on the earth.

Arminian

Noah is six hundred years old when the flood of waters comes upon the earth. Salvation and judgment arrive in ordinary dated life, not in mythic haze.

Wesleyan Arminians hear a man who has long walked with God now meeting the hour he was warned of. Age does not excuse delay; when God has spoken, the righteous still enter by obedient trust.

Reformed

The flood is tied to Noah’s six hundredth year—history under God’s hand, not accident. The remnant’s life and the world’s judgment share a calendar.

God appoints the time. The same decree that numbered Noah’s years numbered the hour when waters would come; providence is precise when it both judges and saves.

Genesis 7:7

7Noah went into the ship with his sons, his wife, and his sons’ wives, because of the floodwaters.

Arminian

Noah, his sons, his wife, and his sons’ wives go into the ark to escape the flood. The household enters together; the purpose is rescue from waters already announced.

The household enters in obedient trust. They are not dragged as props; they go in to escape, receiving the grace of the ark by doing what the LORD said.

Reformed

The whole household goes in—sons, wife, sons’ wives—to escape the waters. God saves a family inside a judged world.

God has decreed the saving of this household. Their entrance is the outworking of His purpose to preserve a remnant; escape is gift before it is strategy.

Genesis 7:8

8Clean animals, unclean animals, birds, and everything that creeps on the ground

Arminian

Clean and unclean animals, birds, and everything that creeps on the ground are named again. The remnant includes the humble crawler as well as the clean beast.

God sincerely means to keep life on the earth. Creatures come as commanded; Noah’s care for them is part of receiving the preserving word, not a footnote to it.

Reformed

Every class of land life is gathered: clean, unclean, bird, creeper. Nothing God intends to keep is left to chance inventory.

God preserves life in particular, kind by kind. The God who will blot out life also specifies which kinds will live; remnant mercy is as ordered as judgment is total for those outside.

Genesis 7:9

9went by pairs to Noah into the ship, male and female, as God commanded Noah.

Arminian

Two and two, male and female, they go into the ark with Noah, as God commanded Noah. Pairing for life and obedience to the command stand together.

Classical Arminians see creatures and household moving at God’s word, yet the obedience is true. Grace is received in the going in; the command is not a script that erases the walk into the door.

Reformed

They enter two and two with Noah, “as God commanded Noah.” The narrator staples the scene to the command.

What God commanded, God brought to pass. Male and female, pair by pair, the remnant is assembled because the word of God is effectual, not merely advisory.

Genesis 7:10

10After the seven days, the floodwaters came on the earth.

Arminian

After the seven days, the floodwaters come on the earth. The pause ends; patience was not in doubt, and so is the arrival of judgment.

Those seven days were sincere space to take the warning. When the waters come, no one can say God rushed; wickedness is judged after an interval of mercy.

Reformed

After seven days the floodwaters come—exactly as spoken. Delay was not uncertainty.

God decreed both the wait and the waters. Patience and judgment are two hands of one purpose; the remnant is already inside when the sentence falls.

Genesis 7:11

11In the six hundredth year of Noah’s life, in the second month, on the seventeenth day of the month, on that day all the fountains of the great deep burst open, and the sky’s windows opened.

Arminian

The date is exact: Noah’s six hundredth year, second month, seventeenth day. Judgment is cosmic, from below and above.

Arminians do not soften this. God judges wickedness with created powers He commands. The dated hour underlines that the warning was history, not metaphor, and the ark was the appointed place to receive mercy.

Reformed

A timestamped day: fountains of the great deep burst, windows of the heavens opened. Earth and sky answer their Maker.

Reformed comfort and trembling together: nothing here is random weather. God decrees the moment the deep breaks and the heavens open; the same sovereignty that times the flood has already seated the remnant in the ark.

Genesis 7:12

12It rained on the earth forty days and forty nights.

Arminian

Rain falls forty days and forty nights—the duration already named in the warning. Judgment lasts as long as God said, not as long as human panic lasts.

The word was true in its patience and true in its storm. Those who received the warning by entering are kept; the rain does not negotiate with unbelief.

Reformed

Forty days and forty nights of rain fulfill the announced sentence. The remnant’s safety and the world’s blotting run on the same clock.

God performs what He decreed. The rain is not nature slipping the leash; it is the LORD carrying out judgment while His appointed household remains under the covering He provided.

Genesis 7:13

13In the same day Noah, and Shem, Ham, and Japheth—the sons of Noah—and Noah’s wife and the three wives of his sons with them, entered into the ship—

Arminian

In the same day Noah, Shem, Ham, Japheth, Noah’s wife, and the three wives of his sons enter the ark. Eight names, one day, one door.

The household enters in obedient trust, listed as persons, not as cargo. Grace that can be received is received by going in when God says the day has come.

Reformed

The very same day—the day the deep bursts—the named household enters. Shem, Ham, Japheth, and the four wives are not afterthoughts.

God decrees the saving of this household down to the roster. Their entrance on that day is covenant particularity: a remnant preserved by name while the world is judged.

Genesis 7:14

14they, and every animal after its kind, all the livestock after their kind, every creeping thing that creeps on the earth after its kind, and every bird after its kind, every bird of every sort.

Arminian

Beasts, livestock, creeping things, birds, every winged creature—each according to its kind—enter with them. Kinds are kept so the earth can be filled again.

The verse presses God’s goodness to creation alongside His judgment of human wickedness. The ark is a means of grace for a living world; Noah’s obedience serves life God intends to keep.

Reformed

Every kind is catalogued into safety: beast, livestock, creeper, bird, winged creature. The remnant is ordered by the Creator’s categories.

God will have a populated earth because He has so determined. Kinds enter because His decree to preserve is as meticulous as His decree to judge.

Genesis 7:15

15Pairs from all flesh with the breath of life in them went into the ship to Noah.

Arminian

They go into the ship to Noah by pairs, of all flesh with the breath of life in it. Breath, pairing, and accompaniment with Noah mark living creatures under mercy.

Life is welcomed in, not merely theoretically offered. The breath of life is preserved where the command is trusted; grace is received by entering with the righteous man God has appointed.

Reformed

All flesh with the breath of life enters two and two with Noah. The ark gathers what God will not blot out.

Breath continues because God wills a remnant of living flesh. The pairing and the going in with Noah are the effectual outworking of that will, not a lucky stampede.

Genesis 7:16

16Those who went in, went in male and female of all flesh, as God commanded him; then the LORD shut him in.

Arminian

Those who went in, went in male and female of all flesh, as God commanded him. Then the LORD shut him in. Obedience fills the ark; God closes the door.

Arminian readers hold both lines: they truly went in as commanded, and the LORD truly shut them in. Grace that can be received is received in the going; the shut door is mercy for those inside and the end of the sincere space to take the warning.

Reformed

They went in as God commanded, then the LORD shut him in. The last verb is the LORD’s: sealing, not Noah’s latch.

Shutting him in is sovereign sealing. God decreed judgment and the saving of this household; the closed door is His own hand, effectual protection for the remnant and the appointed close of the day of entry.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on the call into the ark and the LORD shutting them in, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Here are necessary orders given concerning the brute creatures that they were to be preserved alive with Noah in the ark. He must carefully preserve every species, that no tribe, no, not the least considerable, might entirely perish out of the creation. Observe in this: God's care for man.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 7:2)

Reformed Notable voices

“I have no doubt that Noah was confirmed, as he certainly needed to be, by oracles frequently repeated. He had already sustained, during one hundred years, the greatest and most furious assaults; and the invincible combatant had achieved memorable victories; but the most severe contest of all was, to bid farewell to the world, to renounce society and to bury himself in the ark.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 7:1)
