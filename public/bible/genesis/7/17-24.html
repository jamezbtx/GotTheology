Passage

Genesis 7

Verses 17–24 · ESV · Voices

Judgment falls as the Lord had said: waters increase forty days, lift the ark high above the earth, and cover even the high mountains. All flesh that moved on dry land dies—everything with the breath of life. God blots out every living thing on the ground; only Noah and those with him in the ark are left, and the waters prevail 150 days.

Genesis 7:17

17The flood continued forty days on the earth. The waters increased and bore up the ark, and it rose high above the earth.

Arminian

The forty days unfold as God had spoken. Waters increase in judgment, yet they also bear up the ark so that it rises high above the earth—mercy riding the same flood.

Classical Arminians hear a warning fulfilled, not a surprise. This generation had received Noah’s preaching of righteousness and refused it (ch. 6); those who entered the ark God provided are lifted by the waters that judge the rest. The refuge was real; obedient faith took the way of escape.

Reformed

Forty days keep the word God had spoken. The waters increase and, by the same motion, bear up the ark until it rises high above the earth.

Reformed readers confess judgment and preservation as one decreed act. God both judges the world He said He would judge and lifts the vessel He appointed. The ark’s rising is particular mercy in the midst of wrath.

Genesis 7:18

18The waters prevailed and increased greatly on the earth, and the ark floated on the face of the waters.

Arminian

The refrain deepens: waters prevail and increase greatly, yet the ark floats on their face. Mercy does not cancel the flood; it carries a remnant through it.

Arminian pastoral reading: God provided a real refuge and called Noah’s household to enter. Those who trusted the word and went in are borne on the waters; those who refused the offered way of escape are not. The floating ark is grace received, not grace forced.

Reformed

Prevailing waters and a floating ark belong to one providence. The earth is overwhelmed; the vessel God designed rides the face of the waters.

Reformed comfort: preservation is as particular as the judgment is total outside the ark. The Lord who increased the flood also keeps those He shut in (7:16). The ark floats because God decreed a remnant and means to save it.

Genesis 7:19

19And the waters prevailed so mightily on the earth that all the high mountains under the whole heaven were covered.

Arminian

The narrator piles up the might of the waters until even the high mountains under the whole heaven are covered. There is no high ground left for those who would save themselves.

Classical Arminians take the covering as moral as well as geographic: every human refuge fails when God’s word of judgment is fulfilled. The generation’s ruin is not for lack of warning but for refusing grace already offered. Safety was in the ark God named, not on a peak of one’s own.

Reformed

Mighty waters cover all the high mountains under the whole heaven. The flood is no local inconvenience; it answers God’s global verdict on flesh.

Reformed confession: when God decrees to judge, no elevation of human strength or geography withstands Him. Covering the mountains displays a sovereignty as wide as the word that announced the flood. Mercy, too, is located where God placed it—in the ark, not on the hills.

Genesis 7:20

20The waters prevailed above the mountains, covering them fifteen cubits deep.

Arminian

Fifteen cubits above the mountains leaves no ambiguity: the covering is complete, measured, and sufficient. Judgment is not theatrical; it is thorough.

Arminian emphasis: the precision underlines that God does what He says. Those outside had been summoned by a preacher of righteousness; they would not come. Depth of water answers depth of refusal. Yet the same measurement that drowns every other height still carries the ark God provided for those who entered in faith.

Reformed

Cubit-language makes the covering exact: waters prevail above the mountains, fifteen cubits deep. God does not guess at judgment.

Reformed instinct: measured wrath is still wrath, and it is God’s. The flood’s depth is as appointed as the ark’s dimensions in chapter 6. Particular mercy sits in the vessel designed to ride this very depth; those preserved are preserved by the Lord’s decree, not by finding a taller mountain.

Genesis 7:21

21And all flesh died that moved on the earth, birds, livestock, beasts, all swarming creatures that swarm on the earth, and all mankind.

Arminian

The catalog is grievous: birds, livestock, beasts, swarming creatures, and all mankind. All flesh that moved on the earth dies. Scripture will not let us hurry past the cost of sin.

Classical Arminians refuse both sentimentality and glee. This death is the fruit of a generation that refused grace already offered; it is also the holy sorrow of a world that would not repent under Noah’s preaching. God is not eager to destroy; He is faithful to His word when warning is spurned. The ark remains the mercy standing in the midst of this ruin.

Reformed

All flesh that moved on the earth dies—the list is exhaustive so that no creaturely class is imagined exempt. Mankind is named last, as the image-bearers whose violence filled the earth (6:11–13).

Reformed gravity: blotting out is a decreed act, not a tragedy that escaped God’s hand. He judges what He said He would judge. Pastoral honesty does not soften the verb “died,” nor does it paint God as cruel; it confesses holy justice and, in the next verses, particular mercy for those in the ark.

Genesis 7:22

22Everything on the dry land in whose nostrils was the breath of life died.

Arminian

The breath of life—God’s own gift in creation (2:7)—is here withdrawn from everything on dry land. What God breathed, sin has now forfeited.

Arminian pastoral note: life was gift, and the warning was gift. This generation did not lack a herald of righteousness; it lacked a willing heart. Death of the breathing is the end of refused grace, not the denial that grace had been sincerely set before them. Those who live are those who took refuge where God said to take it.

Reformed

“Breath of life” recalls Eden even as it records death. Everything on dry land that breathed dies—creation’s gift reversed under judgment.

Reformed reading: the Lord who gives breath is Lord to take it according to His righteous decree. This is not chaos; it is the fulfillment of 6:17. The same God will keep breath in those He placed in the ark. Particular mercy does not make the deaths less real; it makes the remnant a work of God, not of chance.

Genesis 7:23

23He blotted out every living thing that was on the face of the ground, man and animals and creeping things and birds of the heavens. They were blotted out from the earth. Only Noah was left, and those who were with him in the ark.

Arminian

“He blotted out” is God’s own act; “only Noah was left, and those who were with him in the ark” is God’s own mercy. Wrath and refuge stand in one sentence. Grace actually saves those in the ark—not a bare possibility, but people kept through the flood.

Classical Arminians stress how they got there: they entered in obedient faith (7:1, 5, 7). The remnant is left by faith-union with the ark God provided, after a generation refused the preaching of righteousness already offered. God saves truly; He does not save apart from the faith He enabled or the refuge He named.

Reformed

God blots out every living thing on the face of the ground; they are blotted out from the earth. Then the gospel-turn of the verse: only Noah was left, and those with him in the ark. Both blotting out and remnant are God’s decreed acts.

Reformed confession: grace here actually saves those in the ark. God appointed this household and kept them; preservation is particular mercy, not a general provision equally effectual for all. “Only Noah was left” is the Lord’s doing—He shut them in, He carried them through, He left them when He left no one else.

Genesis 7:24

24And the waters prevailed on the earth 150 days.

Arminian

Forty days of increase give way to 150 days of prevailing. Judgment is not a brief scare; it lasts until God says it is enough. The ark must keep bearing its people through a long prevailing.

Arminian pastoral close: those inside remain because they remain in the refuge God provided, waiting on His timing. The generation outside had had its time of grace and refused it. For the remnant, faith is not a moment only but continued trust in the God who both judges and saves. Mercy in the midst of wrath still has to be inhabited until the waters recede.

Reformed

The waters prevail 150 days—judgment has duration under God’s hand, not a spasm of anger that spends itself. He governs how long wrath rests on the earth.

Reformed assurance: the same decree that set the flood’s length keeps the remnant for all 150 days. Particular mercy is not fragile. Those God left in the ark He keeps while the waters prevail; the story will turn when He remembers Noah (8:1), because His purpose to save this people is as sure as His purpose to judge the world.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the flood covering the mountains and the death of all flesh—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 7:17–24.

Arminian Notable voices

“All flesh died, all in whose nostrils was the breath of life, of all that was on the dry land, every living substance—And why so? Man only had done wickedly, and justly is God's hand against him, but these sheep what have they done? We are sure God did them no wrong.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 7:21)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses copiously insists upon this fact, in order to show that the whole world was immersed in the waters. Moreover, it is to be regarded as the special design of this narrations that we should not ascribe to fortune, the flood by which the world perished; how ever customary it may be for men to cast some veil over the works of God, which may obscure either his goodness or his judgments manifested in them.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 7:17)
