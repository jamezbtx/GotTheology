Passage

Genesis 23

Verses 1–20 · WEB · Voices

Sarah dies at Hebron. Abraham mourns, then as a sojourner buys the cave of Machpelah from Ephron the Hittite at full price, in public, as a burying place in the land of Canaan—the first owned plot in the promised land.

Genesis 23:1

1Sarah lived one hundred twenty-seven years. This was the length of Sarah’s life.

Arminian

The narrator honors Sarah’s full span. Promise has not yet given her a homeland grave, only a counted life under God’s care.

Those in Wesley’s line hear a completed pilgrimage: grace sustained her years; her story still calls for trust in the God who keeps time and people.

Reformed

One hundred twenty-seven years are recorded as gift, not accident. Sarah’s life is bounded by the God who numbered it.

The mother of the promised seed dies under the same providence that opened her womb. Years belong to the Lord who writes them.

Genesis 23:2

2Sarah died in Kiriath Arba (also called Hebron), in the land of Canaan. Abraham came to mourn for Sarah, and to weep for her.

Arminian

She dies in Canaan, the land of promise, not yet possessed. Abraham’s mourning and weeping are holy; faith does not skip grief.

For the hearer: grace enables honest tears. The sojourner still loves; the covenant does not void the cost of death.

Reformed

Death in Canaan is geographically loaded: she dies inside the promised map, without a deed in hand. Abraham weeps as a husband, not a stoic emblem.

Providence includes the grave. The line of promise passes through mourning; God is not embarrassed by Abraham’s tears.

Genesis 23:3

3Abraham rose up from before his dead and spoke to the children of Heth, saying,

Arminian

He rises from grief into responsible action. Faith does not freeze at the body; it seeks a place to bury with dignity.

Grace does not replace Abraham’s own action here. Abraham must speak, negotiate, and act among neighbors who do not share his hope.

Reformed

Rising from before his dead is the next obedience. Grief has a time; then the patriarch steps into the public square.

God will secure a plot, yet He uses Abraham’s rising and speaking. Providence does not skip human means.

Genesis 23:4

4“I am a stranger and a foreigner living with you. Give me a possession of a burying-place with you, that I may bury my dead out of my sight.”

Arminian

He confesses status: sojourner and foreigner, even in the land God pledged. He asks for a burying place—faith seeking a foothold, not a seized city.

Listen here for Hebrews 11 already: he lives by promise, not by sight. The request is honest; grace does not pretend he already owns Canaan.

Reformed

Sojourner language is covenant truth: the land is promised, not yet inherited. Burial property is the first legal ask of the elect line in Canaan.

God will answer this request by providence. Abraham’s confession of foreignness is the posture of those who wait on a word God has sworn.

Key contrast Both honor Abraham as a sojourner who still acts. Arminians underline grace-enabled, responsible asking; Reformed interpreters stress God securing the first plot for the promised line.

Genesis 23:5

5The children of Heth answered Abraham, saying to him,

Arminian

Neighbors reply. The promise will move through ordinary civic speech, not a miracle deed dropped from heaven.

God often works through human conversation. Abraham must still listen well and answer well.

Reformed

The Hittites’ answer is part of the means. God can govern a city gate as surely as a battlefield.

Comfort: even pagan courtesy sits inside providence. The next words will open a path for the burying place.

Genesis 23:6

6“Hear us, my lord. You are a prince of God among us. Bury your dead in the best of our tombs. None of us will withhold from you his tomb. Bury your dead.”

Arminian

They honor him as a prince of God and offer tombs freely. Courtesy is meant seriously; a gift-tomb would still leave Abraham a guest, not an owner.

Abraham must discern. Grace-enabled wisdom refuses a favor that would keep him landless in the land of promise.

Reformed

Recognition of Abraham as “prince of God” is striking from Hittite lips. Offer of choicest tombs sounds generous and would keep title with them.

God grants public honor, yet the patriarch will not settle for borrowed space. The promise needs a possessed grave, not a borrowed shelf.

Genesis 23:7

7Abraham rose up, and bowed himself to the people of the land, to the children of Heth.

Arminian

He bows. Grief does not excuse rudeness. Sojourning faith treats “the people of the land” with honor.

Enabled grace produces courtesy. The man of promise does not grasp by contempt.

Reformed

Bowing is public respect before the people of the land. The heir of promise is not yet lord of the soil.

Humility here is fitting under providence. God will give title; Abraham gives honor first.

Genesis 23:8

8He talked with them, saying, “If you agree that I should bury my dead out of my sight, hear me, and entreat for me to Ephron the son of Zohar,

Arminian

He specifies a person: Ephron son of Zohar. Faith gets concrete. He asks their mediation, not a private grab.

Arminians see responsible negotiation: if they are willing, then hear and entreat. Grace does not skip consent and process.

Reformed

Named request: Ephron, son of Zohar. The burying place will have a legal owner on the other side of the table.

God channels the grant through a known man and a known city. Particular providence wears a neighbor’s name.

Genesis 23:9

9that he may sell me the cave of Machpelah, which he has, which is in the end of his field. For the full price let him sell it to me among you as a possession for a burial place.”

Arminian

Abraham wants a deed, not a favor. He will pay.

Honest dealing is grace-shaped righteousness. Promise does not license cheap burial or hidden bargains.

Reformed

Full price, public presence, property: Abraham refuses a gift that would leave title cloudy. Machpelah must be made over, not loaned.

God is securing the first owned plot in Canaan. The cave at the field’s end is small, and it is a foothold of sworn land.

Genesis 23:10

10Now Ephron was sitting in the middle of the children of Heth. Ephron the Hittite answered Abraham in the hearing of the children of Heth, even of all who went in at the gate of his city, saying,

Arminian

Ephron is already sitting in the gate—public court. The deal will be witnessed by those who enter the city.

Faith is willing to be overheard. Integrity seeks the gate, not a whispered side-bargain.

Reformed

City-gate hearing makes the transaction civic and binding. All who go in are the audience God appoints.

Providence stages the sale in the open. The promised line’s first acre will have witnesses.

Genesis 23:11

11“No, my lord, hear me. I give you the field, and I give you the cave that is in it. In the presence of the children of my people I give it to you. Bury your dead.”

Arminian

Triple “I give” sounds lavish. In the culture of the gate, such speech often sets up a named price. Abraham must still pay if he wants clear title.

Do not confuse ceremony with conveyance. Grace-enabled prudence hears the offer and still insists on purchase.

Reformed

Public gift-language is the opening move. Field and cave are named; “bury your dead” presses haste on a mourner.

God is not mocked by Near Eastern courtesy. He will have Abraham own what is “given” only when silver changes hands.

Genesis 23:12

12Abraham bowed himself down before the people of the land.

Arminian

He bows again. Courtesy continues while he refuses a gift-trap. Honor and firmness can share a spine.

For the hearer: enabled faith is not rude when it insists. Bowing does not mean yielding the need for a paid deed.

Reformed

A second bow keeps the patriarch low before the people of the land. Title will come without swagger.

God can lift Abraham’s legal standing while Abraham stays bowed. Humility and possession are not rivals here.

Genesis 23:13

13He spoke to Ephron in the audience of the people of the land, saying, “But if you will, please hear me. I will give the price of the field. Take it from me, and I will bury my dead there.”

Arminian

He will give the price of the whole field, not a free cave. “Take it from me” is an earnest offer in public. Burial waits on a clean purchase.

Classical Arminians hear responsible love: he will not bury Sarah on contested ground. Grace funds integrity under pressure.

Reformed

Abraham names payment in the hearing of the land. The field’s price is the condition of burial.

God moves the sojourner to buy what promise had already assigned in another register. Silver serves the oath.

Genesis 23:14

14Ephron answered Abraham, saying to him,

Arminian

The negotiation is not over. Abraham must keep listening until the sum is public.

Patience is part of faith’s work among neighbors. Grace does not skip the last sentence of a deal.

Reformed

The Hittite still speaks. Providence has not finished the price.

God uses Ephron’s next words to fix a number the gate can remember.

Genesis 23:15

15“My lord, listen to me. What is a piece of land worth four hundred shekels of silver between me and you? Therefore bury your dead.”

Arminian

Therefore?” The sum is not small; the shrug is sales talk. Abraham hears the number, not the minimization.

Gracious dealing still counts cost. Faith does not haggle Sarah’s grave into shame, nor pretend the price is nothing.

Reformed

The stated worth is four hundred shekels of silver. Gift-language now has a figure. “Bury your dead” again hurries grief toward close.

God lets the price be named in public. The first Canaanite acre of the promise will cost what the gate heard, not a private discount.

Genesis 23:16

16Abraham listened to Ephron. Abraham weighed to Ephron the silver which he had named in the hearing of the children of Heth, four hundred shekels of silver, according to the current merchants’ standard.

Arminian

He listens and weighs the named silver, merchant-weight, in Hittite hearing. Abraham does not haggle. Sarah will lie in a bought cave.

Grace-enabled integrity pays what was spoken. Sojourning faith keeps its word in the marketplace.

Reformed

He weighs exactly what Ephron named, current merchant weights, before witnesses. The transaction is complete in silver.

God provides the means and the moment. The promised line’s first deed is purchased in the open, down to the shekel.

Key contrast Both honor the full, public payment. Arminians emphasize Abraham’s responsible, grace-enabled integrity; Calvinists stress providence securing a legal foothold for the covenant line.

Genesis 23:17

17So the field of Ephron, which was in Machpelah, which was before Mamre, the field, the cave which was in it, and all the trees that were in the field, that were in all of its borders, were deeded

Arminian

Field, cave, trees, whole area—survey language. What Abraham asked as a burying place becomes a bounded property.

God sincerely keeps giving the land in stages Abraham can receive. This plot is small and authentic; faith thanks God for a cave before a country.

Reformed

Legal description: Machpelah east of Mamre, field, cave, trees, whole area, made over. Title language is theology here.

The God who promised the land now has a surveyed firstfruits in Canaan. Trees and boundaries preach that the oath takes earth.

Genesis 23:18

18to Abraham for a possession in the presence of the children of Heth, before all who went in at the gate of his city.

Arminian

The sojourner now holds a deed his neighbors cannot honestly deny.

Classical Arminians see faith’s public fruit: Abraham acted; God granted. The gate’s witness serves the promise without replacing trust.

Reformed

“As a possession” is the key clause. Witnesses are the city’s traffic, not a private circle.

God gives Abraham standing among the people of the land. The gate confirms what the covenant had already spoken in heaven’s court.

Genesis 23:19

19After this, Abraham buried Sarah his wife in the cave of the field of Machpelah before Mamre (that is, Hebron), in the land of Canaan.

Arminian

“After this”—after the deed—he buries Sarah his wife. Love, grief, and promise meet in a cave at Hebron in Canaan.

The sojourner lays her down in hope. Grace taught him to wait for a rightful grave; resurrection hope later names this same land as not the last word.

Reformed

Burial follows title. Sarah is laid in Machpelah, east of Mamre, Hebron, Canaan—four geographic nails.

The mother of the line sleeps in promised soil. God will gather Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Leah here too. The cave is an earnest of more land, and of life God still owes His dead.

Genesis 23:20

20The field, and the cave that is in it, were deeded to Abraham by the children of Heth as a possession for a burial place.

Arminian

The chapter ends on legal refrain: made over as a possession for a burial place. Promise has a grave-deed in Canaan, received by faithful dealing.

God sincerely pledged land; Abraham, enabled by grace, bought a corner in integrity. The rest of the map still calls for trust.

Reformed

Closing stamp: Hittites make it over as burying property. Human conveyance serves divine grant.

The elect line now owns a tomb in the land of promise. God kept a foothold. Hebron’s cave waits for more of the family—and for the God who raises the dead.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Sarah’s death and the purchased cave at Machpelah: older Arminian and Reformed writers, each in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“I am a stranger and a sojourner with you—Therefore I am unprovided, and must become a suiter to you for a burying-place. This was one occasion which Abraham took to confess that he was a stranger and a pilgrim upon earth. The death of our relations should effectually mind us that we are not at home in this world.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 23:4)

Reformed Notable voices

“It appears from Joshua 15:54 , that this was the more ancient name of the city, which afterwards began to be called Hebron. But there is a difference of opinion respecting the etymology. Some think the name is derived from the fact, that the city consisted of four parts; as the Greeks call the city divided into three orders, Tripoli, and a given region, Decapolis, from the ten cities it contained.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 23:2)
