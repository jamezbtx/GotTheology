Passage

Genesis 33

Verses 1–20 · ESV

After Peniel, Jacob lifts his eyes and Esau comes with four hundred men. Jacob arranges his household, bows seven times, and finds not a sword but an embrace. Gifts are pressed and received; travel plans part them—Esau to Seir, Jacob to Succoth, then safely to Shechem in Canaan, where he buys land and raises an altar: El-Elohe-Israel.

Genesis 33:1

1And Jacob lifted up his eyes and looked, and behold, Esau was coming, and four hundred men with him. So he divided the children among Leah and Rachel and the two female servants.

Arminian

The feared hour arrives: Esau with four hundred. Jacob still acts—dividing the children. Grace at the Jabbok did not erase responsible care; he meets the crisis with planning, not paralysis.

Reformed

What Jacob dreaded in prayer (32:11) now stands on the road. The four hundred are real, yet they arrive under the God who already met Jacob in the night. Providence has not cancelled the meeting; it governs it.

Genesis 33:2

2And he put the servants with their children in front, then Leah with her children, and Rachel and Joseph last of all.

Arminian

The line is ranked by affection and risk. Love is real, and so is fear. Jacob’s ordering is a human, accountable choice—protecting the dearest last—not a picture of a man with no agency left.

Reformed

Even the sequence of the camp sits inside a larger keeping. Rachel and Joseph last does not mean the promise hangs on Jacob’s tactics; the God of Peniel still holds the line that will run through Joseph.

Genesis 33:3

3He himself went on before them, bowing himself to the ground seven times, until he came near to his brother.

Arminian

Jacob goes first and bows seven times. Humility toward the brother he wronged is a real moral step: grace enables repentance’s posture without forcing Esau’s response.

Reformed

The supplanter walks ahead as a servant. Sevenfold bowing is not theater that buys safety; it is the fruit of a man God has already crippled and blessed. The Lord can humble a heel-grabber before a human brother.

Genesis 33:4

4But Esau ran to meet him and embraced him and fell on his neck and kissed him, and they wept.

Arminian

Esau runs, embraces, weeps. The mercy is genuine and personal—he is not a puppet. Reconciliation here is two brothers freely meeting; grace had been seeking this peace, and Esau does not refuse it.

Reformed

The “but” is gospel-shaped: sword-fear meets a brother’s neck. God can turn a heart (cf. Prov 21:1) without making the tears fake. The God Jacob feared would send Esau as a hunter sends him as a weeper.

Key contrast Both refuse a hollow hug. Arminians typically stress Esau’s real, uncoerced mercy and Jacob’s responsible approach; Reformed readers typically stress that God effectually turned the meeting from vengeance to tears without erasing either brother’s agency.

Genesis 33:5

5And when Esau lifted up his eyes and saw the women and children, he said, “Who are these with you?” Jacob said, “The children whom God has graciously given your servant.”

Arminian

Jacob names the children as gracious gift, and himself as “your servant.” Thanksgiving is honest: family is received, not seized. Grace-given offspring are confessed before the brother once cheated.

Reformed

The first word over the household is God’s gracious giving. Offspring of promise appear not as Jacob’s trophy but as the Lord’s gift—providence already stocking the next generation in Canaan’s direction.

Genesis 33:6

6Then the servants drew near, they and their children, and bowed down.

Arminian

The first wave bows as instructed. Households can learn peace; even the least-protected come near instead of fleeing. Obedience in small companies is still a real act.

Reformed

Servants and their children approach under the same mercy that met Jacob. God is settling a whole camp, not only a patriarch’s private fear.

Genesis 33:7

7Leah likewise and her children drew near and bowed down. And last Joseph and Rachel drew near, and they bowed down.

Arminian

Leah, then Rachel and Joseph: the whole family enacts humility. Peace is practiced in order, not only felt in Jacob’s chest. Each drawing-near is a grace-enabled step they actually take.

Reformed

Joseph, last and dearest, still bows. The future savior of the family is already inside a story of lowered pride. God keeps the line while teaching the camp to bend.

Genesis 33:8

8Esau said, “What do you mean by all this company that I met?” Jacob answered, “To find favor in the sight of my lord.”

Arminian

The herds were a plea for favor. Jacob seeks Esau’s goodwill as a responsible peacemaker: gifts can serve reconciliation without buying it like a bribe if the heart is true.

Reformed

“My lord” from the younger matches last night’s limp: God has been reducing Jacob. The company Esau met is means; the favor sought sits under the God who already granted life.

Genesis 33:9

9But Esau said, “I have enough, my brother; keep what you have for yourself.”

Arminian

Esau’s “I have enough, my brother” is real contentment and a real offer. He is not written as a foil only: he can refuse the gift without refusing the man. Kindness here is chosen.

Reformed

“My brother” answers “my lord.” Providence has filled Esau’s hand too; he is not a leftover of the blessing. God can prosper the older without unseating the promise that runs through the younger.

Genesis 33:10

10Jacob said, “No, please, if I have found favor in your sight, then accept my present from my hand. For I have seen your face, which is like seeing the face of God, and you have accepted me.”

Arminian

Jacob links Esau’s accepted face to seeing God’s face—Peniel still echoing. To receive a brother’s pardon is to taste mercy that could have been withheld. He pleads; he does not coerce.

Reformed

The comparison is theological, not flattery only: the God whose face he saw at the Jabbok now shows that face in a brother’s welcome. Acceptance is gift twice over—divine and fraternal—under one Lord.

Key contrast Both hear Peniel in the brother’s face. Arminians typically stress a sincere, received pardon between two agents; Reformed readers typically stress that the God of the night vision effectually showed His favor through Esau’s acceptance.

Genesis 33:11

11Please accept my blessing that is brought to you, because God has dealt graciously with me, and because I have enough.” Thus he urged him, and he took it.

Arminian

He calls the gift a “blessing” and names God’s gracious dealing. Urging until Esau takes it is peacemaking completed: restored fellowship is sealed when the offended brother actually receives. Both remain free in the exchange.

Reformed

The stolen blessing’s shadow meets an offered blessing. God has dealt graciously; Jacob now has “enough.” Providence writes surplus into the cheater’s hand so he can honor the brother without impoverishing the promise.

Genesis 33:12

12Then Esau said, “Let us journey on our way, and I will go ahead of you.”

Arminian

Esau offers escort and shared road. The invitation is brotherly and concrete. Jacob will have to answer it honestly; companionship can be received or declined.

Reformed

The older offers to lead. God has turned threat into convoy—yet the promise’s map still runs toward Canaan, not merely toward Seir. Providence can use a kind offer without making it Jacob’s next obedience.

Genesis 33:13

13But Jacob said to him, “My lord knows that the children are frail, and that the nursing flocks and herds are a care to me. If they are driven hard for one day, all the flocks will die.”

Arminian

The care is real: frail children, nursing stock. Stewardship of the weak is a genuine reason. Whether caution also hides leftover fear, Jacob remains responsible for how he speaks and how he will travel.

Reformed

Pace and tenderness are not unbelief by themselves. The God who gave the flocks also binds Jacob to them. Even so, the narrator will soon show a different destination than the one named.

Genesis 33:14

14Let my lord pass on ahead of his servant, and I will lead on slowly, at the pace of the livestock that are ahead of me and at the pace of the children, until I come to my lord in Seir.”

Arminian

He promises Seir. Slow travel can be love for the little ones—or a polite distance. Scripture lets the tension stand: disciples still answer for words they do not keep.

Reformed

“Until I come to my lord in Seir” is spoken; Canaan is where he will camp. God still shepherds the hesitant. The promise does not rest on Jacob’s travel itinerary being perfectly frank.

Genesis 33:15

15So Esau said, “Let me leave with you some of the people who are with me.” But he said, “What need is there? Let me find favor in the sight of my lord.”

Arminian

A guard is offered and declined. Jacob seeks favor, not an armed escort. Preferring to go without Esau’s men is a real choice—prudence, independence, or leftover wariness; the text does not flatten him into a hero or a villain.

Reformed

Esau’s people would have been a hedge. Jacob asks only favor. The Lord who kept him at the Jabbok will keep him without Esau’s four hundred; providence is enough cover.

Genesis 33:16

16So Esau returned that day on his way to Seir.

Arminian

They part in peace the same day. Esau’s return is his own road, freely taken. Reconciliation does not require fusion of households; it required the embrace already given.

Reformed

Seir receives Esau again. The older brother’s line has a place under God (later Edom) without inheriting Canaan. Providence separates roads without revoking the mercy of the neck and the kiss.

Genesis 33:17

17But Jacob journeyed to Succoth, and built himself a house and made booths for his livestock. Therefore the name of the place is called Succoth.

Arminian

Not Seir: Succoth, a house, booths for stock. The detour is Jacob’s. Grace that reconciled him still leaves room for mixed motives—delay, safety, settlement. He remains accountable for the road he actually takes.

Reformed

Succoth (“booths”) is east of the Jordan, not Esau’s mountain. God still brings him toward the land while the man builds sheds. Imperfect obedience does not cancel the Keep who named him Israel.

Key contrast Both notice he does not go to Seir. Arminians typically stress Jacob’s responsible, mixed choice to settle short; Reformed readers typically stress that God still advances him toward Canaan despite the unkept itinerary.

Genesis 33:18

18And Jacob came safely to the city of Shechem, which is in the land of Canaan, on his way from Paddan-aram, and he camped before the city.

Arminian

“Safely” (or peacefully) into Canaan: the vow’s country, not Laban’s. The pilgrim who feared Esau now camps as a sojourner. Safety is gift to be received with thanks, not presumed as a right.

Reformed

From Paddan-aram to Shechem: the God of the going-out brings him in. “Safely” is the narrator’s verdict on providence. The land of promise holds his tent because the Lord said it would.

Genesis 33:19

19And from the sons of Hamor, Shechem’s father, he bought for a hundred pieces of money the piece of land on which he had pitched his tent.

Arminian

He buys the plot—no raid, no claim by force. Peaceable purchase in Canaan is responsible sojourning: the promise is trusted without stealing a well or a field. Later grief at Shechem (ch. 34) does not rewrite this honest start.

Reformed

A hundred qesitah, Hamor’s sons, a legal stake. Abraham bought a grave; Jacob buys a campsite. God lets the heir own a parcel inside the land He will one day give in full—earnest, not the whole inheritance.

Genesis 33:20

20There he erected an altar and called it El-Elohe-Israel.

Arminian

Altar and name: “God, the God of Israel.” The new name from the Jabbok becomes worship in Canaan. Calling on God is a free, fitting response to mercy received—the right first work after safe arrival.

Reformed

El-Elohe-Israel confesses that the God of the fathers is now the God of this man newly named. Worship marks the land because God kept him. The altar is fruit of preserving grace, planted on purchased soil until a greater altar comes.

Key contrast Both hear covenant worship in the name. Arminians typically stress Jacob’s grateful, responsible confession after delivered mercy; Reformed readers typically stress that the God who named him Israel now publicly claims this man and this plot by His own keeping.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
