Passage

Genesis 36

Verses 20–43 · WEB · Voices

The chapter turns to Seir’s Horite inhabitants, then to Anah who found hot springs, Horite chiefs, and the kings who reigned in Edom before any king reigned over Israel. It closes with Esau’s chiefs listed by clans and dwelling places—Edom’s geography under God’s care.

Genesis 36:20

20These are the sons of Seir the Horite, the inhabitants of the land: Lotan, Shobal, Zibeon, Anah,

Arminian

Before Edom’s kings, the text honors the people already in Seir.

Classical Arminians refuse to skip Horites as extras. God names prior inhabitants because they were truly there.

Reformed

Horites are “inhabitants of the land”—Seir was not empty. God records the people Deuteronomy will later say Esau dispossessed (Deut 2:12, 22).

Providence over territories includes those who dwelt there first.

Genesis 36:21

21Dishon, Ezer, and Dishan. These are the chiefs who came of the Horites, the children of Seir in the land of Edom.

Arminian

Dishon, Ezer, and Dishan complete Seir’s seven sons, called chiefs of the Horites in Edom’s land. Two similar names (Dishon/Dishan) are kept distinct.

The land can hold both Horite chiefs and, in time, Edomite ones.

Reformed

Seven Horite chiefs in the land of Edom. God numbers Seir’s house as carefully as Esau’s.

The stage of Edom was already peopled; the Lord ordered both occupancy and later displacement without fictionalizing the Horites.

Genesis 36:22

22The children of Lotan were Hori and Heman. Lotan’s sister was Timna.

Arminian

The Timna of v. 12 now has a Horite brother—kinship across the two lists.

God lets a sister’s name explain a concubine in Esau’s house. Intermarriage is reported, not sneered at.

Reformed

Timna’s Horite identity ties Amalek’s mother to Seir. Providence knits the inhabitant line to Eliphaz.

The sister is named because the Lord of genealogies does not hide the join.

Genesis 36:23

23These are the children of Shobal: Alvan, Manahath, Ebal, Shepho, and Onam.

Arminian

Shobal’s sons: Alvan, Manahath, Ebal, Shepho, Onam. Five Horite names with the same dignity as Japheth’s grandsons in chapter 10.

Wideness of care: God knows clans Israel will barely meet.

Reformed

Shobal’s five sons are named, another group of Horite clans. God is mapping Seir clan by clan.

These names exist in Scripture because He willed their memory.

Genesis 36:24

24These are the children of Zibeon: Aiah and Anah. This is Anah who found the hot springs in the wilderness, as he fed the donkeys of Zibeon his father.

Arminian

Zibeon’s sons Aiah and Anah—and a narrative pause: this Anah found the hot springs in the wilderness while pasturing his father’s donkeys.

The Bible is not too holy for a herder’s discovery. Common life in Seir is worth a sentence.

Reformed

Anah is identified by a wilderness find and ordinary work. God records a Horite anecdote as true history.

Providence includes hot springs and donkeys, not only thrones.

Genesis 36:25

25These are the children of Anah: Dishon and Oholibamah, the daughter of Anah.

Arminian

The wife of Esau (vv. 2, 14, 18) is here a Horite daughter.

Family ties between Esau and Seir are on the page for anyone willing to read slowly.

Reformed

Oholibamah named from her father’s house. The marriage that linked Esau to the inhabitants is genealogically pinned.

God wanted that pin in both lists.

Genesis 36:26

26These are the children of Dishon: Hemdan, Eshban, Ithran, and Cheran.

Arminian

Dishon’s sons: Hemdan, Eshban, Ithran, Cheran. Four more Horite names, unmocked.

Pastorally: being briefly listed is still being known by God.

Reformed

Dishon’s house is filled out. The Lord counts Seir’s grandsons.

No scorn for a list without stories.

Genesis 36:27

27These are the children of Ezer: Bilhan, Zaavan, and Akan.

Arminian

Ezer’s sons: Bilhan, Zaavan, Akan. Three names, same care.

Peoples on the edge of Israel’s memory still belong to the God of all nations.

Reformed

Ezer’s three take their row. Providence is not in a hurry to reach the kings.

The Horite table must be complete first.

Genesis 36:28

28These are the children of Dishan: Uz and Aran.

Arminian

Dishan’s sons: Uz and Aran. Uz elsewhere names lands; here it is a Horite grandson.

God can reuse names across tables without confusion in His own mind.

Reformed

Dishan, distinct from Dishon, receives two sons. The spelling difference is kept.

Precision in the Horite list is a kind of reverence for God’s bookkeeping.

Genesis 36:29

29These are the chiefs who came of the Horites: chief Lotan, chief Shobal, chief Zibeon, chief Anah,

Arminian

Sons become titled heads, as Esau’s sons did.

Common-grace government is not Israel’s monopoly.

Reformed

The chief-list mirrors v. 20. God confirms Horite polity in Seir.

Structure is given, not invented as a joke at genealogy’s expense.

Genesis 36:30

30chief Dishon, chief Ezer, and chief Dishan. These are the chiefs who came of the Horites, according to their chiefs in the land of Seir.

Arminian

“According to their chiefs in the land of Seir” slows the reader to take each head seriously.

Arminians hear honor: Seir had a constitution before Edom’s kings are named.

Reformed

“According to their chiefs” is deliberate pacing. The land of Seir had ordered leadership under God’s allotment.

Only then does the narrator turn to kings.

Genesis 36:31

31These are the kings who reigned in the land of Edom, before any king reigned over the children of Israel.

Arminian

Kings reigned in Edom before any king ruled over the children of Israel. The note is historical, not a taunt: Edom’s monarchy is earlier, and Scripture tells the truth.

Arminians hear God sincerely governing all peoples. Israel’s later kingship is not the only throne God noticed; Edom’s royal list honors politics outside the chosen line.

Reformed

The narrator timestamps Edom’s kings against Israel’s then-future monarchy. God orders nations on different clocks.

Edom’s earlier kings still sit inside His purpose with Jacob. The delay of Israel’s king is not because God forgot His people; it is His timing—and Edom’s throne is recorded without being baptized as the covenant kingship.

Key contrast Both take v. 31 as sober history, not mockery of genealogies or of Edom. Arminians stress that God sincerely governs and honestly records all peoples’ kings; Reformed commentators emphasize that God sovereignly sequences the nations—Edom’s earlier monarchy still serves His covenant purpose with Jacob without erasing Esau’s royal posterity.

Genesis 36:32

32Bela, the son of Beor, reigned in Edom. The name of his city was Dinhabah.

Arminian

First named king of Edom, with a capital.

A city and a father: Edom’s throne begins in particulars.

Reformed

The king-list opens like any royal chronicle God is willing to preserve. Bela of Dinhabah is not a myth the narrator needed.

Providence over Edom’s first crown.

Genesis 36:33

33Bela died, and Jobab, the son of Zerah of Bozrah, reigned in his place.

Arminian

Bela dies; Jobab son of Zerah of Bozrah reigns. Succession by death, a new city (Bozrah, later famed in prophecy).

Thrones pass. God records Edom’s ordinary royal mortality.

Reformed

Death and replacement: Edom’s kings are mortal under the same Lord. Bozrah enters the map because God placed a king there.

The list’s “in his place” is providence, not chance.

Genesis 36:34

34Jobab died, and Husham of the land of the Temanites reigned in his place.

Arminian

Jobab dies; Husham of the Temanites reigns. Teman, already a clan name, now a royal catchment.

Regions of Edom take turns on the throne—local identity.

Reformed

A Temanite king means Eliphaz’s famous branch can yield a crown. God ties the king-list back to the clan-list.

Geography and dynasty meet under His rule.

Genesis 36:35

35Husham died, and Hadad, the son of Bedad, who struck Midian in the field of Moab, reigned in his place. The name of his city was Avith.

Arminian

Husham dies; Hadad son of Bedad, who struck Midian in Moab, reigns from Avith. One king has a deed, not only a name.

Edomite military history is allowed on the page. God does not reduce every foreign victory to a footnote of Israel.

Reformed

A Midian-defeating Hadad is remembered with his city. God superintends wars east of the Jordan even when Israel has no king yet.

The deed sits in Edom’s chronicle because the Lord of hosts is Lord there too.

Genesis 36:36

36Hadad died, and Samlah of Masrekah reigned in his place.

Arminian

Hadad dies; Samlah of Masrekah reigns. Another city, another name, no extra tale.

Brief notices still honor a reign.

Reformed

Masrekah’s Samlah is a full row in God’s book. Silence about exploits is not contempt.

The Lord numbers reigns He does not narrate at length.

Genesis 36:37

37Samlah died, and Shaul of Rehoboth by the river, reigned in his place.

Arminian

Samlah dies; Shaul of Rehoboth by the river (Hebrew: “the River”) reigns. Edom’s horizon can reach a riverine Rehoboth.

Nations have wide connections; the record is not embarrassed by them.

Reformed

Rehoboth-on-the-River is a geographic marker God kept. Whether one takes “the River” as the Euphrates, the point is an Edomite king with a named origin beyond a local village.

Providence stretches Edom’s royal map.

Genesis 36:38

38Shaul died, and Baal Hanan the son of Achbor reigned in his place.

Arminian

Shaul dies; Baal-hanan son of Achbor reigns. A theophoric name with “Baal” is reported without panic or sneer.

The narrator can tell the truth about an Edomite name and still keep the man in the list.

Reformed

Baal-hanan son of Achbor: God records even names that may confess another worship. Common-grace kingship differs from covenant faith.

The throne in Edom remains under the Lord’s government.

Genesis 36:39

39Baal Hanan the son of Achbor died, and Hadar reigned in his place. The name of his city was Pau. His wife’s name was Mehetabel, the daughter of Matred, the daughter of Mezahab.

Arminian

Baal-hanan dies; Hadar reigns in Pau. Unusually, a queen-mother line is given: Mehetabel, daughter of Matred, the daughter of Mezahab.

Women close the king-list. God is willing to name Edomite wives and grandmothers.

Reformed

Hadar of Pau, with Mehetabel’s pedigree. The extra names are not ornaments; they are history God preserved.

A foreign court gets a matriline in Holy Scripture because the Lord of nations keeps more than Israel’s family album.

Genesis 36:40

40These are the names of the chiefs who came from Esau, according to their families, after their places, and by their names: chief Timna, chief Alvah, chief Jetheth,

Arminian

A final chief-list of Esau by clans, dwelling places, and names: Timna, Alvah, Jetheth. This roster is territorial as well as genealogical.

The places where the chiefs lived are part of the record. God locates Edomite heads where they lived.

Reformed

Chiefs “according to their families, after their places, and.” Providence now maps Edom as land tenure, not only blood.

Timna as a chief-name may echo the earlier woman; God can reuse names in a people’s geography.

Genesis 36:41

41chief Oholibamah, chief Elah, chief Pinon,

Arminian

Oholibamah, Elah, Pinon—three more chiefs, one sharing a wife’s name. Clans can carry a mother’s name into a district.

The list keeps Edom concrete.

Reformed

Pinon and Elah sit beside Oholibamah as territorial chiefs. God is closing the chapter with geography.

Dwelling places are part of the claim that Edom truly possessed a land.

Genesis 36:42

42chief Kenaz, chief Teman, chief Mibzar,

Arminian

Kenaz, Teman, Mibzar—names already heard as sons now heard as places and chiefs. Continuity of clan and country.

God lets readers see the same Edom from two angles without despising either.

Reformed

Kenaz and Teman return as territorial titles. The double appearance is structure, not a slip.

The Lord who named sons now names seats.

Genesis 36:43

43chief Magdiel, and chief Iram. These are the chiefs of Edom, according to their habitations in the land of their possession. This is Esau, the father of the Edomites.

Arminian

Magdiel and Iram close the chiefs of Edom—that is, Esau, father of Edom—listed by their homes in the land they possessed.

The last word is possession: Edom had a land. Arminian readers hear God sincerely granting and recording a nation’s home outside Israel’s inheritance.

Reformed

The chapter ends as it began: Esau is Edom, now with chiefs in the land of their possession. Election of Jacob did not leave Esau landless in the narrator’s theology.

God ordered Edom’s holdings to serve His wider purpose with Israel—borders, later prophets, even Deuteronomy’s charge not to contend for Seir—while still telling the truth that the land was theirs.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic Arminian and Reformed comment on Genesis 36:20–43, in the writers’ own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“These are the sons of Seir—In the midst of the genealogy of the Edomites is inserted the genealogy of the Horites, those Canaanites, or Hittites, (compare Gen 26:34,) that were the natives of mount Seir. Mention is made of them, Gen 14:6, and of their interest in mount Seir before the Edomites took possession of it, Deu 2:12,22. This comes in here, not only to give light to the story, but to be a standing reflexion upon the Edomites for intermarrying with them, by which it is likely they learned their way, and corrupted themselves.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 36:20)

Reformed Notable voices

“These are the kings that reigned, etc We must keep in memory what we have said a little before, that reprobates are suddenly exalted, that they may immediately fall, like the herb upon the roofs, which is destitute of root, and has a hasty growth, but withers the more quickly. To the two sons of Isaac had been promised the honor that kings should spring from them.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 36:31)
