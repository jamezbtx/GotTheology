Passage

Genesis 36

Verses 1–19 · ESV · Voices

Genesis pauses Jacob’s story to record Esau’s generations: he is Edom. Canaanite wives, sons born in Canaan, then a move to Seir because the brothers’ possessions were too great to dwell together. Chiefs rise from Esau’s sons. God keeps the book of a nation outside the chosen line.

Genesis 36:1

1These are the generations of Esau (that is, Edom).

Arminian

The toledot formula now belongs to Esau, named Edom. God pauses Jacob’s story to honor a brother’s real nation.

Classical Arminians hear sincere regard for peoples outside the chosen line: Esau’s generations are history God wants told, not a foil He discards.

Reformed

“Generations of Esau” is covenant bookkeeping that includes the non-elect brother. Edom exists because God records and rules him.

Reformed readers refuse to treat the list as filler: providence maps Edom beside Jacob without erasing Esau’s posterity.

Genesis 36:2

2Esau took his wives from the Canaanites: Adah the daughter of Elon the Hittite, Oholibamah the daughter of Anah the daughter of Zibeon the Hivite,

Arminian

Esau’s Canaanite wives are named—Adah, Oholibamah—with fathers and peoples. Marriage choices are real history, earlier a grief to Isaac and Rebekah, yet the women are not mocked.

Arminian pastoral: God still records these households. Common grace lets families form even when the wives come from Canaan.

Reformed

The narrator lists Hittite and Hivite kinship with precision. God knows the households that will father Edom.

Reformed accent: providence over marriage and clan is as real as providence over the promise. Naming is not approval of every choice; it is truthful government of history.

Genesis 36:3

3and Basemath, Ishmael’s daughter, the sister of Nebaioth.

Arminian

Basemath, Ishmael’s daughter, sister of Nebaioth, ties Esau to Abraham’s other son. Kinship beyond Jacob is still Abrahamic family.

Arminians gladly note God remembering Ishmael’s house in Esau’s marriage: mercy toward more than one branch.

Reformed

Ishmael’s line enters Edom’s tree by name. God weaves Abraham’s wider family without confusing it with the line of promise through Isaac and Jacob.

The sister of Nebaioth is not an accident in the record.

Genesis 36:4

4And Adah bore to Esau, Eliphaz; Basemath bore Reuel;

Arminian

Adah bears Eliphaz; Basemath bears Reuel. Two mothers, two named sons—ordinary fruitfulness under God’s care.

The births are gifts to a household God will not pretend never existed.

Reformed

First sons of Edom are catalogued because the Lord numbers them. Eliphaz and Reuel will head clans.

Providence begins Edom with names, not with a blur.

Genesis 36:5

5and Oholibamah bore Jeush, Jalam, and Korah. These are the sons of Esau who were born to him in the land of Canaan.

Arminian

Oholibamah bears Jeush, Jalam, and Korah. The summary: sons born to Esau in Canaan—before the move to Seir.

Arminian reading: Canaan was their first home; God tells the truth about where Edom’s fathers started.

Reformed

Three more sons complete the Canaan-born list. God timestamps Edom’s origin in the land Jacob will inherit.

The later settlement in Seir does not rewrite this beginning.

Genesis 36:6

6Then Esau took his wives, his sons, his daughters, and all the members of his household, his livestock, all his beasts, and all his property that he had acquired in the land of Canaan. He went into a land away from his brother Jacob.

Arminian

Esau takes the whole household and property acquired in Canaan and goes away from Jacob. The separation is a responsible family decision, echoing Abraham and Lot.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere, peaceful parting: God lets both brothers prosper and choose space rather than crowding into strife.

Reformed

The move is narrated under God’s eye. Esau leaves Canaan with real wealth; Jacob remains in the land of sojourning.

Reformed confession: providence uses this departure so the promise geography and Edom’s future can both stand—without treating Esau’s household as a discarded extra.

Key contrast Both honor a real, prosperous parting. Arminians typically stress Esau’s responsible choice and God’s sincere care for both households; Reformed readers typically stress that God orders the separation so Jacob’s line keeps the land of sojourning while Edom is given a history of its own.

Genesis 36:7

7For their possessions were too great for them to dwell together. The land of their sojournings could not support them because of their livestock.

Arminian

Possessions too great; the land of sojournings cannot support both. Abundance, not hatred, drives the split—common grace made them too rich to share one range.

Arminian pastoral: God can bless two brothers at once. The record honors economic fact, not a cartoon of elect versus leftover.

Reformed

The same reason that parted Abram and Lot (13:6) now parts Esau and Jacob. God allots more livestock than one sojourning land can hold.

Reformed accent: overflow is providence. The Lord who promised Jacob the land also made Esau too prosperous to stay—ordering nations by gift, not by erasing the older brother.

Key contrast Shared reading: prosperity, not mere spite, separates them. Arminians typically emphasize common-grace abundance and a sincere, wide care for both camps; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s sovereign allotment of space so the covenant sojourner and Edom can both exist under His purpose.

Genesis 36:8

8So Esau settled in the hill country of Seir. (Esau is Edom.)

Arminian

Esau settles in the hill country of Seir. Parenthesis: Esau is Edom. Identity of a people is stated twice so no reader misses it.

Classical Arminians receive Edom as a nation God names and locates—real geography, real brother, sincerely governed.

Reformed

“Esau is Edom” is theological geography: the older brother is a people under God’s rule, settled in Seir by the narrator’s report.

Reformed note: Jacob’s election does not un-name Esau. God plants Edom in the hills that will later border Israel, still serving His counsel toward the covenant line.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat “Esau is Edom” as a slur. Arminians typically hear God honoring a real nation’s identity and land; Reformed readers typically hear God locating Edom by decree beside the promise, the brother’s story ordered without being erased.

Genesis 36:9

9These are the generations of Esau the father of the Edomites in the hill country of Seir.

Arminian

A second generations heading: Esau the father of the Edomites in Seir. The chapter now tracks the nation in its land, not only sons born in Canaan.

God’s bookkeeping includes a people after they move.

Reformed

The toledot restarts in Seir because Edom’s story is located there. “Father of the Edomites” is a national title under providence.

The Lord of nations writes Edom’s hill-country generations as carefully as Jacob’s.

Genesis 36:10

10These are the names of Esau’s sons: Eliphaz the son of Adah the wife of Esau, Reuel the son of Basemath the wife of Esau.

Arminian

Eliphaz of Adah, Reuel of Basemath—sons matched again to mothers. The list is careful, not careless.

Arminians honor the mothers named: God records women in Edom’s tree.

Reformed

The names of verse 4 return as clan heads. God repeats to fix the structure of Edom.

Providence is not bored by restatement.

Genesis 36:11

11The sons of Eliphaz were Teman, Omar, Zepho, Gatam, and Kenaz.

Arminian

Eliphaz’s sons: Teman, Omar, Zepho, Gatam, Kenaz. Teman will later name a region of wisdom in Edom.

Each grandson is a real clan, not a sermon illustration.

Reformed

Five names under Eliphaz map future chiefs. God numbers Edom’s firstborn house.

Reformed reading: even Teman’s later fame sits inside this list God already wrote.

Genesis 36:12

12(Timna was a concubine of Eliphaz, Esau’s son; she bore Amalek to Eliphaz.) These are the sons of Adah, Esau’s wife.

Arminian

Parenthesis: Timna, Eliphaz’s concubine, bears Amalek. The later enemy of Israel is first a child in Esau’s house—named without sneer.

Classical Arminians insist the record is fair history: Amalek is not born a cartoon. God sincerely tells the truth about a people who will later oppose Israel; their beginning is still a birth.

Reformed

Amalek is planted in the genealogy before the wilderness wars. God knows the foe in the concubine’s son without unmaking him as a person.

Reformed accent: providence already locates Amalek inside Edom’s firstborn line. The later conflict serves God’s purpose with Israel; it does not authorize pretending Amalek was never Esau’s grandson.

Key contrast Both refuse to mock the birth notice. Arminians typically stress honest history and a people who remain morally responsible later; Reformed readers typically stress that God sovereignly placed Amalek in this tree, the future enemy already inside His counsel, without erasing the child Timna bore.

Genesis 36:13

13These are the sons of Reuel: Nahath, Zerah, Shammah, and Mizzah. These are the sons of Basemath, Esau’s wife.

Arminian

Reuel’s sons: Nahath, Zerah, Shammah, Mizzah—Basemath’s grandsons. Four names, equal dignity of listing.

God attends to the second wife’s house as well as the first.

Reformed

Reuel’s four are the other main branch of Esau’s sons by wives. The catalog is balanced because God is precise.

These will be chiefs in the land of Edom.

Genesis 36:14

14These are the sons of Oholibamah the daughter of Anah the daughter of Zibeon, Esau’s wife: she bore to Esau Jeush, Jalam, and Korah.

Arminian

Oholibamah’s sons again: Jeush, Jalam, Korah—with her father’s names attached. The Hivite/Horite connection is not hidden.

Arminian note: God remembers the wife whose kinship runs toward Seir’s inhabitants.

Reformed

The third wife’s sons are restated with lineage. Oholibamah ties Esau to Anah and Zibeon, names that will return among the Horites.

Providence already knits Edom to Seir’s people.

Genesis 36:15

15These are the chiefs of the sons of Esau. The sons of Eliphaz the firstborn of Esau: the chiefs Teman, Omar, Zepho, Kenaz,

Arminian

Chiefs (clan leaders) of Esau’s sons begin with Eliphaz the firstborn: Teman, Omar, Zepho, Kenaz. Family becomes political structure.

Classical Arminians see ordered society as common grace: God lets Edom have heads of clans.

Reformed

“Chiefs” marks government under God’s leave. Eliphaz’s house supplies the first titles in Edom.

Reformed confession: nations receive rulers by providence, not only Israel.

Genesis 36:16

16Korah, Gatam, and Amalek; these are the chiefs of Eliphaz in the land of Edom; these are the sons of Adah.

Arminian

Korah, Gatam, and Amalek complete Eliphaz’s chiefs in Edom, sons of Adah. Amalek is a chief, not a rumor.

The list honors Edom’s internal order; it does not ask readers to cheer every later deed.

Reformed

Chiefs in the land of Edom include Amalek. God records political reality in the older brother’s country.

The firstborn’s clans are mapped because the Lord of nations keeps books.

Genesis 36:17

17These are the sons of Reuel, Esau’s son: the chiefs Nahath, Zerah, Shammah, and Mizzah; these are the chiefs of Reuel in the land of Edom; these are the sons of Basemath, Esau’s wife.

Arminian

Reuel’s four become chiefs in Edom, sons of Basemath. Second-wife clans receive the same office-language.

God’s fairness in the list is pastoral: no branch of Esau is a throwaway line.

Reformed

Four chiefs of Reuel match four sons. The symmetry is historical structure under God.

Edom’s government is dual-housed from Esau’s two named wives besides Oholibamah.

Genesis 36:18

18These are the sons of Oholibamah, Esau’s wife: the chiefs Jeush, Jalam, and Korah; these are the chiefs born of Oholibamah the daughter of Anah, Esau’s wife.

Arminian

Oholibamah’s three sons are chiefs too. The wife from Anah’s line has her own titled offspring.

Arminians hear honor for a Canaanite/Horite-linked mother in Edom’s constitution.

Reformed

Three chiefs born of Oholibamah close Esau’s family government. God includes the Seir-linked wife in Edom’s ruling names.

Providence does not flatten the households into one.

Genesis 36:19

19These are the sons of Esau (that is, Edom), and these are their chiefs.

Arminian

Summary: sons of Esau (that is, Edom) and their chiefs. The identity refrain returns so the reader cannot miss the nation’s name.

The first half of the chapter has done its work: a real people with real heads.

Reformed

Closing formula of this Esau-toledot: sons and chiefs, Esau is Edom. God has established the nation that will live beside Israel.

Reformed close of the section: election of Jacob did not require silence about Edom’s constitution.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Esau’s generations and the move to Seir—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 36:1–19.

Arminian Notable voices

“That only the names of Esau's sons and grand-sons are recorded: not their history, for it is the church that Moses preserves the records of, not of those that were without. The elders only that lived by faith obtained a good report. Nor doth the genealogy go any farther than the third and fourth generation.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 36:10)

Reformed Notable voices

“Though Esau was an alien from the Church in the sight of God; yet since he also, as a son of Isaac, was favored with a temporal blessing, Moses celebrates his race, and inscribes a sufficiently lengthened catalogue of the people born from him. This commemoration, however, resembles an honorable sepulture. For although Esau, with his posterity, took the precedence; yet this dignity was like a bubble, which is comprised under the figure of the world, and which quickly perishes.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 36:1)
