Passage

Genesis 9

Verses 1–17 · ESV · Voices

After the flood, God blesses Noah and his sons, restores the call to fill the earth, sets limits around blood and human life, and establishes a covenant with all flesh—signed by the bow in the clouds—that a flood will never again destroy the earth.

Genesis 9:1

1And God blessed Noah and his sons and said to them, “Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth.

Arminian

The first word after judgment is blessing. God restates the creation mandate to a small family standing on a washed earth: live, multiply, fill.

Classical Arminians hear sincere goodwill in this charge. The God who preserved eight souls now invites the human family to flourish again—grace that restores vocation, not a closed circle of favor.

Reformed

Blessing is God’s speech that makes a future possible. The same word that once filled Eden now reconstitutes the world after the flood.

Reformed readers hear covenant initiative: fruitfulness is gift before it is duty. God does not wait for Noah to rebuild confidence; He speaks life into a frightened remnant.

Genesis 9:2

2The fear of you and the dread of you shall be upon every beast of the earth and upon every bird of the heavens, upon everything that creeps on the ground and all the fish of the sea. Into your hand they are delivered.

Arminian

Dominion is restated in a fallen key: fear and dread now mark the creatures’ relation to humankind. Rule is real, yet it is not a license for cruelty.

Arminian pastoral note: stewardship remains a responsible trust. What God delivers into human hands can be used with gratitude or abused; the mandate assumes moral agents who answer to the Giver.

Reformed

Providence bridles the beasts so that human life can continue. Dominion after the flood is preserved gift, not recovered innocence.

Reformed accent: “into your hand they are delivered” names God’s act. Human rule stands only because the Creator still restrains what would otherwise devour the remnant.

Genesis 9:3

3Every moving thing that lives shall be food for you. And as I gave you the green plants, I give you everything.

Arminian

Diet widens by gift: “I give you everything.” Food is received, not seized as if the world were ownerless.

Both traditions can preach thanksgiving here. Arminians often add that such common kindness displays God’s heart toward the whole human family, a daily mercy meant to be received with a good conscience.

Reformed

Provision is donation. Flesh is granted as the plants were granted—by the Lord’s hand, not by human theft.

Reformed comfort: common grace is concrete. God stocks a ruined world so that life may go on under His promise, until saving mercy is more fully unveiled in the covenant of grace.

Genesis 9:4

4But you shall not eat flesh with its life, that is, its blood.

Arminian

A limit sits beside the gift. Blood is identified with life, and life remains God’s to fence. Appetite is not lord.

Arminian reading stresses responsible freedom under a holy boundary. Grace that widens the table also trains gentleness; we may receive food without treating life as cheap.

Reformed

Permission is not absolutized. God retains claim on life itself, even when He grants flesh for food.

The Reformed note is reverence: every meal still belongs inside God’s rule. Liberty to eat is real because the Giver said so—and the same Giver may still say “but.”

Genesis 9:5

5And for your lifeblood I will require a reckoning: from every beast I will require it and from man. From his fellow man I will require a reckoning for the life of man.

Arminian

Human blood is not a private matter. God Himself requires a reckoning from beast and from neighbor. Life is sacred because it is His.

Classical Arminians hear both protection and accountability. The God who loves His image-bearers will not shrug at violence; moral agency before this requirement is real.

Reformed

The Judge of all the earth undertakes the defense of human life. Reckless bloodshed meets a God who notices.

Reformed pastoral weight: this is not mere social contract. Providence and justice meet—God will require what murder tries to hide, whether courts are awake or asleep.

Genesis 9:6

6“Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed, for God made man in his own image.

Arminian

The ground of the sentence is the image of God. Violence against a neighbor is assault on a dignity God stamped, not a mere breach of tribal custom.

Arminians and Reformed stand together against cheapening life. The Arminian accent often lingers on the wideness of that dignity: every human being, not a favored few, bears the image and may not be treated as expendable.

Reformed

Image-bearing is the theological reason human life is fenced. God sees His own likeness assaulted in the victim, and He authorizes a human reckoning.

Reformed confession holds the remnant of the image even after the fall: enough dignity remains that murder is uniquely heinous, and enough of God’s order remains that justice is not optional.

Key contrast Both rest the sanctity of life on the imago Dei. Arminians typically stress that this dignity and God’s care extend sincerely to every person; Reformed theology typically adds that the image also explains why God Himself will not let human blood be cheap in a world He still governs.

Genesis 9:7

7And you, be fruitful and multiply, increase greatly on the earth and multiply in it.”

Arminian

The blessing is repeated so fear will not freeze the family. Increase is commanded as hope, not as grim survivalism.

Pastoral takeaway: God’s will for the world after judgment is life. The remnant is summoned to trust that filling the earth is still a holy assignment.

Reformed

Repetition is encouragement. God binds the family to a future He intends to give.

The mandate is promise-shaped: they will increase because the Lord who speaks also preserves the stage on which increase happens.

Genesis 9:8

8Then God said to Noah and to his sons with him,

Arminian

The sons are addressed with their father. The coming covenant is not a private whisper to one hero; it is spoken to a household that will become the nations.

Arminian warmth: God deals with a people in real history, and the next generation is already included in the address.

Reformed

God names the parties before He names the terms. Noah and his sons stand as the human side of a bond God is about to swear.

Reformed instinct: the Word comes first. What follows will be God’s established covenant, not a treaty Noah proposed.

Genesis 9:9

9“Behold, I establish my covenant with you and your offspring after you,

Arminian

“I establish” puts the initiative wholly in God, yet the scope is astonishing: Noah and offspring after him. This is kindness that runs down the generations of a still-sinful race.

Classical Arminians often read here a sincere, world-embracing common grace—the God who could have ended flesh instead pledges a stable world in which the gospel can be preached and heard.

Reformed

Covenant is God’s condescension. He binds Himself by promise so that fearful people may have more than a mood to stand on.

Reformed theology typically distinguishes this Noahic bond as common grace—real, gracious preservation of the world—while the saving covenant of grace in Christ remains the particular hope of the elect.

Key contrast Both confess God as the One who establishes the covenant. Arminians often hear in this universal pledge a display of God’s goodwill toward all humanity; Reformed readers often hear a covenant of preservation that holds the stage of history, distinct from the effectual covenant of saving grace.

Genesis 9:10

10and with every living creature that is with you, the birds, the livestock, and every beast of the earth with you, as many as came out of the ark; it is for every beast of the earth.

Arminian

Beasts are named as covenant partners in their own way. God’s mercy is not stingy; the ark’s passengers share the world’s new stability.

The pastoral picture is wide: creation is not disposable scenery. The God who saved animals through the waters still claims them in His promise.

Reformed

The covenant’s creaturely breadth shows common grace in the strict sense: God preserves non-human life for His purposes, without implying that birds believe.

Reformed wonder: providence is meticulous and kind. The world is kept as a theater of glory, not abandoned to chaos after the flood.

Genesis 9:11

11I establish my covenant with you, that never again shall all flesh be cut off by the waters of the flood, and never again shall there be a flood to destroy the earth.”

Arminian

The content is negative and massive: no more global flood. Judgment was real; so is the “never again.”

Arminian preaching finds here a God who can both judge and then bind Himself to patience. The world’s continued existence is mercy, a space in which sinners may yet turn.

Reformed

God specifies what He swears: all flesh will not again be cut off by floodwaters. The earth’s stability is promised, not guessed.

Reformed comfort amid later storms: rain is no longer a candidate for unmaking the world. History now runs toward a different consummation, under a pledged sky.

Genesis 9:12

12And God said, “This is the sign of the covenant that I make between me and you and every living creature that is with you, for all future generations:

Arminian

A sign is added for “all future generations.” God stoops to help memory. Faith is not left to fog.

Classical Arminians treasure signs as assisting grace: God strengthens trust without erasing the call to look, remember, and rest in the promise.

Reformed

Word and sign belong together. The covenant is made; the sign serves faith across generations yet unborn.

Reformed sacrament instinct is already here in seed: God confirms His pledge by a visible token so weak minds may hold a strong promise.

Genesis 9:13

13I have set my bow in the cloud, and it shall be a sign of the covenant between me and the earth.

Arminian

The bow is “my bow”—God’s, hung in the cloud as a sign between Him and the earth. Warfare imagery yields to pledged peace.

Arminian pastoral use: look up when weather frightens you. The God who judges also wants the world to see a token of His kindness and to trust Him.

Reformed

Calvin’s reading still pastors well: the natural arch is consecrated as pledge. God assigns a new office to what rain already produces.

Reformed confidence: the sign does not create the covenant; it seals a word God has spoken. Nature itself is pressed into the service of promise.

Genesis 9:14

14When I bring clouds over the earth and the bow is seen in the clouds,

Arminian

God claims even the clouds: “when I bring.” Dark weather is not outside His care, and the bow appears in the very place fear looks.

The invitation is simple: when you see it, remember who hangs it there. Common sights become calls to trust.

Reformed

Providence includes the storm and the sign. God brings clouds and, in them, the reminder of His oath.

The Reformed eye sees no luck in the sky. What alarms and what reassures arrive under the same Lord.

Genesis 9:15

15I will remember my covenant that is between me and you and every living creature of all flesh. And the waters shall never again become a flood to destroy all flesh.

Arminian

“I will remember” is for our sake. God does not forget as we do; He speaks so we will know the oath still stands when rivers rise.

Arminian comfort: the promise is as wide as “all flesh.” Preservation of the world is a kindness God means, not an accident of climate.

Reformed

Divine remembering is covenant faithfulness, not a jogged memory. The sign points us to a God who is not unmindful of what He swore.

Reformed rest: the world’s continuance is anchored in God’s character. Waters have a divinely set limit until the day He ends history another way.

Genesis 9:16

16When the bow is in the clouds, I will see it and remember the everlasting covenant between God and every living creature of all flesh that is on the earth.”

Arminian

The covenant is called everlasting and again embraces every living creature. God’s seeing of the bow is a preacher’s gift to anxious people.

Classical Arminians often linger on “all flesh”: here is a God whose patience toward the world is real, a stage on which saving grace is sincerely offered in the gospel.

Reformed

“Everlasting” names duration God guarantees. He sees the bow and keeps the bond with all flesh as to their earthly preservation.

Reformed distinction remains pastoral, not cold: this is not the whole of salvation, but it is a true everlasting kindness by which God holds the world until redemption is complete in Christ.

Key contrast Both call the rainbow covenant God’s own everlasting kindness. Arminians typically emphasize its universal reach as of a piece with God’s goodwill toward all; Reformed theology typically treats it as common-grace preservation, certain and wide, yet not identical with saving election in Christ.

Genesis 9:17

17God said to Noah, “This is the sign of the covenant that I have established between me and all flesh that is on the earth.”

Arminian

The speech closes by restating the sign and the parties: God and all flesh. Noah is told so that the church after him can tell it too.

The fitting response is trust and humility. We live on borrowed earth under a promised sky—mercy that should make us gentle with life and ready to hear the fuller gospel.

Reformed

Established, not pending. The covenant is God’s done deed, signed in the cloud for all flesh on the earth.

Reformed doxology: from blessing to bow, the Lord has restabilized the world by oath. Gratitude, not anxiety, is the right weather for those who live under this sign.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on providence, covenant kindness, and God’s care for all flesh—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside the rainbow promise.

Arminian Notable voices

“I consider Divine Providence to be that solicitous, continued, and universally present inspection and oversight of God, according to which he exercises a general care over the whole world, but evinces a particular concern for all his intelligent creatures without any exception, with the design of preserving and governing them… I declare that it preserves, regulates, governs and directs all things and that nothing in the world happens fortuitously or by chance.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments, “The Providence of God”
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)

Reformed Notable voices

“I think the celestial arch which had before existed naturally, is here consecrated into a sign and pledge; and thus a new office is assigned to it… As often as the rain shall alarm you, look upon the bow. For although it may seem to cause the rain to overflow the earth, it shall nevertheless be to you a pledge of returning dryness.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 9:13)
“The distance between God and the creature is so great, that although reasonable creatures do owe obedience unto Him as their Creator, yet they could never have any fruition of Him as their blessedness and reward, but by some voluntary condescension on God’s part, which He hath been pleased to express by way of covenant.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · 7.1 (1647)
