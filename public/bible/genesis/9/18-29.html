Passage

Genesis 9

Verses 18–29 · ESV · Voices

Noah’s three sons go forth from the ark to people the earth; then the rescued patriarch plants a vineyard, falls in shameful drunkenness, and a family crisis ends in curse on Canaan and blessing on Shem and Japheth—before Noah’s long life closes.

Genesis 9:18

18The sons of Noah who went forth from the ark were Shem, Ham, and Japheth. (Ham was the father of Canaan.)

Arminian

The new world has three named sons, and Ham is already marked as Canaan’s father. Genealogy is about to become nations.

The parenthetical note is literary, not a racial theory. Scripture prepares us for Canaan’s later story; it does not license contempt for any people made in God’s image.

Reformed

Moses names the ark’s human seed and flags Canaan in advance. Providence is already tracing lines that later history will fill.

Reformed reading should be just as careful: this verse is table-of-nations setup, not a charter for supremacy. The God who blessed all three sons in 9:1 has not revoked the image of God.

Genesis 9:19

19These three were the sons of Noah, and from these the people of the whole earth were dispersed.

Arminian

The whole earth’s peoples disperse from one family. Human unity precedes later division.

Arminian pastoral implication: every nation shares this table. The gospel that will bless the nations speaks to relatives, not to foreign species.

Reformed

One household becomes the world’s population. The blessing to fill the earth is already working.

Reformed wonder at efficacy: from eight souls, God replenishes the planet. Dispersion is not a denial of common origin.

Genesis 9:20

20Noah began to be a man of the soil, and he planted a vineyard.

Arminian

The preacher of righteousness returns to ordinary work. Culture and cultivation restart.

A vineyard is a good gift. What follows will warn us not that farming is suspect, but that even holy people can mishandle God’s gifts.

Reformed

Noah takes up the soil again. Life after deliverance includes labor, not only altars.

The narrative is about to show that rescue from water did not rescue the heart from weakness. Common vocations remain the scene of both gratitude and fall.

Genesis 9:21

21He drank of the wine and became drunk and lay uncovered in his tent.

Arminian

The text does not flinch: Noah became drunk and lay uncovered. Grace that saved him through the flood did not make sin impossible.

Classical Arminians take the warning at face value. Remaining corruption is real; watchfulness is required. A great saint’s failure is neither denied nor treated as trivial.

Reformed

Deliverance from the old world did not deliver Noah from the flesh. The patriarch’s shame is recorded that we might fear drunkenness and pride.

Reformed pastoral use: election and preservation of the church’s line do not equal impeccability. God keeps His purposes through flawed people, never by calling their sin good.

Genesis 9:22

22And Ham, the father of Canaan, saw the nakedness of his father and told his two brothers outside.

Arminian

Ham sees and tells. The offense is not accidental glance but exposure—contempt that turns a father’s shame into a story.

Honor of parents is a real moral field. Arminian preaching names Ham’s agency: he could have covered; he advertised. Grace does not erase that choice.

Reformed

Moses again ties Ham to Canaan, because the sequel will fall there. The sin is dishonor: seeing and spreading what love would hide.

Reformed readers can admit both a wicked act and a providence that will later judge Canaanite culture, without making God the author of Ham’s scorn.

Genesis 9:23

23Then Shem and Japheth took a garment, laid it on both their shoulders, and walked backward and covered the nakedness of their father. Their faces were turned backward, and they did not see their father's nakedness.

Arminian

The brothers reverse Ham’s path: a garment, backward steps, covered shame, averted eyes. Love refuses to feast on another’s fall.

This is still the right family instinct. Covering is not pretending sin never happened; it is refusing to make spectacle of a parent’s humiliation.

Reformed

Piety toward a father becomes a picture of reverence. Shem and Japheth honor the man God used to save them, even when he is undignified.

Reformed ethics here is concrete: honor is a deed. The Spirit later praises such piety because it guards a holy order Ham treated as a joke.

Genesis 9:24

24When Noah awoke from his wine and knew what his youngest son had done to him,

Arminian

Noah wakes and knows. The story does not tell us every private detail; it tells us enough that a real wrong was done.

Responsibility runs both ways: the father’s drunkenness remains his, and the son’s deed remains his. Grace-enabled repentance would have been the better sequel for both.

Reformed

Knowledge after wine is bitter. Noah’s awakening does not undo the night, but it becomes the occasion of prophetic speech.

God can even use a humbled patriarch’s mouth. That does not baptize the drunkenness; it does mean the following words carry more than a hangover’s irritation.

Genesis 9:25

25he said, “Cursed be Canaan; a servant of servants shall he be to his brothers.”

Arminian

The curse names Canaan, not “Blackness,” not a race invented later. In the canon this looks toward Canaanite peoples who will oppose Israel—never a permission slip for kidnapping or chattel slavery of Africans.

Christians in both camps must say this plainly. Using Genesis 9 to bless racial slavery is a twisting of Scripture. The God who required a reckoning for man’s lifeblood (v. 5) is not authorizing contempt for image-bearers.

Reformed

Noah’s word is treated as prophetic: the line of Canaan will know servitude in the land’s later story. Ham is not erased, but the stroke falls where God will later judge particular nations for particular sins.

Reformed interpreters who honor providence still reject racist abuse of this verse. Election and judgment in history are God’s, and they never licensed the American or any other slave trade as “the curse of Ham.”

Genesis 9:26

26He also said, “Blessed be the Lord, the God of Shem; and let Canaan be his servant.

Arminian

Shem’s blessing is first a blessing of the Lord, Shem’s God. The center is worship, not clan pride.

The line of promise will run through Shem toward Abraham and, at last, toward Christ—good news for the nations, including whoever will dwell in those tents by faith.

Reformed

“Blessed be the Lord, the God of Shem” locates the gift in God. Shem is privileged as the bearer of the Name, not as a racial superior.

Reformed hope reads forward: from Shem’s God will come the covenant of grace. Servitude language about Canaan is historical-theological, not a theory of perpetual human worth.

Genesis 9:27

27May God enlarge Japheth, and let him dwell in the tents of Shem, and let Canaan be his servant.”

Arminian

Japheth is enlarged and invited into Shem’s tents—an early hint that outsiders may share Shem’s worship. The gospel later makes that hospitality explicit.

Arminian reading gladly hears inclusion: Gentile peoples may come in. The blessing is missional, not a wall.

Reformed

Calvin and others heard here the later ingathering of the nations into the church that began among Shem’s descendants. Enlargement is God’s doing.

Reformed comfort: Japheth’s dwelling in Shem’s tents is not an accident of empire but a promised widening of the household of faith—fulfilled as Gentiles are grafted into the covenant people in Christ.

Genesis 9:28

28After the flood Noah lived 350 years.

Arminian

Long years remain after both rescue and shame. God is patient with a flawed patriarch.

Time to live, work, and perhaps repent is itself mercy. The story does not freeze Noah in the vineyard scene forever.

Reformed

Three and a half centuries after the flood show the new world’s stability under the rainbow oath. Noah sees generations multiply.

Providence is long-suffering. The man God used is kept in life while the nations take shape from his sons.

Genesis 9:29

29All the days of Noah were 950 years, and he died.

Arminian

Even the survivor of the flood dies. Deliverance from water was not exemption from the grave.

The chapter that began in blessing ends in mortality—and still under promise. The God of the bow remains the hope of those who die waiting for a better rescue.

Reformed

Nine hundred fifty years, and he died. The refrain of Genesis 5 returns. The flood did not repeal death.

Reformed gospel horizon: the Noahic world is preserved so that, in due time, the last Adam would come. Noah’s death is not the end of God’s oath; it is another marker that we need a Savior stronger than a patriarch.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Noah’s vineyard, Ham’s shame, and the blessing of Shem—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 9:18–29.

Arminian Notable voices

“Sometime after his departure out of the ark he returned to his old employment, from which he had been diverted by the building of the ark first, and probably after by the building an house for himself and family. And he planted a vineyard—And when he had gathered his vintage, probably he appointed a day of mirth and feasting in his family, and had his sons and their children with him, to rejoice with him in the increase of his house, as well as in the increase of his vineyard; and we may suppose he prefaced his feast with a sacrifice to the honour of God.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 9:20)

Reformed Notable voices

“I do not so explain. the words, as if he then, for the first time, began to give his attention to the cultivation of the fields; but, (in my opinion,) Moses rather intimates, that Noah, with a collected mind, though now an old man, returned to the culture of the fields, and to his former labors. It is, however, uncertain whether he had been a vine-dresser or not.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 9:20)
