Passage

Genesis 3

Verses 1–7 · ESV · Voices

The serpent questions God’s word, the woman answers, and a lie that they will not die opens a path of desire. She takes and eats, gives to her husband who is with her, and he eats; their eyes open to shame, and fig leaves become the first cover. Genesis 3:1–7 is the turn from innocence to transgression: a real command, a real choice, and a world now knowing good and evil from the inside.

Genesis 3:1

1Now the serpent was more crafty than any other beast of the field that the LORD God had made. He said to the woman, “Did God actually say, ‘You shall not eat of any tree in the garden’?”

Arminian

The narrator calls the serpent “more crafty” than the beasts the LORD God had made. The enemy’s first words twist the gift into a question: did God really forbid every tree? Both camps hear Satan’s instrument here, not a second god, and both hear a command that was already clear.

Classical Arminians stress that this is a real temptation addressed to creatures still innocent and able to refuse. The question is designed to be answered from God’s generous word; they were not shoved toward unbelief, and grace had already given them light enough to say no.

Reformed

Craftiness belongs to a creature the LORD God had made. Providence has not lost the garden; the tempter is permitted to speak, not licensed as a rival Creator. The question already distorts the wide permission of chapter 2.

Reformed readers confess God’s ordaining permission of this hour without making Him the author of the lie. The first assault is on the word. What follows will show that God’s speech was sufficient; the craft is in recasting gift as stinginess.

Genesis 3:2

2And the woman said to the serpent, “We may eat of the fruit of the trees in the garden,

Arminian

She answers with what is true: they may eat of the trees. She has not yet taken the fruit; she still names the garden as gift. The conversation is not over.

Arminian pastoral reading: memory of God’s generosity is still available. She can still refuse the serpent by clinging to what she has just confessed. Innocence here is not ignorance; it is a living chance to trust the Giver.

Reformed

She begins well: the trees are for food. The danger is not that she speaks, but that she remains in dialogue with a questioner who does not love her.

Reformed note: even a true sentence can be the first step of lingering where we should flee. The word already given in 2:16–17 was enough. Shared ground holds—the permission was clear; the crisis is the company she keeps.

Genesis 3:3

3but God said, ‘You shall not eat of the fruit of the tree that is in the midst of the garden, neither shall you touch it, lest you die.’”

Arminian

She reports the ban: that tree is not for them, and death is in view. She also adds “neither shall you touch it,” and frames the warning as “lest you die.” The command is still in sight, even if the wording is not a perfect echo of 2:17.

Classical Arminians need not pile a system on the extra clause. God had spoken; she knew a limit; she remains responsible to keep it. The remembered word is already light enough for a real refusal.

Reformed

The prohibition is acknowledged: that tree is not theirs, and death is the stated end. Whether “touch it” is caution or a slight thickening of God’s speech, the law was not obscure.

Reformed instinct: adding to—or softening “you shall surely die” into “lest you die”—can be a hairline crack in trust. Shared ground still holds: the command was clear. The issue will not be lack of information.

Genesis 3:4

4But the serpent said to the woman, “You will not surely die.

Arminian

The serpent now contradicts God outright: “You will not surely die.” Temptation has moved from a question to a lie. The woman is invited to treat the LORD as untrustworthy.

Arminian emphasis: a lie can be refused. Innocence is not deafness to God’s voice; it is the still-open possibility of believing Him. She is not yet a sinner for hearing the sentence; she will be for trusting it.

Reformed

Here is the rival gospel: God is wrong about death. Unbelief is offered as wisdom. The Creator’s word and the creature’s word now stand opposed.

Reformed reading: this is not a small clarification. The fall, when it comes, will be unbelief first—refusing God’s threat and, in the next breath, His character. The enemy cannot create; he can only recast God.

Genesis 3:5

5For God knows that when you eat of it your eyes will be opened, and you will be like God, knowing good and evil.”

Arminian

The lie wears a promise: opened eyes, likeness to God, knowing good and evil. Desire is baited with a false ascent, and the Giver is painted as a withholder.

Classical Arminians hear pride’s invitation without denying that the pair could still say no. Likeness to God was already theirs as image-bearers (1:26–27); the serpent offers autonomy instead of communion. The appeal is strong, not irresistible.

Reformed

“For God knows…” accuses love of envy. To be “like God” here means to seize the knowledge of good and evil on our own terms—pride after unbelief.

Reformed accent: the temptation is theological before it is dietary. Distrust of God’s goodness and a grab at His prerogative will soon appear in the eating. Shared ground: this is not a search for wisdom God had forbidden as cruel; it is a bid to be judge in His place.

Genesis 3:6

6So when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes, and that the tree was to be desired to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate, and she also gave some to her husband who was with her, and he ate.

Arminian

She sees that the tree is good for food, a delight to the eyes, and desired to make one wise; she takes, eats, and gives to her husband who is with her, and he eats. The verbs are hers and his. No coercion is named.

Classical Arminians stress free, uncoerced eating in innocence: a real choice, then inherited corruption for their children. Adam’s eating is not a footnote. 1 Timothy 2:14 notes that he was not deceived as she was—without treating the woman as the sole villain. Both ate; both fell.

Reformed

Sight, desire, taking, eating, giving, eating: unbelief ripens into act. The husband is “with her.” Romans 5 will name Adam as the covenant head through whom sin and death enter; 1 Timothy 2:14 adds that he was not deceived—he walked in with his eyes more open, and that is not an excuse to scorn Eve.

Reformed reading: this is federal as well as personal. God ordained to permit the fall without authoring the sin. Pride and distrust, not a shove, undo the pair; the race will now stand in Adam unless a new Head is given.

Genesis 3:7

7Then the eyes of both were opened, and they knew that they were naked. And they sewed fig leaves together and made themselves loincloths.

Arminian

Eyes open, and they know they are naked. Shame follows sin as surely as fig leaves follow shame. What the serpent promised as godlike knowing arrives as exposure.

Arminian pastoral note: conscience still works. They feel the rupture they chose. Covering themselves is real human agency after the fall—busy, inadequate, and already a confession that something is wrong. Both camps agree: the pair cannot sew their way back to 2:25.

Reformed

The knowledge they seized is not the wisdom they were sold. Nakedness, once innocent (2:25), is now shame. Fig-leaf loincloths are our first religion of self-repair.

Reformed diagnosis: guilt and corruption are already at work. Common-grace instinct still seeks cover, but the cover is too thin. God will have to clothe them later; here we see that opened eyes without grace only teach us to hide.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the serpent’s temptation and the first disobedience—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 3:1–7.

Arminian Notable voices

“Multitudes of them fell; but this that attacked our first parents, was surely the prince of the devils. Whether it was only the appearance of a serpent, or a real serpent, acted and possessed by the devil, is not certain. The devil chose to act his part in a serpent, because it is a subtle creature.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 3:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“Now the serpent was more subtil In this chapter, Moses explains, that man, after he had been deceived by Satan revolted from his Maker, became entirely changed and so degenerate, that the image of God, in which he had been formed, was obliterated. He then declares, that the whole world, which had been created for the sake of man, fell together with him from its primary original; and that in this ways much of its native excellence was destroyed.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 3:1)
