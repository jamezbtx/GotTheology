Passage

Genesis 3

Verses 16–24 · ESV

Pain in childbearing, a disordered desire and rule between wife and husband, a cursed ground, sweat, and dust-to-dust mortality: the LORD names what sin has done. Yet He clothes them with skins, and Eve is named mother of all living, before cherubim and a flaming sword bar the way to the tree of life. Genesis 3:16–24 is judgment with mercy in it—exile that both punishes and protects, while the promise of 3:15 still stands.

Genesis 3:16

16To the woman he said, “I will surely multiply your pain in childbearing; in pain you shall bring forth children. Your desire shall be for your husband, and he shall rule over you.”

Arminian

Pain is multiplied in childbearing; children will still come, but through pain. “Your desire shall be for your husband, and he shall rule over you.” After the fall, the closest bond is disordered—not erased.

Classical Arminians typically hear consequence, not a new creation ordinance of domination. This is what sin does to marriage; it is not a license for abuse. In Christ the curse-tinged rule is to be redeemed, not baptized as God’s ideal for husbands.

Reformed

The woman’s sentence is pain and a bent relationship: desire and rule now grate rather than serve. Both camps historically refuse to treat this as permission to harm.

Reformed readers often hear creation order intensified as judgment—headship was not invented here (2:18–24), but after the fall it is heavy. The LORD names a wound He did not author as sin, even as He remains Lord over the household that must now live east of innocence.

Genesis 3:17

17And to Adam he said, “Because you have listened to the voice of your wife and have eaten of the tree of which I commanded you, ‘You shall not eat of it,’ cursed is the ground because of you; in pain you shall eat of it all the days of your life;

Arminian

Adam is judged for listening to his wife and eating the forbidden tree. The ground is cursed because of him; in pain he will eat of it all his days. His listening was a real, unforced revolt.

Arminian emphasis: the earth suffers because the image-bearer refused the word. Grace had given a clear command; the sentence answers that refusal, not an unseen decree that made eating inevitable. Shared ground: Adam is not a bystander in this verse.

Reformed

“Because you have listened… and have eaten”: the covenant head is addressed, and the ground is cursed “because of you.” Romans 5 will make this representative: in Adam death spread to all.

Reformed accent: God ordained to permit this without being the author of Adam’s sin. The field now fights the farmer because the representative fought his Maker. Judgment is personal and cosmic at once. Federal headship is already on the soil.

Genesis 3:18

18thorns and thistles it shall bring forth for you; and you shall eat the plants of the field.

Arminian

Thorns and thistles, yet still “the plants of the field.” The curse is real; so is remaining provision. Work is not abolished; it is made hard.

Arminian pastoral note: common kindness in a judged world invites gratitude and repentance, not presumption. The ground yields food enough to live and enough frustration to remember the fall. Mercy here does not cancel the need of the promised Seed.

Reformed

Thorns answer the man who reached for a tree. Yet he will eat. Mercy is stitched into judgment: the field still feeds, though it now resists.

Reformed instinct: this is common-grace provision under curse. God does not owe the rebel a harvest, and He still gives one. The world is not discarded; it is placed under a discipline that looks toward redemption.

Genesis 3:19

19By the sweat of your face you shall eat bread, till you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; for you are dust, and to dust you shall return.”

Arminian

Sweat, bread, return to the ground: dust to dust. Mortality is now the human story. The warning of 2:17 has arrived as a way of being, not only as a moment.

Classical Arminians take this as the real consequence of a chosen revolt. Death is not a metaphor. The promise of 3:15 still sits over this dust—life will have to come as gift, received by faith, not as a right seized from the tree.

Reformed

Bread by sweat until dust returns to dust. The wages of sin are not delayed forever. Out of the ground he was taken; to it he goes.

Reformed confession: original guilt and corruption now have a cemetery. Federal death in Adam is as wide as the race. Even here the sentence is measured—he will eat until he returns, not be unmade on the spot. Common-grace mercy remains in the bread of the interval.

Genesis 3:20

20The man called his wife’s name Eve, because she was the mother of all living.

Arminian

The man names his wife Eve, “because she was the mother of all living.” After dust-talk, he speaks life. The name leans on the promise just given.

Arminian reading: this is faith’s instinct, not denial. If the Seed of the woman will bruise the serpent, motherhood is not futile. Hope names her against the grain of verse 19—and that hope is the gospel already offered.

Reformed

Eve—“living”—is named under a death sentence. The line will continue because God said enmity and offspring, not because the pair can guarantee a future.

Reformed comfort: providence keeps the mother of all living in the story He will save. The name is a confession that life still belongs to the LORD who judged them. Shared ground: this is not cynicism; it is hope under sentence.

Genesis 3:21

21And the LORD God made for Adam and for his wife garments of skins and clothed them.

Arminian

The LORD God makes garments of skins and clothes them. Fig leaves are replaced by a covering He provides. They do not dress themselves out of this shame.

Classical Arminians see mercy that invites faith in the promised Seed. Do not over-claim a “first sacrifice” the text does not name; do claim a God who covers. The same Lord who sentenced them now stoops to clothe them.

Reformed

Skins instead of leaves: God covers what they cannot cover. Many Reformed readers hear a type of substitutionary covering—life given for a covering—while still refusing to force later ritual onto a verse that does not say “sacrifice.”

Reformed gospel instinct: clothing is grace in the midst of judgment. The Judge is also the dresser of Adam and Eve. What He provides is enough to walk east in, not enough to reopen the tree of life. Shared ground: this covering is gift.

Genesis 3:22

22Then the LORD God said, “Behold, the man has become like one of us in knowing good and evil. Now, lest he reach out his hand and take also of the tree of life and eat, and live forever—”

Arminian

“Behold, the man has become like one of us in knowing good and evil.” The seized likeness is tragic, not triumphant. The unfinished sentence—lest he take also of the tree of life and live forever—treats endless life in sin as a horror to be prevented.

Arminian note: exile is judgment and protection. They chose revolt; they must not be immortalized in it. The barred tree is severe kindness, and 3:15 is still held out on the other side of the gate.

Reformed

The “us” of holy counsel names a knowledge now twisted by guilt. Living forever in that state would freeze rebellion. The LORD speaks a purpose before He acts.

Reformed accent: God sovereignly bars the tree. This is not panic; it is mercy in judgment. The man who grabbed at divine prerogative will not also grab immortality on his own terms. Shared ground: exile keeps them from being sealed in sin.

Genesis 3:23

23therefore the LORD God sent him out from the garden of Eden to work the ground from which he was taken.

Arminian

Therefore the LORD God sent him out to work the ground from which he was taken. Vocation continues east of Eden: the soil of his making is now the soil of his labor and his grave.

Classical Arminians hear a real consequence of a chosen revolt. Sending is not theater. Yet the man is sent to work, not to nowhere—the promise of the Seed goes with a working, mortal pair who may still believe it.

Reformed

“Therefore” ties the expulsion to the danger of the tree of life. God sends; the man does not wander out on a whim. Work remains, now outside the garden.

Reformed reading: sovereign exile. The ground from which he was taken will occupy him until it receives him. Judgment assigns the task; providence still gives him a place to do it. Common-grace mercy: he is sent to work, not erased.

Genesis 3:24

24He drove out the man, and at the east of the garden of Eden he placed the cherubim and a flaming sword that turned every way to guard the way to the tree of life.

Arminian

He drove out the man, and east of the garden He placed cherubim and a flaming sword turning every way to guard the way to the tree of life. Return on our terms is impossible.

Arminian pastoral close: this is the cost of the revolt they chose, and it is also protection from a cursed immortality. The way to life will have to be gift through the promised Seed, not a back door past the sword. The promise of 3:15 is still held out east of the gate.

Reformed

Drove out, placed, guarded: the verbs are God’s. Cherubim and a turning sword make the bar visible. No human hero will rush the tree.

Reformed confession: the sovereign bar stands until God Himself opens a better way. The church later learns that way in the wounded Seed of 3:15. Mercy and judgment share this gate—life is not seized; it is given. Shared ground: exile is both sentence and shield.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
