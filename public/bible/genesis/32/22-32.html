Genesis 32

Verses 22–32 · ESV · Voices

The same night Jacob sends wives, servants, and the eleven across the Jabbok and is left alone. A man wrestles him till dawn, puts the hip out, and is held for a blessing. Jacob becomes Israel at Peniel—face of God—and limps into sunrise while Israel remembers the sinew.

Genesis 32:22

22The same night he arose and took his two wives, his two female servants, and his eleven children, and crossed the ford of the Jabbok.

Arminian

Night crossing: two wives, two servants, eleven children, the ford of the Jabbok. He moves the family toward the feared meeting before he is finished with God.

Arminian reading: love of the household is real agency. Grace does not freeze him on the near bank.

Reformed

The Jabbok is named because the wrestle is coming. Family is sent while the night still belongs to prayer.

Reformed note: providence times the ford. The heir will meet God before he meets Esau.

Genesis 32:23

23He took them and sent them across the stream, and everything else that he had.

Arminian

He sends them across, and everything else that he had. The last assets go. Alone is now possible.

Classical Arminians hear a chosen solitude. He makes room to cling; the stream is not an accident.

Reformed

Wives, children, goods—all put over. Calvin: solitude suited prayer at the extremity of peril.

Reformed framing: God often strips the stage before He grapples.

Genesis 32:24

24And Jacob was left alone. And a man wrestled with him until the breaking of the day.

Arminian

Left alone, a man wrestles him until daybreak. Hosea 12:3–4 will name weeping and supplication. The fight is bodily and spiritual.

Arminian emphasis: clinging is a real, grace-enabled contest. He is not a puppet in a night pageant; he holds, and the hold matters.

Reformed

Moses says a man; the chapter will say God. Faith’s trials are wrestling with God, who as antagonist tests strength He also supplies.

Reformed core: He fights against us and for us. The visible bout is the church’s daily hidden one.

Key contrast Both deny that Jacob prevails by native muscle. Arminians typically stress enabled, resistible clinging in prayer; Reformed readers typically stress that God Himself apportions the conflict and grants the strength that wins.

Genesis 32:25

25When the man saw that he did not prevail against Jacob, he touched his hip socket, and Jacob’s hip was put out of joint as he wrestled with him.

Arminian

The man does not prevail, then touches the hip; the joint gives. Wesley: not Jacob’s strength—heaven’s—and the disjointing shows it was God, for no man does that with a touch.

Arminian pastoral: wound and help can arrive together. The limp will preach dependence without canceling the hold.

Reformed

Victory without an intact thigh. Calvin: the faithful come forth conquerors only by being injured in the conflict, lest the flesh grow haughty.

Reformed humility: exaltation is joined to weakness. God is made perfect in the limp.

Genesis 32:26

26Then he said, “Let me go, for the day has broken.” But Jacob said, “I will not let you go unless you bless me.”

Arminian

“Let me go, for the day has broken.” Jacob: I will not, unless you bless me. Hosea’s tears sit under the sentence.

Classical Arminians hear holy insistence. Grace that began the fight now meets a will that refuses empty dawn. The blessing is asked, not seized as loot.

Reformed

God concedes the name of victory to the servant and is “ready to depart,” that Jacob may rejoice in grace given.

Reformed perseverance: never grow weary till the Lord recedes of His own accord. Blessing is begged from the Greater—proof Jacob now knows who holds him.

Key contrast Shared ground: blessing must come from this One. Arminians typically hear a genuine condition—hold on, ask; Reformed readers typically hear God willing the plea He then answers.

Genesis 32:27

27And he said to him, “What is your name?” And he said, “Jacob.”

Arminian

“What is your name?” “Jacob”—supplanter, heel-grabber, the old title spoken in the dark.

Arminian note: he has to say the old name before the new. Confession of what he has been is part of the blessing’s door.

Reformed

The question is not ignorance. God draws the name out so it can be retired in public.

Reformed reading: identity is surrendered before it is remade. Jacob cannot keep the heel-name as his last word.

Genesis 32:28

28Then he said, “Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel, for you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed.”

Arminian

No longer Jacob but Israel: striven with God and with men, and prevailed. A prince with God, Wesley says, is one mighty in prayer—and therefore not helpless before Esau.

Arminian joy: prevailing is given, yet it is truly Jacob’s clinging. The new name honors a contest grace made possible, not a merit badge for cunning.

Reformed

The name carries the thing: God transfers the praise of His own strength into Jacob’s title so the man will go to later wars as a victor already named.

Reformed gift: Israel is not self-made. He ruled with God because God let him, armed him, and then wrote the name.

Key contrast Both hear a new name as grace. Arminians typically underline a real striving that could have let go; Reformed readers typically underline God’s decision to let the servant prevail by strength God supplied.

Genesis 32:29

29Then Jacob asked him, “Please tell me your name.” But he said, “Why is it that you ask my name?” And there he blessed him.

Arminian

Jacob asks the man’s name and is refused; yet he is blessed there. Full sight is not yet. The blessing does not wait for a complete file on God.

Classical Arminians: asking for more light is pious; grabbing control by a name is not. He receives blessing without the lever of the Name.

Reformed

Calvin: the time of full revelation was not yet; Christ the Sun had not risen. “Why do you ask?” bounds curiosity without canceling kindness.

Reformed limit: God blesses and still hides. Patriarchs walked in morning twilight; we are not to demand more than our hour.

Genesis 32:30

30So Jacob called the name of the place Peniel, saying, “For I have seen God face to face, and yet my life has been delivered.”

Arminian

Peniel—face of God—because he has seen God face to face and yet his life is delivered. Survival is itself the miracle.

Arminian wonder: the Holy One can be met and not consume. Delivered life is mercy after a night of holding on.

Reformed

Not a private souvenir: Calvin says the vision refers to the whole church. Face to face would undo us unless He sustained us.

Reformed gratitude: he names the place so grace will not perish from memory. Seeing God and living is indulgence, not entitlement.

Genesis 32:31

31The sun rose upon him as he passed Penuel, limping because of his hip.

Arminian

Sunrise, Penuel, a limp. The night’s mark walks into ordinary day. Some think the halt lasted his life; if so, honor outweighed the hitch.

Arminian pastoral: healed relationship with God may still leave a hitch in the gait. Carry the limp as reminder, not as regret that you held on.

Reformed

The sun that rises is God’s, and so is the limp. The mark proves the night was no dream.

Reformed witness: weakness clings to the conqueror so no Israelite forgets whose help won.

Genesis 32:32

32Therefore to this day the people of Israel do not eat the sinew of the thigh that is on the hip socket, because he touched the socket of Jacob’s hip on the sinew of the thigh.

Arminian

To this day Israel will not eat that sinew, because He touched that socket. A table custom keeps Peniel in the body of a people.

Classical Arminians: memory can be trained. Grace that met one man now catechizes children at meals.

Reformed

Moses notes a lasting abstinence. Infancy of the church used an outward sign; the memory still belongs to us even in a freer age.

Reformed close: God disciplines a people by a sinew so the wound of mercy is not forgotten.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jacob’s wrestling, prayer, and the God who wounds to bless—so each camp speaks in its own voice at Peniel.

Arminian Notable voices

“It was not in his own strength that he wrestled, nor by his own strength that he prevails; but by strength derived from heaven… The angel put out Jacob’s thigh, to shew him what he could do, and that it was God he was wrestling with.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 32:25)
“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)

Reformed Notable voices

“We do not fight against him, except by his own power, and with his own weapons; for he, having challenged us to this contest, at the same time furnishes us with means of resistance, so that he both fights against us and for us… he fights against us with his left hand, and for us with his right hand.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 32:24)
“The most wise, righteous, and gracious God doth oftentimes leave, for a season, His own children to manifold temptations… to humble them, to raise them to a more close and constant dependence for their support upon Himself, and to make them more watchful against all future occasions of sin.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · 5.5 (1647)
