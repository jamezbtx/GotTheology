Passage

Genesis 22

Verses 1–19 · WEB · Voices

After the birth of Isaac and the sending of Ishmael, God tests Abraham: take your only son, whom you love, and offer him on a mountain in Moriah. Abraham rises early, walks three days, answers Isaac that God will provide the lamb, binds his son, and is stopped by the angel of the LORD. A ram is offered instead. The mount is named “The LORD will provide,” and the oath of blessing is sworn because Abraham did not withhold his son.

Genesis 22:1

1After these things, God tested Abraham, and said to him, “Abraham!” He said, “Here I am.”

Arminian

God tests Abraham; He does not tempt him to evil. The call is personal—“Abraham!”—and the answer is availability: “Here I am.”

This is a trial of love. Grace has brought Abraham this far; the test will reveal whether he will keep clinging to the Giver above the gift. The response remains his, enabled but not forced.

Reformed

“After these things” follows Isaac’s birth and Ishmael’s sending. The Tester is God; the test is not Satan’s snare but the Father’s proving.

God already knows the heart, yet He appoints trials that make faith public. Abraham’s “Here I am” is the fruit of a life God has been forming.

Key contrast Both deny that God entices to sin. Arminians stress a genuine, grace-enabled trial Abraham might have refused; Reformed interpreters stress a Father-appointed proving that will display faith God Himself sustains.

Genesis 22:2

2He said, “Now take your son, your only son, Isaac, whom you love, and go into the land of Moriah. Offer him there as a burnt offering on one of the mountains which I will tell you of.”

Arminian

The command piles love on love: your son, your only son, whom you love. Moriah is unnamed in detail until obedience walks.

This is no theatrical order. Abraham must actually go. Grace does not undo the horror of the ask; it enables trust that God’s character is better than the knife.

Reformed

Isaac is named as the loved only son—the child of promise. The burnt offering would seem to unmake Genesis 12–21.

The God who promised offspring now commands the offering. Faith must hold promise and command together until God Himself resolves them.

Key contrast Both refuse to flatten the command. Arminians emphasize Abraham’s costly, enabled choice to obey; Reformed readers highlight God’s sovereignty in commanding what only He can later explain and provide.

Genesis 22:3

3Abraham rose early in the morning, and saddled his donkey; and took two of his young men with him, and Isaac his son. He split the wood for the burnt offering, and rose up, and went to the place of which God had told him.

Arminian

Early rising, saddled donkey, cut wood: obedience is practical, not delayed debate. Isaac is taken along as commanded.

Classical Arminians honor prompt faith. Assisting grace does not wait for full explanation; Abraham walks while the wound is still fresh.

Reformed

Scripture records no argument from Abraham. Morning action follows night’s word. Wood is prepared by the father who must also carry the knife.

Providence is already in the details—the place God had told him, the son in tow. Obedience here is faith with a saddle and an axe.

Genesis 22:4

4On the third day Abraham lifted up his eyes, and saw the place far off.

Arminian

Three days of walking with the command unanswered. Sight of the place from afar lengthens the trial rather than ending it.

Faith has time to turn back and does not. Grace sustains a long obedience, not only a moment’s zeal.

Reformed

The third day becomes the horizon of the test. Abraham sees the appointed mountain before he climbs it.

Reformed readers hear providence in the delay: God times the seeing. The place is not stumbled upon; it is shown.

Genesis 22:5

5Abraham said to his young men, “Stay here with the donkey. The boy and I will go over there. We will worship, and come back to you.”

Arminian

“Worship” and “come back” sit together. Abraham speaks of return while carrying wood for a burnt offering.

Arminians hear Hebrews 11 in seed form: he trusts God can restore Isaac. The confession is sincere hope, not a polite lie to the servants.

Reformed

The servants are left behind. Worship is named as the errand, and return is spoken as fact.

Abraham’s “we will come again” rests on God’s power to raise, even if the knife falls. Promise still governs the mountain.

Genesis 22:6

6Abraham took the wood of the burnt offering and laid it on Isaac his son. He took in his hand the fire and the knife. They both went together.

Arminian

Isaac bears the wood; Abraham bears fire and knife. They walk together—father and son in one hard fellowship.

The scene invites later readers toward another Son who carried wood up a hill. Faith here is companionship under a terrible word.

Reformed

Wood on the beloved son; instruments of death in the father’s hand. Both of them together is the narrator’s refrain.

Reformed typology is cautious but authentic: the promised seed bears the wood toward the place God chose. The Father’s purpose and the son’s path meet on the road.

Genesis 22:7

7Isaac spoke to Abraham his father, and said, “My father?” He said, “Here I am, my son.” He said, “Here is the fire and the wood, but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?”

Arminian

Isaac’s “My father!” mirrors Abraham’s “Here I am.” The missing lamb is the child’s honest question.

Faith must answer a son without cynicism. The question is not rebellion; it is trust asking for the piece that is not yet visible.

Reformed

The boy sees fire and wood and names the absence. Abraham answers with presence—“Here I am, my son”—before he answers with theology.

The lamb question is the hinge of the chapter. God will fill what Isaac cannot yet see.

Genesis 22:8

8Abraham said, “God will provide himself the lamb for a burnt offering, my son.” So they both went together.

Arminian

“God will provide himself the lamb.” Provision is God’s business; walking on is Abraham’s. They go together still.

Arminians hear living faith speaking before the ram appears. Grace taught this sentence; Abraham must keep walking as if it is true.

Reformed

The verb is God’s: He will provide himself the lamb is not Abraham’s invention but the Lord’s supply.

Jehovah-jireh is confessed before it is seen. Faith names God’s character, then takes the next step together.

Key contrast Both treasure this confession. Arminians stress faith that keeps walking on a promise it might still refuse; Reformed commentators emphasize that God Himself will supply the lamb His purpose requires.

Genesis 22:9

9They came to the place which God had told him of. Abraham built the altar there, and laid the wood in order, bound Isaac his son, and laid him on the altar, on the wood.

Arminian

Altar, ordered wood, bound son. Isaac is not a toddler wrestled down; the text lets him be laid on the wood.

Abraham’s obedience and Isaac’s yieldedness. Grace is at work in more than one will on that mountain.

Reformed

The place is the one God told. Building and binding complete the commanded sequence up to the edge.

Nothing here is accidental architecture. The altar is where promise and command finally meet, awaiting God’s interruption.

Genesis 22:10

10Abraham stretched out his hand, and took the knife to kill his son.

Arminian

The hand and the knife are authentic. Faith is not a metaphor at this point; it is a reached arm.

Classical Arminians do not romanticize the moment. Obedience has gone as far as it can without becoming the sin God never wanted.

Reformed

The narrator slows on the reach. Intent is complete; the slaughter is a breath away.

God allowed the trial to this brink so that the halt and the ram would be unmistakably His.

Genesis 22:11

11The LORD’s angel called to him out of the sky, and said, “Abraham, Abraham!” He said, “Here I am.”

Arminian

Heaven breaks in with a doubled name. Abraham answers the same way he began: “Here I am.”

The Tester also rescues. Grace that asked now stops the knife; the man who kept answering is still listening.

Reformed

The angel of the LORD calls from heaven—urgent, personal, twice. The same availability that received the test now receives the stay.

Intervention is God’s timing, not Abraham’s invention. The voice from heaven owns both the trial and the halt.

Genesis 22:12

12He said, “Don’t lay your hand on the boy or do anything to him. For now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me.”

Arminian

“Now I know” is covenant language of demonstrated fear, not divine ignorance. The boy is spared; the fear of God has been shown.

Classical Arminians stress that Abraham truly did not withhold. Grace enabled a free, costly yes that could have been a no.

Reformed

The hand is forbidden; the knowledge is declared. Fear of God is proved in not withholding the only son.

God “knows” here as Judge who now publicly owns what He had ordained to display. The son is safe because the LORD says so.

Key contrast Both hear deep fear of God in not withholding Isaac. Arminians emphasize a demonstrated, resistible obedience; Reformed readers emphasize God’s appointed proof of a fear He Himself worked.

Genesis 22:13

13Abraham lifted up his eyes, and looked, and saw that behind him was a ram caught in the thicket by his horns. Abraham went and took the ram, and offered him up for a burnt offering instead of his son.

Arminian

Eyes lift; a ram is caught. “Instead of his son” is substitution in a sentence. The provided lamb is not a slogan yet—it is blood on the same wood.

God supplies what He required. Faith receives the substitute and does not insist on finishing the first terrible offering.

Reformed

Behold: a ram already caught, not hunted up by panic. The burnt offering proceeds, but the son lives.

Reformed heart of the scene: instead. Another dies in Isaac’s place. The mountain preaches provision and substitution before the Law is given.

Key contrast Both confess a substitute. Arminians stress receiving the ram God provides in place of the son; Reformed interpreters usually emphasize God’s prepared victim, already caught, accomplishing the offering His justice required.

Genesis 22:14

14Abraham called the name of that place “The LORD Will Provide”. As it is said to this day, “On the LORD’s mountain, it will be provided.”

Arminian

The place gets a name so memory will not shrink to private relief. Jehovah-jireh becomes a proverb: on the LORD’s mount it shall be provided.

Name the provision, then live as if future need will meet the same God. Grace that provided once still invites trust.

Reformed

Naming is theology. “The LORD will provide” turns a past ram into a standing confession.

The proverb looks beyond Moriah. What God provided here trains the church to look to another mount where He provides Himself.

Genesis 22:15

15The LORD’s angel called to Abraham a second time out of the sky,

Arminian

A second call follows the offering. God is not finished once the knife is down.

Mercy keeps speaking. The same voice that stopped the hand now opens the oath.

Reformed

Heaven speaks again. The trial’s end is not silence but further word.

The angel halts the knife, the ram is provided, the place is named, and only then does God swear his oath. God interprets what He has done by swearing.

Genesis 22:16

16and said, “‘I have sworn by myself,’ says the LORD, ‘because you have done this thing, and have not withheld your son, your only son,

Arminian

God swears by Himself. The ground named is Abraham’s deed: you have not withheld your only son.

Classical Arminians take “because” seriously. Obedient faith is the appointed condition under which the sworn blessing is confirmed—never meriting the promise, yet truly engaged by grace.

Reformed

The LORD swears by Himself—there is none greater. “Because you have done this” attaches the oath to the demonstrated fear.

The promise was already given; the oath now confirms it through the obedience God produced. Hebrews 6 will call this unchangeable counsel.

Key contrast Both hear a divine oath tied to not withholding Isaac. Arminians typically treat obedient faith as an enabled condition of the confirmation; Reformed commentators often treat the obedience as God’s appointed means of confirming a promise He had already sworn to keep.

Genesis 22:17

17that I will bless you greatly, and I will multiply your offspring greatly like the stars of the heavens, and like the sand which is on the seashore. Your offspring will possess the gate of his enemies.

Arminian

Double surely: bless and multiply. Stars and sand return, now with gates of enemies possessed.

The blessing is wide enough for nations yet to come. Grace that confirmed Abraham still invites his offspring to walk in the same trust.

Reformed

Blessing and multiplication are God’s certainties. Offspring like stars and sand will take the enemy’s gate.

The oath’s content is victory and fruitfulness God Himself performs. The seed’s future does not rest on Abraham’s nerve remaining high.

Genesis 22:18

18All the nations of the earth will be blessed by your offspring, because you have obeyed my voice.’”

Arminian

All nations blessed in the offspring—gospel width from Moriah. “Because you have obeyed my voice” again ties the story to hearing that acts.

Arminian readers preach this as sincere global mercy. The Seed is Christ; the nations are truly invited; obedience of faith remains the way people share the blessing.

Reformed

The Abrahamic promise to the nations is restated from the mountain. Obedience of Abraham’s voice-hearing is named as the because.

Galatians 3 hears this offspring as Christ. Nations are blessed in Him by God’s sworn purpose, not by their climbing Moriah on their own.

Key contrast Both confess worldwide blessing in Abraham’s offspring. Arminians press a sincere offer received by obedient faith; Reformed readers underline God’s sworn, effectual blessing of the nations in the promised Seed.

Genesis 22:19

19So Abraham returned to his young men, and they rose up and went together to Beersheba. Abraham lived at Beersheba.

Arminian

Return to the servants fulfills “we will come again.” Beersheba becomes home again after the mount.

Faith comes down the mountain and lives. The test does not become a permanent theater; it becomes a named place and a kept oath.

Reformed

They go together to Beersheba; Abraham lives there. Ordinary geography resumes under an extraordinary oath.

The God who provided also returns His man to ordinary life. The ram, the name, and the oath travel with him.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on trial, faith, and the provided lamb, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“God did tempt Abraham — that is, try him, whether he loved him best. Not that God was ignorant how the case stood, but to make it evident, both to Abraham and to others… God never tempts any man to sin; but he tries his people’s faith and obedience.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 22:1)
“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)

Reformed Notable voices

“This was no ordinary trial… Abraham is commanded to slay his only and beloved son, in whom the hope of salvation was placed. Faith, however, at length overcame… He who had received the promise was ready to offer up his only begotten.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 22:1–2; cf. Heb 11:17)
“Faith… is the work of the Spirit of Christ in their hearts, and is ordinarily wrought by the ministry of the Word… By this faith, a Christian believeth to be true whatsoever is revealed in the Word… acting differently upon that which each particular passage thereof containeth; yielding obedience to the commands, trembling at the threatenings, and embracing the promises of God for this life, and that which is to come.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · 14.1–2 (1647)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
