Passage

Genesis 39

Verses 1–18 · ESV

Joseph is sold into Potiphar’s house. The LORD is with him; blessing spills onto an Egyptian household. Potiphar’s wife presses him day after day; he refuses as sin against God, flees, and is framed by the garment he left behind.

Genesis 39:1

1Now Joseph had been brought down to Egypt, and Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh, the captain of the guard, an Egyptian, had bought him from the Ishmaelites who had brought him down there.

Arminian

Brought down, bought, named owners. The narrator does not flinch at slavery’s facts. Classical Arminians still see a man, not a pawn: Joseph will soon have to choose under this roof.

Reformed

The Ishmaelites of chapter 37 deliver him to Potiphar, captain of the guard. Reformed readers hear providence in the “down”: this house sits on the road to prison and, later, to famine-saving power.

Genesis 39:2

2The LORD was with Joseph, and he became a successful man, and he was in the house of his Egyptian master.

Arminian

Presence first, then success. Arminian reading: the LORD’s nearness does not erase Joseph’s work in the house; it enables a faithful walk in a pagan master’s rooms.

Reformed

The chapter’s thesis: “the LORD was with Joseph.” Reformed accent: success is explained before it is seen. Location in an Egyptian house is not absence of God; it is God with him there.

Genesis 39:3

3His master saw that the LORD was with him and that the LORD caused all that he did to succeed in his hands.

Arminian

Even Potiphar can see whose hand makes the work flourish. Arminians hear a witness to outsiders: faithful labor under God is meant to be visible, not hidden piety.

Reformed

The LORD caused the success; the master merely saw it. Reformed confession: common-grace eyes can notice fruit God produces. The cause is named twice: the LORD was with him, and the LORD caused it.

Genesis 39:4

4So Joseph found favor in his sight and attended him, and he made him overseer of his house and put him in charge of all that he had.

Arminian

Favor, attendance, charge of all. Trust is given; it can still be betrayed. Arminian pastoral: stewardship is a real responsibility. Joseph will soon refuse to use this trust as a bed.

Reformed

Favor in Potiphar’s sight follows the LORD’s presence, not charm alone. Reformed note: overseer of all is providence loading Joseph with a test equal to the trust.

Genesis 39:5

5From the time that he made him overseer in his house and over all that he had, the LORD blessed the Egyptian’s house for Joseph’s sake; the blessing of the LORD was on all that he had, in house and field.

Arminian

An Egyptian house is blessed for Joseph’s sake—Abraham’s “you will be a blessing” already leaking into Egypt. Arminians gladly trace mercy beyond Israel’s line; outsiders taste the LORD’s kindness through a faithful servant.

Reformed

Blessing on house and field is the Abrahamic promise in miniature. Reformed accent: “for Joseph’s sake” is covenantal, not luck. God prospers a pagan household because He is with the man He sent there.

Key contrast Both hear Abraham’s blessing reaching Egypt. Arminians typically stress sincere kindness that spills to the nations through a willing servant; Reformed readers typically stress that the LORD sovereignly blesses for Joseph’s sake as He advances the covenant.

Genesis 39:6

6So he left all that he had in Joseph’s charge, and because of him he had no concern about anything but the food he ate. Now Joseph was handsome in form and appearance.

Arminian

Total trust, then the narrator’s warning: Joseph is handsome. Gift and vulnerability sit in one verse. Arminian reading prepares a real temptation that must be refused, not a script Joseph cannot help.

Reformed

Potiphar’s ease and Joseph’s appearance are both under the author’s lamp. Reformed readers do not treat beauty as fate. The LORD who blesses the house will also keep the man when desire turns predatory.

Genesis 39:7

7And after a time his master’s wife cast her eyes on Joseph and said, “Lie with me.”

Arminian

Desire looks, then commands. The sin is named without perfume. Classical Arminians insist Joseph can refuse: the command is not his destiny.

Reformed

“After a time” marks a test after prosperity. Reformed note: the demand is blunt because wickedness often is. What follows will show whether “the LORD was with him” includes holy fear.

Genesis 39:8

8But he refused and said to his master’s wife, “Behold, because of me my master has no concern about anything in the house, and he has put everything that he has in my charge.

Arminian

He refused—first word. Then he reasons from trust: my master has no concern because of me. Arminian pastoral: saying no is a grace-enabled act. Loyalty to a human master is already a reason to stop.

Reformed

Refusal is the fruit of the LORD’s presence, not a solo moral stunt. Reformed accent: Joseph names the trust he has been given. Integrity here is stewardship under God, spoken out loud.

Genesis 39:9

9He is not greater in this house than I am, nor has he kept back anything from me except you, because you are his wife. How then can I do this great wickedness and sin against God?”

Arminian

The climax is Godward: great wickedness, sin against God. Marriage is named; so is the LORD. Arminians hear a conscience that can still say no. Adultery is not a private slip; it is sin against God, freely refused.

Reformed

Joseph’s theology of sex is vertical. Reformed confession: the decisive argument is not career risk but God. Presence in Potiphar’s house includes the God who sees in secret. The refusal is real; its root is fear of the LORD.

Key contrast Both prize the refusal as sin-against-God. Arminians typically stress Joseph’s responsible, grace-enabled no; Reformed readers typically stress that the LORD’s presence produces this holy fear, not mere willpower.

Genesis 39:10

10And as she spoke to Joseph day after day, he would not listen to her, to lie beside her or to be with her.

Arminian

Day after day: temptation repeats. He would not listen—ongoing refusal, not a single heroic hour. Arminian application: perseverance in the small nos, including avoiding even “being with her.”

Reformed

Sustained pressure, sustained resistance. Reformed comfort: the God who is with Joseph keeps him through repetition. Distance (“not… with her”) is wisdom, not priggishness.

Genesis 39:11

11But one day, when he went into the house to do his work and none of the men of the house was there in the house,

Arminian

Ordinary work, empty house. Opportunity is not permission. Arminians refuse the excuse that circumstance made the sin inevitable; Joseph still has to choose in the quiet.

Reformed

Providence includes this empty house. Reformed reading: the test is arranged in time, yet the LORD remains with him. Isolation will prove the fear of God or the lack of it.

Genesis 39:12

12she caught him by his garment, saying, “Lie with me.” But he left his garment in her hand and fled and got out of the house.

Arminian

Caught, commanded, fled. Leaving the garment is costly and right. Arminian pastoral: sometimes holiness looks like running. Fleeing is not cowardice; it is obedience when words have already been spent.

Reformed

Flight is the appointed means. Reformed note: Joseph’s feet preach what his mouth already said. The LORD with him does not make him statue-still; it sends him out of the house.

Genesis 39:13

13And as soon as she saw that he had left his garment in her hand and had fled out of the house,

Arminian

She sees the garment and the flight. Evidence of his refusal becomes her prop. Arminian warning: scorned desire can turn to lying in a breath.

Reformed

The narrator slows the turn from assault to plot. Reformed readers watch providence leave a garment in her hand—the very token that will send Joseph toward the prison God will use.

Genesis 39:14

14she called to the men of her household and said to them, “See, he has brought among us a Hebrew to laugh at us. He came in to me to lie with me, and I cried out with a loud voice.

Arminian

She reverses the story and racializes it: “a Hebrew to laugh at us.” False witness plus contempt. Arminians name both as sin. Joseph’s ethnicity is weaponized; the household is invited to hate.

Reformed

Slander recruits the staff. Reformed accent: “he has brought among us” blames Potiphar too. God is still with the Hebrew she mocks; her loud voice does not rewrite what happened.

Genesis 39:15

15And as soon as he heard that I lifted up my voice and cried out, he left his garment beside me and fled and got out of the house.”

Arminian

She rearranges the garment: “beside me,” not torn away as he fled. Lying is careful. Arminian reading: words can invert holiness into guilt. Truth still exists even when the room believes her.

Reformed

The lie is almost the truth, re-aimed. Reformed note: God hears the real cry Joseph never made. The garment will travel to Potiphar; so will this fiction.

Genesis 39:16

16Then she laid up his garment by her until his master came home,

Arminian

She stores the prop. Delay is part of the trap. Arminian pastoral: sin often waits for an audience. Joseph cannot retrieve his name from a folded cloak.

Reformed

The garment waits like a loaded witness. Reformed readers see the master’s coming as the next hinge of providence—toward prison, not toward clearing.

Genesis 39:17

17and she told him the same story, saying, “The Hebrew servant, whom you have brought among us, came in to me to laugh at me.

Arminian

Same story, aimed at the husband: your Hebrew, your fault. Arminians hear the cruelty of pinning assault on the innocent and on the man who trusted him.

Reformed

“Whom you have brought” spreads the blame. Reformed accent: the LORD brought Joseph to Egypt; she credits Potiphar. Two accounts of agency now compete; only one is true.

Genesis 39:18

18But as soon as I lifted up my voice and cried, he left his garment beside me and fled out of the house.”

Arminian

The speech ends where verse 15 ended. Repetition hardens the lie. Arminian warning: a story told twice is still false. Joseph’s next hearing will be a prison door, not a fair trial.

Reformed

The lie is complete. Reformed comfort is not in Potiphar’s justice but in the refrain still to come: the LORD was with Joseph—even after this sentence.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
