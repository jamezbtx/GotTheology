Passage

Genesis 19

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

After Abraham’s intercession, the two angels enter Sodom at evening. Lot sits in the gate, rises to host them, and presses them into his house. The men of the city surround it and demand the guests; Lot’s plea—and his dreadful offer of his daughters—does not stop them. The angels pull Lot inside and strike the attackers with blindness: judgment begun, and Lot preserved.

Genesis 19:1

1The two angels came to Sodom in the evening, and Lot was sitting in the gate of Sodom. When Lot saw them, he rose to meet them and bowed himself with his face to the earth

Arminian

The two angels come at evening, and Lot is sitting in the gate—a public place, not a hidden tent. He rises, bows, and meets them. Hospitality is a real, grace-enabled act; the man who once pitched toward Sodom still knows how to honor strangers.

Classical Arminians hear both kindness and compromise. Sitting in the gate means Lot is woven into the city’s life. Grace still meets him here; his rising is a responsible welcome, not a script he could not refuse.

Reformed

The messengers of the Lord arrive where Abraham’s prayer left them: Sodom at evening. Lot is already in the gate. Providence has placed a sojourner where rescue will find him.

Reformed comfort: these are not chance travelers. The same God who heard Abraham now walks the city in judgment and, for Abraham’s sake, in mercy. Lot’s bow does not create that mercy; it meets it.

Genesis 19:2

2and said, “My lords, please turn aside to your servant's house and spend the night and wash your feet. Then you may rise up early and go on your way.” They said, “No; we will spend the night in the town square.”

Arminian

Lot offers his house, water for feet, and an early departure. The visitors prefer the square. He knows—better than they let on—that the square is not safe. Hospitality here is urgent moral care, not mere manners.

Arminian pastoral note: Lot’s invitation is a real yes to protecting guests. Grace enables such welcome; the city’s later violence will show why the square was no place to spend the night.

Reformed

“My lords” and a servant’s house: Lot echoes Abraham’s hospitality of chapter 18, now inside a doomed city. The angels’ “No” to the square is not ignorance of danger.

Reformed reading: God orders the path into Lot’s house. The night will expose Sodom, and the house will become the line of preservation. Welcome is the means; the Lord’s purpose is the cause.

Genesis 19:3

3But he pressed them strongly; so they turned aside to him and entered his house. And he made them a feast and baked unleavened bread, and they ate.

Arminian

He pressed them strongly, then made a feast and unleavened bread. Insistence is love of strangers under a dark sky. Lot still practices the old virtue of the tent, even as a resident of Sodom.

Classical Arminians honor this as genuine goodness that can coexist with later failure. Pressing guests is a responsible act; it does not cancel the city’s guilt or excuse what Lot will soon say.

Reformed

The feast is providence as well as courtesy. Unleavened bread and a table in Sodom become the setting where judgment will knock. God uses Lot’s urgency to bring His messengers under that roof.

Reformed note: hospitality does not atone for the city. It is the Lord’s kindness locating His servants—and Lot—where He will both expose wickedness and shut a door.

Genesis 19:4

4But before they lay down, the men of the city, the men of Sodom, both young and old, all the people to the last man, surrounded the house.

Arminian

Before they lie down, the men of the city—young and old, to the last man—surround the house. Guilt is corporate and personal: each comes. This is not a slur on a people for later ages; it is this city’s chosen violence.

Arminian sobriety: Abraham asked for ten righteous. The narrator shows a city that presents itself as one mob. Their surrounding is resistible wickedness they actually commit.

Reformed

The outcry of chapter 18 is no rumor. Young and old, to the last man, the city rings the house. God’s coming judgment will not be arbitrary; the evidence gathers at the door.

Reformed weight: providence does not invent this sin. It lets the city show its heart. The same Lord who searches Sodom now lets the search become public.

Genesis 19:5

5And they called to Lot, “Where are the men who came to you tonight? Bring them out to us, that we may know them.”

Arminian

They demand the guests be brought out “that we may know them”—a violent claim on persons who came under a roof. Scripture names this as wickedness, not as a joke and not as a license to hate anyone later.

Classical Arminians insist the men remain responsible. Desire that violates the neighbor is sin they can and should refuse. Grace had left a witness in Lot’s house; they press against it.

Reformed

The demand unmasks the city. To “know” the guests is to treat image-bearers as prey. Holy judgment is not a mood; it answers real assault.

Reformed sobriety: God is not cruel to name this evil. The outcry was true. What they want at the door will soon meet a blindness they cannot argue down—justice, not caprice.

Genesis 19:6

6Lot went out to the men at the entrance, shut the door after him,

Arminian

Lot goes out and shuts the door after him. He puts himself between the mob and the guests. Courage and compromise will stand in the same man in the next verses.

Arminian reading: this is real agency. He could have hidden. He steps out, still about to plead with them as “brothers,” still responsible for how he will try to save the house.

Reformed

The door becomes a line. Lot shuts it behind him, and for a moment the sojourner stands as a shield. God has not yet pulled him in.

Reformed comfort: even this brave step sits inside preservation already underway. The messengers are in the house; the Lord who sent them will not let the door be the last word.

Genesis 19:7

7and said, “I beg you, my brothers, do not act so wickedly.

Arminian

“Do not act so wickedly.” Lot still knows the name of the deed. The appeal is moral and sincere: brothers should not do this. Wickedness is not fate; it is an act he begs them to refuse.

Classical Arminians hear a real call. They can still turn back. That they will not is their hardness, not proof that pleading is pointless. Lot’s word is true even if his next word is not.

Reformed

Even a compromised man can tell the truth about sin. “Wickedly” is the right word. The city is not misunderstood; it is being asked to stop.

Reformed accent: the plea also serves the narrative of justice. When they refuse a clear “do not,” the Judge of all the earth is shown to be right. Hardness is exposed, not invented.

Genesis 19:8

8Behold, I have two daughters who have not known any man. Let me bring them out to you, and do to them as you please. Only do nothing to these men, for they have come under the shelter of my roof.”

Arminian

Lot offers his daughters. Scripture records the words; it does not bless them. Guests are not saved by sacrificing the vulnerable. The daughters’ dignity is not his to trade, however desperate the hospitality code.

Arminian pastoral grief: this is Lot’s sin, not a model. Fear and a twisted ethic do not erase responsibility. The city’s wickedness is the fire; Lot’s proposal is a tragic failure inside it, never an endorsement.

Reformed

The narrator lets us hear how far Sodom’s night has reached into Lot’s reasoning. Protection of guests is real; offering his daughters is wicked. God will rescue Lot despite this sentence, not because of it.

Reformed note: preservation is coming from the angels, not from Lot’s bargain. The text exposes a righteous man (as Peter later calls him) still capable of a grievous wrong. Mercy will have to be sovereign.

Genesis 19:9

9But they said, “Stand back!” And they said, “This fellow came to sojourn, and he has become the judge! Now we will deal worse with you than with them.” Then they pressed hard against the man Lot, and drew near to break the door down.

Arminian

They will not have even his dreadful bargain. “Stand back”—then insult: the sojourner has become the judge. They press to break the door and threaten worse to Lot. Refusal of warning is their own.

Classical Arminians see escalating, resistible violence. They hate being named wicked more than they hate the deed. Grace had given a “do not”; they choose the door instead.

Reformed

The city resents judgment more than it wants a substitute. “He has become the judge” is the old sneer at a moral word. Then they move to break the door.

Reformed weight: hardness now shows itself complete. Providence lets them press, and the next verse will show whose hand is stronger. Lot cannot manage this mob; the Lord must.

Genesis 19:10

10But the men reached out their hands and brought Lot into the house with them and shut the door.

Arminian

The men reach out, bring Lot in, and shut the door. Rescue is from outside his strength. He was nearly lost at the entrance he had tried to manage.

Arminian warmth: delivering grace is real and personal. They pull him in. He remains a man who will later linger; tonight he is kept. Mercy meets him in the act of being seized for good.

Reformed

Hands stronger than the mob’s bring Lot inside. The door shuts because God intends him kept. This is preservation, not Lot’s success.

Reformed comfort: the same messengers who came to inspect now save. Sovereign mercy does what hospitality and pleading could not. Abraham’s God has not forgotten the nephew in the gate.

Genesis 19:11

11And they struck with blindness the men who were at the entrance of the house, both small and great, so that they wore themselves out groping for the door.

Arminian

Blindness falls on small and great, yet they wear themselves out groping for the door. Judgment is real, and they still pursue the sin they can no longer see to finish.

Classical Arminians take the stroke as holy and the groping as responsible. Even under judgment they do not repent. The assault is stopped; their hearts are not yet. This is warning, not a cue for later contempt of persons.

Reformed

The Lord begins the overthrow with a blindness that both halts violence and unveils hardness. They exhaust themselves seeking the door they cannot find.

Reformed sobriety: judgment is already underway, and it is just. Sight is taken; the will to harm is not. God preserves the house and exposes the city in one act—mercy for Lot, justice at the threshold.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Lot’s guests in Sodom and the city’s violence—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 19:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“Here were old and young all from every quarter—The old were not past it, and the young were soon come up to it. Either they had no magistrates to protect the peaceable, or their magistrates were themselves aiding and abetting. I have two daughters—This was unadvisedly and unjustifiably offered. It is true, of two evils we must chose the less, but of two sins we must chose neither, nor ever do evil that good may come of it.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 19:4, 8)

Reformed Notable voices

“Here, in a single crime, Moses sets before our eyes a lively picture of Sodom. For it is hence obvious, how diabolical was their consent in all wickedness, since they all rose so suddenly to perpetrate an act so filthy and detestable. In the meantime we obtain a specimen of the more than iron hardness to which they were grown callous.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 19:4)
