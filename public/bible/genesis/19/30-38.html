Passage

Genesis 19

Verses 30–38 · ESV · Voices

After Sodom falls, Lot’s wife is gone and Zoar no longer feels safe. He takes his two daughters into a cave in the hills. Fear, wine, and a plan to preserve a line lead to incest; the sons born are named as fathers of Moab and Ammon. Scripture records the shame without praise and without spectacle. Grace still holds in Abraham’s wider story; this night is not made holy by later nations.

Genesis 19:30

30Now Lot went up out of Zoar and lived in the hills with his two daughters, for he was afraid to live in Zoar. So he lived in a cave with his two daughters.

Arminian

Lot leaves Zoar for a cave in the hills, “for he was afraid.” Rescue has already happened; fear now chooses isolation with his two daughters.

Arminian pastoral: terror after judgment is real, yet it remains a responsible path. Grace had spared him for Abraham’s sake. Hiding from Zoar is not seeking the Lord who led him out; isolation here is a choice, not a command.

Reformed

The man first told to flee to the hills now goes there by dread of Zoar, not by trust. Providence has him in a cave; the motive named is fear.

Reformed note: God had already been merciful (19:16, 29). Isolation sits inside His government without becoming wisdom. Fear can be governed without being approved, and a cave is not a sacrament of safety.

Genesis 19:31

31And the firstborn said to the younger, “Our father is old, and there is not a man on earth to come in to us after the manner of all the earth.

Arminian

The firstborn maps the world: father old, no man left for ordinary marriage. Zoar still stood; Abraham’s house still lived. Fear shrinks the earth to a cave.

Classical Arminians name the error without mocking the women. Trauma distorts sight; it does not erase agency. They reason, they speak, they will act—and the premise is false. False fear is still a responsible misreading.

Reformed

“Not a man on earth” is unbelief dressed as realism. The Judge of Sodom had not emptied the world of help, husbands, or kin.

Reformed accent: God had preserved a remnant and a promised line nearby. Their counsel treats providence as already finished. Despair of ordinary means is the first step of the plan, not a word from heaven.

Genesis 19:32

32Come, let us make our father drink wine, and we will lie with him, that we may preserve offspring from our father.”

Arminian

Wine, then lying with their father, “that we may preserve offspring.” The end is a line; the means are drunkenness and incest. Scripture lets us hear the plan so we will not baptize it.

Arminian moral: a desired future does not license this night. They are not cartoons; they are responsible. Fear of a dead line cannot make the act love, nor can a family crisis turn wine into a tool of righteousness.

Reformed

Offspring-anxiety without faith invents a way. Abraham waits on a promised seed; here seed is seized by wine.

Reformed refusal: God can raise children without this. Later nations in His plan do not mean He scripted the sin. The scheme is theirs; providence is not a permit, and a hoped-for line never justifies these means.

Genesis 19:33

33So they made their father drink wine that night. And the firstborn went in and lay with her father. He did not know when she lay down or when she arose.

Arminian

They make him drink; the firstborn lies with her father; he does not know. The narrator is spare on purpose. Shame is recorded, not staged.

Arminian warning: drunkenness is sin that blinds. Lot is not innocent because he cannot remember; the daughters are not innocent because the plan “worked.” Both remain accountable in different ways, without cruelty and without excuse.

Reformed

“He did not know” names Lot’s stupor, not God’s approval. Wine opened the door; incest walked through.

Reformed pastoral: God records what He does not commend. A line preserved through this night is not a decree that the act was holy. Providence can govern a cave without authoring its sin, and drunkenness is never a mitigating miracle.

Genesis 19:34

34The next day, the firstborn said to the younger, “Behold, I lay last night with my father. Let us make him drink wine tonight also. Then you go in and lie with him, that we may preserve offspring from our father.”

Arminian

The next day she tells the younger and repeats the plan. This is no single panicked hour; it is counsel renewed in daylight.

Arminian note: persistence is agency. Recruiting a sister is a second choice, not fate. Fear may explain; it does not reduce the younger to a prop or the firstborn to a monster. Both can still refuse.

Reformed

Morning light does not undo the scheme; it extends it. The same words—“preserve offspring”—cover a second night.

Reformed sobriety: hardening can be as ordinary as a next-day conversation. God’s later use of Moab and Ammon will not turn this counsel into wisdom. Overruling providence is not applause.

Genesis 19:35

35So they made their father drink wine that night also. And the younger arose and lay with him, and he did not know when she lay down or when she arose.

Arminian

Wine again; the younger lies with him; again he does not know. Two nights, two willing steps, one stupefied father.

Classical Arminians keep the picture human: a younger sister is not less responsible for being younger. She acts. Lot drinks again. Grace that saved them from fire is not consent to this, and repetition makes the sin clearer, not lighter.

Reformed

The formula repeats so we cannot call it accident. Stupor twice is still drunkenness; the second night is still incest.

Reformed comfort is not in this verse but later, in a God who can judge and still keep Abraham’s promise. This is grief under sovereignty, not a wink. The Lord remains holy while the cave does not.

Genesis 19:36

36Thus both the daughters of Lot became pregnant by their father.

Arminian

Both daughters become pregnant by their father. Consequence follows the plan. The text is clinical, not prurient; it will not let us look away or linger.

Arminian pastoral: pregnancy is not the Lord’s smile on the means. Children will live; the act remains sin. Grace still runs in Abraham’s household, not as a covering that calls this good.

Reformed

Conception in this cave is under God’s government of all life, never His applause. He can give breath without endorsing how it was sought.

Reformed confession: providence extends to the womb. That doctrine fences despair; it is not a license. God remains holy while He permits what He will later overrule for ends of His own.

Genesis 19:37

37The firstborn bore a son and called his name Moab. He is the father of the Moabites to this day.

Arminian

The firstborn names the son Moab; he is father of the Moabites “to this day.” A people begins in shame. Moses tells Israel their neighbor’s origin without gloating.

Arminian close: later mercy—Ruth the Moabitess in David’s line—is grace in history, not a retroactive blessing of this night. God can redeem a story without rewriting the sin.

Reformed

Moab enters the table of nations because God orders history, including peoples born from this cave.

Reformed wonder: the same Lord will later fence Moab from Israel’s assembly and still bring Ruth into Messiah’s genealogy. Nations in His plan never excuse the incest that named this one.

Genesis 19:38

38The younger also bore a son and called his name Ben-ammi. He is the father of the Ammonites to this day.

Arminian

The younger names Ben-ammi; he fathers the Ammonites. Two sons, two nations, one father who did not know. The chapter that began with angels ends in a genealogy of disgrace.

Arminian gospel: Abraham’s promise still stands next door. Lot was saved; this coda does not cancel mercy, and mercy does not cancel the need to name drunkenness and incest as sin.

Reformed

Ammon too is “to this day”—a real people under God’s government, later neighbor, rival, and relative to Israel.

Reformed close: providence writes nations even through sin. The act remains inexcusable. The God who remembered Abraham will keep the covenant line; He does not need this cave, and He does not call it righteousness.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the cave, the daughters’ scheme, and Moab and Ammon—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 19:30–38.

Arminian Notable voices

“He feared to dwell in Zoar—Here is the great trouble and distress that Lot was brought into after his deliverance, Ge 19:29. He was frightened out of Zoar, durst not dwell there, either because he was conscious to himself that it was a refuge of his own chusing, and that therein he had foolishly prescribed to God, and therefore could not but distrust his safety in it.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 19:30)

Reformed Notable voices

“And the firstborn said Here Moses narrates a miracle, which rightly brings the readers to astonishment. For, how could that unchaste intercourse come into the mind of the daughters of Lot, while the terrible punishment of God of the Sodomites stood still before their eyes, and while they knew that the scandalous and sinful lusts were the chief causes thereof?”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 19:31)
