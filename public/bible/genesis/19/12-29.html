Passage

Genesis 19

Verses 12–29 · ESV · Voices

The angels warn Lot to take his household out of Sodom. His sons-in-law treat the warning as a joke. Lot lingers; they seize his hand—the LORD being merciful—and set him outside. He is granted flight to little Zoar. Sulfur and fire fall; Lot’s wife looks back and becomes a pillar of salt. Abraham looks toward the plain, and the narrator names why Lot lived: God remembered Abraham.

Genesis 19:12

12Then the men said to Lot, “Have you anyone else here? Sons-in-law, sons, daughters, or anyone you have in the city, bring them out of the place.

Arminian

The visitors ask who else is Lot’s—sons-in-law, sons, daughters, anyone in the city—and tell him to bring them out. Warning is spoken so a household may actually flee.

Classical Arminians hear mercy offered, not a closed list. Grace enables flight; Lot is responsible to gather those he can. The door of escape is sincerely opened to all he will bring.

Reformed

Anyone Lot has in the city is named for rescue while the sentence on the place is already moving. The question is whom mercy will pull out.

Reformed readers hear particular deliverance. God is gathering a remnant, not reopening Sodom’s case. Judgment proceeds; rescue is for those He will bring out.

Genesis 19:13

13For we are about to destroy this place, because the outcry against its people has become great before the LORD, and the LORD has sent us to destroy it.”

Arminian

Destruction is near because the outcry against the people has become great before the LORD, who has sent these messengers to destroy. Heaven has heard real wickedness, not a rumor.

Arminian pastoral note: the warning is timed and moral. God judges after real outcry. Those who hear still have a moment to take the offered flight.

Reformed

The outcry has become great, and the LORD has sent them to destroy. Judicial judgment is commissioned, not guessed at.

Reformed confession: God is Judge of cities as of persons. What Genesis 18 heard, Genesis 19 now carries out. Wrath here is holy, not impulsive.

Genesis 19:14

14So Lot went out and said to his sons-in-law, who were to marry his daughters, “Up! Get out of this place, for the LORD is about to destroy the city.” But he seemed to his sons-in-law to be jesting.

Arminian

Lot goes out and urges his sons-in-law: Get out—the LORD is about to destroy the city. They take him for a jester. A true warning can still be mocked.

Classical Arminians grieve this as responsible unbelief. Grace enabled Lot to speak; they were not forced to scoff. Mercy offered can be refused; jesting at judgment is their choice.

Reformed

Lot warns; the sons-in-law hear a joke. Unbelief can sit inside a household that has been told the truth.

Reformed weight: treating the LORD’s sentence as jest does not delay it. Lot’s word is not emptied by their laugh; the destroyers still come.

Genesis 19:15

15As morning dawned, the angels urged Lot, saying, “Up! Take your wife and your two daughters who are here, lest you be swept away in the punishment of the city.”

Arminian

Dawn, and the angels urge again: take wife and two daughters who are here, lest you be swept away. The summons is repeated and aimed at those still within reach.

Arminian hearing: assisting grace keeps calling. “Up!” is a real command they can obey this morning. Responsible flight is how sweeping-away is refused.

Reformed

Morning does not soften the sentence. The angels urge because punishment will sweep the city, and those “who are here” are the remnant still to be led out.

Reformed accent: urgency is mercy inside a fixed judgment. The command is the means of the deliverance God is already putting into motion.

Genesis 19:16

16But he lingered. So the men seized him and his wife and his two daughters by the hand, the LORD being merciful to him, and they brought him out and set him outside the city.

Arminian

“But he lingered.” Even warned, Lot delays. Then they seize him, his wife, and his daughters by the hand—the LORD being merciful—and set them outside. Mercy that takes the hand is still mercy.

Classical Arminians do not deny the grip. Lingered hearts need more than advice. Yet lingering was Lot’s real hesitation, and the way out remains a grace-enabled going he must continue.

Reformed

He lingered, so they seized him. The narrator names the cause: the LORD being merciful. Deliverance here is effectual—hands on theirs, feet set outside the city.

Reformed comfort: God does not wait for Lot’s pace to become heroic. Mercy that saves can overrule delay without calling delay faith. The remnant is outside because God took hold.

Genesis 19:17

17And as they brought them out, one said, “Escape for your life. Do not look back or stop anywhere in the valley. Escape to the hills, lest you be swept away.”

Arminian

Escape for your life. Do not look back or stop in the valley; escape to the hills, lest you be swept away. The path is plain: flee, and do not linger in sight of what God is judging.

Arminian pastoral: grace has set them outside; they must now run. Looking back is a resistible pull. The warning treats them as agents who can still obey.

Reformed

Life depends on flight, and flight has a path: not the valley, not a backward glance, but the hills. The same mercy that seized now commands.

Reformed note: “lest you be swept away” is not theater. God appoints both the rescue and the manner of it. Safety is in His word, not in Lot’s sense of the map.

Genesis 19:18

18And Lot said to them, “Oh, no, my lords.

Arminian

Lot answers, “Oh, no, my lords.” Freshly pulled out, he still bargains. Fear speaks; he does not simply run as told.

Classical Arminians hear a real, mixed heart. Mercy has been shown; now he must receive the next instruction. “Oh, no” is Lot’s own word, not a script he could not refuse.

Reformed

The man set outside still objects. Deliverance has not made him bold. God will hear the plea without surrendering the judgment.

Reformed reading: remaining fear does not unmake the mercy already taken. The LORD’s purpose to save Lot can bear a trembling mouth and still keep the remnant.

Genesis 19:19

19Behold, your servant has found favor in your sight, and you have shown me great kindness in saving my life. But I cannot escape to the hills, lest the disaster overtake me and I die.

Arminian

Lot confesses favor, kindness, and a saved life—then says he cannot escape to the hills lest disaster overtake him. Gratitude and unbelief share a sentence.

Arminian note: he names mercy truly, then shrinks from the commanded path. Grace-enabled flight still asks for trust. Fear is understandable; it is not the same as wisdom.

Reformed

He admits great kindness in saving his life, then pleads inability. The remnant’s weakness is on the page.

Reformed accent: God has already saved; Lot’s “I cannot” does not veto that mercy. Providence can grant a nearer city without making fear the author of rescue.

Genesis 19:20

20Behold, this city is near enough to flee to, and it is a little one. Let me escape there—is it not a little one?—and my life will be saved!”

Arminian

He points to a nearby “little” city: let me escape there, and my life will be saved. Zoar is requested as a smaller, nearer refuge than the hills.

Classical Arminians see petition, not seizure. Lot asks. Mercy offered may stoop to a weaker faith, and he remains responsible to go where he is then sent.

Reformed

“Is it not a little one?” is bargaining geography. God can spare a town as a corridor for the remnant.

Reformed comfort: even the requested city sits inside the LORD’s government. Lot’s life will be saved because mercy governs the map, not because smallness is a loophole.

Genesis 19:21

21He said to him, “Behold, I grant you this favor also, that I will not overthrow the city of which you have spoken.

Arminian

“I grant you this favor also”—the city will not be overthrown. Added kindness meets the plea. God is not stingy with the man He has begun to save.

Arminian warmth: further favor is sincerely given. Lot asked; heaven granted. Mercy can include a nearer shelter without cancelling the call to escape quickly.

Reformed

Favor is granted, not earned: this city will stand. The Judge who rains fire can also withhold it from one town.

Reformed confession: additional mercy is as sovereign as the first. God determines what He will not overthrow for the remnant’s sake, and He says so.

Genesis 19:22

22Escape there quickly, for I can do nothing till you arrive there.” Therefore the name of the city was called Zoar.

Arminian

Escape there quickly; “I can do nothing till you arrive.” Zoar means little. Judgment waits on Lot’s arrival—an astonishing kindness that still requires haste.

Arminian pastoral: delay is no longer safe. Grace has opened Zoar; responsible obedience is to run there now. Mercy is not permission to linger at the rim of the valley.

Reformed

The destroyer waits until Lot arrives. Effectual deliverance even times the fire. Zoar is named for its smallness, and it stands because God said it would.

Reformed weight: “I can do nothing till you arrive” is appointed patience, not weakness. The remnant must be in place before judicial judgment falls.

Genesis 19:23

23The sun had risen on the earth when Lot came to Zoar.

Arminian

Sunrise, and Lot comes to Zoar. The hour of arrival is dated by the sun. He did, at last, enter the little city granted him.

Classical Arminians honor the arriving. Grace enabled the flight; Lot actually came. Morning light meets a man who took the opened way—late, lingering, and still saved by going where he was sent.

Reformed

The sun is up when Lot reaches Zoar. Providence clocks the remnant into shelter as day begins.

Reformed instinct: arrival is God’s timing as much as Lot’s steps. The little city of refuge is occupied because mercy meant him there when fire fell.

Genesis 19:24

24Then the LORD rained on Sodom and Gomorrah sulfur and fire from the LORD out of heaven.

Arminian

Then the LORD rained on Sodom and Gomorrah sulfur and fire from the LORD out of heaven. Brimstone is the LORD’s own rain on cities whose outcry had become great.

Arminian gravity: God judges after warning and outcry. The same Lord who offered flight now acts. This is holy sorrow, not a cue for later contempt of persons.

Reformed

Sulfur and fire fall “from the LORD out of heaven.” Judicial judgment is the LORD’s work, twice named.

Reformed confession: heaven is not a bystander. What Genesis 18 announced, Genesis 19 performs. God rains what He has decreed; the remnant is already in Zoar.

Genesis 19:25

25And he overthrew those cities, and all the valley, and all the inhabitants of the cities, and what grew on the ground.

Arminian

He overthrew those cities, the valley, the inhabitants, and what grew on the ground. The judgment is total for that place—people and pasture together.

Classical Arminians do not minimize this. Wickedness has an end. Mercy had been offered in flight; overthrow is what refusal meets. The text is grief, not a license to hate.

Reformed

Cities, valley, inhabitants, vegetation: the overthrow is comprehensive. The Judge of all the earth does what is just.

Reformed reading: nothing here is accidental blight. The remnant’s escape makes the sentence clearer, not crueler. Judicial judgment is holy.

Genesis 19:26

26But Lot's wife, behind him, looked back, and she became a pillar of salt.

Arminian

Lot’s wife, behind him, looked back, and she became a pillar of salt. “Do not look back” was a real command; she treated it as optional. Divided love can stand a few yards from rescue and still turn.

Arminian pastoral: remember Lot’s wife. Grace had seized her hand; she remained responsible not to gaze at what God was judging. Looking back is a live, resistible act.

Reformed

Behind him, she looked back, and salt is the memorial. The command of verse 17 is now a grave.

Reformed note: proximity to Lot is not salvation. God meant the warning; her glance meets judicial judgment even on the road of flight. Mercy that seizes still requires the path He named.

Genesis 19:27

27And Abraham went early in the morning to the place where he had stood before the LORD.

Arminian

Abraham goes early to the place where he had stood before the LORD—the intercession ground of chapter 18. He returns to prayer’s geography to see what the Judge has done.

Classical Arminians honor this watching. The friend of God does not gloat; he goes to the standing-place of plea. Intercession was sincere; now he will look at the answer.

Reformed

Early morning, Abraham stands again where he stood before the LORD. The intercessor is taken back to the scene of the dialogue.

Reformed comfort: the place of prayer is also the place of providence. God who heard Abraham will now show him the valley.

Genesis 19:28

28And he looked down toward Sodom and Gomorrah and toward all the land of the valley, and he looked and, behold, the smoke of the land went up like the smoke of a furnace.

Arminian

He looks toward Sodom and Gomorrah and the land of the valley: smoke going up like the smoke of a furnace. What he sees is judgment completed, not a rumor.

Arminian grief: the furnace-smoke is the end of a people who would not repent. Abraham’s looking is witness, not triumph. Mercy had been sought for the city; ten righteous were not found.

Reformed

Behold: smoke like a furnace. The well-watered valley is now a column of burning.

Reformed weight: Abraham sees what God has done. Judicial judgment is public enough for the patriarch to read it on the skyline. The LORD keeps His word to judge—and, as the next verse says, to remember.

Genesis 19:29

29So it was that, when God destroyed the cities of the valley, God remembered Abraham and sent Lot out of the midst of the overthrow when he overthrew the cities in which Lot had lived.

Arminian

When God destroyed the cities of the valley, God remembered Abraham and sent Lot out of the midst of the overthrow. Lot lives because mercy ran through Abraham’s standing before the LORD.

Classical Arminians hear remembrance as covenant kindness that still treats Lot as a man sent out. Intercession was not ignored. Grace remembered, and Lot was actually brought out.

Reformed

The narrator’s verdict: God remembered Abraham and sent Lot out. Effectual deliverance is anchored in God’s covenant regard for the patriarch.

Reformed confession: Lot’s rescue is not luck, nor merely Lot’s last-minute run. The God who judged the valley remembered His friend and pulled the nephew from the midst. Remnant mercy rides on God’s faithfulness.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on mercy dragging Lot out, and fire on the cities of the plain—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 19:12–29.

Arminian Notable voices

“It was not time to trifle, when the destruction was just at the door. But he seemed to them as one that mocked—They thought perhaps that the assault which the Sodomites had just now made upon his house had disturbed his head, and put him into such a fright that be knew not what he said. They that made a jest of every thing, made a jest of that, and so perished in the overthrow.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 19:14)

Reformed Notable voices

“At length the angels declare for what purpose they came, and what they were about to do. For so great was the indignity of the last act of this people, that Lot must now see how impossible it was for God to bear with them any longer. And, in the first place, they declare, that they are come to destroy the city, because the cry of it was waxen great.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 19:12)
