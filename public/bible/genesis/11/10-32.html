Passage

Genesis 11

Verses 10–32 · ESV

After Babel, the camera quiets on Shem. Ordinary years carry the promise-line through Arpachshad, Shelah, Eber, Peleg, Reu, Serug, and Nahor to Terah. Abram, Nahor, and Haran are named; Haran dies in Ur; Sarai is barren. Terah takes Abram, Lot, and Sarai toward Canaan, settles in Haran, and dies there—the setup for chapter 12.

Genesis 11:10

10These are the generations of Shem. When Shem was 100 years old, he fathered Arpachshad two years after the flood.

Arminian

After Babel’s noise, the camera quiets on Shem. A hundred years old, two years after the flood, he fathers Arpachshad. The promise-line is a date and a birth.

Classical Arminians receive this as ordinary grace: a family kept through judgment so blessing can still reach the nations. The story is not only empires and towers; it is a named son in a named year.

Reformed

Generations of Shem restart the spine that will run to Abram. God timestamps the line two years after the flood.

Reformed accent: Calvin noted that Scripture reckons the world’s years from this family as a mark of election’s honor, not the clan’s moral superiority. Providence keeps a thread when the nations scatter.

Genesis 11:11

11And Shem lived after he fathered Arpachshad 500 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Shem lives five hundred years after Arpachshad and has other sons and daughters. The named heir is not the only child.

Arminian pastoral: long years and unnamed siblings are still gift. God loves more than the spotlight name; fruitfulness around the promise-line is real family, not leftover.

Reformed

Five centuries of further life mean Shem outlasts many grandsons. Other sons and daughters fill the house while the recorded line stays thin.

Reformed note: God can keep a promise through one named son without pretending the others do not exist. Ordinary years are the medium of His keeping.

Genesis 11:12

12When Arpachshad had lived 35 years, he fathered Shelah.

Arminian

Thirty-five years, then Shelah. The links are short and unadorned.

Classical Arminians see responsible begetting: a man lives, a son is born. The road toward Abram is walked in ordinary decades, not in constant miracle-language.

Reformed

Arpachshad fathers Shelah at thirty-five. The genealogy does not pause to explain.

Reformed comfort: God is counting. Each fathered son is another kept stitch in the fabric that will reach Terah and Abram.

Genesis 11:13

13And Arpachshad lived after he fathered Shelah 403 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Four hundred three years after Shelah, with other sons and daughters. Life around the line is long and full.

Arminian note: unnamed children still receive years. The promise-line does not empty the rest of the household of dignity.

Reformed

The refrain returns: long life, other sons and daughters. God records duration as well as descent.

Reformed accent: providence is not only the next name; it is centuries in which that name’s family simply lives.

Genesis 11:14

14When Shelah had lived 30 years, he fathered Eber.

Arminian

Shelah at thirty fathers Eber—the name that will mark the Hebrews.

Arminian pastoral: an ordinary age, a weighty son. God can hang a people’s later name on a quiet birth.

Reformed

Eber enters. The line that will be called Hebrew is still just a boy in a list.

Reformed reading: election’s thread is being drawn through Shelah’s house. The significance is God’s, not yet the child’s fame.

Genesis 11:15

15And Shelah lived after he fathered Eber 403 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Again the long after-years and other children. Shelah’s story is mostly time.

Classical Arminians let the repetition preach: faithfulness looks like decades of unnamed life around a named son.

Reformed

Four hundred three years after Eber. The clock is the theology.

Reformed note: God is not bored by duration. The promise survives in ordinary years as surely as in the later call.

Genesis 11:16

16When Eber had lived 34 years, he fathered Peleg.

Arminian

Eber fathers Peleg at thirty-four. Chapter 10 already tied Peleg to the earth’s division.

Arminian reading: the Babel scatter and this birth belong to the same world. A man names a son in the days the earth is divided; human history and God’s spreading purpose meet in a family.

Reformed

Peleg’s name still carries the division of the earth. The genealogy will not let Babel float free of Shem’s house.

Reformed accent: God timestamps nations and a son together. The line continues through the very era He used to fill the world.

Genesis 11:17

17And Eber lived after he fathered Peleg 430 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Eber lives four hundred thirty years after Peleg—the longest of these post-flood lives in the list.

Arminian pastoral: long life here is gift. The ancestor of the Hebrews is given time, other children, and a place in the story. Ordinary years remain the way God keeps a people.

Reformed

Eber’s after-years are the longest among these names. God is free to portion life as He will.

Reformed confession: the Hebrew name’s source is kept through centuries. The line does not run on human vitality alone; the Lord assigns the years.

Genesis 11:18

18When Peleg had lived 30 years, he fathered Reu.

Arminian

Peleg at thirty fathers Reu. Lives are already shortening beside Shem’s centuries.

Arminian note: ordinary years still suffice to carry a son. The promise does not need antediluvian lifespans to stay alive.

Reformed

Thirty years, then Reu. The pace of the list is becoming more like later history.

Reformed accent: God shortens the years and still keeps the thread. The mandate and the line both proceed on His clock.

Genesis 11:19

19And Peleg lived after he fathered Reu 209 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Two hundred nine years after Reu—Peleg’s remaining life is shorter than his fathers’.

Classical Arminians hear mortality settling toward the world Abram will know. Gift remains: sons, daughters, and enough years to hand the line on.

Reformed

Peleg’s after-span is markedly less. The narrator does not moralize; he numbers.

Reformed reading: providence governs even the shrinking of years. The promise-line is not threatened by a shorter life.

Genesis 11:20

20When Reu had lived 32 years, he fathered Serug.

Arminian

Reu fathers Serug at thirty-two. Another quiet link.

Arminian pastoral: most of God’s work toward Abram looks like this—a name, an age, a son. Do not despise the uneventful.

Reformed

Serug is born. The chain is now deep into the post-flood world.

Reformed note: unnamed decades are still decreed time. God is moving the story by births more than by speeches.

Genesis 11:21

21And Reu lived after he fathered Serug 207 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Two hundred seven years, other sons and daughters. The refrain is almost a lullaby—and still true.

Arminian reading: fruitfulness around the line continues. God is kind in the repetitive gifts.

Reformed

The formula holds. Reu’s house is larger than the one name we track.

Reformed accent: God’s bookkeeping includes the “other” children. Election of a line is not contempt for the rest of the family.

Genesis 11:22

22When Serug had lived 30 years, he fathered Nahor.

Arminian

Serug at thirty fathers Nahor—Abram’s grandfather is now in view, still two generations out.

Classical Arminians feel the story leaning forward. Ordinary begetting is how God sets the stage for a call not yet spoken.

Reformed

Nahor appears. The names begin to sound like the household of chapter 12.

Reformed comfort: providence is already naming the grandfather of the man God will call. The future promise is being prepared in a quiet list.

Genesis 11:23

23And Serug lived after he fathered Nahor 200 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Two hundred years after Nahor. Round numbers, other children, no anecdote.

Arminian pastoral: two centuries of unnamed life still sit inside God’s care. The line does not have to be famous yet to be kept.

Reformed

Serug’s two hundred years close another generation. God needs no extra plot.

Reformed note: the promise-line advances by duration. What looks uneventful is still His keeping.

Genesis 11:24

24When Nahor had lived 29 years, he fathered Terah.

Arminian

Nahor at twenty-nine fathers Terah. Ages keep dropping; the next name is Abram’s father.

Arminian reading: we are nearly at the household that will leave Ur. Ordinary years have carried us from Shem’s ark-memory to Terah’s door.

Reformed

Terah is born when Nahor is twenty-nine. The genealogy’s aim is coming into focus.

Reformed accent: God has been aiming this list. Terah exists because the Lord intended a father for Abram.

Genesis 11:25

25And Nahor lived after he fathered Terah 119 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

One hundred nineteen years after Terah—the shortest remaining span yet. Nahor’s generation feels nearer to later limits.

Classical Arminians take the shortening as wise disposal, not a narrative accident. The stage for Abram is a world of shorter lives and nearer graves.

Reformed

Nahor’s after-years are brief beside Shem’s. The narrator still adds other sons and daughters.

Reformed confession: God portions life. A shorter patriarch does not weaken the oath that will be spoken over his grandson.

Genesis 11:26

26When Terah had lived 70 years, he fathered Abram, Nahor, and Haran.

Arminian

Seventy years, then three sons: Abram, Nahor, and Haran. The name the next chapter will call is listed with his brothers. Privilege sits inside a real family.

Classical Arminians linger on Abram named among others. God’s coming call is sincere and particular, yet it does not erase Nahor and Haran. The promise-line is a man who will have to go; his brothers remain part of the story of ordinary kinship.

Reformed

Moses places Abram first, not necessarily as firstborn, but as the son toward whom the book is driving. Three names, one calling soon to fall.

Reformed accent: God has been narrowing from Shem to Terah to this trio. Election of Abram will not be a random draw; it is the point this genealogy has been serving. The brothers are real; the choice is God’s.

Key contrast Both honor Abram named among brothers. Arminians typically read a particular call that will still address a responsible man inside a real family; Reformed readers typically read the genealogy’s narrowing as God’s sovereign preparation of the man He will call.

Genesis 11:27

27Now these are the generations of Terah. Terah fathered Abram, Nahor, and Haran; and Haran fathered Lot.

Arminian

A new generations formula: Terah’s house. Haran fathers Lot, who will travel in the next chapters.

Arminian pastoral: the promise household already includes a nephew. God’s setup for chapter 12 is a family, not a solitary hero.

Reformed

Terah’s generations begin. Lot is named before the journey, because providence already sees the companion—and the later strife.

Reformed note: the camera has left the long list of years and opened a household. God is arranging the cast for the call.

Genesis 11:28

28Haran died in the presence of his father Terah in the land of his kindred, in Ur of the Chaldeans.

Arminian

Haran dies before his father’s face, in Ur of the Chaldeans. Grief sits in the genealogy. The land of kindred is also a place of graves.

Classical Arminians refuse to skip the death. Ordinary years include loss. Ur is already costly before anyone leaves it; the later call will not be tourism.

Reformed

Haran dies in Terah’s presence, in Ur. The promise-line is not spared family funerals.

Reformed accent: God records the place—Ur of the Chaldeans—because this is the soil from which He will shortly call. Death in the father’s sight is part of the setup, not an interruption God failed to notice.

Genesis 11:29

29And Abram and Nahor took wives. The name of Abram’s wife was Sarai, and the name of Nahor’s wife, Milcah, the daughter of Haran the father of Milcah and Iscah.

Arminian

Abram and Nahor take wives: Sarai and Milcah, Haran’s daughter. Marriages knit the wounded family together.

Arminian reading: human agency again—taking wives, naming them. The household that will hear God’s call is being formed in ordinary covenanting, including a brother’s child as Nahor’s wife.

Reformed

Two marriages, two names. Milcah’s father is the dead Haran; Iscah is mentioned and left.

Reformed comfort: God is stocking the family that will later supply Rebekah from Nahor’s line. Even the in-laws of Abram sit inside providence.

Genesis 11:30

30Now Sarai was barren; she had no child.

Arminian

Now Sarai was barren; she had no child. The promise-line arrives at an empty cradle before any promise is spoken.

Classical Arminians hear the ache. God will later give Isaac by gracious power; here the text simply tells the truth. Barrenness is the human condition into which the call must come. Hope will have to be gift.

Reformed

The narrator underlines barrenness twice: barren, no child. Calvin saw God designing to show, in Abram’s person, how the church would arise—as from a dry root.

Reformed confession: the line God has so carefully listed ends, humanly, in a closed womb. The next chapter’s offspring-promise will shine against this sentence. Election and miracle will have to do what years and names cannot.

Genesis 11:31

31Terah took Abram his son and Lot the son of Haran, his grandson, and Sarai his daughter-in-law, his son Abram’s wife, and they went forth together from Ur of the Chaldeans to go into the land of Canaan, but when they came to Haran, they settled there.

Arminian

Terah takes Abram, Lot, and Sarai from Ur toward Canaan, then settles in Haran. The aim is Canaan; the halt is Haran. Motive is mixed in the text—a family move later revealed as tied to God’s call.

Arminian pastoral: they go forth together, then stop. Settling short of Canaan is a real human pause. Chapter 12 will require Abram to finish what Haran interrupted. Grace already has them on the road; the stop is not the destination.

Reformed

The caravan leaves Ur for Canaan and sits down in Haran. Moses lets Terah’s name lead, yet the destination is the land God will specify.

Reformed accent: this is setup, not arrival. Providence extracts the household from Chaldea and holds them in Haran until Terah’s death and Abram’s call. The stop is God’s timing as much as a family’s weariness.

Key contrast Both see Ur-to-Canaan as the true direction and Haran as a halt. Arminians typically stress a real, responsible settling short of the land—movement that must later become Abram’s obedience; Reformed readers typically stress that God is relocating the chosen household on His schedule, Haran included.

Genesis 11:32

32The days of Terah were 205 years, and Terah died in Haran.

Arminian

Terah’s days are two hundred five years; he dies in Haran. The chapter ends at a graveside on the way, not in Canaan.

Classical Arminians close quietly: a father dies mid-journey, and the son will have to hear a fresh word. Ordinary years, a real death, and a city that is not the promised land—this is how Genesis prepares chapter 12. The call will not land on a man with no history.

Reformed

Terah dies in Haran. The genealogy’s last number is a death, and the place is still not Canaan.

Reformed close: God has brought the line from Shem to a grave in Mesopotamia so that the next sentence can be, “Go.” The promise-line is intact; the father is buried; the call of chapter 12 will be pure initiative, already prepared by these ordinary years.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
