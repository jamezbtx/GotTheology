Passage

Genesis 11

Verses 1–9 · ESV · Voices

After the flood, the whole earth still shares one language. People settle in Shinar, brick a city and a tower, and reach for a name lest they be dispersed. The LORD comes down, confuses their speech, and scatters them over the face of all the earth—judgment on pride that also fills the world. The place is called Babel.

Genesis 11:1

1Now the whole earth had one language and the same words.

Arminian

One language and the same words is still a gift. Shared speech lets neighbors help one another; it is not yet the tower.

Classical Arminians start here with human capacity under common kindness. Unity of tongue is light they can use to fill the earth—or to huddle against God’s spreading word. The gift is real; what they do with it will soon be their own responsible choice.

Reformed

Moses opens with a kindness: the whole earth still holds one speech. Calvin heard here God’s will that a sacred bond of society be retained by a common language.

Reformed accent: language is the Lord’s provision before it is a human tool. The later confusion will be adventitious punishment, not the original design. Providence already governs the world’s one lip.

Genesis 11:2

2And as people migrated from the east, they found a plain in the land of Shinar and settled there.

Arminian

They migrate from the east, find a plain in Shinar, and settle. Movement looks like the earth beginning to be filled; settling is the moment they stop.

Arminian pastoral: finding a fertile plain is not sin. Choosing it as a permanent cluster, against the call to spread, is a human project they will soon name out loud. Grace had given them a world; they prefer a pad.

Reformed

Migration and settlement sit under God’s eye. Shinar will be Babel’s stage; the plain is found, not invented.

Reformed reading: they dwell by clustering. The God who told the earth to be filled watches a people halt in one valley. The next verses will show that halt as proud policy, not mere geography.

Genesis 11:3

3And they said to one another, “Come, let us make bricks, and burn them thoroughly.” And they had brick for stone, and bitumen for mortar.

Arminian

They speak to one another: Come, let us make bricks. Human agency is on the surface—mutual exhortation, technique, bitumen for mortar.

Classical Arminians notice the responsible “us.” No one forces the kiln. They overcome the lack of stone by invention. Light enough to cooperate for a city is light enough they might have cooperated to scatter; they spend it on the tower instead.

Reformed

Calvin marked the mutual urging: every man put his hand to the work and impelled others. Brick for stone shows they were not lured by ease.

Reformed weight: difficulty does not humble them. They wear themselves out on a hard project because ambition has already chosen its monument. Providence will later turn even this industry into the means of the scatter.

Genesis 11:4

4Then they said, “Come, let us build ourselves a city and a tower with its top in the heavens, and let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be dispersed over the face of the whole earth.”

Arminian

City, tower, name, and the fear of dispersal: they say out loud what they intend. The tower’s top in the heavens is boast; the deeper aim is to refuse being spread over the earth.

Arminian reading: this is resistible light refused. Genesis 9 had said, fill the earth. Wesley heard their answer: God orders them to scatter; they reply, we will live and die together. Human agency builds the city; pride writes the charter. God will check that pride so the nations might yet inhabit the world—and, in due time, seek Him (Acts 17:26–27).

Reformed

“Let us make a name” is theology in a slogan. Calvin heard mad ambition and proud contempt of God: an immortal name on earth, building as if against the Lord’s will.

Reformed confession: to erect a citadel was not the whole crime; to raise an eternal monument to themselves was headstrong pride joined with contempt of God. The earth-filling mandate will not be vetoed by a skyline. What they fear—dispersal—God has already willed as blessing, and He will not let pride thwart it.

Key contrast Both hear pride and a refusal to fill the earth. Arminians typically stress a responsible, resistible “Come, let us” that God will check so nations might still seek Him; Reformed readers typically stress that a self-made name cannot overturn a decreed mandate—the scatter is already God’s purpose.

Genesis 11:5

5And the LORD came down to see the city and the tower, which the children of man had built.

Arminian

The LORD comes down to see. The language is pastoral, after the manner of men: the Judge looks at what the children of man have actually built.

Arminian gravity: worms of the earth have defied heaven, yet God inspects before He confuses. The seeing is sincere. Pride is not ignored; neither is it crushed without a holy looking.

Reformed

Descent is spoken with respect to us, not as if God moved house. He shows He is not ignorant of Babylon’s pile.

Reformed note: God may seem to take no notice while the wicked succeed, then appear as Avenger. The children of man built; the Lord will judge what He has seen. Patience is not absence.

Genesis 11:6

6And the LORD said, “Behold, they are one people, and they have all one language, and this is only the beginning of what they will do. And nothing that they propose to do will now be impossible for them.

Arminian

The Lord’s speech begins: one people, one language, and this only the beginning. United, they can propose almost anything.

Classical Arminians hear a real assessment of human solidarity. Shared speech is power they are using against God’s spreading will. He will cross the design—not because He cannot abide human culture, but because this culture has set itself to stay and to make a name.

Reformed

Behold is God’s own commentary. Calvin heard irony: they are compacted, they share a tongue—by what method can they be broken? The answer is His.

Reformed accent: “nothing that they propose will now be impossible” is not a limit on God. It names what fallen unity will dare unless He interrupts. The decree to interrupt is already in the next breath.

Genesis 11:7

7Come, let us go down and there confuse their language, so that they may not understand one another’s speech.”

Arminian

The quote continues: Come, let us go down and confuse their language. “Let us” answers their “let us.” Confusion is a means: they will not understand, so they cannot keep building as one.

Arminian pastoral: God checks pride without annihilating the builders. Tongues become both judgment and mercy—stopping the tower, sending families out, leaving the nations in a world where they might grope toward Him (Acts 17). The light they resisted is not the last word of kindness.

Reformed

“Let us go down” answers their “Come, let us.” Calvin referred this to God’s own eternal wisdom and power, as in “Let us make man.”

Reformed confession: confounding speech is the appointed instrument. Providence over tongues is meticulous—not random noise, but a decreed means so their counsel fails. The scatter is not Plan B; it is God doing what pride refused.

Genesis 11:8

8So the LORD dispersed them from there over the face of all the earth, and they left off building the city.

Arminian

So the LORD dispersed them, and they left off building. The project stops because they cannot work as one. Scattering is judgment on the name-seekers and, at the same time, the way the earth is finally filled.

Classical Arminians hold both: they brought on themselves what they feared, and God used that check so families would take the lands they would not choose. Human agency built; human agency also walks away when speech fails. Mercy hides inside the sentence.

Reformed

The Lord dispersed them from there. Calvin distinguished honorable spreading under blessing from this ignominious scattering, like members of a lacerated body.

Reformed weight: pride cannot thwart the earth-filling mandate. What they would have blocked by a city, God accomplishes by decree. They left off because He cut the bond; the nations exist because He said so.

Key contrast Both see judgment and a filled earth in the same dispersal. Arminians typically emphasize God checking a resistible project so nations might yet live and seek Him; Reformed readers typically emphasize that the scatter was decreed—pride’s city cannot cancel providence.

Genesis 11:9

9Therefore its name was called Babel, because there the LORD confused the language of all the earth. And from there the LORD dispersed them over the face of all the earth.

Arminian

The name they wanted becomes Babel: confusion. The Lord confused the language of all the earth and from there dispersed them. Languages are wound and gift together.

Arminian close: Clarke saw the very means they used to stay become the instrument of spreading. God checks pride so the map of nations opens—and so that, later, those nations might seek the Lord who determined their times and dwelling places (Acts 17:26–27). Pentecost will one day gather what Babel divided, without erasing the mercy of many tongues.

Reformed

They gain a name, Calvin said, but not such as they would have chosen. God brands the site with disgrace and still fills the world from it.

Reformed comfort: providence over tongues includes later gospel in every language. Judgment is real; so is the Lord’s freedom to turn confusion into the theater of one gospel for many peoples. Babel does not get the last word—God does.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on pride, confused tongues, and the scattering of the nations—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 11:1–9.

Arminian Notable voices

“Two things it seems they aimed at in building: to make them a name—they would do something to be talked of by posterity—and to prevent their dispersion. It was done in disobedience to that command, replenish the earth. God orders them to scatter. No, say they, we will live and die together.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Genesis 11:3–4)
“Coming to the fertile plains of Shinar they proposed to settle themselves there, instead of spreading themselves over all the countries of the earth, according to the design of God; but God, miraculously interposing, confounded or frustrated their rebellious design, which was inconsistent with his will. He had purposed that men should be dispersed over the earth, and therefore caused the means which they were using to prevent it to become the grand instrument of its accomplishment.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Genesis 11:4, 9)

Reformed Notable voices

“Nothing, indeed, is here noticed, except their mad ambitions and proud contempt of God. Let us build a tower whose top may reach to heaven, and let us get ourselves a name. For whatsoever might happen, they wish to have an immortal name on earth; and thus they build, as if in opposition to the will of God. And doubtless ambition not only does injury to men, but exalts itself even against God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on 11:1–4)
“Thus they brought upon themselves the very thing they feared, and that more speedily and more mischievously to themselves; for now they were not only divided in place, but in language too. God did intend to disperse and separate them, that so by degrees they might possess the whole earth, which God had made for that purpose.”
Matthew Poole · English Annotations on the Holy Bible (on Genesis 11:5, 8)
