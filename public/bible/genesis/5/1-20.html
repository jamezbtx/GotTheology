Passage

Genesis 5

Verses 1–20 · ESV · Voices

After Cain’s line in chapter 4, Genesis 5 opens the “book of the generations of Adam”—image-bearing humanity named Man, then the Sethite succession marching toward Noah. The refrain “and he died” sounds the curse after Eden; the long years still display God’s patience preserving a people.

Genesis 5:1

1This is the book of the generations of Adam. When God created man, he made him in the likeness of God.

Arminian

The chapter opens as a “book of generations,” locating Adam’s family in God’s creative act: man was made in the likeness of God. Likeness is gift before it is task—dignity bestowed on the race, not earned by the later Sethite line.

Classical Arminians hear here a sincere, universal dignity: every image-bearer is addressed by the God who made them. That likeness is marred by the fall, yet grace still calls men and women to walk with their Maker; the genealogy that follows is a line of people who remain responsible before that call.

Reformed

“Generations of Adam” is more than a family list; it is history under God’s hand. The likeness of God is the original glory of humanity, stated here as God’s doing—“he made him.”

Reformed reading stresses that this likeness is now the backdrop for a fallen line. Providence will keep a people through these names, but the image is not a ladder sinners climb; it is the dignity God gave and the standard against which the coming death-refrain will sound.

Key contrast Arminians typically stress remaining, universally addressed image-dignity that grace still calls into communion; Reformed readers typically stress the original gift now set over a fallen race whose story God sovereignly orders toward His covenant purpose.

Genesis 5:2

2Male and female he created them, and he blessed them and named them Man when they were created.

Arminian

Male and female together receive blessing and the name “Man.” Humanity’s identity is God’s naming, not self-invention, and the blessing is spoken over the pair as a shared vocation.

Pastoral Arminian note: the God who blessed at creation still sincerely blesses and calls. Gendered humanity is not an accident of the Sethite story; it is the creational stage on which faith and obedience are later invited.

Reformed

God creates, blesses, and names. The triad is all divine initiative: identity (“Man”) is conferred, not negotiated. Male and female belong to the one humanity God addressed in the beginning.

Reformed comfort: the blessing that named the race is the same Lord’s prerogative that will later preserve a line through death. History begins with God’s word over humanity, and that word is not revoked even when the curse is heard in the refrain “and he died.”

Genesis 5:3

3When Adam had lived 130 years, he fathered a son in his own likeness, after his image, and named him Seth.

Arminian

Seth is born in Adam’s likeness and image—language that echoes 1:26–27 but now passes through a fallen father. The image of God is not erased; it is now also Adam’s image, a son who bears both dignity and the frailty of the first man.

Classical Arminians refuse a straw man that denies original sin. They confess inherited corruption and mortality, yet they also insist that Seth remains an image-bearer whom God may graciously enable to respond. The line toward Noah is a corporate gift that does not cancel personal calling.

Reformed

“His own likeness, after his image” is the hinge of the chapter. What Adam transmits is not only creational dignity but the image of a man who has fallen. Seth is truly Adam’s son—and that is both gift and grief.

Reformed theology hears original sin here without requiring later Pauline vocabulary from the text itself: the likeness now running in the bloodline is Adam’s, and only God’s preserving purpose will carry this family toward Noah and the promised seed.

Key contrast The shared confession is that Seth bears both God’s image and Adam’s; the accent differs. Arminians stress remaining dignity and a still-sincere call under enabling grace; Reformed readers stress transmitted corruption in Adam’s likeness, the line kept by providence more than by native spiritual health.

Genesis 5:4

4The days of Adam after he fathered Seth were 800 years; and he had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Adam’s long years after Seth are not empty: other sons and daughters fill the earth. The text will track one line, but it does not pretend that only Seth existed. God’s patience gives time for a multiplying family.

Pastoral note: long life here is space for repentance and for raising children under the knowledge of God. Grace grants years; those years remain a field of real response, not a guarantee that every child will walk with the Lord.

Reformed

Eight hundred years after Seth display God’s forbearance. The unnamed sons and daughters matter to the filling of the earth, yet the narrator’s interest is the appointed line.

Providence is already selective in the telling: many children, one named succession. The Reformed accent is not contempt for the others but confidence that God is shepherding a particular story through Adam’s house.

Genesis 5:5

5Thus all the days that Adam lived were 930 years, and he died.

Arminian

The first “and he died” lands with the weight of Genesis 3. Long life is not immunity; the wages of the fall still collect. Adam, image-bearer and father of Seth, returns to dust.

Classical Arminians preach this without softening: death is the common lot after Eden, and it warns every generation. Yet the years God gave were also space in which Adam could seek mercy. Mortality is certain; the call to walk with God remains sincere for those who still live.

Reformed

Nine hundred thirty years, and he died. The refrain that will dominate the chapter begins with the first man. God’s word in the garden was not idle.

Reformed pastoral weight: death is not a natural bump in an otherwise healthy race; it is the curse working itself out. The line continues only because God keeps it. Certainty of death under Adam makes the later exception of Enoch, and the later hope named Noah, shine as God’s acts.

Key contrast Both camps hear the curse in “and he died.” Arminians often pair that certainty with the years as a season of responsible seeking; Reformed readers emphasize death as the judicial consequence Adam transmits, a sentence only God can interrupt or finally heal.

Genesis 5:6

6When Seth had lived 105 years, he fathered Enosh.

Arminian

Seth, the appointed son after Abel’s death, becomes a father in his turn. Enosh’s name (frail man) already whispers the humility Genesis 4:26 associated with calling on the Lord.

The genealogy is a covenant line, yet each fathering is also a human act under grace. Seth does not save his son by blood; Enosh must live his own years before God. Enabling grace can meet that household without erasing their need to trust.

Reformed

Seth fathers Enosh at 105 years—another link God is pleased to record. The promised seed-story (3:15) is not abandoned; it is being quietly threaded.

Reformed reading: the named succession is providence, not bare biology. God preserves a people through ordinary births so that hope is not lost in Cain’s violence or in Adam’s death.

Genesis 5:7

7Seth lived after he fathered Enosh 807 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Seth’s centuries after Enosh are a long ministry of fatherhood. Other sons and daughters share the family, even if the narrator follows one name.

Arminian pastoral accent: years after a child’s birth are years of influence. Grace enables parents to teach and to walk; it does not coerce the household. Seth’s longevity is patience granted to a man who had seen both worship (4:26) and the world’s wound.

Reformed

Eight hundred seven years keep Seth on the stage while Enosh grows and the line thickens. Unnamed children again fill the earth under God’s leave.

The Reformed note is quiet sovereignty: God allots the span, multiplies the family, and still marks the one name that will carry the story forward. Patience here is not indecision; it is purposeful delay toward Noah.

Genesis 5:8

8Thus all the days of Seth were 912 years, and he died.

Arminian

Seth too dies. The son named in hope after Abel does not escape the dust. Nine hundred twelve years end in the same refrain as Adam’s.

Death after a long life still preaches: no patriarch is his own savior. The God who gave Seth as a token of grace (4:25) also requires him to yield his breath. Those who remain are called to walk while it is day.

Reformed

Seth’s total is told so that the reader will feel the pattern: lived, fathered, died. The line of promise is a line of mortals.

Reformed comfort is severe and kind: God keeps the succession through death, not around it. Seth’s grave is not the failure of the promise; it is the condition under which God continues to write the generations toward Noah.

Genesis 5:9

9When Enosh had lived 90 years, he fathered Kenan.

Arminian

Enosh, whose name evokes frailty, becomes a father at ninety. Weak humanity still receives the gift of a son. The story does not wait for strong people; it runs through those who must call on the Lord.

Prevenient grace meets frail households. Kenan’s birth is not Enosh saving himself; it is God continuing a people who remain responsible to seek Him in their weakness.

Reformed

Enosh fathers Kenan—frailty begetting the next name. God is not embarrassed to build His purpose on “mortal man.”

Providence uses the weak without waiting for a better stock. The Reformed accent: the genealogical line is God’s keeping, and Kenan arrives because the Lord of the generations willed another link.

Genesis 5:10

10Enosh lived after he fathered Kenan 815 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Eight hundred fifteen years after Kenan give Enosh a long evening of family life. Frail man is granted time, children, and the ordinary work of a household.

Those years are a sincere space for walking with God and teaching sons. Grace enables; it can also be neglected. Enosh’s generation is not a blank between famous names.

Reformed

The same long after-years show God’s forbearance toward Enosh’s house. Other sons and daughters again witness that the earth is being filled even as the narrator tracks one thread.

God measures the span. Reformed readers thank Him for unnoticed centuries that still belong to His counsel—patience that preserves a people until the next named father.

Genesis 5:11

11Thus all the days of Enosh were 905 years, and he died.

Arminian

Enosh dies at 905. The man whose name means frailty proves it in the end. Calling on the Lord’s name (4:26) did not cancel the grave.

Pastoral Arminian word: piety does not bargain away mortality in this chapter. What it does is summon the living to seek God while breath lasts. The refrain is a warning and a mercy—time is limited, the call is real.

Reformed

Nine hundred five years, and he died. The pattern holds. Frailty is not only a name; it is the condition of the race under the curse.

Reformed preaching lets the refrain do its work: death is the rule in Adam. God may later take Enoch, and He will later spare a family in the ark, but Enosh’s end confirms that salvation will have to be God’s doing, not human longevity.

Genesis 5:12

12When Kenan had lived 70 years, he fathered Mahalalel.

Arminian

Kenan fathers Mahalalel at seventy—the youngest first-fathering so far. The name Mahalalel (“praise of God”) turns the genealogy toward worship, even if the text does not pause to explain it.

A shorter wait to fatherhood still sits inside enabling grace: Kenan receives a son, and that son’s very name can catechize a house to praise. Corporate line and personal vocation meet in an ordinary birth.

Reformed

Kenan at seventy is another appointed begetting. Whether or not we press the etymology, the name Mahalalel sits in a line God is writing.

Providence does not need famous deeds at every step. The Reformed note is simply that God continues the generations—Mahalalel is gift and link, kept for a purpose larger than Kenan’s household could see.

Genesis 5:13

13Kenan lived after he fathered Mahalalel 840 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Kenan’s 840 years after Mahalalel are a long faithfulness-or-drift window. Other children share the family table; only one name will be followed.

Arminian reading honors the unnamed: they too are image-bearers under a sincere call. The recorded line is covenant history, not a claim that God never addressed the others.

Reformed

Eight hundred forty years keep Kenan’s generation in play. God fills the earth through many children while marking Mahalalel for the succession.

Quiet sovereignty again: the narrator’s choice of names is theological. God is preserving a particular story through Kenan’s house, even when most of that house remains unnamed.

Genesis 5:14

14Thus all the days of Kenan were 910 years, and he died.

Arminian

Kenan’s 910 years close like the rest. Praise in a son’s name does not exempt the father from dying. The chapter will not let readers baptize longevity as salvation.

Walk while you have the years. Grace is patient in this list; it is not permissive of presumption. Kenan’s death is the shared human story after Eden, and it asks for a living trust in the God who outlasts our span.

Reformed

Nine hundred ten, and he died. Kenan is gathered to the pattern. The curse is not losing force as names accumulate.

Reformed pastoral accent: God can be trusted with a line that keeps dying. The promise does not rest on Kenan’s endurance but on the Lord who numbers the days and still has Noah—and beyond him, the seed—in view.

Genesis 5:15

15When Mahalalel had lived 65 years, he fathered Jared.

Arminian

Mahalalel fathers Jared at sixty-five. “Praise of God” begets a son whose name is often related to descent. Worshiping houses still send children into a descending, mortal world.

The birth is grace and task: a father received, a child to raise. Enabling grace can turn a home toward praise; it does not make Jared faithful by blood alone.

Reformed

Another early fathering, another kept link. Jared will be the father of Enoch—the genealogy is quietly approaching its first great interruption.

Providence is already arranging that approach. Reformed readers do not need to force typology onto Jared’s name; it is enough that God places him as the father of the man who will walk with God.

Genesis 5:16

16Mahalalel lived after he fathered Jared 830 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Eight hundred thirty years after Jared give Mahalalel a long season beside the son who will father Enoch. Praise of God, if it is more than a name, has time to be practiced in a crowded household.

Those years are a real history of response. The genealogy’s sparseness should not hide that: grace granted time; Mahalalel’s house had to use it or neglect it.

Reformed

Mahalalel’s after-years are the long runway toward Enoch. Other sons and daughters again fill out the generation while God tracks Jared.

The Reformed instinct is patience with providence: God is not hurried, and He is not idle. Eight centuries of ordinary life can still be the appointed preparation for a son who will walk with God.

Genesis 5:17

17Thus all the days of Mahalalel were 895 years, and he died.

Arminian

Mahalalel dies at 895—shorter than several fathers before him, still immense, still mortal. Praise of God does not cancel the refrain.

A life that can be named for praise still ends. The living are therefore summoned to praise now. Death after Eden is the shared ground; the sincere call is to honor God before the years close.

Reformed

Eight hundred ninety-five years, and he died. Even a name associated with praise is placed under the curse’s clock.

God’s purpose is not tied to Mahalalel remaining on earth. The line moves on because the Lord moves it on. Certainty of death here makes Enoch’s coming exception more stark, not less.

Genesis 5:18

18When Jared had lived 162 years, he fathered Enoch.

Arminian

Jared waits longer than his recent fathers—162 years—then Enoch is born. The most famous walker in the chapter is still someone’s son, born in ordinary time.

Enoch’s later communion is not a denial of this ordinary beginning. Grace that will meet Enoch as a man already met him as a child in Jared’s house. Responsible walking later does not erase the gift of being fathered now.

Reformed

Jared fathers Enoch at 162. The man whom God will take is first a child in the appointed line. Exceptional grace later does not make this birth accidental.

Reformed reading: God provides the father and the son. Enoch’s story will highlight divine action, but it begins as one more kept generation in a list God is ruling toward Noah.

Genesis 5:19

19Jared lived after he fathered Enoch 800 years and had other sons and daughters.

Arminian

Jared lives 800 years after Enoch—long enough, in the narrative’s pacing, to outlast much of Enoch’s earthly span. A father can watch a son walk with God, and still have other children to raise.

Those centuries are a pastoral picture: one child’s unusual path does not retire the rest of the family from the call. Grace remains sincere toward the unnamed sons and daughters as well.

Reformed

Eight hundred years after Enoch keep Jared in the story while God does something singular with the son. Other children are acknowledged; the spotlight stays where the narrator places it.

Providence can run an ordinary father’s longevity alongside an extraordinary son’s communion. Jared’s years are part of the same counsel that will take Enoch and still lead the line through Methuselah to Noah.

Genesis 5:20

20Thus all the days of Jared were 962 years, and he died.

Arminian

Jared’s 962 years—among the longest—still end, “and he died.” Nearness to Enoch in the family tree is not a charm against the grave.

The refrain prepares the contrast of the next verses. Most saints, even fathers of the devout, die. The call is not to envy Enoch in advance but to walk faithfully in the years God actually gives.

Reformed

Nine hundred sixty-two years, and he died. Jared receives no exemption. The approaching Enoch story will break the pattern; Jared confirms the pattern first.

Reformed pastoral weight: God may glorify Himself in an exception without making exception the rule. Jared dies under the curse; the line continues by God’s will, not by a father’s nearness to a holy son.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Adam’s generations and the image passed down through Seth—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 5:1–20.

Arminian Notable voices

“The first words of the chapter are the title of argument of the whole chapter; it is the book of the generations of Adam—It is the list or catalogue of the posterity of Adam, not of all, but only of the holy seed, and of whom as concerning the flesh Christ came; the names, ages, and deaths of those that were the successors of the first Adam in the custody of the promise, and the ancestors of the second Adam.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 5:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“We have lately said that Moses traces the offspring of Adam only through the line of Seth, to propose for our consideration the succession of the Church. In saying that Seth begat a son after his own image, he refers in part to the first origin of our nature: at the same time its corruption and pollution is to be noticed, which having been contracted by Adam through the fall, has flowed down to all his posterity.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 5:3)
