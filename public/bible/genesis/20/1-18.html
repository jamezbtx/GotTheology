Passage

Genesis 20

Verses 1–18 · ESV · Voices

After the destruction of the cities of the plain, Abraham sojourns in Gerar and again presents Sarah as his sister. Abimelech takes her; God warns the king in a dream, restrains him from sin, and restores Sarah through Abraham’s prophetic prayer—promise threatened, then preserved by the LORD.

Genesis 20:1

1From there Abraham journeyed toward the territory of the Negeb and lived between Kadesh and Shur; and he sojourned in Gerar.

Arminian

Abraham moves south and sojourns in Gerar—still a guest, still on the road of promise. Location changes; vocation does not.

Classical Arminians notice a real choice of place under God’s care. Grace accompanies the wanderer, yet the next verse will show how fear can still script the stay.

Reformed

“From there” continues providence after judgment on the cities. The patriarch’s sojourn is not random; Gerar becomes the next stage of the promise’s testing.

Reformed framing: God shepherds Abraham even into foreign courts. The field of the next crisis is appointed, not accidental.

Genesis 20:2

2And Abraham said of Sarah his wife, “She is my sister.” And Abimelech king of Gerar sent and took Sarah.

Arminian

The old Egypt tactic returns: “She is my sister.” Sarah is taken. Half-truth still endangers the wife of promise.

Arminian honesty: this is culpable unbelief, not clever diplomacy. Grace had already rescued them in chapter 12; repeating the scheme is a responsible failure, not fate.

Reformed

Abraham speaks; the king takes. The promise-line is immediately at risk in a pagan court.

Reformed note: election does not make the patriarch flawless. God will have to keep Sarah, because Abraham’s word has put her—and the seed—in jeopardy.

Key contrast Both refuse to baptize the lie. Arminians typically stress Abraham’s resistible, repeated unbelief under a still-sincere promise; Reformed readers typically stress that the same failure cannot void God’s purpose to preserve the line.

Genesis 20:3

3But God came to Abimelech in a dream by night and said to him, “Behold, you are a dead man because of the woman whom you have taken, for she is a man's wife.”

Arminian

God, not Abraham, protects Sarah. A night dream tells a Gentile king he is a dead man because he has taken another man’s wife.

Arminian reading: revelation reaches Abimelech so he may turn. The warning is severe and sincere—life is offered on the far side of obedience.

Reformed

Initiative is God’s: He comes, He names the crime, He announces death. Sarah is “a man’s wife” under divine witness.

Reformed comfort: the promise does not sleep in Gerar. God interrupts the king before the damage becomes irreversible.

Genesis 20:4

4Now Abimelech had not approached her. So he said, “Lord, will you kill an innocent people?”

Arminian

The narrator clears Abimelech of consummation. He pleads, “Lord, will you kill an innocent people?”—echoing Abraham’s own intercession for a city.

Classical Arminians hear a Gentile appealing to God’s justice. Integrity matters; God is not arbitrary, and the king’s protest assumes a moral Lord who can be addressed.

Reformed

Restraint is already a fact: he had not approached her. His plea for an “innocent people” still comes under the God who has spoken death.

Reformed note: even a pagan king knows to argue innocence before the LORD. Providence has kept the household from a sin not yet completed.

Genesis 20:5

5Did he not himself say to me, ‘She is my sister’? And she herself said, ‘He is my brother.’ In the integrity of my heart and the innocence of my hands I have done this.”

Arminian

Abimelech cites both spouses’ words and claims integrity of heart and innocent hands. He was deceived into the taking.

Arminian pastoral point: words have public consequence. Abraham and Sarah’s collusion placed a neighbor in moral danger; responsibility for the lie is not erased by the king’s sincerity.

Reformed

The king’s defense is twofold: he was told a lie, and his own heart was not predatory. Genesis lets that claim stand to be answered by God in the next verse.

Reformed reading: God will vindicate the king’s integrity without excusing Abraham. Truth-telling in Gerar still belongs to the Lord of the promise.

Genesis 20:6

6Then God said to him in the dream, “Yes, I know that you have done this in the integrity of your heart, and it was I who kept you from sinning against me. Therefore I did not let you touch her.

Arminian

God agrees: the king’s heart was integral. Then He adds the deeper mercy—“it was I who kept you from sinning against me.” Touch was restrained.

Classical Arminians hear both integrity and preventing grace. God can check a path before it becomes adultery; the king is still called to obey the word that follows.

Reformed

Divine knowledge and divine restraint meet. Integrity is granted, yet sin against God is averted because God did not let him touch her.

Reformed accent: preservation of the promise is God’s act. Abimelech’s clean hands are themselves a gift of providence, not autonomous luck.

Key contrast Both credit God with keeping Abimelech from touching Sarah. Arminians typically describe preventing grace that still leaves the king a real, obedient respondent; Reformed readers typically stress sovereign restraint that effectually guards the covenant wife.

Genesis 20:7

7Now then, return the man's wife, for he is a prophet, so that he will pray for you, and you shall live. But if you do not return her, know that you shall surely die, you and all who are yours.”

Arminian

Return her: Abraham is a prophet who must pray so the king may live. Refusal means death for household as well as king.

Arminian gravity: the command is genuine. Life is offered through restitution and intercession; the warning is not theater. Grace restores through means the king can actually take.

Reformed

Abraham, despite the lie, is named prophet. Prayer is appointed as the path of the king’s life; refusal is certain death.

Reformed framing: God uses a flawed prophet as means of mercy. The outcome—life or death—rests on the word God has spoken over Gerar.

Key contrast Both take the life-or-death command as real. Arminians typically emphasize Abimelech’s responsible return under sincere threat and offer; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s appointed means (prophet’s prayer) that will effect the king’s life.

Genesis 20:8

8So Abimelech rose early in the morning and called all his servants and told them all these things. And the men were very much afraid.

Arminian

Early rising, full disclosure, shared fear. The dream becomes public policy in the household.

Arminian note: holy fear can be a right first response to God’s warning. The court treats the word as true and urgent—an example Abraham’s own fear did not match.

Reformed

The king does not delay. Servants hear, and fear spreads as the proper echo of God’s night word.

Reformed comfort: God can create the fear of God in Gerar that Abraham assumed was absent. Providence already begins to reverse the patriarch’s misreading.

Genesis 20:9

9Then Abimelech called Abraham and said to him, “What have you done to us? And how have I sinned against you, that you have brought on me and my kingdom a great sin? You have done to me things that ought not to be done.”

Arminian

The pagan king catechizes the patriarch: What have you done? You brought a great sin on us. Things that ought not to be done.

Classical Arminians let the rebuke stand. Moral law is not Israel’s private code; Abraham’s scheme harmed a neighbor and a kingdom, and he is rightly called to account.

Reformed

Abimelech’s questions expose the scandal: the man of promise has imported sin into Gerar. “Ought not” is natural-law speech under God’s prior warning.

Reformed humility: common grace and conscience can shame the elect. God uses a Gentile king to name the patriarch’s wrong.

Genesis 20:10

10And Abimelech said to Abraham, “What did you see, that you did this thing?”

Arminian

“What did you see?” presses motive. The king wants the perception that produced the lie.

Arminian pastoral edge: fear-based seeing distorts neighbors. Abraham must own what he thought he saw—and how that seeing failed love.

Reformed

The question is diagnostic: what sight drove the deed? Unbelief had a vision of Gerar that God is about to contradict.

Reformed note: wrong seeing of a place can still serve God’s larger exposing of the heart.

Genesis 20:11

11Abraham said, “I did it because I thought, ‘There is no fear of God at all in this place, and they will kill me because of my wife.’

Arminian

Abraham confesses a thought: no fear of God here, therefore murder for Sarah’s sake. He judged a whole people unseen.

Arminian warning: anxious unbelief slanders neighbors and withholds trust from the God who had already protected him in Egypt. The fear of God was in Gerar; it was thin in Abraham’s plan.

Reformed

The motive is stated: assumed atheism of the place, assumed death. The father of faith reasoned from fear, not from promise.

Reformed irony: God has just displayed fear of God in Abimelech’s court. Providence unmasks the patriarch’s misjudgment without discarding him.

Key contrast Both name Abraham’s motive as fearful unbelief. Arminians typically stress the culpable, correctable misreading of Gerar; Reformed readers typically stress that God still keeps the promise while exposing the patriarch’s false sight.

Genesis 20:12

12Besides, she is indeed my sister, the daughter of my father though not the daughter of my mother, and she became my wife.

Arminian

He adds a technical defense: she is a half-sister who became his wife. True kinship does not make the concealment righteous.

Arminian clarity: selective facts can still deceive. Marriage was the relevant truth in Gerar; hiding it remains sin even if genealogy is accurate.

Reformed

The half-truth is admitted. Genesis records the kinship without endorsing the use Abraham made of it.

Reformed reading: legal precision cannot cover covenant infidelity. God had already called her “a man’s wife.”

Genesis 20:13

13And when God caused me to wander from my father's house, I said to her, ‘This is the kindness you must do me: at every place to which we come, say of me, “He is my brother.”’

Arminian

He traces the tactic to the original call: a standing request that Sarah call him brother “at every place.” Fear was policy, not a one-off.

Classical Arminians hear how a grace-begun pilgrimage can carry a habitual scheme. “Kindness” asked of Sarah was costly; the pattern needed repentance, not repetition.

Reformed

Even “God caused me to wander” is turned into rationale for a traveling lie. Divine calling is named; trust is not practiced.

Reformed gravity: the God who called Abraham out still has to rescue the policy Abraham packed for the road.

Genesis 20:14

14Then Abimelech took sheep and oxen, and male servants and female servants, and gave them to Abraham, and returned Sarah his wife to him.

Arminian

Restitution is concrete: livestock, servants, and Sarah returned as wife. The king obeys the dream with open-handed repair.

Arminian hope: wrong can be unwound in history. Grace uses a Gentile’s restitution to put the marriage—and the promise—back in the open.

Reformed

Sarah comes home by the king’s gift and God’s prior command. Abimelech’s goods accompany the return.

Reformed note: God can enrich the patriarch even through the aftermath of his own sin—without calling the sin wisdom.

Genesis 20:15

15And Abimelech said, “Behold, my land is before you; dwell where it pleases you.”

Arminian

Land is offered freely: dwell where it pleases you. The feared killer becomes a host.

Arminian irony: the place Abraham read as godless now practices generosity. Neighbor-love from Gerar rebukes the sojourner’s suspicion.

Reformed

Hospitality replaces hostility. The king’s land is placed at Abraham’s disposal under the God who warned him.

Reformed comfort: providence turns a death-dream into dwelling rights. The promise of land is tasted even in a foreign grant.

Genesis 20:16

16To Sarah he said, “Behold, I have given your brother a thousand pieces of silver. It is a sign of your innocence in the eyes of all who are with you, and before everyone you are vindicated.”

Arminian

Abimelech addresses Sarah, names “your brother,” and gives silver as public covering—her innocence vindicated before all.

Arminian pastoral care: Sarah is not a silent prop. The king’s speech restores her honor in the community Abraham’s ruse had compromised.

Reformed

A thousand pieces of silver function as public vindication. Sarah’s name is cleared in the eyes of all.

Reformed reading: God sees to the honor of the woman through whom the seed must come. Even the awkward “your brother” cannot hide that she is restored as wife.

Genesis 20:17

17Then Abraham prayed to God, and God healed Abimelech, and also healed his wife and female slaves so that they bore children.

Arminian

The prophet prays; God heals the king, his wife, and female slaves so they bear children. Intercession is effective means, not empty rite.

Classical Arminians see prayer as God’s appointed way of restoring what sin had closed. Abraham must serve the very household he endangered—grace repairing through a responsible act of petition.

Reformed

Prayer and healing are paired: Abraham asks, God heals, wombs open. The prophet’s office, announced in the dream, is now performed.

Reformed confession: God is the healer. He uses the patriarch’s prayer as means while remaining the One who opens wombs.

Key contrast Both tie healing to Abraham’s prayer. Arminians typically emphasize appointed, responsible intercession God sincerely uses; Reformed readers typically emphasize God’s effectual healing through the means He ordained.

Genesis 20:18

18For the Lord had closed all the wombs of the house of Abimelech because of Sarah, Abraham's wife.

Arminian

The closing of wombs is now explained: because of Sarah, Abraham’s wife. God had already been acting for her honor before the prayer.

Arminian gravity and hope: the LORD defends marriage and promise in a foreign house. Fertility itself can become a sign—judgment first, then mercy when the wife is restored.

Reformed

The narrator names the LORD as the closer of wombs. “Because of Sarah, Abraham’s wife” is covenant logic, not caprice.

Reformed doxology: God guarded the matriarch at the level of life itself. The same Lord who closes also heals—sovereign over Gerar’s future as over Abraham’s.

Key contrast Both see the closed wombs as the LORD’s defense of Sarah. Arminians typically stress moral, responsive dealings that invite restoration; Reformed readers typically stress God’s sovereign, covenant protection of the promised line.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Abraham, Abimelech, and God restraining a king from sin—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 20:1–18.

Arminian Notable voices

“And Abraham sojourned in Gerar—We are not told upon what occasion he removed, whether terrified by the destruction of Sodom, or, as some of the Jewish writers say, because he was grieved at Lot's incest with his daughters, and the reproach which the Canaanites cast upon him for his kinsman's sake. The king of Gerar sent and took her—To his house, in order to the taking of her to his bed.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 20:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“What Moses related respecting the destruction of Sodom, was a digression. He now returns to the continuation of his history, and proceeds to show what happened to Abraham; how he conducted himself, and how the Lord protected him; till the promised seed, the future source of the Church, should be born unto him. He also says, that Abraham came into the South country; not that he traveled beyond the limits of the inheritance given to him, but left his former abode, and went towards the South.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 20:1)
