Passage

Genesis 17

Verses 1–14 · ESV · Voices

Thirteen years after Ishmael’s birth, the LORD appears to ninety-nine-year-old Abram as God Almighty: walk before Me and be blameless. He names him Abraham, pledges an everlasting covenant to be God to him and his offspring, grants Canaan, and gives circumcision as the covenant’s sign in the flesh—including the household and the warning of being cut off.

Genesis 17:1

1When Abram was ninety-nine years old the LORD appeared to Abram and said to him, “I am God Almighty; walk before me, and be blameless,

Arminian

After years of silence and Ishmael in the tent, God Almighty appears: walk before Me, be blameless. The call is moral and personal, not a private destiny chart.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere summons. El Shaddai’s power does not cancel Abram’s responsibility to walk. Grace appears; the aged pilgrim must still live before God’s face.

Reformed

Ninety-nine and still waiting, Abram meets El Shaddai. Appearance precedes command. Blameless walking sits under Almighty speech, not self-improvement.

Reformed comfort: the LORD who appears is the One who will keep the promise. Vocation and power arrive together; Abram does not invent either.

Genesis 17:2

2that I may make my covenant between me and you, and may multiply you greatly.”

Arminian

Covenant and multiplication are God’s “I may / I will,” aimed at relationship. Walking before Him is the fitting human side of a bond He initiates.

Arminian note: the covenant is sincerely offered and must be kept (v. 9). Grace makes a people; people are still called to walk.

Reformed

“My covenant” is God’s making. Multiplication is gift speech, not Abram’s fertility plan.

Reformed accent: the bond is unilateral in origin. God sets the terms between Himself and Abram before any knife is lifted.

Genesis 17:3

3Then Abram fell on his face. And God said to him,

Arminian

Abram falls; God keeps speaking. Worship is the first answer to revelation.

Arminian pastoral reading: face-down is a real response grace enables. The conversation continues because God still addresses the man on the ground.

Reformed

Prostration meets further speech. God is not done when Abram is overwhelmed.

Reformed note: the covenant is declared over a worshiper, not negotiated by an equal. God talks while Abram is on his face.

Genesis 17:4

4“Behold, my covenant is with you, and you shall be the father of a multitude of nations.

Arminian

Behold: the covenant is already “with you.” Father of nations stretches far beyond one household. The horizon is missionary, not tribal only.

Classical Arminians hear a wide mercy. Nations will be gathered; the promise sincerely aims beyond Abram’s camp, to be received by faith.

Reformed

“Is with you” is present gift. Fatherhood of nations is God’s decree over a still-childless (by Sarah) man.

Reformed reading: election of Abram is unto a multitude God will make. The nations in view are the Lord’s work through the promised line.

Genesis 17:5

5No longer shall your name be called Abram, but your name shall be Abraham, for I have made you the father of a multitude of nations.

Arminian

Name change: exalted father becomes father of a multitude. God names the future into the present. “I have made you” is grace-speech Abraham must wear.

Arminian emphasis: he receives a new identity and must live it. The name is gift; walking in it is the pilgrim’s ongoing yes.

Reformed

God renames before Sarah conceives. “I have made you” is perfect-tense promise, as sure as the Speaker.

Reformed comfort: identity is assigned, not earned. Abraham is what God calls him while the tent is still without Isaac.

Genesis 17:6

6I will make you exceedingly fruitful, and I will make you into nations, and kings shall come from you.

Arminian

Fruit, nations, kings: all “I will.” Royal lines will come from a man who cannot produce them. Blessing is meant to overflow into peoples and rulers.

Arminian pastoral note: fruitfulness is gift that still meets human history. Kings from Abraham serve the wider blessing promised in chapter 12.

Reformed

Triple “I will make.” Fruitfulness is God’s manufacturing, including kings.

Reformed accent: monarchy in the promise already leans toward the greater Son. God writes political futures the patriarch cannot script.

Genesis 17:7

7And I will establish my covenant between me and you and your offspring after you throughout their generations for an everlasting covenant, to be God to you and to your offspring after you.

Arminian

The heart of the chapter: God will be God to Abraham and to his offspring, generation after generation. Everlasting covenant is relationship, not a mere contract clause.

Classical Arminians treasure the pledge and the offspring’s call to keep it (v. 9). Grace establishes; descendants must not treat the bond as magic. God sincerely offers Himself as their God.

Reformed

Establishment, offspring, everlasting: covenant of grace in patriarchal form. “To be God to you” is the gospel in Old Testament dress.

Reformed confession: God binds Himself to a people He will keep. Generations are included because He elects a line, not because each child autonomously invents faith—yet faith remains the way the promise is embraced (Rom 4).

Key contrast Both hear God pledging Himself to Abraham’s house. Arminians typically stress a sincere, keepable covenant that offspring may receive or break; Reformed readers typically stress one covenant of grace, established by God, effectual for the elect across generations.

Genesis 17:8

8And I will give to you and to your offspring after you the land of your sojournings, all the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession, and I will be their God.”

Arminian

Land of sojourning becomes promised possession. “I will be their God” repeats the better gift underneath the soil.

Arminian reading: place and presence together. The sojourner may trust the Giver for Canaan and, more, for belonging.

Reformed

Deed and deity: Canaan as everlasting possession, and “I will be their God” as the deeper clause.

Reformed note: the land gift serves the God-with-them promise. Hebrews will read the patriarchs as seeking a better country; the oath still stands in God’s mouth.

Genesis 17:9

9And God said to Abraham, “As for you, you shall keep my covenant, you and your offspring after you throughout their generations.

Arminian

“As for you” turns to duty: keep it, you and your offspring. Covenant is not spectator sport. Generations must actually keep what God established.

Arminian pastoral weight: grace that establishes also commands. Households are responsible to walk in the bond, not merely inherit a name.

Reformed

Keeping is required because the covenant is real. Offspring are addressed inside God’s establishment, not left to invent religion.

Reformed accent: obligation sits under gift. The command to keep does not make the covenant a wage; it marks a people God has claimed.

Genesis 17:10

10This is my covenant, which you shall keep, between me and you and your offspring after you: Every male among you shall be circumcised.

Arminian

The keepable form is named: every male circumcised. Wesley calls it token, sign, and seal—God’s promises confirmed, their duty bound. Consent to be His people takes a bodily shape.

Classical Arminians hear a real ordinance for real households, including children and foreigners in the house (vv. 12–13). Faith receives the sign; the sign does not replace faith.

Reformed

Circumcision is called “my covenant” as the appointed sign of it. Calvin: God inscribes the bond in Abraham’s flesh as memorial of adoption.

Reformed reading: the sacrament belongs to the one covenant of grace (WCF 7.5). Household males receive the sign because the promise runs through a people, not only through adult decisions.

Genesis 17:11

11You shall be circumcised in the flesh of your foreskins, and it shall be a sign of the covenant between me and you.

Arminian

Flesh and sign: the mark is in the body, pointing beyond the body. Arminius: it seals the promise of the blessed seed, the nations blessed in him, and God as God to Abraham and his seed—through the righteousness of faith.

Arminian caution: the sign without the thing is empty. Put away the corruption the rite signified; keep the God who gave it.

Reformed

Sign of the covenant, not a human badge. Calvin: the promise is the soul of the sign; torn away, only a phantom remains. Paul will call it a seal of the righteousness of faith (Rom 4:11).

Reformed comfort: God chose a lowly mark to preach pollution of nature and salvation from Abraham’s seed. The visible word serves faith; it does not replace the Speaker.

Key contrast Both treat circumcision as God’s covenant sign, not magic. Arminians typically stress the sign sealing promise and binding duty, to be received in faith; Reformed readers typically stress it as a sacrament of the covenant of grace, a visible word God uses to confirm faith in the elect.

Genesis 17:12

12He who is eight days old among you shall be circumcised. Every male throughout your generations, whether born in your house or bought with your money from any foreigner who is not of your offspring,

Arminian

Eight days: infants in the covenant community receive the sign. House-born and purchased foreigners too. Wesley notes the stranger’s child looking toward Gentiles brought in by faith.

Arminian pastoral reading: God is kind to households and to the outsider under Abraham’s roof. The sign is not a prize for adult achievement.

Reformed

Infancy and household breadth are God’s idea. The sign runs along the life of the family and even the servant bought with money.

Reformed accent: covenant administration includes children of believers and those joined to the house. Continuity with baptism’s household logic is later drawn (Col 2:11–12) without flattening every difference.

Genesis 17:13

13both he who is born in your house and he who is bought with your money, shall surely be circumcised. So shall my covenant be in your flesh an everlasting covenant.

Arminian

Surely circumcised: no class of male in the house is optional. The covenant is “in your flesh,” lasting as God’s bond lasts.

Arminian note: belonging to Abraham’s house meant submitting to Abraham’s God. The mark preached that the body itself is under promise and command.

Reformed

House-born and bought stand under the same “shall surely.” Everlasting covenant takes fleshly location.

Reformed reading: God brands a people in history. The everlasting is His faithfulness; the flesh is the temporary sacrament until the greater circumcision in Christ.

Genesis 17:14

14Any uncircumcised male who is not circumcised in the flesh of his foreskin shall be cut off from his people; he has broken my covenant.”

Arminian

Cut off: refusing the sign is breaking the covenant. Privilege can be forfeited. The warning is as sincere as the promise.

Classical Arminians hear real peril. Being born in the house does not make the bond unbreakable. Keep the covenant; do not treat the sign as optional jewelry.

Reformed

Breach is named: uncircumcision as covenant-breaking, with cutting-off as the sentence.

Reformed sobriety: sacraments have sanctions. Visible membership can be cut off; God’s electing purpose is not thereby mocked. The severity guards the holiness of the sign He gave.

Key contrast Both take “cut off” as a real covenant sanction. Arminians typically stress that people may truly break what God sincerely established; Reformed readers typically distinguish visible-covenant cutting off from God’s unbreakable hold on the elect.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on circumcision as covenant sign and seal—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Abraham’s naming and the mark in the flesh.

Arminian Notable voices

“The token of the covenant, is circumcision, for the sake of which the covenant is itself called the covenant of circumcision… It is called a sign and seal… A confirmation to Abraham and his seed of those promises which were God’s part of the covenant… An obligation upon Abraham and his seed to that duty which was their part of the covenant… They who will have God to be to them a God, must consent to be to him a people.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 17:10)
“Circumcision is the sign of the covenant into which God entered with Abraham to seal or witness the promise about the blessed seed that should be born of him, about all nations which were to be blessed in him, and about constituting him the father of many nations, and the heir of the world through the righteousness of faith; and that God was willing to be his God and the God of his seed after him.”
Jacobus Arminius · Works, Vol. 2, Disputation LXI, “On the Sacraments of the Old Testament”

Reformed Notable voices

“God inscribes his covenant in the flesh of Abraham. For circumcision was as a solemn memorial of that adoption, by which the family of Abraham had been elected to be the peculiar people of God… since the promise is the very soul of the sign, whenever it is torn away from the sign, nothing remains but a lifeless and vain phantom.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 17:9–11)
“This covenant was differently administered in the time of the law, and in the time of the gospel: under the law, it was administered by promises, prophecies, sacrifices, circumcision, the paschal lamb, and other types and ordinances delivered to the people of the Jews, all foresignifying Christ to come; which were, for that time, sufficient and efficacious, through the operation of the Spirit, to instruct and build up the elect in faith in the promised Messiah.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · 7.5 (1647)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
