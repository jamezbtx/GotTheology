Passage

Genesis 17

Verses 15–27 · ESV · Voices

God renames Sarai as Sarah, promises a son from her, and names him Isaac. Abraham laughs and pleads for Ishmael; God blesses Ishmael with twelve princes yet fixes the everlasting covenant on Isaac. Abraham circumcises his whole house that very day—himself at ninety-nine, Ishmael at thirteen.

Genesis 17:15

15And God said to Abraham, “As for Sarai your wife, you shall not call her name Sarai, but Sarah shall be her name.

Arminian

Sarai becomes Sarah—princess. The wife is not a footnote to the covenant with the husband. God names her future as He named Abraham’s.

Arminian warmth: she is addressed in dignity. The promise now runs publicly through this woman, not around her.

Reformed

God speaks her new name to Abraham. Sarah’s identity is assigned by the same mouth that renamed him.

Reformed note: the matriarch is inside the decree. Offspring will not be a side arrangement; they will be Sarah’s.

Genesis 17:16

16I will bless her, and moreover, I will give you a son by her. I will bless her, and she shall become nations; kings of peoples shall come from her.”

Arminian

Double “I will bless her,” then a son by her, nations, and kings from her. The barren woman is made a mother of peoples.

Classical Arminians hear kindness that specifies Sarah. The earlier Hagar plan is not God’s last word. Blessing is sincere and located in this marriage.

Reformed

Blessing twice named, then gift of a son by her. Nations and kings come from Sarah because God speaks them.

Reformed accent: the line is closed to human workaround. Isaac will be miracle-child, not the product of chapter 16’s scheme.

Genesis 17:17

17Then Abraham fell on his face and laughed and said to himself, “Shall a child be born to a man who is a hundred years old? Shall Sarah, who is ninety years old, bear a child?”

Arminian

Face to the ground, laugh in the heart: the ages are named. Faith here sounds like stunned arithmetic. God does not crush him for the laugh.

Arminian pastoral reading: honest difficulty is not unbelief’s twin. Abraham stays in the conversation; he will still obey the same day.

Reformed

Prostration plus laughter. The hundred and the ninety are real obstacles God intends to override.

Reformed comfort: the promise is so large it draws a laugh. Romans 4 will remember a faith that did not weaken—here we also see the man still counting years. God holds both the laugh and the oath.

Genesis 17:18

18And Abraham said to God, “Oh that Ishmael might live before you!”

Arminian

He pleads for the son he has. Love for Ishmael is not rebellion; it is a father’s cry. He asks that this boy live before God.

Arminian note: intercession is welcome. God will answer with blessing for Ishmael without letting the plea rewrite the covenant heir.

Reformed

Ishmael is named before God. Abraham would settle the promise on the child already in the tent.

Reformed reading: prayer is real, and God’s “No, but” (v. 19) is still mercy. The heir is Isaac by decree, not by Abraham’s available option.

Genesis 17:19

19God said, “No, but Sarah your wife shall bear you a son, and you shall call his name Isaac. I will establish my covenant with him as an everlasting covenant for his offspring after him.

Arminian

No, but: Sarah shall bear; name him Isaac (he laughs). The everlasting covenant is established with this son and his offspring. Promise narrows to a named child without cruelty to Ishmael.

Classical Arminians hear both the refusal and the gift. God’s plan is particular; it is also announced so Abraham may trust and obey.

Reformed

“No, but” is sovereign kindness. Isaac is named before conception; the covenant is established with him.

Reformed accent: election of the seed here is not abstract. God chooses Isaac, not Ishmael, as covenant heir—Paul’s later allegory (Gal 4) already has this narrative root.

Key contrast Both confess Isaac as the covenant son by God’s word. Arminians typically stress the announced, trustable particularity of the promise; Reformed readers typically stress God’s free choice of the heir apart from Abraham’s preferred plan.

Genesis 17:20

20As for Ishmael, I have heard you; behold, I have blessed him and will make him fruitful and multiply him greatly. He shall father twelve princes, and I will make him into a great nation.

Arminian

“I have heard you.” Ishmael is blessed, fruitful, a great nation, twelve princes. The child Abraham loves is not discarded.

Arminian pastoral comfort: God hears the cry for the other son. Common kindness and national greatness are real gifts, even when they are not the covenant of Isaac.

Reformed

Heard, blessed, multiplied: Ishmael receives genuine promises short of the covenant line.

Reformed distinction: providence can make a great nation without making that nation the bearer of the everlasting covenant. Gift is graded; God is not stingy.

Genesis 17:21

21But I will establish my covenant with Isaac, whom Sarah shall bear to you at this time next year.”

Arminian

But: covenant with Isaac, calendar attached—“this time next year.” Faith now has a date. Sarah will bear; the wait has an end.

Arminian urgency: the word is specific enough to believe. Next year is not vague spirituality; it is a promise to keep in view.

Reformed

Establishment is repeated and timed. Sarah, Isaac, next year: God schedules the impossible.

Reformed comfort: the covenant does not float. It lands on a birth God has already placed on the clock.

Genesis 17:22

22When he had finished talking with him, God went up from Abraham.

Arminian

The conversation ends; God goes up. Revelation is not endless chatter. Abraham is left with a command to keep that very day.

Arminian application: when God has spoken, obey. The going-up is not abandonment; it is trust that the word stands without the vision lingering.

Reformed

Finished talking, God went up. Theophany has a close. What remains is the word already given.

Reformed note: Abraham must now act under a spoken covenant, not under a remaining glow. Providence continues when the appearance lifts.

Genesis 17:23

23Then Abraham took Ishmael his son and all those born in his house or bought with his money, every male among the men of Abraham’s house, and he circumcised the flesh of their foreskins that very day, as God had said to him.

Arminian

That very day: Ishmael, house-born, bought—every male. Obedience is prompt and complete. The laugh and the plea do not cancel the knife.

Classical Arminians honor this as grace-enabled doing. Abraham keeps the covenant he was told to keep. Household leadership here is costly and immediate.

Reformed

Same-day obedience matches the speech just ended. Every male of the house is marked “as God had said.”

Reformed reading: faith that receives the promise also submits the household to the sign. Ishmael is circumcised though not the covenant heir—visible membership under Abraham’s roof.

Genesis 17:24

24Abraham was ninety-nine years old when he was circumcised in the flesh of his foreskin.

Arminian

Ninety-nine and marked. The father takes the sign first. Age is no exemption.

Arminian pastoral word: leaders go first into costly obedience. The promise does not spare Abraham the pain he requires of his house.

Reformed

The narrator dates the cut. Ninety-nine underlines miracle and humiliation together.

Reformed accent: the heir of the world (Rom 4:13) is a marked old man. God’s sacrament lands on weak flesh.

Genesis 17:25

25And Ishmael his son was thirteen years old when he was circumcised in the flesh of his foreskin.

Arminian

Thirteen: Ishmael is old enough to remember this. He is included in the household sign even as the covenant speech pointed to Isaac.

Arminian care: the boy is not nothing to God. Blessing and mark both touch him; his later path will still be his own.

Reformed

Age is recorded so the two circumcisions sit in history, not myth. Thirteen-year-old Ishmael receives the sign under Abraham.

Reformed note: sharing the sign is not sharing Isaac’s role. Providence distinguishes even at the same knife.

Genesis 17:26

26That very day Abraham and his son Ishmael were circumcised.

Arminian

Father and son, same day. The bond of blood and the bond of the sign meet. Unity of obedience does not erase the later fork in their stories.

Arminian reading: shared rites still leave personal futures. Each must live before God after the cut.

Reformed

The day is emphasized again. Two generations marked together under one command.

Reformed comfort: God got what He required that day. The narrative slows to let the reader see the word become flesh in a household.

Genesis 17:27

27And all the men of his house, those born in the house and those bought with money from a foreigner, were circumcised with him.

Arminian

The whole male house, including the foreigner bought with money, is circumcised with him. Covenant hospitality is costly and wide.

Arminian close: none in Abraham’s care is left unmarked. Those under his roof share the sign; the God of Abraham intends a people, not a private mystic.

Reformed

House-born and foreign-bought: the closing shot is a fully marked household.

Reformed accent: Abraham’s obedience is federal and public. The everlasting covenant of vv. 7–14 now has a visible community in Canaan, waiting for Isaac next year.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Sarah named, Isaac promised, and Ishmael blessed—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 17:15–27.

Arminian Notable voices

“Now it was that Abraham rejoiced to see Christ's day, now he saw it and was glad, John 8:56, for as he saw heaven in the promise of Canaan, so he saw Christ in the promise of Isaac, and said, Shall a child be born to him that is an hundred years old? - He doth not here speak of it, as at all doubtful, for we are sure he staggered not at the promise, Rom 4:20, but as wonderful, and that which could not be effected but by the almighty power of God.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 17:17)

Reformed Notable voices

“For Abraham not only adores God, but in giving him thanks, testifies that he receives and embraces what was promised concerning a son. Hence also we infer that he laughed, not because he either despised, or regarded as fabulous, or rejected, the promise of God; but, as is commonly wont to happen in things which are least expected, partly exulting with joy, and partly being carried beyond himself in admiration, he breaks forth into laughter.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 17:17)
