Passage

Genesis 1

Verses 14–19 · ESV · Voices

Day four places lights in the expanse already made: for signs and seasons, days and years, and to give light on the earth. Verses 14–19 refuse to treat sun and moon as deities; they are creatures assigned to rule the day and night as servants. The week’s rhythm now has visible markers in the sky.

Genesis 1:14

14And God said, “Let there be lights in the expanse of the heavens to separate the day from the night. And let them be for signs and for seasons, and for days and years,

Arminian

God said, “Let there be lights… to separate the day from the night,” and to be for signs, seasons, days, and years. The sky’s lamps serve earthly time, worship, and work—not the other way around.

Arminian pastoral note: seasons are gifts for planting, gathering, and holy assembly. The lights are creatures with jobs; they are not fate written in the stars.

Reformed

Lights are appointed to separate day from night and to mark times. Their offices come by command.

Reformed reading: sun, moon, and stars execute what God enjoins. “Signs and seasons” belong to nature’s order and human calendars—not to diviners claiming secret rule from the sky.

Genesis 1:15

15and let them be lights in the expanse of the heavens to give light upon the earth.” And it was so.

Arminian

“…and let them be lights in the expanse… to give light upon the earth.” And it was so. Purpose is stated: the earth is to be lit.

Classical Arminians hear a world aimed at habitation. Light upon the earth is hospitality; the same God who will later call image-bearers already arranges their days.

Reformed

The lights exist to give light upon the earth, and it was so. Means serve an end God names.

Reformed comfort: illumination of the earth is not the stars’ hobby. God assigned the gift; fulfillment follows the word as surely as on day one. Nature’s shining is obedience.

Genesis 1:16

16And God made the two great lights—the greater light to rule the day and the lesser light to rule the night—and the stars.

Arminian

God made the two great lights—the greater to rule the day, the lesser the night—and the stars. “Rule” here is service: lamps take turns; they do not ascend a throne besides God’s.

Arminian note: naming them by function rather than as deities dethrones the sky. Creaturely “rule” is delegated task, a faint analog of the stewardship humans will receive—never rival worship.

Reformed

Two great lights and the stars are made and given rule over day and night as ministries, not as gods.

Reformed accent: “Rule” does not shrink God’s power; the sun is still a servant, the moon a handmaid. He governs days and nights by their ministry as a king by charioteers.

Genesis 1:17

17And God set them in the expanse of the heavens to give light on the earth,

Arminian

God set them in the expanse to give light on the earth. Placement is personal: they do not wander into usefulness.

Classical Arminians gladly confess a locating God. Gifts are not only made but set—providence as careful placement for the earth’s good, so that light arrives as intended kindness, not as stray fire in the sky.

Reformed

God set them in the expanse with a purpose clause: to give light on the earth. The verse is almost all placement and aim.

Reformed confession: stations in the sky are assigned. The lights shine where He put them; cosmic order is government, not glittering chance. What He sets, He means to use.

Genesis 1:18

18to rule over the day and over the night, and to separate the light from the darkness. And God saw that it was good.

Arminian

They are to rule over day and night and separate light from darkness; God saw that it was good. Day-one separation now has standing servants.

Arminian reading: goodness includes ordered time. The world’s clock is a kindness so that creatures may work, rest, and remember the Giver rather than fear the dark as a god.

Reformed

Rule, separation, and the verdict “good” close the lights’ commission. Day and night now have appointed officers.

Reformed note: the same God who divided light from darkness on day one now administers that division through appointed bodies. Instruments change; the Ruler does not.

Genesis 1:19

19And there was evening and there was morning, the fourth day.

Arminian

Evening and morning, the fourth day. The sky is furnished; the week’s middle is lights, not monsters.

Classical Arminians take the day’s rest-point as praise. What pagans deified, Genesis clocks in as day-four workmanship—good, limited, and on time. Worship belongs to the Maker of the lamps, not to the lamps themselves.

Reformed

The fourth day ends in the established cadence of evening and morning. Even this celestial work is folded into a counted day.

Reformed instinct: even the calendar-makers in the sky are timed by God. The week is His before it is ours; sun and moon keep hours they did not invent.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on sun, moon, and stars as servants, not deities—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 1:14–19.

Arminian Notable voices

“This is the history of the fourth day's work, the creating the sun, moon and stars. Of this we have an account, In general, verse 14, 15. where we have, The command given concerning them. Let there be lights in the firmament of heaven—God had said, Ge 1:3 Let there be light, and there was light; but that was, as it were, a chaos of light, scattered and confused; now it was collected and made into several luminaries, and so rendered both more glorious and more serviceable.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 1:14)

Reformed Notable voices

“Let there be lights Moses passes onwards to the fourth day, on which the stars were made. God had before created the light, but he now institutes a new order in nature, that the sun should be the dispenser of diurnal light, and the moon and stars should shine by night. For Moses relates nothing else than that God ordained certain instruments to diffuse through the earth, by reciprocal changes, that light which had been previously created.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 1:14)
