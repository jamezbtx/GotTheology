Passage

Genesis 1

Verses 6–8 · ESV

After light, God continues the week by making an expanse in the midst of the waters. Verses 6–8 are day two: sky-space named Heaven, waters above and below distinguished, evening and morning. The Creator is not only originator but arranger—making room in which later life will live and move.

Genesis 1:6

6And God said, “Let there be an expanse in the midst of the waters, and let it separate the waters from the waters.”

Arminian

God said, “Let there be an expanse in the midst of the waters,” to separate waters from waters. Day two continues creation by speech: space is opened, not discovered.

Arminian pastoral weight: God makes room. The expanse is hospitality in cosmic form—a place where birds will later fly and rain will later fall, all by the same generous word.

Reformed

The second day’s word commands an expanse to divide waters from waters. Distinction again comes by decree, not by the waters’ own sorting.

Reformed accent: the Creator structures the cosmos as He wills. Heaven and the deep do not negotiate; they are placed. Government of the world is as original as its existence.

Genesis 1:7

7And God made the expanse and separated the waters that were under the expanse from the waters that were above the expanse. And it was so.

Arminian

God made the expanse and separated waters under it from waters above it—“and it was so.” Word and deed agree; the world answers Him.

Classical Arminians gladly confess that creation obeys. “It was so” is not fate crushing nature; it is the fitting response of what God makes to the God who speaks in truth.

Reformed

God made and separated, and it was so. The clause closes the gap between command and fulfillment.

Reformed comfort: God’s word in nature does not return empty. Waters above and below take their stations because He made the expanse; whatever secondary processes we later describe remain servants of that word.

Genesis 1:8

8And God called the expanse Heaven. And there was evening and there was morning, the second day.

Arminian

God called the expanse Heaven; evening and morning mark the second day. Naming again: the sky is His, not a canopy of competing deities.

Arminian note: the week moves by God’s naming and timing. Heaven as gift-space invites later praise, not anxiety that the sky might collapse without our management.

Reformed

He names the expanse Heaven and completes the second day. Title and time belong to the Maker.

Reformed reading: to call it Heaven is to claim it. The visible sky is creature, not god; its order is the Lord’s ongoing work, as sure as the day He first named it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
