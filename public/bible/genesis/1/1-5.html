Passage

Genesis 1

Verses 1–5 · WEB · Voices

The Bible opens not with Israel or the law but with God speaking a world into being. Genesis 1:1–5 is day one of the creation week: heavens and earth, the formless deep, the Spirit hovering, then light summoned, named, and judged good. Evening and morning mark the first day. The pericope establishes that everything begins with God’s word, not with rival gods or eternal matter.

Genesis 1:1

1In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.

Arminian

Genesis opens with God as Creator of the heavens and the earth “in the beginning”—before clocks, nations, or rival deities. The verb is making what was not; the subject is God; the scope is all that is.

Arminian readers gladly confess creation ex nihilo by God’s free goodness. God was not compelled to make a world; He freely spoke one into being. That same freedom marks a Giver, not a necessary emanation of being.

Reformed

“In the beginning” locates every creature after God’s act. Heavens and earth are not eternal matter waiting for decoration; they come to be because God created.

Reformed readers hear sovereignty already in the first sentence. Nothing exists except by His purpose; the world’s foundation is God’s will to create, not chance or a council of gods.

Genesis 1:2

2The earth was formless and empty. Darkness was on the surface of the deep and God’s Spirit was hovering over the surface of the waters.

Arminian

The earth is “formless and empty,” darkness over the deep—yet the Spirit of God is already hovering over the waters. Unfinished is not abandoned; the Spirit attends the unformed world.

God’s presence precedes the world’s usefulness. The Spirit’s hovering is life-ward care over what cannot yet live on its own—kindness in the grammar of creation, not yet a debate about conversion.

Reformed

Formless void and darkness name an unordered state, not a second eternal principle. The Spirit of God hovers—sustaining what would otherwise collapse into nothing.

Even this chaos is not outside God’s government. The Spirit’s secret efficacy holds the mass until the Word distinguishes it; providence begins as early as verse 2.

Genesis 1:3

3God said, “Let there be light,” and there was light.

Arminian

God said, “Let there be light,” and there was light. Speech and effect meet without struggle; light is gift, not wrestled from darkness as from an equal rival.

Arminian interpreters hear a God who addresses and accomplishes. The creating word is truly effective; it also reveals a Lord who works by speaking—a pattern of address, not mute force.

Reformed

Fiat light: God speaks, and light is. No instrument is named; the Word itself is enough.

The creating word does not wait on creaturely consent. What God wills to be, is. Light’s origin is His mouth, so later lights in the sky will be servants, not sources.

Genesis 1:4

4God saw the light, and saw that it was good. God divided the light from the darkness.

Arminian

God saw the light, and saw that it was good, then separated light from darkness. Goodness is God’s own evaluation; distinction is His gift of order.

The world is truly good as made, not a trap or illusion. God’s seeing is delight in a work; separation makes a livable rhythm rather than a dualism of equal powers.

Reformed

God sees and judges the light good, then divides light from darkness. His verdict is the standard; His separation is the first structuring act after the word.

Nothing is good because we deem it so, but because God approves His work. Order is not an accident of the elements; it is the Creator’s holy sorting.

Genesis 1:5

5God called the light “day”, and the darkness he called “night”. There was evening and there was morning, the first day.

Arminian

Naming is lordship; time itself is a creaturely gift with a beginning.

Arminian readers receive the day as God’s good structure for life that will soon be called to work and rest. The week starts because God gives names and bounds—not because matter invented a clock.

Reformed

Naming Day and Night is God’s prerogative; the first day’s evening and morning unfold under His rule.

Time is not autonomous. God called, and the first day was. The week’s pattern will later tutor Israel—and every reader—that history runs inside God’s government.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on creation from nothing and the first light, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“The heaven and the earth—that is, the world, including the whole frame and furniture of the universe. God created, that is, made it out of nothing. There was not any pre-existent matter out of which the world was produced. In the beginning—that is, in the beginning of time. Time began with the production of those beings that are measured by time. Before the beginning of time there was none but that Infinite Being that inhabits eternity.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 1:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“Therefore his meaning is, that the world was made out of nothing. Hence the folly of those is refuted who imagine that unformed matter existed from eternity. Let this, then, be maintained in the first place, that the world is not eternal but was created by God. And it is certain that the world had been begun by the same efficacy of the Word by which it was completed.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 1:1, 3)
