Passage

Genesis 1

Verses 1–5 · ESV

The Bible opens not with Israel or the law but with God speaking a world into being. Genesis 1:1–5 is day one of the creation week: heavens and earth, the formless deep, the Spirit hovering, then light summoned, named, and judged good. Evening and morning mark the first day. The pericope establishes that everything begins with God’s word, not with rival gods or eternal matter.

Genesis 1:1

1In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.

Arminian

Genesis opens with God as Creator of the heavens and the earth “in the beginning”—before clocks, nations, or rival deities. The verb is making what was not; the subject is God; the scope is all that is.

Classical Arminians gladly confess creation ex nihilo by God’s free goodness. God was not compelled to make a world; He freely spoke one into being. That same freedom marks a Giver, not a necessary emanation of being.

Reformed

“In the beginning” locates every creature after God’s act. Heavens and earth are not eternal matter waiting for decoration; they come to be because God created.

Reformed readers hear sovereignty already in the first sentence. Nothing exists except by His purpose; the world’s foundation is God’s will to create, not chance or a council of gods.

Genesis 1:2

2The earth was without form and void, and darkness was over the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters.

Arminian

The earth is “without form and void,” darkness over the deep—yet the Spirit of God is already hovering over the waters. Unfinished is not abandoned; the Spirit attends the unformed world.

Arminian pastoral note: God’s presence precedes the world’s usefulness. The Spirit’s hovering is life-ward care over what cannot yet live on its own—kindness in the grammar of creation, not yet a debate about conversion.

Reformed

Formless void and darkness name a real unordered state, not a second eternal principle. The Spirit of God hovers—sustaining what would otherwise collapse into nothing.

Reformed comfort: even this chaos is not outside God’s government. The Spirit’s secret efficacy holds the mass until the Word distinguishes it; providence begins as early as verse 2.

Genesis 1:3

3And God said, “Let there be light,” and there was light.

Arminian

God said, “Let there be light,” and there was light. Speech and effect meet without struggle; light is gift, not wrestled from darkness as from an equal rival.

Classical Arminians hear a God who addresses and accomplishes. The creating word is truly effective; it also reveals a Lord who works by speaking—a pattern of genuine address, not mute force.

Reformed

Fiat light: God speaks, and light is. No instrument is named; the Word itself is enough.

Reformed accent: the creating word does not wait on creaturely consent. What God wills to be, is. Light’s origin is His mouth, so later lights in the sky will be servants, not sources.

Genesis 1:4

4And God saw that the light was good. And God separated the light from the darkness.

Arminian

God saw that the light was good, then separated light from darkness. Goodness is God’s own evaluation; distinction is His gift of order.

Arminian reading: the world is truly good as made, not a trap or illusion. God’s seeing is delight in a real work; separation makes a livable rhythm rather than a dualism of equal powers.

Reformed

God sees and judges the light good, then divides light from darkness. His verdict is the standard; His separation is the first structuring act after the word.

Reformed instinct: nothing is good because we deem it so, but because God approves His work. Order is not an accident of the elements; it is the Creator’s holy sorting.

Genesis 1:5

5God called the light Day, and the darkness he called Night. And there was evening and there was morning, the first day.

Arminian

God names light Day and darkness Night; evening and morning make the first day. Naming is lordship; time itself is a creaturely gift with a beginning.

Classical Arminians receive the day as God’s good structure for life that will soon be called to work and rest. The week starts because God gives names and bounds—not because matter invented a clock.

Reformed

Naming Day and Night is God’s prerogative; the first day’s evening and morning unfold under His rule.

Reformed note: time is not autonomous. God called, and the first day was. The week’s pattern will later tutor Israel—and every reader—that history runs inside God’s government.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
