Passage

Genesis 31

Verses 22–55 · ESV · Voices

On the third day Laban hears that Jacob has fled and pursues him seven days into Gilead, but God warns Laban in a dream to say nothing to Jacob, good or bad. The camps meet; Laban charges trickery and stolen gods; Jacob pleads fear that the daughters would be taken by force. Rachel hides the teraphim in a camel's saddle while Laban searches the tents. Jacob's anger then recounts twenty years of labor, and he names the God who saw his affliction. They raise a heap—Galeed and Mizpah—calling God to watch between them; Jacob swears by the Fear of Isaac, they sacrifice and eat, and in the morning Laban blesses his grandchildren and daughters and turns home.

Genesis 31:22

22When it was told Laban on the third day that Jacob had fled,

Arminian

Laban learns of the flight on the third day—ordinary news that still leaves him a responsible pursuer. Delay is mercy, not an excuse to hunt without conscience.

Reformed

Three days pass before the report arrives. Providence has already given Jacob a head start on the road of promise before Laban ever saddles for Gilead.

Genesis 31:23

23he took his kinsmen with him and pursued him for seven days and followed close after him into the hill country of Gilead.

Arminian

Laban freely gathers kinsmen and pursues seven days into Gilead; the threat is real human agency, not fate. Grace will restrain harm, but the chase itself is a chosen act.

Reformed

Seven days of pursuit cannot outrun the God who will meet Laban in the night. The close follow into Gilead is permitted only so that the confrontation unfolds under a word already prepared.

Genesis 31:24

24But God came to Laban the Aramean in a dream by night and said to him, "Be careful not to say anything to Jacob, either good or bad."

Arminian

God comes and warns: say nothing to Jacob, good or bad. The night word is a genuine command Laban can and must heed, grace restraining harm by a sincere warning.

Reformed

The dream is effectual restraint. God comes to the Aramean, speaks, and will keep Jacob from Laban's mouth and hand before the camps even meet.

Key contrast Both honor the dream as God's real word. Arminians typically stress a genuine warning Laban can heed; Reformed readers typically stress effectual restraint that preserves the promised seed.

Genesis 31:25

25And Laban overtook Jacob. Now Jacob had pitched his tent in the hill country, and Laban with his kinsmen pitched tents in the hill country of Gilead.

Arminian

Two camps pitch in Gilead by real travel and choice. The meeting is now a moral confrontation, not a raid already finished in blood.

Reformed

Over-taking is allowed, but only after the night word. Providence sets the tents facing each other under a restraint Laban did not invent.

Genesis 31:26

26And Laban said to Jacob, "What have you done, that you have tricked me and driven away my daughters like captives of the sword?

Arminian

Laban names trickery and daughters driven like captives of the sword—real family harm. Deceit remains culpable even when flight was needed.

Reformed

The charge is loud, yet God has already limited what Laban can do. The conflict now unfolds under a spoken leash, not under Laban's full power.

Genesis 31:27

27Why did you flee secretly and trick me, and did not tell me, so that I might have sent you away with mirth and songs, with tambourine and lyre?

Arminian

Laban claims he would have sent them with music. Whether sincere or self-serving, secret flight still cost a genuine farewell, and stealth is a real choice.

Reformed

The protest of tambourine and lyre cannot rewrite twenty years. God, not Laban's feast, is the true sender of Jacob toward the land of promise.

Genesis 31:28

28And why did you not permit me to kiss my sons and my daughters farewell? Now you have done foolishly.

Arminian

Withholding a farewell kiss is a real grief. Jacob's fear does not erase the family cost of leaving in stealth, and Laban's charge of folly must be heard.

Reformed

Wounded affection sits beside the dream that already forbade harm. Folly is named; the God who spoke in the night still holds the scene.

Genesis 31:29

29It is in my power to do you harm. But the God of your father spoke to me last night, saying, 'Be careful not to say anything to Jacob, either good or bad.'

Arminian

Laban admits both power to harm and a genuine divine warning. Grace restrains a man who could still choose violence; the night word is a real command, not theater.

Reformed

"It is in my power" meets "But the God of your father." Effectual speech from the dream now governs the confrontation and keeps Jacob for the promise.

Genesis 31:30

30And now you have gone away because you longed greatly for your father's house, but why did you steal my gods?"

Arminian

Homesickness is granted; stealing household gods is a real crime Laban may justly name. Theft remains theft even when Laban's own piety is mixed.

Reformed

The teraphim charge will expose Rachel, yet the God who spoke in the night is no household idol. Providence will keep Jacob even through a theft he does not yet know.

Genesis 31:31

31Jacob answered and said to Laban, "Because I was afraid, for I thought that you would take your daughters from me by force.

Arminian

Jacob answers with honest fear: he thought Laban would take the daughters by force. Moral agency includes fear, flight, and the need to tell the truth about motive.

Reformed

The fear is real, and so is the God who has already blocked Laban. Jacob's motive does not carry the story; the night word does.

Genesis 31:32

32Anyone with whom you find your gods shall not live. In the presence of our kinsmen point out what I have that is yours, and take it." Now Jacob did not know that Rachel had stolen them.

Arminian

Jacob's rash vow is real speech with deadly stakes; ignorance of Rachel's theft does not make the oath wise. He invites a public search he cannot control.

Reformed

The narrator's aside is mercy: Jacob does not know. Providence will keep the search from blood even under a foolish curse.

Genesis 31:33

33So Laban went into Jacob's tent and into Leah's tent and into the tent of the two female servants, but he did not find them. And he went out of Leah's tent and entered Rachel's.

Arminian

Laban searches tent by tent; theft is treated as a real offense requiring evidence. The hunt is thorough, and still a moral act, not mere theater.

Reformed

The search proceeds tent by tent, yet the gods remain hidden. God can frustrate a hunt without Jacob lifting a hand.

Genesis 31:34

34Now Rachel had taken the household gods and put them in the camel's saddle and sat on them. Laban felt all about the tent, but did not find them.

Arminian

Rachel's ruse is real moral agency—theft plus concealment—not a cute trick beyond judgment. Sitting on the teraphim is a chosen deceit.

Reformed

The saddle hides what Laban cannot find. Providence uses even a daughter's deceit to keep the confrontation from the blood Jacob's vow invited.

Genesis 31:35

35And she said to her father, "Let not my lord be angry that I cannot rise before you, for the way of women is upon me." So he searched but did not find the household gods.

Arminian

Rachel's excuse is another layer of deceit toward her father. Grace that hides the idols does not baptize the lie; she remains a responsible actor.

Reformed

Laban searches and does not find. The Fear of Isaac is preserving a household even while teraphim sit under a saddle and a false word.

Genesis 31:36

36Then Jacob became angry and berated Laban. Jacob said to Laban, "What is my offense? What is my sin, that you have hotly pursued me?

Arminian

Anger and a demand for named sin are fair once the search fails. Jacob may seek justice without denying his own earlier stealth.

Reformed

The hot pursuit now looks empty. God has already rebuked Laban; Jacob's protest rides a deliverance already given.

Genesis 31:37

37For you have felt through all my goods; what have you found of all your household goods? Set it here before my kinsmen and your kinsmen, that they may decide between us two.

Arminian

Public evidence before kinsmen is a sincere appeal to communal justice. Empty hands should end the charge, and neighbors are called to decide.

Reformed

Nothing of Laban's is found because God kept the search from succeeding. The court of kinsmen sits inside providence.

Genesis 31:38

38These twenty years I have been with you. Your ewes and your female goats have not miscarried, and I have not eaten the rams of your flocks.

Arminian

Twenty years of faithful labor is real stewardship Jacob may justly claim. Grace does not erase honest work or the right to name it.

Reformed

Flocks kept and rams uneaten are the fruit of a God who was with Jacob. The record is true; the Giver stands behind the years.

Genesis 31:39

39What was torn by wild beasts I did not bring to you. I bore the loss of it myself. From my hand you required it, whether stolen by day or stolen by night.

Arminian

Jacob bore losses Laban required—real injustice in the wage. Moral agency includes both the shepherd's integrity and the master's greed.

Reformed

What Laban required, God saw. The night rebuke answers years of extracted loss, not only one secret flight.

Genesis 31:40

40There I was: by day the heat consumed me, and the cold by night, and my sleep fled from my eyes.

Arminian

Heat, cold, and sleeplessness are not pious exaggeration. Hardship freely endured can still be named before a hard kinsman.

Reformed

Heat, cold, and sleeplessness were the path by which God kept His man. Suffering served the promise, not Laban's kindness.

Genesis 31:41

41These twenty years I have been in your house. I served you fourteen years for your two daughters, and six years for your flock, and you have changed my wages ten times.

Arminian

Changed wages ten times name Laban's culpable cheating. Jacob's fourteen years and six were real service that should have been honored.

Reformed

Fourteen and six are counted years under a God who did not permit the cheat to empty Jacob. The ledger is human; the preservation is divine.

Genesis 31:42

42If the God of my father, the God of Abraham and the Fear of Isaac, had not been on my side, surely now you would have sent me away empty-handed. God saw my affliction and the labor of my hands and rebuked you last night."

Arminian

Jacob credits the God of his father with seeing affliction and rebuking Laban. Grace that takes a side still leaves Laban a warned moral agent who heard a real word.

Reformed

Without the God of Abraham and the Fear of Isaac, Jacob would be empty-handed. Effectual help preserved the promised man, and last night's rebuke was God's own.

Key contrast Both let Jacob give God the credit. Arminians typically stress God seeing affliction and issuing a genuine rebuke Laban must heed; Reformed readers typically stress that the Fear of Isaac effectually kept Jacob for the promise.

Genesis 31:43

43Then Laban answered and said to Jacob, "The daughters are my daughters, the children are my children, the flocks are my flocks, and all that you see is mine. But what can I do this day for these my daughters or for their children whom they have borne?

Arminian

Laban still claims everything, then admits he cannot harm his own daughters and their children—a real, if late, moral check. Family love can restrain what pride would seize.

Reformed

Possession-talk meets the limit God already set. What Laban calls mine cannot stop the return God commanded or the rebuke already spoken.

Genesis 31:44

44Come now, let us make a covenant, you and I. And let it be a witness between you and me."

Arminian

The offer of covenant is a sincere bid for peace that both men can actually keep. Vows are real moral speech, not empty ceremony.

Reformed

A witness between them will soon be named as God Himself. Human treaty sits under divine oversight that has already restrained harm.

Genesis 31:45

45So Jacob took a stone and set it up as a pillar.

Arminian

Jacob's pillar is a chosen sign of peace, not magic. He acts responsibly to mark the word they will swear.

Reformed

A stone rises as God once met him at Bethel. The God of the promise now fences the border with a pillar Jacob is moved to set.

Genesis 31:46

46And Jacob said to his kinsmen, "Gather stones." And they took stones and made a heap, and they ate there by the heap.

Arminian

Shared stones and a shared meal are real peace-making. Eating by the heap seals intent, not mere ceremony, and kinsmen take part.

Reformed

The heap and the meal are appointed means. God is turning pursuit into a table under His eye.

Genesis 31:47

47Laban called it Jegar-sahadutha, but Jacob called it Galeed.

Arminian

Two tongues name one heap of witness. Language differs; the moral claim of a witnessed peace is the same and meant to bind.

Reformed

Aramaic and Hebrew meet at a border God has drawn. The names will serve the oath He overhears.

Genesis 31:48

48Laban said, "This heap is a witness between you and me today." Therefore he named it Galeed,

Arminian

Laban's naming makes the heap a public witness. Today's word is meant to bind tomorrow's conduct between two responsible men.

Reformed

Today is God's timing after the night dream. The witness-heap stands because harm was already checked by the God who spoke.

Genesis 31:49

49and Mizpah, for he said, "The LORD watch between you and me, when we are out of one another's sight.

Arminian

The LORD watch between you and me is a sincere invocation of God's moral gaze when distance removes human eyes. The prayer assumes God really sees, and that both must live accordingly.

Reformed

Mizpah is more than sentiment: the LORD Himself is the Watcher who keeps the promised line beyond Laban's reach, in sight or out of sight.

Key contrast Both take the watch as God's real presence. Arminians typically hear a sincere prayer for moral accountability when apart; Reformed readers typically hear God as the effectual Watcher who already preserved Jacob.

Genesis 31:50

50If you oppress my daughters, or if you take wives besides my daughters, although no one is with us, see, God is witness between you and me."

Arminian

The terms are concrete: do not oppress, do not take other wives. God as witness makes the vow morally serious even when no human eye remains.

Reformed

God is witness whether Laban is fully sincere or not. The daughters of the promise live under a Gaze Laban cannot dismiss.

Genesis 31:51

51Then Laban said to Jacob, "See this heap and the pillar, which I have set between you and me.

Arminian

Laban claims the setting of the markers. Taking responsibility for a boundary can still be a true step toward peace.

Reformed

What Laban set sits inside what God already set in the dream. The border is human stone and divine will together.

Genesis 31:52

52This heap is a witness, and the pillar is a witness, that I will not pass over this heap to you, and you will not pass over this heap and this pillar to me, to do harm.

Arminian

Mutual non-aggression is a real covenant duty both can keep. The heap forbids harm as a chosen peace, not a mere monument.

Reformed

The prohibition of harm echoes the night word. God has already forbidden Jacob's injury; the stones now publish what heaven spoke.

Genesis 31:53

53The God of Abraham and the God of Nahor, the God of their father, judge between us." So Jacob swore by the Fear of his father Isaac,

Arminian

Laban invokes ancestral names in a shared formula; Jacob swears only by the Fear of Isaac. The distinction of worship is a responsible, public act.

Reformed

Jacob will not merge the Fear of Isaac with Nahor's gods. The oath that binds him is the God who keeps the promise and has already judged the night.

Genesis 31:54

54and Jacob offered a sacrifice in the hill country and called his kinsmen to eat bread. They ate bread and spent the night in the hill country.

Arminian

Sacrifice and shared bread are sincere covenant seals. They spend the night as men at peace, not as raiders still choosing harm.

Reformed

Worship and meal close the chase. The God who warned Laban now lets the camp sleep under a peace He has secured.

Genesis 31:55

55Early in the morning Laban arose and kissed his grandchildren and his daughters and blessed them. Then Laban departed and returned home.

Arminian

Morning kisses and blessing are a real farewell at last. Laban's return home is a chosen end to hostility, grace having restrained the harm he named.

Reformed

Laban blesses and goes. Jacob is left on the road of promise because God restrained the pursuer, witnessed the heap, and sent him home.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Laban’s pursuit, the dream-warning, and the Mizpah covenant—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 31:22–55.

Arminian Notable voices

“Speak not, either good or bad—That is, say nothing against his going on with his journey, for the thing proceedeth from the Lord. The same Hebraism we have, Gen 24:50. The safety of good men is very much owing to the hold God has of the consciences of bad men, and the access he has to them.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 31:24)

Reformed Notable voices

“The Lord gave to his servant the interval of a three-days' journey, so that having passed the Euphrates, he might enter the boundaries of the promised land. By afterward permitting Jacob to be intercepted in the midst of his journey, God intended to tender his own interposition the more illustrious. It seemed desirable that Jacob's course should not be interrupted, and that he should not be filled with alarm by the hostile approach of his father-in-law; but when Laban, like a savage wild beast, breathing nothing but slaughter, is suddenly restrained by the Lord, this was far more likely to confirm the faith of the holy man, and therefore far more useful to him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 31:22)
