Passage

Genesis 4

Verses 1–16 · WEB · Voices

After Eden, life continues east of the garden: Eve bears Cain and Abel, the brothers bring offerings, the Lord regards Abel’s gift and not Cain’s, and Cain’s anger ripens into the first murder. God still speaks, warns, judges, and even marks the guilty with a severe mercy before Cain walks away from His presence.

Genesis 4:1

1The man knew Eve his wife. She conceived, and gave birth to Cain, and said, “I have gotten a man with the LORD’s help.”

Arminian

Marriage, conception, and birth resume after the fall. Eve names Cain with thanks: she has “gotten a man with the LORD’s help.” Grace has not abandoned the household that sinned.

Arminians hear sincere divine help in ordinary life. God assists what He commands; Eve’s gratitude treats the Lord as a partner in the gift of a child, not a distant decree.

Reformed

The first birth outside Eden is already under the Lord’s hand. Eve’s confession locates the child in providence: gotten, yes, but “with the LORD’s help.”

Even after judgment, God continues the human story He means to save. Life is not luck; the Lord remains the giver of every generation.

Genesis 4:2

2Again she gave birth, to Cain’s brother Abel. Abel was a keeper of sheep, but Cain was a tiller of the ground.

Arminian

A second son arrives, and two honest vocations appear—flock and field. Brotherhood and work are gifts, not yet stained by the murder that follows.

Different callings can both honor God. Grace does not demand identical work; it summons each person to offer what they have in faith.

Reformed

Abel the shepherd and Cain the farmer stand as brothers with work in a world still yielding bread. Providence orders ordinary labor after the curse.

Vocation itself is not the divide. The later contrast will be worship and the heart, not sheep versus soil as such.

Genesis 4:3

3As time passed, Cain brought an offering to the LORD from the fruit of the ground.

Arminian

Cain does come. He brings fruit of the ground to the Lord—worship is not withheld at the start. The problem is not that farming cannot yield a gift.

God invites approach. Bringing an offering is a genuine act for which Cain is responsible; later refusal will not be blamed on a closed door he never saw.

Reformed

Time passes, and Cain presents produce as an offering to the Lord. Worship is already directed Godward, not merely civic.

The Lord is the audience of the gift. What follows will show that not every approach is received, because God Himself regards or does not regard.

Genesis 4:4

4Abel also brought some of the firstborn of his flock and of its fat. The LORD respected Abel and his offering,

Arminian

Abel brings firstborn and fat portions—costly, first, and choice. Hebrews 11:4 names the difference as faith. The Lord regards both the man and the gift.

Wesleyan Arminians stress faith as a grace-enabled but trust. Abel’s offering is accepted because he comes believing; the same Lord sincerely summons Cain to a like faith.

Reformed

Abel’s gift is marked as first and best, and the Lord’s regard rests on Abel and on what he brought. Hebrews 11:4 traces this to faith—itself God’s gift.

Acceptance begins with the Lord’s regard, not with human bargaining. God looks with favor as He wills, and faith is the appointed way that favor is received.

Key contrast Both read Abel’s acceptance through faith (Heb 11:4) and note the quality of the gift. Arminians typically treat that faith as enabled yet resistible trust Abel actually exercised; Calvin’s heirs generally treat the Lord’s regard as sovereign grace that produces and receives such faith.

Genesis 4:5

5but he didn’t respect Cain and his offering. Cain was very angry, and the expression on his face fell.

Arminian

Cain and his offering are not regarded. The text joins person and gift; anger and a fallen face follow. Rejection exposes the heart more than it creates it.

Grace that accepted Abel also addressed Cain. Rage is a responsible response he need not have chosen; the fall of the face is the first step toward the field.

Reformed

The Lord’s no regard is as particular as His yes. Cain’s anger unmasks what was already wrong: he will not have God as God if God will not ratify him.

Fallen desire, left to itself, answers holy distinction with wrath. The story is not of a capricious slight but of a heart that will not bow.

Genesis 4:6

6The LORD said to Cain, “Why are you angry? Why has the expression of your face fallen?

Arminian

God speaks to the angry man. The questions are pastoral, not merely forensic: Why this heat? God asks the angry man why his face has fallen. The Lord still pursues Cain with words.

This is assisting grace in conversation. God enlightens and invites self-examination before the crime; Cain remains free to answer or to harden.

Reformed

The Judge is also the Questioner. God does not wait for blood before He addresses the heart. Holy interrogation is mercy.

The Lord’s word comes to Cain unasked. Revelation interrupts anger; whether Cain yields, the initiative is still God’s.

Genesis 4:7

7If you do well, won’t it be lifted up? If you don’t do well, sin crouches at the door. Its desire is for you, but you are to rule over it.”

Arminian

The offer is plain: do well, and there is a lifting up of the face. Sin crouches like a beast, desiring Cain, and he is commanded to master it. Warning and way sit together.

The accent falls on genuine, resistible help. God does not mock Cain with an impossible “if.” Prevenient grace makes doing well possible; Cain’s later murder is a refusal, not a script he could not but read.

Reformed

God’s command and warning are true: well-doing is the path of acceptance, and sin is a predator at the door. Duty is not canceled by the fall.

After Genesis 3, “you are to rule” names what Cain ought to do and cannot do from himself. The word is a means of grace and an exposure of inability apart from the Lord who must give the mastery He commands.

Key contrast Both take the warning as sincere and sin as a power to be ruled. Arminians emphasize that enabling grace made obedience a live option Cain resisted; Calvinists typically stress that the command is right and that ruling sin is God’s gift, which Cain’s fall shows he lacked.

Genesis 4:8

8Cain said to Abel, his brother, “Let’s go into the field.” While they were in the field, Cain rose up against Abel, his brother, and killed him.

Arminian

Words, then a field, then murder. Cain rises against the brother whose offering God regarded. 1 John 3:12 later names the motive: evil works against righteous ones.

The warning of verse 7 was not theater. Cain could have ruled sin and did not. Agency here is tragic and true; love of God and brother was the rejected path.

Reformed

The first death in Scripture is a brother’s hand. Speech becomes cover; the field becomes a grave. Sin crouching at the door now stands over Abel.

This is the seed of the serpent striking the seed of the woman (3:15) in history’s first act. Human will is active—and it is will in bondage to hate unless God restrains.

Genesis 4:9

9The LORD said to Cain, “Where is Abel, your brother?” He said, “I don’t know. Am I my brother’s keeper?”

Arminian

God asks a locating question, as in Eden. Cain lies and shrugs: “Am I my brother’s keeper?” The moral world he denies is the one that will judge him.

Responsibility for the neighbor is not optional spirituality. Grace that restores us to God also binds us to keep one another; Cain’s question is the voice of resisted love.

Reformed

The Lord still seeks. “Where is Abel?” is not ignorance; it is summons. Cain’s lie and counter-question try to unmake kinship.

Fallen man will not be his brother’s keeper because he will not have God as Father. The question unmasks a heart that wants autonomy more than truth.

Genesis 4:10

10The LORD said, “What have you done? The voice of your brother’s blood cries to me from the ground.

Arminian

Blood has a voice, and God hears it. Hidden violence is not hidden from heaven. “What have you done?” presses Cain to name the deed.

Justice is personal. The Lord of all hears the cry of the injured; no one is beyond His concern, and no murderer can silence that cry by walking away.

Reformed

Abel’s blood cries; God is the hearer. Hebrews 12:24 will later set Christ’s blood over this cry as a better word.

Providence is not deaf. The ground itself becomes a witness because the Lord of the earth has taken the victim’s part.

Genesis 4:11

11Now you are cursed because of the ground, which has opened its mouth to receive your brother’s blood from your hand.

Arminian

The ground that took Abel’s blood now turns against Cain. Curse follows crime, fitting the sin: the farmer is struck in his field.

Judgment is not arbitrary. God answers guilt with serious consequence; warning unheeded becomes sentence, and that sentence is just.

Reformed

Cain is cursed from the ground—the very sphere of his work. Creation itself is enlisted in God’s verdict.

The Judge of all the earth does right. Blood received by the soil becomes a covenant lawsuit; Cain’s hand wrote the indictment God now reads back.

Genesis 4:12

12From now on, when you till the ground, it won’t yield its strength to you. You will be a fugitive and a wanderer on the earth.”

Arminian

Fruitless labor and restless exile: the ground withholds strength, and Cain will wander. Sin promised mastery and delivered homelessness.

Grace had offered a lifted face; refusal yields a fugitive life. The consequences are pedagogical as well as penal—meant to show where resisted warning leads.

Reformed

Exile is more than geography. The worker loses the ground’s strength; the man loses settled place. God names the future Cain has chosen and the future God assigns.

Even this sentence is measured. Cain lives; Abel’s blood is answered; the earth remains under the Lord’s rule as Cain walks it as a wanderer.

Genesis 4:13

13Cain said to the LORD, “My punishment is greater than I can bear.

Arminian

Cain finally speaks to God, but of the load, not of Abel. “Greater than I can bear” can mean penalty or guilt; either way, he feels the weight without yet naming repentance.

Conviction differs from turning. Grace still hears him; a cry of distress can become a plea for mercy if he will have it. Self-pity is not yet faith.

Reformed

The first recorded human response to this sentence is complaint about its size. Cain measures the punishment against his capacity, not against Abel’s blood.

Terror of consequence can exist without godly sorrow. The Lord will answer with a limit, but the heart on display is still curved inward.

Genesis 4:14

14Behold, you have driven me out today from the surface of the ground. I will be hidden from your face, and I will be a fugitive and a wanderer on the earth. Whoever finds me will kill me.”

Arminian

Cain names the worst losses: ground, God’s face, safety. Hiddenness from the face of the Lord is spiritual death walking. Fear of vengeance now rules him.

He dreads being killed more than he mourns having killed. Yet even this fear is a place where mercy may still meet him if he will seek the face he says he is losing.

Reformed

Exile from the ground and hiddenness from God’s face are the true horror. Cain then adds the dread of blood-revenge—projecting his own violence onto others.

To be hidden from the Lord’s face is the curse inside the curse. Protection, if it comes, will have to be God’s act, not Cain’s bargaining.

Genesis 4:15

15The LORD said to him, “Therefore whoever slays Cain, vengeance will be taken on him sevenfold.” The LORD appointed a sign for Cain, so that anyone finding him would not strike him.

Arminian

Not so! Mercy interrupts the spiral. A mark and a sevenfold warning protect the murderer from being murdered. Judgment is serious; so is restraint.

Arminian readers see common and particular kindness here: God sincerely spares Cain’s life and holds back human vengeance. That kindness still calls for repentance; it does not license the next Lamech.

Reformed

God answers Cain’s fear with a limit He sets: no private revenge, a sign of protection, sevenfold vengeance as deterrent. The Judge remains the guardian of life.

Common grace restrains further bloodshed. The Lord who regards Abel also preserves Cain for His own purposes—justice without abandoning the world to Cain’s own logic.

Key contrast Both see protecting mercy inside judgment. Arminians typically emphasize a sincere sparing that still invites Cain to turn; Reformed readers emphasize God’s sovereign restraint of evil and His right to mark and preserve even the guilty for His ends.

Genesis 4:16

16Cain left the LORD’s presence, and lived in the land of Nod, east of Eden.

Arminian

Cain leaves the LORD’s presence, and settles in Nod—“wandering” become an address, still east of Eden. Distance is chosen as well as sentenced.

God had spoken, warned, and marked. Walking away is the last refusal in this scene. The door of return is not described as locked; the man simply goes.

Reformed

Departure from the Lord’s presence is the spiritual geography of the verse. Nod east of Eden continues the exile begun in chapter 3, now deepened by blood.

To settle away from that presence is itself judgment. Yet the story is not over; God will still write a line of promise through another son while this wanderer builds a city.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Cain, Abel, and the blood that cries from the ground: older Arminian and Reformed writers, each in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“We are sure there was a good reason for this difference: that Governor of the world, though an absolute sovereign, doth not act arbitrarily in dispensing his smiles and frowns. There was a difference in the characters of the persons offering: Cain was a wicked man, but Abel was a righteous man, Mt 23:35. There was a difference in the offerings they brought.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 4:4)

Reformed Notable voices

“God is said to have respect unto the man to whom he vouchsafes his favor. We must, however, notice the order here observed by Moses; for he does not simply state that the worship which Abel had paid was pleasing to God, but he begins with the person of the offerer; by which he signifies, that God will regard no works with favor except those the doer of which is already previously accepted and approved by him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 4:4)
