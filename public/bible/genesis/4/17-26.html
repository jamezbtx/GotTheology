Passage

Genesis 4

Verses 17–26 · ESV

Cain’s line multiplies: a city named for Enoch, generations down to Lamech, polygamy, tents, music, metalwork, and a boast of multiplied vengeance. The chapter then turns back to Adam and Eve: Seth is appointed in Abel’s place, Enosh is born, and people begin to call upon the name of the Lord.

Genesis 4:17

17Cain knew his wife, and she conceived and bore Enoch. When he built a city, he called the name of the city after the name of his son, Enoch.

Arminian

Life continues for the marked man: a wife, a son, a city named Enoch. Culture rises even among those who have walked from the Lord’s presence.

Arminian pastoral caution: achievement is not the same as peace with God. Cities can be built in Nod. Grace still allows family and work; it does not baptize distance from the Lord as safety.

Reformed

Providence preserves Cain’s household. A city appears early—human making under a curse, named for a son. Common life is not snuffed out.

Reformed reading: God restrains chaos enough for civilization to start, even east of Eden. That is common grace, not proof that Cain’s way has become the way of life.

Genesis 4:18

18To Enoch was born Irad, and Irad fathered Mehujael, and Mehujael fathered Methushael, and Methushael fathered Lamech.

Arminian

Names pass down to Lamech. Generations accumulate without the text pausing to praise their faith. Lineage can carry either worship or wound.

Arminian note: history is morally weighted. Each generation remains responsible before God; a famous ancestor neither saves nor dooms a child who must still answer grace.

Reformed

The genealogy is spare and purposeful: it runs to Lamech, where the line’s character will show. God records what He will later contrast with Seth.

Reformed accent: providence writes family trees. The Lord of history is not absent from Cain’s descendants even when the narrative withholds revival.

Genesis 4:19

19And Lamech took two wives. The name of the one was Adah, and the name of the other Zillah.

Arminian

Lamech takes two wives—the first polygamy in Scripture, a departure from the one-flesh pattern of Genesis 2. The text reports; it does not applaud.

Arminian ethics: God’s creational design remains the standard. Distorted marriage is a human choice under resistible conscience, not a new command from heaven.

Reformed

Two wives named is not progress; it is fracture of the Edenic pattern. Lamech’s house already tells on his heart.

Reformed note: after the fall, desire rewrites God’s gifts. Common life continues, but the shape of the household now advertises autonomy rather than covenant fidelity.

Genesis 4:20

20Adah bore Jabal; he was the father of those who dwell in tents and have livestock.

Arminian

Jabal fathers tent-life and herding. Skills that can serve neighbor and worship also appear in Cain’s line. Culture is not cursed as such.

Arminian thanks: God lets good arts arise among mixed people. Those gifts should be received with gratitude and used under His moral will, not as a rival salvation.

Reformed

Pastoral and nomadic crafts have a “father.” Genesis honors skill without confusing it with holiness.

Reformed doctrine of common grace: the Lord distributes making and herding even where saving faith is not the theme. Culture can be real gift while the heart remains unreconciled.

Genesis 4:21

21His brother's name was Jubal; he was the father of all those who play the lyre and pipe.

Arminian

Jubal fathers lyre and pipe. Music enters the record as human joy and craft. Beauty can praise God or decorate pride.

Arminian pastoral word: the same grace that gives song invites its use in calling on the Lord (v. 26). Instruments do not save; they can serve love or Lamech’s boast.

Reformed

Music too is fathered in this line. The Creator’s world still yields melody after murder and exile.

Reformed wonder: common grace includes the arts. Lyre and pipe are not Seth’s monopoly; God is generous with gifts He will one day reclaim for true worship.

Genesis 4:22

22Zillah also bore Tubal-cain; he was the forger of all instruments of bronze and iron. The sister of Tubal-cain was Naamah.

Arminian

Bronze and iron, and a sister named. Technology arrives—tools that can till or kill. Naamah’s naming reminds us daughters belong in the story too.

Arminian responsibility: skill is morally open. Forging is a trust; people remain accountable for whether metal serves life or multiplies Lamech’s violence.

Reformed

Metallurgy is credited to Tubal-cain; Naamah is remembered by name. Genesis will not let culture be anonymous, nor only male.

Reformed note: God allows the age of bronze and iron to dawn in a restless line. Providence governs the tools of history long before Israel’s law will regulate their use.

Genesis 4:23

23Lamech said to his wives: “Adah and Zillah, hear my voice; you wives of Lamech, listen to what I say: I have killed a man for wounding me, a young man for striking me.

Arminian

Lamech composes violence. A wound is answered with a killing; a strike with a young man’s death. The song teaches his wives to admire excess.

Arminian warning: culture without repentance curdles. Music and metal now soundtrack revenge. Grace still names this as sin to be refused, not as fate to be sung.

Reformed

The first poem after Eden is a taunt of blood. Lamech inflates injury into license to kill. Art becomes propaganda for the self.

Reformed gravity: when God is not feared, strength writes its own law. This is the seed of Cain matured—sin crouching, then composing.

Genesis 4:24

24If Cain’s revenge is sevenfold, then Lamech’s is seventy-sevenfold.”

Arminian

Cain’s protective sevenfold becomes Lamech’s seventy-sevenfold boast. Mercy is twisted into a brand. He claims a vengeance God never assigned him.

Classical Arminians see responsible escalation. God’s kindness to Cain was meant to restrain killing; Lamech resists that intent and multiplies harm by choice.

Reformed

Lamech hijacks God’s number. What was the Lord’s deterrent for Cain becomes a human claim to unlimited payback.

Reformed contrast: Jesus will later invert seventy-seven into forgiveness (Matt 18:22). Here the line of Cain baptizes revenge; only grace can break the math.

Key contrast Both refuse Lamech’s boast as distortion of God’s mark on Cain. Arminians typically stress a resistible misuse of mercy Lamech chose; Reformed readers typically stress the deep bondage and pride of a line God still limits even as it hymns its own wrath.

Genesis 4:25

25And Adam knew his wife again, and she bore a son and called his name Seth, for she said, “God has appointed for me another offspring instead of Abel, for Cain killed him.”

Arminian

The camera returns to Adam and Eve. Seth is “appointed” in place of Abel. Eve reads the birth as God’s answer to murder, not as chance.

Arminian hope: God sincerely continues the promise of offspring (3:15). He appoints a line through which grace will keep offering life; hearers are summoned to trust that appointment rather than Cain’s city.

Reformed

Seth’s name is theology: God appointed another seed. The promise does not die in Abel’s field. Eve interprets history by God’s act, not by Lamech’s song.

Reformed comfort: election of a line here is mercy after blood. The Lord preserves the offspring through whom He will bring the Serpent-crusher, and He does it as appointment, not accident.

Key contrast Both hear Eve’s “appointed” as God’s faithful answer to Abel’s death. Arminians typically stress ongoing, sincere provision of a promised seed people may trust; Reformed readers typically stress God’s sovereign appointment of the line He will keep for redemption.

Genesis 4:26

26To Seth also a son was born, and he called his name Enosh. At that time people began to call upon the name of the Lord.

Arminian

Enosh is born, and public worship surfaces: people begin to call upon the name of the Lord. Against Lamech’s voice, another voice rises—prayer, not boast.

Classical Arminians treasure this as grace restoring true religion in history. Calling on the Lord is a real human act enabled from above; the name is offered to be invoked, and the church still lives by that call.

Reformed

A son, then a liturgy. “Call upon the name of the Lord” is the first note of gathered worship in Genesis—the opposite of Nod’s self-song.

Reformed doxology: God not only appoints Seth; He grants a people who invoke His name. True worship is His gift in a violent world, the line along which promise will travel until the better blood speaks.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
