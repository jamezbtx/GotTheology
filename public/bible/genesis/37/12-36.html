Passage

Genesis 37

Verses 12–36 · WEB · Voices

Joseph is sent from Hebron to Shechem, then Dothan. The brothers conspire, strip the robe, throw him in a pit, sell him for twenty shekels, and fake a wild-animal death. Jacob mourns toward Sheol while Joseph is sold in Egypt to Potiphar.

Genesis 37:12

12His brothers went to feed their father’s flock in Shechem.

Arminian

Flocks at Shechem—old covenant ground, still tense after chapter 34. Distance from father is already a moral risk.

Ordinary work can become cover for hate. They remain accountable wherever the sheep go.

Reformed

Shechem again: the map of promise hosts the plot. Pasture is the errand God will use to move Joseph.

Geography is theological. The brothers go; providence is already walking Joseph toward them.

Genesis 37:13

13Israel said to Joseph, “Aren’t your brothers feeding the flock in Shechem? Come, and I will send you to them.” He said to him, “Here I am.”

Arminian

Israel sends; Joseph answers “Here I am”—the old servant’s yes. Obedience to a father is virtue, even in a spoiled house.

Grace makes Joseph available for the errand, but the choice to go is his. He could refuse the road; he goes. The yes is his, and it will cost him.

Reformed

The sending is Israel’s; the “Here I am” is Joseph’s. Father and son act; God aims the journey at Dothan and Egypt.

Like Isaac on Moriah’s road, the beloved son is sent. Providence rides an ordinary welfare check.

Genesis 37:14

14He said to him, “Go now, see whether it is well with your brothers, and well with the flock; and bring me word again.” So he sent him out of the valley of Hebron, and he came to Shechem.

Arminian

Peace-check and report: Hebron to Shechem. Joseph is still the father’s eyes. Love’s errand will be twisted by hate.

Seeking a brother’s shalom is a sincere good. That good can be refused by those he seeks.

Reformed

From Hebron’s valley—Machpelah country—to Shechem. The father wants word of welfare; God is sending a son toward a pit.

The errand is humanly wise and divinely freighted. “Bring me word” will return as a bloody robe, then years later as grain.

Genesis 37:15

15A certain man found him, and behold, he was wandering in the field. The man asked him, “What are you looking for?”

Arminian

A nameless man finds a lost seeker. Providence often wears an ordinary question: What are you seeking?

Joseph keeps seeking brothers, not abandoning the charge. Help received is still a choice to continue.

Reformed

The man is unnamed because the Finder behind him needs no credit line.

Without this stranger, Dothan never happens. God shepherds the shepherd-boy by a field encounter.

Genesis 37:16

16He said, “I am looking for my brothers. Tell me, please, where they are feeding the flock.”

Arminian

He names the quest: brothers, flock, please. Courtesy and duty still mark him on the road.

Seeking brothers is the right want. That they will not be brotherly does not make his seeking foolish before God.

Reformed

“I am looking for my brothers” is the sentence of the chapter. He seeks them; they will sell him.

The seeker is being led to the very men God will use, through their sin, to plant him in Egypt.

Genesis 37:17

17The man said, “They have left here, for I heard them say, ‘Let’s go to Dothan.’” Joseph went after his brothers, and found them in Dothan.

Arminian

Directions to Dothan; he goes and finds them. Persistence completes the errand. Finding is not yet safety.

Classical Arminians: he could have gone home when Shechem was empty. He presses on—responsible love walking into danger.

Reformed

Heard speech, Dothan, found. Every link is thin; together they are a chain God holds.

Joseph “went after” and “found” because the Lord meant Egypt. The man’s overheard line is a hinge of redemptive history.

Genesis 37:18

18They saw him afar off, and before he came near to them, they conspired against him to kill him.

Arminian

Sight from afar, conspiracy before nearness. Murder is planned prior to conversation. Hate has already reached a verdict.

This is freely chosen violence. Distance does not lessen guilt; they agree to kill a brother still walking toward them.

Reformed

Afar, then conspiracy, then kill. The plot precedes the greeting. Providence will intercept the knife, not the malice.

God knows the conspiracy and still lets Joseph arrive. Their counsel is wicked; it cannot finally unseat the dream.

Genesis 37:19

19They said to one another, “Behold, this dreamer comes.

Arminian

“This dreamer” is a sneer at God’s word. They reduce revelation to a nickname.

Mockery of a God-given dream is mockery of the Giver. Speech here is already blood-talk.

Reformed

The title they mean as insult is the truth of the chapter. The dreamer comes, and the dreams will outlive the pit.

God lets them name the office they hate. “Dreamer” is unwitting confession.

Genesis 37:20

20Come now therefore, and let’s kill him, and cast him into one of the pits, and we will say, ‘An evil animal has devoured him.’ We will see what will become of his dreams.”

Arminian

Kill, pit, lie, then a taunt at the dreams. They aim to test God’s word by murder. Cover story is planned with the crime.

Classical Arminians: this is resistible, high-handed sin. “We will see” is unbelief volunteering for judgment.

Reformed

Murder plus propaganda: animal story ready-made. They think death can audit heaven’s dreams.

They will see what becomes of the dreams—in Egypt, with grain, on their faces. God will answer the taunt without making them innocent.

Key contrast Both treat the plot as true guilt. Arminians typically press a freely chosen attempt to kill God’s word; Reformed commentators often underline that the dreams will stand by sovereign purpose even through this crime.

Genesis 37:21

21Reuben heard it, and delivered him out of their hand, and said, “Let’s not take his life.”

Arminian

Reuben hears and rescues—at least from immediate death. “Said, Let’s not take his life” is a moral stand, mixed with later failure.

Conscience can still speak in a murderous circle. Grace that restrains is not nothing, even when it is incomplete.

Reformed

Reuben’s hearing becomes a stay of execution. God uses the firstborn’s scruple to keep Joseph alive.

Rescue here is providential, not yet conversion of the pack. A brother’s partial mercy serves a larger keeping.

Genesis 37:22

22Reuben said to them, “Shed no blood. Throw him into this pit that is in the wilderness, but lay no hand on him”—that he might deliver him out of their hand, to restore him to his father.

Arminian

No blood, wilderness pit, no hand—stated plan to restore him to Jacob. Motive is named: rescue, not sadism.

Reuben’s righteousness is mixed. He opposes murder and still consents to a pit. Partial obedience remains praiseworthy and insufficient.

Reformed

The narrator gives Reuben’s secret aim: restore him to his father. God will restore Joseph another way, to another “father” in Pharaoh’s house first.

Even a half-plan is taken up. The pit meant as shelter becomes the sales floor. Providence edits Reuben’s rescue.

Genesis 37:23

23When Joseph came to his brothers, they stripped Joseph of his tunic, the tunic of many colors that was on him;

Arminian

Joseph arrives and is stripped at once. The robe of favor is torn off before the pit. Humiliation is deliberate.

Envy loves to undress a gift. They could have clothed peace; they choose exposure.

Reformed

The many-colored robe comes off in their hands. What Israel made, brothers unmake.

God will later clothe Joseph in Egypt’s linen. Stripping is injustice and, in His hands, a doorway.

Genesis 37:24

24and they took him, and threw him into the pit. The pit was empty. There was no water in it.

Arminian

Taken, thrown, empty pit, no water. They do not have to drown him; dryness is its own cruelty.

This is chosen harm against a brother who came seeking their welfare. The cistern is empty of water, but the brothers are full of guilt.

Reformed

Empty, waterless: details that keep him alive. A wet pit would end the dreams.

Reformed mercy in the dark: God appoints a dry hole. The pit is their sin and His holding cell.

Genesis 37:25

25They sat down to eat bread, and they lifted up their eyes and looked, and saw a caravan of Ishmaelites coming from Gilead, with their camels bearing spices and balm and myrrh, going to carry it down to Egypt.

Arminian

They eat while he sits in the hole. Then a Gilead caravan: gum, balm, myrrh, Egypt-bound. Appetite and commerce walk in together.

Callousness is a decision. Looking up, they could repent; they will bargain instead.

Reformed

Meal, then Ishmaelites, then Egypt in the sentence. Spices for the Nile while a brother starves below.

God times the caravan. Their lunch-break is the hinge of Israel-in-Egypt. Means are mercantile; end is salvation’s setup.

Genesis 37:26

26Judah said to his brothers, “What profit is it if we kill our brother and conceal his blood?

Arminian

Judah calculates: profit versus concealed blood. He names “our brother” even as he monetizes him.

This is not repentance yet. It is harm-reduction with a ledger. Still, refusing murder is a fork they take.

Reformed

Judah’s question is economic and moral at once. Blood concealed still cries; he prefers a sale.

The brother who will later pledge himself for Benjamin here prices Joseph. God will redeem Judah’s line; He does not pretty this moment.

Genesis 37:27

27Come, and let’s sell him to the Ishmaelites, and not let our hand be on him; for he is our brother, our flesh.” His brothers listened to him.

Arminian

Sell him, no hand upon him, because flesh and brother—twisted mercy. They listen. Avoiding the knife is distinct from love.

Classical Arminians: listening to Judah is a responsible group choice. They could have pulled him up and gone home.

Reformed

“Our flesh” becomes the slogan of a sale. Hands stay clean of blood and dirty with silver.

God lets their compromise stand, because Egypt needs Joseph alive. Their listening is guilty; it is also the road He chose to use.

Genesis 37:28

28Midianites who were merchants passed by, and they drew and lifted up Joseph out of the pit, and sold Joseph to the Ishmaelites for twenty pieces of silver. The merchants brought Joseph into Egypt.

Arminian

Drawn up, sold for twenty shekels, taken to Egypt. The lift from the pit is not liberation; it is a price. Silver for a brother’s life.

Trafficking is chosen. Later, Joseph will say God meant it for good (50:20)—which never unmakes the sale as sin.

Reformed

The narrator stacks traders the way providence stacks means.

Lifted from a pit and sold, he goes down to Egypt for a people’s future. God writes straight on crooked silver.

Key contrast Both hold the sale as wicked and God as not absent. Arminians emphasize the brothers’ free crime that God can later overrule; Calvinists stress God intending Egypt through the crime without calling the crime good.

Genesis 37:29

29Reuben returned to the pit, and saw that Joseph wasn’t in the pit; and he tore his clothes.

Arminian

Reuben returns too late and tears clothes. Partial rescue fails. Grief here is not denied, if mixed with fear of Jacob.

Good intentions that leave a brother in a pit can still miss him. Responsibility includes follow-through.

Reformed

Reuben finds the pit empty and tears his clothes. Reuben’s plan is outrun by the sale God will use.

The firstborn cannot restore Joseph. Another lifting—God’s—is already on the road to Egypt.

Genesis 37:30

30He returned to his brothers, and said, “The child is no more; and I, where will I go?”

Arminian

He feels the hole in the family and in his role.

Crisis can still turn to truth-telling. Instead, the group will manufacture a story.

Reformed

Gone, and no path for Reuben. The question is self-preservation as much as grief.

The boy is not gone from God’s map. He is gone from the pit toward Potiphar, which they cannot see.

Genesis 37:31

31They took Joseph’s tunic, and killed a male goat, and dipped the tunic in the blood.

Arminian

Goat blood on the robe—Jacob’s old trick (ch. 27) returns as a costume of death. They know how to deceive a father with a garment.

This is crafted lying. Grace that could have confessed is stuffed with goat hair and dye.

Reformed

Slaughtered goat, dipped robe: a counterfeit of Joseph’s death. The house that once fooled Isaac now fools Israel.

God lets the deceiver be deceived. The blood is false; the going-down to Egypt is true.

Genesis 37:32

32They took the tunic of many colors, and they brought it to their father, and said, “We have found this. Examine it, now, and see if it is your son’s tunic or not.”

Arminian

They send, they do not quite say “we killed him.” Please identify puts the verdict in Jacob’s mouth. Cowardice writes the script.

“Your son,” not “our brother.” Language sheds kinship to shed guilt.

Reformed

Found robe, father’s identification requested. They outsource the lie’s conclusion to love’s panic.

The many-colored sign of favor becomes the exhibit of a false death. God will keep the true son alive anyway.

Genesis 37:33

33He recognized it, and said, “It is my son’s tunic. An evil animal has devoured him. Joseph is without doubt torn in pieces.”

Arminian

He identifies and fills in their story: fierce animal, torn without doubt. Grief believes a lie because sons withhold truth.

Deception is a violence against a father’s mind. They could have undone it with one sentence.

Reformed

Jacob preaches their sermon for them. “Without doubt” is the certainty of despair, not of fact.

Reformed comfort beneath the error: Joseph is not torn. God hides a living son under a father’s true tears and false conclusion.

Genesis 37:34

34Jacob tore his clothes, and put sackcloth on his waist, and mourned for his son many days.

Arminian

The father’s mourning is not theatrical; it is love wounded by a lie.

Long grief is human and holy. God does not scold Jacob for tears the brothers earned.

Reformed

Patriarchal mourning rites, stretched across many days. The elect household is in sackcloth while the elect son rides south.

God can hold both—the father’s lament and the son’s preservation—without rushing either.

Genesis 37:35

35All his sons and all his daughters rose up to comfort him, but he refused to be comforted. He said, “For I will go down to Sheol to my son, mourning.” His father wept for him.

Arminian

Comforters include the architects of the grief. He refuses comfort and speaks of Sheol. Hypocrisy sits beside a true wound.

Classical Arminians: they could comfort with confession. Rising to soothe without repenting is another cruelty.

Reformed

He will not be comforted; he names Sheol as his path to the boy. The narrator adds: his father wept for him.

God records the tears. Comfort will come years later from the “dead” son himself, by the same brothers’ mouths.

Genesis 37:36

36The Midianites sold him into Egypt to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh’s, the captain of the guard.

Arminian

Meanwhile—Egypt, Potiphar, captain of the guard. Two stages: Hebron’s funeral and Pharaoh’s house. Joseph is sold again, still alive.

Human trafficking reaches a palace. God can yet work good; the sales remain unjust. The next chapters will test Joseph’s faithful response under grace.

Reformed

Meanwhile is a doctrine: while Jacob weeps, God seats Joseph in the guard-captain’s house. Officer of Pharaoh is not accident.

The dream is in transit, not dead. Sold to Potiphar, the sojourner’s great-grandson is exactly where the famine-plan requires him to be.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

The pit, the sale, and Jacob’s mourning: older Arminian and Reformed writers, each in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Reuben of all the brothers had most reason to be jealous of Joseph, for he was the first-born, and so entitled to those distinguishing favours which Jacob was conferring on Joseph, yet he proves his best friend. Reuben's temper seems to have been soft and effeminate, which had betrayed him to the sin of uncleanness, while the temper of the two next brothers, Simeon and Levi, was fierce, which betrayed them to the sin of murder, a sin which Reuben startled at the thought of.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 37:21)

Reformed Notable voices

“Some think that Joseph was twice sold in the same place. For it is certain, since Median was the son of Abraham and Keturah, that his sons were distinct from the sons of Ishmael: and Moses has not thoughtlessly put down these different names. But I thus interpret the passage: that Joseph was exposed for sale to any one who chose, and seeing the purchase of him was declined by the Midianites, he was sold to the Ishmaelites.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 37:28)
