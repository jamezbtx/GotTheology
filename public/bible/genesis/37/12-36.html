Passage

Genesis 37

Verses 12–36 · ESV · Voices

Joseph is sent from Hebron to Shechem, then Dothan. The brothers conspire, strip the robe, throw him in a pit, sell him for twenty shekels, and fake a wild-animal death. Jacob mourns toward Sheol while Joseph is sold in Egypt to Potiphar.

Genesis 37:12

12Now his brothers went to pasture their father's flock near Shechem.

Arminian

Flocks at Shechem—old covenant ground, still tense after chapter 34. Distance from father is already a moral risk.

Arminian note: ordinary work can become cover for hate. They remain accountable wherever the sheep go.

Reformed

Shechem again: the map of promise hosts the plot. Pasture is the errand God will use to move Joseph.

Reformed reading: geography is theological. The brothers go; providence is already walking Joseph toward them.

Genesis 37:13

13And Israel said to Joseph, “Are not your brothers pasturing the flock at Shechem? Come, I will send you to them.” And he said to him, “Here I am.”

Arminian

Israel sends; Joseph answers “Here I am”—the old servant’s yes. Obedience to a father is real virtue, even in a spoiled house.

Arminian pastoral: grace-enabled availability. He could refuse the road; he goes. The yes is his, and it will cost him.

Reformed

The sending is Israel’s; the “Here I am” is Joseph’s. Father and son act; God aims the journey at Dothan and Egypt.

Reformed accent: like Isaac on Moriah’s road, the beloved son is sent. Providence rides an ordinary welfare check.

Genesis 37:14

14So he said to him, “Go now, see if it is well with your brothers and with the flock, and bring me word.” So he sent him from the Valley of Hebron, and he came to Shechem.

Arminian

Peace-check and report: Hebron to Shechem. Joseph is still the father’s eyes. Love’s errand will be twisted by hate.

Arminian note: seeking a brother’s shalom is a sincere good. That good can be refused by those he seeks.

Reformed

From Hebron’s valley—Machpelah country—to Shechem. The father wants word of welfare; God is sending a son toward a pit.

Reformed comfort: the errand is humanly wise and divinely freighted. “Bring me word” will return as a bloody robe, then years later as grain.

Genesis 37:15

15And a man found him wandering in the fields. And the man asked him, “What are you seeking?”

Arminian

A nameless man finds a lost seeker. Providence often wears an ordinary question: What are you seeking?

Arminian reading: Joseph keeps seeking brothers, not abandoning the charge. Help received is still a choice to continue.

Reformed

Wandering, found, asked. The man is unnamed because the Finder behind him needs no credit line.

Reformed note: without this stranger, Dothan never happens. God shepherds the shepherd-boy by a field encounter.

Genesis 37:16

16“I am seeking my brothers,” he said. “Tell me, please, where they are pasturing the flock.”

Arminian

He names the quest: brothers, flock, please. Courtesy and duty still mark him on the road.

Arminian pastoral: seeking brothers is the right want. That they will not be brotherly does not make his seeking foolish before God.

Reformed

“I am seeking my brothers” is the sentence of the chapter. He seeks them; they will sell him.

Reformed irony under sovereignty: the seeker is being led to the very men God will use, through their sin, to plant him in Egypt.

Genesis 37:17

17And the man said, “They have gone away, for I heard them say, ‘Let us go to Dothan.’” So Joseph went after his brothers and found them at Dothan.

Arminian

Directions to Dothan; he goes and finds them. Persistence completes the errand. Finding is not yet safety.

Classical Arminians: he could have gone home when Shechem was empty. He presses on—responsible love walking into danger.

Reformed

Heard speech, Dothan, found. Every link is thin; together they are a chain God holds.

Reformed confession: Joseph “went after” and “found” because the Lord meant Egypt. The man’s overheard line is a hinge of redemptive history.

Genesis 37:18

18They saw him from afar, and before he came near to them they conspired against him to kill him.

Arminian

Sight from afar, conspiracy before nearness. Murder is planned prior to conversation. Hate has already reached a verdict.

Arminian gravity: this is freely chosen violence. Distance does not lessen guilt; they agree to kill a brother still walking toward them.

Reformed

Afar, then conspiracy, then kill. The plot precedes the greeting. Providence will intercept the knife, not the malice.

Reformed accent: God knows the conspiracy and still lets Joseph arrive. Their counsel is wicked; it cannot finally unseat the dream.

Genesis 37:19

19They said to one another, “Here comes this dreamer.

Arminian

“This dreamer” is a sneer at God’s word. They reduce revelation to a nickname.

Arminian warning: mockery of a God-given dream is mockery of the Giver. Speech here is already blood-talk.

Reformed

The title they mean as insult is the truth of the chapter. The dreamer comes, and the dreams will outlive the pit.

Reformed irony: God lets them name the office they hate. “Dreamer” is unwitting confession.

Genesis 37:20

20Come now, let us kill him and throw him into one of the pits. Then we will say that a fierce animal has devoured him, and we will see what will become of his dreams.”

Arminian

Kill, pit, lie, then a taunt at the dreams. They aim to test God’s word by murder. Cover story is planned with the crime.

Classical Arminians: this is resistible, high-handed sin. “We will see” is unbelief volunteering for judgment.

Reformed

Murder plus propaganda: animal story ready-made. They think death can audit heaven’s dreams.

Reformed thunder: they will see what becomes of the dreams—in Egypt, with grain, on their faces. God will answer the taunt without making them innocent.

Key contrast Both treat the plot as true guilt. Arminians typically stress a freely chosen attempt to kill God’s word; Reformed readers typically stress that the dreams will stand by sovereign purpose even through this crime.

Genesis 37:21

21But when Reuben heard it, he rescued him out of their hands, saying, “Let us not take his life.”

Arminian

Reuben hears and rescues—at least from immediate death. “Let us not take his life” is a real moral stand, mixed with later failure.

Arminian hope: conscience can still speak in a murderous circle. Grace that restrains is not nothing, even when it is incomplete.

Reformed

Reuben’s hearing becomes a stay of execution. God uses the firstborn’s scruple to keep Joseph alive.

Reformed note: rescue here is providential, not yet conversion of the pack. A brother’s partial mercy serves a larger keeping.

Genesis 37:22

22And Reuben said to them, “Shed no blood; throw him into this pit here in the wilderness, but do not lay a hand on him”—that he might rescue him out of their hand to restore him to his father.

Arminian

No blood, wilderness pit, no hand—stated plan to restore him to Jacob. Motive is named: rescue, not sadism.

Arminian complexity: mixed righteousness. He opposes murder and still consents to a pit. Partial obedience remains praiseworthy and insufficient.

Reformed

The narrator gives Reuben’s secret aim: restore him to his father. God will restore Joseph another way, to another “father” in Pharaoh’s house first.

Reformed reading: even a half-plan is taken up. The pit meant as shelter becomes the sales floor. Providence edits Reuben’s rescue.

Genesis 37:23

23So when Joseph came to his brothers, they stripped him of his robe, the robe of many colors that he wore.

Arminian

Arrival, then stripping. The robe of favor is torn off before the pit. Humiliation is deliberate.

Arminian pastoral: envy loves to undress a gift. They could have clothed peace; they choose exposure.

Reformed

The many-colored robe comes off in their hands. What Israel made, brothers unmake.

Reformed note: God will later clothe Joseph in Egypt’s linen. Stripping is real injustice and, in His hands, a doorway.

Genesis 37:24

24And they took him and threw him into a pit. The pit was empty; there was no water in it.

Arminian

Taken, thrown, empty pit, no water. They do not have to drown him; dryness is its own cruelty.

Arminian grief: this is chosen harm against a brother who came seeking their welfare. Empty cistern, full guilt.

Reformed

Empty, waterless: details that keep him alive. A wet pit would end the dreams.

Reformed mercy in the dark: God appoints a dry hole. The pit is their sin and His holding cell.

Genesis 37:25

25Then they sat down to eat. And looking up they saw a caravan of Ishmaelites coming from Gilead, with their camels bearing gum, balm, and myrrh, on their way to carry it down to Egypt.

Arminian

They eat while he sits in the hole. Then a Gilead caravan: gum, balm, myrrh, Egypt-bound. Appetite and commerce walk in together.

Arminian horror: callousness is a decision. Looking up, they could repent; they will bargain instead.

Reformed

Meal, then Ishmaelites, then Egypt in the sentence. Spices for the Nile while a brother starves below.

Reformed confession: God times the caravan. Their lunch-break is the hinge of Israel-in-Egypt. Means are mercantile; end is salvation’s setup.

Genesis 37:26

26Then Judah said to his brothers, “What profit is it if we kill our brother and conceal his blood?

Arminian

Judah calculates: profit versus concealed blood. He names “our brother” even as he monetizes him.

Arminian candor: this is not repentance yet. It is harm-reduction with a ledger. Still, refusing murder is a real fork they take.

Reformed

Judah’s question is economic and moral at once. Blood concealed still cries; he prefers a sale.

Reformed irony: the brother who will later pledge himself for Benjamin here prices Joseph. God will redeem Judah’s line; He does not pretty this moment.

Genesis 37:27

27Come, let us sell him to the Ishmaelites, and let not our hand be upon him, for he is our brother, our own flesh.” And his brothers listened to him.

Arminian

Sell him, no hand upon him, because flesh and brother—twisted mercy. They listen. Avoiding the knife is not the same as love.

Classical Arminians: listening to Judah is a responsible group choice. They could have pulled him up and gone home.

Reformed

“Our own flesh” becomes the slogan of a sale. Hands stay clean of blood and dirty with silver.

Reformed accent: God lets their compromise stand, because Egypt needs Joseph alive. Their listening is guilty; it is also the road He chose to use.

Genesis 37:28

28Then Midianite traders passed by. And they drew Joseph up and lifted him out of the pit, and sold him to the Ishmaelites for twenty shekels of silver. They took Joseph to Egypt.

Arminian

Drawn up, sold for twenty shekels, taken to Egypt. The lift from the pit is not liberation; it is a price. Silver for a brother’s life.

Arminian lament: trafficking is chosen. Later, Joseph will say God meant it for good (50:20)—which never unmakes the sale as sin.

Reformed

Midianites, Ishmaelites, twenty shekels, Egypt. The narrator stacks traders the way providence stacks means.

Reformed gospel-shadow: lifted from a pit and sold, he goes down to Egypt for a people’s future. God writes straight on crooked silver.

Key contrast Both hold the sale as wicked and God as not absent. Arminians typically stress the brothers’ free crime that God can later overrule; Reformed readers typically stress God intending Egypt through the crime without calling the crime good.

Genesis 37:29

29When Reuben returned to the pit and saw that Joseph was not in the pit, he tore his clothes

Arminian

Reuben returns too late and tears clothes. Partial rescue fails. Grief here is real, if mixed with fear of Jacob.

Arminian note: good intentions that leave a brother in a pit can still miss him. Responsibility includes follow-through.

Reformed

Empty pit, torn clothes. Reuben’s plan is outrun by the sale God will use.

Reformed reading: the firstborn cannot restore Joseph. Another lifting—God’s—is already on the road to Egypt.

Genesis 37:30

30and returned to his brothers and said, “The boy is gone, and I, where shall I go?”

Arminian

“The boy is gone” and “where shall I go?”—panic more than confession. He feels the hole in the family and in his role.

Arminian pastoral: crisis can still turn to truth-telling. Instead, the group will manufacture a story.

Reformed

Gone, and no path for Reuben. The question is self-preservation as much as grief.

Reformed note: the boy is not gone from God’s map. He is gone from the pit toward Potiphar, which they cannot see.

Genesis 37:31

31Then they took Joseph's robe and slaughtered a goat and dipped the robe in the blood.

Arminian

Goat blood on the robe—Jacob’s old trick (ch. 27) returns as a costume of death. They know how to deceive a father with a garment.

Arminian hardness: this is crafted lying. Grace that could have confessed is stuffed with goat hair and dye.

Reformed

Slaughtered goat, dipped robe: a counterfeit of Joseph’s death. The house that once fooled Isaac now fools Israel.

Reformed irony: God lets the deceiver be deceived. The blood is false; the going-down to Egypt is true.

Genesis 37:32

32And they sent the robe of many colors and brought it to their father and said, “This we have found; please identify whether it is your son's robe or not.”

Arminian

They send, they do not quite say “we killed him.” “Please identify” puts the verdict in Jacob’s mouth. Cowardice writes the script.

Arminian rebuke: “your son,” not “our brother.” Language sheds kinship to shed guilt.

Reformed

Found robe, father’s identification requested. They outsource the lie’s conclusion to love’s panic.

Reformed grief: the many-colored sign of favor becomes the exhibit of a false death. God will keep the true son alive anyway.

Genesis 37:33

33And he identified it and said, “It is my son's robe. A fierce animal has devoured him. Joseph is without doubt torn to pieces.”

Arminian

He identifies and fills in their story: fierce animal, torn without doubt. Grief believes a lie because sons withhold truth.

Arminian ache: deception is a violence against a father’s mind. They could have undone it with one sentence.

Reformed

Jacob preaches their sermon for them. “Without doubt” is the certainty of despair, not of fact.

Reformed comfort beneath the error: Joseph is not torn. God hides a living son under a father’s true tears and false conclusion.

Genesis 37:34

34Then Jacob tore his garments and put sackcloth on his loins and mourned for his son many days.

Arminian

Torn clothes, sackcloth, many days. The father’s mourning is not theatrical; it is love wounded by a lie.

Arminian pastoral: long grief is human and holy. God does not scold Jacob for tears the brothers earned.

Reformed

Patriarchal mourning rites, stretched across many days. The elect household is in sackcloth while the elect son rides south.

Reformed note: God can hold both—the father’s lament and the son’s preservation—without rushing either.

Genesis 37:35

35All his sons and all his daughters rose up to comfort him, but he refused to be comforted and said, “No, I shall go down to Sheol to my son, mourning.” Thus his father wept for him.

Arminian

Comforters include the architects of the grief. He refuses comfort and speaks of Sheol. Hypocrisy sits beside a true wound.

Classical Arminians: they could comfort with confession. Rising to soothe without repenting is another cruelty.

Reformed

He will not be comforted; he names Sheol as his path to the boy. The narrator adds: his father wept for him.

Reformed pathos: God records the tears. Comfort will come years later from the “dead” son himself, by the same brothers’ mouths.

Genesis 37:36

36Meanwhile the Midianites had sold him in Egypt to Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh, the captain of the guard.

Arminian

Meanwhile—Egypt, Potiphar, captain of the guard. Two stages: Hebron’s funeral and Pharaoh’s house. Joseph is sold again, still alive.

Arminian close: human trafficking reaches a palace. God can yet work good; the sales remain unjust. The next chapters will test Joseph’s faithful response under grace.

Reformed

Meanwhile is a doctrine: while Jacob weeps, God seats Joseph in the guard-captain’s house. Officer of Pharaoh is not accident.

Reformed close: the dream is in transit, not dead. Sold to Potiphar, the sojourner’s great-grandson is exactly where the famine-plan requires him to be.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the pit, the sale, and Jacob’s mourning—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 37:12–36.

Arminian Notable voices

“Reuben of all the brothers had most reason to be jealous of Joseph, for he was the first-born, and so entitled to those distinguishing favours which Jacob was conferring on Joseph, yet he proves his best friend. Reuben's temper seems to have been soft and effeminate, which had betrayed him to the sin of uncleanness, while the temper of the two next brothers, Simeon and Levi, was fierce, which betrayed them to the sin of murder, a sin which Reuben startled at the thought of.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 37:21)

Reformed Notable voices

“Some think that Joseph was twice sold in the same place. For it is certain, since Median was the son of Abraham and Keturah, that his sons were distinct from the sons of Ishmael: and Moses has not thoughtlessly put down these different names. But I thus interpret the passage: that Joseph was exposed for sale to any one who chose, and seeing the purchase of him was declined by the Midianites, he was sold to the Ishmaelites.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 37:28)
