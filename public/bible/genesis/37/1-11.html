Passage

Genesis 37

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

Jacob dwells in Canaan. The Joseph story opens: a seventeen-year-old bringing a bad report, a father’s favoritism and a many-colored robe, brothers who cannot speak peace, and two dreams of sheaves and stars that deepen hatred while Israel keeps the saying in mind.

Genesis 37:1

1Jacob lived in the land of his father's sojournings, in the land of Canaan.

Arminian

The camera returns to Canaan after Esau’s line. Jacob lives where Isaac sojourned—still a dweller, not yet a nation.

Arminian note: promise still waits on faith’s long walk. God sincerely holds the land-word; the household must live inside it without seizing it by violence.

Reformed

Canaan is named as the father’s sojourning land. The elect line is home and not home yet.

Reformed comfort: God parks Jacob where the oath is aimed. Sojourning is not delay of purpose; it is the stage of purpose.

Genesis 37:2

2These are the generations of Jacob. Joseph, being seventeen years old, was pasturing the flock with his brothers. He was a boy with the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah, his father's wives. And Joseph brought a bad report of them to their father.

Arminian

“Generations of Jacob” then names Joseph. At seventeen he pastures with Bilhah and Zilpah’s sons and brings a bad report. Youth, work, and speech already strain the house.

Classical Arminians do not baptize the report as either wholly holy or wholly snitching. Real agency is in play: Joseph speaks; brothers will answer. Grace does not erase family sin; it will yet overrule it.

Reformed

The toledot of Jacob is Joseph’s story. Seventeen, among the concubines’ sons, a bad report to the father—the rift is already in the record.

Reformed reading: God is not surprised by a talkative son or offended brothers. The line toward Egypt begins in ordinary household friction under providence.

Genesis 37:3

3Now Israel loved Joseph more than any other of his sons, because he was the son of his old age. And he made him a robe of many colors.

Arminian

Israel loves Joseph more—old-age son, visible robe. Favoritism is named, not excused. Love that costumes one child wounds the rest.

Arminian pastoral: grace-enabled parents are still responsible. God’s later use of this robe does not make the partiality righteous.

Reformed

Favor is stated as fact: more than any other, because of old age, sealed with a robe. The garment will become a plot device of hatred and a lie.

Reformed note: God can weave even Israel’s foolish love into the path of promise. The robe is not election; Joseph’s later preservation is.

Genesis 37:4

4But when his brothers saw that their father loved him more than all his brothers, they hated him and could not speak peacefully to him.

Arminian

They see, they hate, they cannot speak peace. The verb is theirs. Favoritism tempts; hatred is chosen.

Classical Arminians press guilt here: grace that could restrain is resisted. Peaceable speech was possible; they would not.

Reformed

Sight of the father’s love becomes hatred that shuts the mouth of peace. The brothers’ sin is real, not a stage cue.

Reformed accent: God will govern the outcome; He does not author their malice as innocence. Inability to speak peace is moral, not mechanical.

Key contrast Both refuse to excuse the brothers. Arminians typically stress resistible hatred they could have refused; Reformed readers typically stress guilty unbelief under a providence that will still save through the wound.

Genesis 37:5

5Now Joseph had a dream, and when he told it to his brothers they hated him even more.

Arminian

A dream is given; telling it adds fuel. Revelation does not cancel prudence, and brothers remain accountable for added hate.

Arminian reading: God may disclose a future; hearers may still rebel. The dream is sincere light they refuse.

Reformed

The dream is God’s initiative; the telling becomes the occasion of deeper hate. Heaven speaks; the house hardens.

Reformed comfort: the dream will stand. Hatred cannot unspeak what God has shown, even when the dreamer is young and the audience hostile.

Genesis 37:6

6He said to them, “Hear this dream that I have dreamed:

Arminian

“Hear this dream” is a summons. Joseph wants the house to listen. Dreams in Genesis often carry God’s word, not private ego alone.

Arminian note: hearing is a real duty. They can attend or mock; grace does not force their ears.

Reformed

He commands a hearing for what he dreamed. The brothers are made witnesses of a word they will try to kill.

Reformed note: God puts His future in a teenager’s mouth. The command to hear is already a kind of judgment on those who will not.

Genesis 37:7

7Behold, we were binding sheaves in the field, and behold, my sheaf arose and stood upright. And behold, your sheaves gathered around it and bowed down to my sheaf.”

Arminian

Sheaves bow to Joseph’s sheaf—harvest imagery of rule. The picture is from God; the family must decide whether to receive it.

Classical Arminians: divine disclosure invites trust, not murder. Later famine-bowing (ch. 42) will show the word was true, not a boy’s grab for power.

Reformed

Three “beholds”: his sheaf rises; theirs gather and bow. God sketches Egypt before Egypt exists.

Reformed confession: the dream is decree in parable. What God shows, He will perform—through pits and prisons they cannot imagine.

Genesis 37:8

8His brothers said to him, “Are you indeed to reign over us? Or are you indeed to rule over us?” So they hated him even more for his dreams and for his words.

Arminian

They hear reign and rule and hate both dream and words. The questions are scorn, not inquiry. Speech becomes a reason to wound.

Arminian warning: rejecting a God-given word is a free, guilty act. Hatred “even more” is piled choice, not fate.

Reformed

Reign and rule are the right interpretation—and they hate it. Dreams and words together become the target.

Reformed reading: they understand enough to rebel. God will yet have Joseph rule in Egypt; their contempt cannot veto the sheaf.

Genesis 37:9

9Then he dreamed another dream and told it to his brothers and said, “Behold, I have dreamed another dream. Behold, the sun, the moon, and eleven stars were bowing down to me.”

Arminian

A second dream widens the first: sun, moon, eleven stars. God repeats for confirmation. Telling it again is either faith or naiveté—or both.

Arminian pastoral: repeated light increases responsibility. The household is not under-informed; it is under-willing.

Reformed

God doubles the vision—celestial bowing now, not only grain. Two dreams, one purpose, as Pharaoh will later learn.

Reformed accent: confirmation is mercy and severity. The word stands; the family is being readied, against its will, for a future bow.

Genesis 37:10

10But when he told it to his father and to his brothers, his father rebuked him and said to him, “What is this dream that you have dreamed? Shall I and your mother and your brothers indeed come to bow ourselves to the ground before you?”

Arminian

Israel rebukes, naming father, mother, brothers in the bow. Even the favored son is checked. The question is protest, yet it tracks the dream’s meaning.

Arminian note: parental rebuke can be mixed—protecting peace, resisting God. Jacob remains a responsible hearer, not a puppet of favoritism.

Reformed

The father hears and rebukes, spelling out the implication better than he likes. Mother and brothers bowing is the offense of the vision.

Reformed comfort: God can use a rebuke to preserve the saying. Jacob’s mouth resists; Jacob’s mind will keep it (v. 11).

Genesis 37:11

11And his brothers were jealous of him, but his father kept the saying in mind.

Arminian

Jealousy in the brothers; pondering in the father. Two responses to one word. Grace invites a better hearing than envy.

Classical Arminians: jealousy is culpable; keeping the saying is a seed of faith. Neither is coerced. The story now turns on what they will do with both.

Reformed

Jealousy and treasuring split the house. Acts 7 will call this jealousy; Luke’s Mary-like “kept” sits on Israel’s side.

Reformed close: God plants the saying in the patriarch while letting brothers stew. Providence holds the word until Egypt makes it plain.

Key contrast Both see two real responses. Arminians typically stress jealous refusal versus grace-enabled pondering; Reformed readers typically stress God keeping His word in Jacob while jealousy runs its guilty course.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Joseph’s dreams and the brothers’ hatred—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 37:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“Though he was now very young, about seventeen years old, yet he was pious and devout, and this fitted him for God's gracious discoveries to him. Joseph had a great deal of trouble before him, and therefore God gave him betimes this prospect of his advancement, to support and comfort him.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 37:5)

Reformed Notable voices

“By the word tvldvt toledoth we are not so much to understand a genealogy, as a record of events, which appears more clearly from the context. For Moses having thus commenced, does not enumerate sons and grandsons, but explains the cause of the envy of Joseph's brethren, who formed a wicked conspiracy against him, and sold him as a slave: as if he had said "Having briefly summed up the genealogy of Esau, I now revert to the series of my history, as to what happened to the family of Jacob." Moreover, Moses being about to speak of the abominable wickedness of Jacob's sons, begins with the statement, that Joseph was dear beyond the rest to his father, because he had begotten him in his old age: and as a token of tender love, had clothed him with a coat woven of many colors.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 37:2)
