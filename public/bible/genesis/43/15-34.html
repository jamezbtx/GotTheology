Passage

Genesis 43

Verses 15–34 · WEB · Voices

The brothers stand before Joseph with Benjamin, gifts, and double money. Fear at the house meets the steward’s “Peace,” Simeon’s return, and a noon meal: bows that fulfill the old dreams, hidden weeping for Rachel’s son, seats by birth order, and Benjamin’s fivefold portion.

Genesis 43:15

15The men took that present, and they took double money in their hand, and Benjamin; and got up, went down to Egypt, and stood before Joseph.

Arminian

Present, double money, Benjamin: they actually go. Standing before Joseph is obedience with the boy in tow.

The journey is a chosen act under pressure. They keep the father’s charge; they still do not know whose face they face.

Reformed

The caravan God wanted is complete. They stand before the brother whose dreams they once hated.

“stood before Joseph” is providence’s punch line. Egypt is not a detour; it is the table being set.

Genesis 43:16

16When Joseph saw Benjamin with them, he said to the steward of his house, “Bring the men into the house, and butcher an animal, and prepare; for the men will dine with me at noon.”

Arminian

Seeing Benjamin unlocks hospitality: slaughter, make ready, noon. The test is not over, but kindness begins.

Joseph’s sight of the full brother moves him toward table, not toward revenge. He remains responsible for how he uses power.

Reformed

Benjamin in view, the house is opened. A feast is commanded before the brothers understand they are guests, not defendants.

The unrecognized lord prepares a meal the dreams implied. Compassion is already governing the timetable.

Genesis 43:17

17The man did as Joseph commanded, and the man brought the men to Joseph’s house.

Arminian

The steward obeys; they are brought in. Location change is enough to rattle them.

Servants can keep faith with a master’s word. The brothers’ next fear will be their interpretation, not the steward’s malice.

Reformed

As told, to the house. Joseph’s will moves through a steward the way God’s will is moving through Joseph.

They enter a house prepared for them before they asked to enter.

Genesis 43:18

18The men were afraid, because they were brought to Joseph’s house; and they said, “Because of the money that was returned in our sacks the first time, we’re brought in; that he may seek occasion against us, attack us, and seize us as slaves, along with our donkeys.”

Arminian

Fear writes a worst-case script: assault, slavery, seized donkeys. Guilt from chapter 37 still hears every kindness as a trap.

Classical Arminians do not mock the fear. Conscience can misread mercy. They remain free to seek the steward rather than bolt.

Reformed

Brought to the house, they invent a prosecution. The money Joseph hid becomes, in their minds, a pretext for bondage.

The men who sold a brother into slavery now fear being made servants. Providence lets old sin interpret new grace—until peace is spoken.

Genesis 43:19

19They came near to the steward of Joseph’s house, and they spoke to him at the door of the house,

Arminian

They go to the door, not past it in panic. Speech before entry is wisdom.

Their fear drives them to speak before anyone accuses them. They try to clear the ledger before sitting down.

Reformed

The door is the place of confession. They intercept the steward with the story of the silver.

God has a word of peace waiting on the other side of this conversation (v. 23).

Genesis 43:20

20and said, “Oh, my lord, we indeed came down the first time to buy food.

Arminian

Oh, my lord: they state the innocent purpose of trip one. Buying food, not spying, remains their claim.

They keep telling the truth they have. Integrity is still their assignment, even when the whole truth about Joseph is hidden.

Reformed

The first descent is named as commerce. They do not know they are explaining themselves to their brother’s household.

“buy food” is accurate and small. God is doing more than a grain deal in this doorway.

Genesis 43:21

21When we came to the lodging place, we opened our sacks, and behold, each man’s money was in the mouth of his sack, our money in full weight. We have brought it back in our hand.

Arminian

They volunteer the returned silver before they are charged.

They bring back the money though no one has summoned them. Grace-enabled honesty carries the money back even when they did not take it.

Reformed

Lodging-place discovery, full weight, returned. The narrator already showed Joseph ordered this.

They repay a gift they cannot explain. God is teaching them to handle silver without treachery.

Genesis 43:22

22We have brought down other money in our hand to buy food. We don’t know who put our money in our sacks.”

Arminian

Other money for food, plus ignorance about the sacks. They will not claim a miracle they do not understand.

“We don’t know” is humble speech. Mystery is allowed; theft is denied.

Reformed

Double payment plus confessed ignorance. The hidden actor is Joseph—and behind him, God.

Not knowing is the right posture. The One who put the money there will name Himself later.

Genesis 43:23

23He said, “Peace be to you. Don’t be afraid. Your God, and the God of your father, has given you treasure in your sacks. I received your money.” He brought Simeon out to them.

Arminian

Peace, do not be afraid. The steward theologies the silver: your God and your father’s God put treasure there. Simeon is produced alive.

The emphasis is on a Gentile mouth speaking covenant comfort. Mercy is announced as gift, not oversight. They may receive peace rather than keep rehearsing doom.

Reformed

The steward speaks peace before the meal. Treasure attributed to their God—Joseph’s household knows the Name the brothers fear. Simeon’s appearance seals the word.

God put the treasure there, through Joseph’s command. The steward becomes an unexpected preacher of providence.

Key contrast Both take “your God…has put treasure” as true theology from an unlikely speaker. Arminians stress peace sincerely offered for them to receive; Reformed interpreters stress that God actually placed the silver by His own hand, using Joseph.

Genesis 43:24

24The man brought the men into Joseph’s house, and gave them water, and they washed their feet. He gave their donkeys fodder.

Arminian

Water, feet, fodder: ordinary mercy after terror. Guests are treated as guests.

Small kindnesses train fearful hearts. Receive the washbasin as well as the grain.

Reformed

Hospitality is complete: men and donkeys. The house of the lord of Egypt serves Hebrews.

Provision precedes the noon revealing-without-revealing. God waters them in the place they dreaded.

Genesis 43:25

25They prepared the present for Joseph’s coming at noon, for they heard that they should eat bread there.

Arminian

They ready the gift for noon, having heard they will eat bread. Fear shifts toward protocol.

They still try to manage the man with Canaan’s produce. The table will out-give the present.

Reformed

Noon meal announced; the present is staged. Bread in Egypt is the famine’s answer, served by the brother they sold.

They prepare a gift for the one who has already prepared a slaughter.

Genesis 43:26

26When Joseph came home, they brought him the present which was in their hand into the house, and bowed themselves down to the earth before him.

Arminian

Present offered, faces to the ground. The sheaf-dream of 37:7 takes posture without their knowing.

Wesleyan Arminians see responsible homage to a ruler—and a hidden fulfillment. Bowing is their act; God had told the story years prior.

Reformed

Bowing to the ground is the dream made flesh. Joseph comes home; they fall.

Not one extra miracle is needed. Ordinary protocol completes what God showed a seventeen-year-old.

Genesis 43:27

27He asked them of their welfare, and said, “Is your father well, the old man of whom you spoke? Is he yet alive?”

Arminian

Welfare, father, still alive: the ruler’s questions are a son’s. He asks what his heart already aches to know.

Pastorally: power can still ask tenderly. Joseph chooses inquiry over unmasking—for now.

Reformed

Shalom of the old man: Joseph’s first concern after the bow is Jacob. “Still alive” is the question of 45:3 already burning.

Providence has kept the father through famine so this question can be asked in a palace.

Genesis 43:28

28They said, “Your servant, our father, is well. He is still alive.” They bowed down humbly.

Arminian

Alive and well, they say, and bow again. Double prostration underlines both courtesy and the dream.

They honor the man and unwittingly honor the brother. Truth about Jacob is spoken; the larger truth waits.

Reformed

Servant-father, still alive, second bow. The stars of 37:9 keep dipping.

God extracts worship-shaped homage from men who once said, “We shall see what will become of his dreams.”

Genesis 43:29

29He lifted up his eyes, and saw Benjamin, his brother, his mother’s son, and said, “Is this your youngest brother, of whom you spoke to me?” He said, “God be gracious to you, my son.”

Arminian

Eyes lifted: Benjamin, mother’s son. Blessing falls—“God be gracious to you, my son.” Joseph’s first public word to him is grace.

The full brother is not a tool in a test only. He is blessed. Grace can be spoken before identity is disclosed.

Reformed

Mother’s son named by the narrator so we feel the Rachel-ache. “My son” from the unrecognized brother is almost too much theology for the room.

The blessing Joseph speaks is the blessing God has already been to this house in famine.

Genesis 43:30

30Joseph hurried, for his heart yearned over his brother; and he sought a place to weep. He entered into his room, and wept there.

Arminian

Compassion grows warm; he hurries to a chamber to weep. Power does not freeze feeling. He seeks a place rather than break in front of them.

Joseph has a heart, and it breaks into tears. Joseph is not a machine of providence. He must still govern when he returns (v. 31).

Reformed

Joseph’s compassion is warm, and his weeping is hidden. The man God sent ahead to preserve life (45:5) is not stoic.

Providence includes a brother’s tears. The chamber is mercy for Joseph and delay for the test still to come.

Key contrast Both refuse a cold Joseph. Arminians underline his responsible, feeling agency in stepping away to weep; Reformed readers emphasize that even these tears belong inside the compassion God has been working for the family’s preservation.

Genesis 43:31

31He washed his face, and came out. He controlled himself, and said, “Serve the meal.”

Arminian

Washed face, self-control, serve the food. He returns to the table rather than to revenge or to untimely revealing.

Governing one’s spirit is an act. The meal is chosen. Confession can wait until the test of chapter 44 is ripe.

Reformed

Control is named. The same Joseph who wept now orders lunch.

Timing of disclosure is in God’s hand through Joseph’s restraint. Food comes first; the revelation of who he is must wait.

Genesis 43:32

32They served him by himself, and them by themselves, and the Egyptians who ate with him by themselves, because the Egyptians don’t eat with the Hebrews, for that is an abomination to the Egyptians.

Arminian

Three tables: Joseph, Hebrews, Egyptians. Abomination fences the meal. He is still hidden—too Hebrew to sit with them as brother, too Egyptian to sit with them as kin.

Cultural walls are authentic. God is feeding them across a barrier they cannot yet cross.

Reformed

Segregated service keeps the secret. The abomination is Egypt’s; the irony is heaven’s: the Hebrew lord cannot eat with Hebrews as their brother yet.

Even the seating chart serves concealment until Judah’s later plea.

Genesis 43:33

33They sat before him, the firstborn according to his birthright, and the youngest according to his youth, and the men marveled with one another.

Arminian

Birthright to youth: he seats them in exact order. Amazement is the right response. How does this Egyptian know their ages?

The sign is offered to minds that can wonder. They are responsible to notice that they are known.

Reformed

The seating is omniscience-by-brotherhood. Firstborn to youngest, no mistakes.

God lets Joseph play the part of a knowing lord because he is one. Amazement is almost recognition, not quite.

Genesis 43:34

34He sent portions to them from before him, but Benjamin’s portion was five times as much as any of theirs. They drank, and were merry with him.

Arminian

Fivefold portion for Benjamin—the old favoritism restaged as a test. Will jealousy flare as in chapter 37? They drink and are merry instead.

Arminian readers see a moral exam at the table. Grace has been working if they can watch Benjamin honored and still feast in peace.

Reformed

Five times as much: Joseph probes the old wound with generosity. Merriment is the surprising fruit.

God is already softening the men who once could not speak peace to Joseph. The cup of chapter 44 will test whether the merriment holds when Benjamin is threatened.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Classic comment on Simeon restored, Benjamin’s portion, and bows that fulfill the dream, from both sides of the debate.

Arminian Notable voices

“Your God, and the God of your father, has given you treasure in your sacks—Hereby he shews that he had no suspicion of dishonesty in them: for what we get by deceit we cannot say God gives it us. He silences their farther enquiry about it: ask not how it came thither, providence brought it you, and let that satisfy you. It appears by what he said, that by his master's instructions he was brought to the knowledge of the true God, the God of the Hebrews.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 43:23)

Reformed Notable voices

“Here we perceive the fraternal disposition of Joseph; though it is uncertain whether he was perfectly reconciled, as I will shortly show, in its proper place. If, however, remembering the injury, he loved his brethren less than before, he was still far from having vindictive feelings towards them. But because it was something suspicious that foreigners and men of ignoble rank should be received in a friendly manner, like known guests, to a banquet, by the chief governor of the kingdom, the sons of Jacob would conceive a new fear; namely, that he wished to cast them all into chains; and that their money had been craftily concealed in their sacks, in order that it might prove the occasion of accusation against them.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 43:16)
