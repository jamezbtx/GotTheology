Passage

Genesis 43

Verses 15–34 · ESV · Voices

The brothers stand before Joseph with Benjamin, gifts, and double money. Fear at the house meets the steward’s “Peace,” Simeon’s return, and a noon meal: bows that fulfill the old dreams, hidden weeping for Rachel’s son, seats by birth order, and Benjamin’s fivefold portion.

Genesis 43:15

15So the men took this present, and they took double the money with them, and Benjamin. They arose and went down to Egypt and stood before Joseph.

Arminian

Present, double money, Benjamin: they actually go. Standing before Joseph is obedience with the boy in tow.

Arminian note: the journey is a chosen act under pressure. They keep the father’s charge; they still do not know whose face they face.

Reformed

The caravan God wanted is complete. They stand before the brother whose dreams they once hated.

Reformed accent: “stood before Joseph” is providence’s punch line. Egypt is not a detour; it is the table being set.

Genesis 43:16

16When Joseph saw Benjamin with them, he said to the steward of his house, “Bring the men into the house, and slaughter an animal and make ready, for the men are to dine with me at noon.”

Arminian

Seeing Benjamin unlocks hospitality: slaughter, make ready, noon. The test is not over, but kindness begins.

Arminian pastoral: Joseph’s sight of the full brother moves him toward table, not toward revenge. He remains responsible for how he uses power.

Reformed

Benjamin in view, the house is opened. A feast is commanded before the brothers understand they are guests, not defendants.

Reformed note: the unrecognized lord prepares a meal the dreams implied. Compassion is already governing the timetable.

Genesis 43:17

17The man did as Joseph told him and brought the men to Joseph’s house.

Arminian

The steward obeys; they are brought in. Location change is enough to rattle them.

Arminian reading: servants can keep faith with a master’s word. The brothers’ next fear will be their interpretation, not the steward’s malice.

Reformed

As told, to the house. Joseph’s will moves through a steward the way God’s will is moving through Joseph.

Reformed accent: they enter a house prepared for them before they asked to enter.

Genesis 43:18

18And the men were afraid because they were brought to Joseph’s house, and they said, “It is because of the money, which was replaced in our sacks the first time, that we are brought in, so that he may assault us and fall upon us to make us servants and seize our donkeys.”

Arminian

Fear writes a worst-case script: assault, slavery, seized donkeys. Guilt from chapter 37 still hears every kindness as a trap.

Classical Arminians do not mock the fear. Conscience can misread mercy. They remain free to seek the steward rather than bolt.

Reformed

Brought to the house, they invent a prosecution. The money Joseph hid becomes, in their minds, a pretext for bondage.

Reformed irony: the men who sold a brother into slavery now fear being made servants. Providence lets old sin interpret new grace—until peace is spoken.

Genesis 43:19

19So they went up to the steward of Joseph’s house and spoke with him at the door of the house,

Arminian

They go to the door, not past it in panic. Speech before entry is wisdom.

Arminian note: responsible fear talks. They try to clear the ledger before sitting down.

Reformed

The door is the place of confession. They intercept the steward with the story of the silver.

Reformed reading: God has a word of peace waiting on the other side of this conversation (v. 23).

Genesis 43:20

20and said, “Oh, my lord, we came down the first time to buy food.

Arminian

Oh, my lord: they state the innocent purpose of trip one. Buying food, not spying, remains their claim.

Arminian emphasis: they keep telling the truth they have. Integrity is still their assignment, even when the whole truth about Joseph is hidden.

Reformed

The first descent is named as commerce. They do not know they are explaining themselves to their brother’s household.

Reformed note: “buy food” is accurate and small. God is doing more than a grain deal in this doorway.

Genesis 43:21

21And when we came to the lodging place we opened our sacks, and there was each man’s money in the mouth of his sack, our money in full weight. So we have brought it again with us,

Arminian

Full weight, brought again. They volunteer the returned silver before they are charged.

Arminian pastoral: restitution without a subpoena. Grace-enabled honesty carries the money back even when they did not take it.

Reformed

Lodging-place discovery, full weight, returned. The narrator already showed Joseph ordered this.

Reformed accent: they repay a gift they cannot explain. God is teaching them to handle silver without treachery.

Genesis 43:22

22and we have brought other money down with us to buy food. We do not know who put our money in our sacks.”

Arminian

Other money for food, plus ignorance about the sacks. They will not claim a miracle they do not understand.

Arminian note: “we do not know” is humble speech. Mystery is allowed; theft is denied.

Reformed

Double payment plus confessed ignorance. The hidden actor is Joseph—and behind him, God.

Reformed reading: not knowing is the right posture. The One who put the money there will name Himself later.

Genesis 43:23

23He replied, “Peace to you, do not be afraid. Your God and the God of your father has put treasure in your sacks for you. I received your money.” Then he brought Simeon out to them.

Arminian

Peace, do not be afraid. The steward theologies the silver: your God and your father’s God put treasure there. Simeon is produced alive.

Classical Arminians hear a Gentile mouth speaking covenant comfort. Mercy is announced as gift, not oversight. They may receive peace rather than keep rehearsing doom.

Reformed

Shalom before the meal. Treasure attributed to their God—Joseph’s household knows the Name the brothers fear. Simeon’s appearance seals the word.

Reformed comfort: God put the treasure there, through Joseph’s command. The steward becomes an unexpected preacher of providence.

Key contrast Both take “your God…has put treasure” as true theology from an unlikely speaker. Arminians typically stress peace sincerely offered for them to receive; Reformed readers typically stress that God actually placed the silver by His own hand, using Joseph.

Genesis 43:24

24And when the man had brought the men into Joseph’s house and given them water, and they had washed their feet, and when he had given their donkeys fodder,

Arminian

Water, feet, fodder: ordinary mercy after terror. Guests are treated as guests.

Arminian pastoral: small kindnesses train fearful hearts. Receive the washbasin as well as the grain.

Reformed

Hospitality is complete: men and donkeys. The house of the lord of Egypt serves Hebrews.

Reformed note: provision precedes the noon revealing-without-revealing. God waters them in the place they dreaded.

Genesis 43:25

25they prepared the present for Joseph’s coming at noon, for they heard that they should eat bread there.

Arminian

They ready the gift for noon, having heard they will eat bread. Fear shifts toward protocol.

Arminian note: they still try to manage the man with Canaan’s produce. The table will out-give the present.

Reformed

Noon meal announced; the present is staged. Bread in Egypt is the famine’s answer, served by the brother they sold.

Reformed accent: they prepare a gift for the one who has already prepared a slaughter.

Genesis 43:26

26When Joseph came home, they brought into the house to him the present that they had with them and bowed down to him to the ground.

Arminian

Present offered, faces to the ground. The sheaf-dream of 37:7 takes posture without their knowing.

Classical Arminians see responsible homage to a ruler—and a hidden fulfillment. Bowing is their act; God had told the story years prior.

Reformed

Bowing to the ground is the dream made flesh. Joseph comes home; they fall.

Reformed wonder: not one extra miracle is needed. Ordinary protocol completes what God showed a seventeen-year-old.

Genesis 43:27

27And he inquired about their welfare and said, “Is your father well, the old man of whom you spoke? Is he still alive?”

Arminian

Welfare, father, still alive: the ruler’s questions are a son’s. He asks what his heart already aches to know.

Arminian pastoral: power can still ask tenderly. Joseph chooses inquiry over unmasking—for now.

Reformed

Shalom of the old man: Joseph’s first concern after the bow is Jacob. “Still alive” is the question of 45:3 already burning.

Reformed note: providence has kept the father through famine so this question can be asked in a palace.

Genesis 43:28

28They said, “Your servant our father is well; he is still alive.” And they bowed their heads and prostrated themselves.

Arminian

Alive and well, they say, and bow again. Double prostration underlines both courtesy and the dream.

Arminian reading: they honor the man and unwittingly honor the brother. Truth about Jacob is spoken; the larger truth waits.

Reformed

Servant-father, still alive, second bow. The stars of 37:9 keep dipping.

Reformed accent: God extracts worship-shaped homage from men who once said, “We shall see what will become of his dreams.”

Genesis 43:29

29And he lifted up his eyes and saw his brother Benjamin, his mother’s son, and said, “Is this your youngest brother, of whom you spoke to me? God be gracious to you, my son!”

Arminian

Eyes lifted: Benjamin, mother’s son. Blessing falls—“God be gracious to you, my son.” Joseph’s first public word to him is grace.

Arminian warmth: the full brother is not a tool in a test only. He is blessed. Grace can be spoken before identity is disclosed.

Reformed

Mother’s son named by the narrator so we feel the Rachel-ache. “My son” from the unrecognized brother is almost too much theology for the room.

Reformed comfort: the blessing Joseph speaks is the blessing God has already been to this house in famine.

Genesis 43:30

30Then Joseph hurried out, for his compassion grew warm for his brother, and he sought a place to weep. And he entered his chamber and wept there.

Arminian

Compassion grows warm; he hurries to a chamber to weep. Power does not freeze feeling. He seeks a place rather than break in front of them.

Classical Arminians linger here: a real heart, real tears. Joseph is not a machine of providence. He must still govern when he returns (v. 31).

Reformed

Warm compassion, hidden weeping. The man God sent ahead to preserve life (45:5) is not stoic.

Reformed reading: providence includes a brother’s tears. The chamber is mercy for Joseph and delay for the test still to come.

Key contrast Both refuse a cold Joseph. Arminians typically stress his responsible, feeling agency in stepping away to weep; Reformed readers typically stress that even these tears belong inside the compassion God has been working for the family’s preservation.

Genesis 43:31

31Then he washed his face and came out. And controlling himself he said, “Serve the food.”

Arminian

Washed face, self-control, serve the food. He returns to the table rather than to revenge or to untimely revealing.

Arminian note: governing one’s spirit is a real act. The meal is chosen. Confession can wait until the test of chapter 44 is ripe.

Reformed

Control is named. The same Joseph who wept now orders lunch.

Reformed accent: timing of disclosure is in God’s hand through Joseph’s restraint. Food first; identity later.

Genesis 43:32

32They served him by himself, and them by themselves, and the Egyptians who ate with him by themselves, because the Egyptians could not eat with the Hebrews, for that is an abomination to the Egyptians.

Arminian

Three tables: Joseph, Hebrews, Egyptians. Abomination fences the meal. He is still hidden—too Hebrew to sit with them as brother, too Egyptian to sit with them as kin.

Arminian pastoral: cultural walls are real. God is feeding them across a barrier they cannot yet cross.

Reformed

Segregated service keeps the secret. The abomination is Egypt’s; the irony is heaven’s: the Hebrew lord cannot eat with Hebrews as their brother yet.

Reformed note: even the seating chart serves concealment until Judah’s later plea.

Genesis 43:33

33And they sat before him, the firstborn according to his birthright and the youngest according to his youth. And the men looked at one another in amazement.

Arminian

Birthright to youth: he seats them in exact order. Amazement is the right response. How does this Egyptian know their ages?

Arminian reading: the sign is offered to minds that can wonder. They are responsible to notice that they are known.

Reformed

The seating is omniscience-by-brotherhood. Firstborn to youngest, no mistakes.

Reformed wonder: God lets Joseph play the part of a knowing lord because he is one. Amazement is almost recognition, not quite.

Genesis 43:34

34Portions were taken to them from Joseph’s table, but Benjamin’s portion was five times as much as any of theirs. And they drank and were merry with him.

Arminian

Fivefold portion for Benjamin—the old favoritism restaged as a test. Will jealousy flare as in chapter 37? They drink and are merry instead.

Classical Arminians see a real moral exam at the table. Grace has been working if they can watch Benjamin honored and still feast in peace.

Reformed

Five times as much: Joseph probes the old wound with generosity. Merriment is the surprising fruit.

Reformed accent: God is already softening the men who once could not speak peace to Joseph. The cup of chapter 44 will test whether the merriment holds when Benjamin is threatened.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Simeon restored, Benjamin’s portion, and bows that fulfill the dream—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 43:15–34.

Arminian Notable voices

“Your God, and the God of your father, has given you treasure in your sacks—Hereby he shews that he had no suspicion of dishonesty in them: for what we get by deceit we cannot say God gives it us. He silences their farther enquiry about it: ask not how it came thither, providence brought it you, and let that satisfy you. It appears by what he said, that by his master's instructions he was brought to the knowledge of the true God, the God of the Hebrews.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 43:23)

Reformed Notable voices

“Here we perceive the fraternal disposition of Joseph; though it is uncertain whether he was perfectly reconciled, as I will shortly show, in its proper place. If, however, remembering the injury, he loved his brethren less than before, he was still far from having vindictive feelings towards them. But because it was something suspicious that foreigners and men of ignoble rank should be received in a friendly manner, like known guests, to a banquet, by the chief governor of the kingdom, the sons of Jacob would conceive a new fear; namely, that he wished to cast them all into chains; and that their money had been craftily concealed in their sacks, in order that it might prove the occasion of accusation against them.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 43:16)
