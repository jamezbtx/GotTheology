Passage

Genesis 43

Verses 1–14 · ESV · Voices

The famine bites again. Judah refuses a second trip without Benjamin, pledges himself as surety, and Israel yields: gifts, double money, the boy, and a prayer to God Almighty for mercy before the man—plus a bereaved father’s surrender.

Genesis 43:1

1Now the famine was severe in the land.

Arminian

Severity, not a passing shortage. Canaan’s need pushes the stalled household back toward Egypt.

Arminian note: hardship is real and morally weighty. Hunger will press choices; it does not erase them. The brothers must still decide how to go.

Reformed

The famine Joseph interpreted now presses Jacob’s tent. Severity is providence, not accident.

Reformed accent: God uses empty jars to move the sons toward the brother they sold. The land’s pain serves the covenant’s path to Egypt.

Genesis 43:2

2And when they had eaten the grain that they had brought from Egypt, their father said to them, “Go again, buy us a little food.”

Arminian

The first load is gone. Israel says go again, as if the man in Egypt had not named Benjamin. Need speaks; memory lags.

Classical Arminians hear a father’s responsible care and a real delay. He still hopes to feed the house without sending the remaining son of Rachel.

Reformed

Empty sacks reopen the file. “A little food” understates what the journey will cost.

Reformed reading: the grain God stored in Egypt is meant to draw them down again. The father’s short request sits inside a longer decree.

Genesis 43:3

3But Judah said to him, “The man solemnly warned us, saying, ‘You shall not see my face unless your brother is with you.’

Arminian

Judah reports the solemn warning. No face, no grain—without Benjamin. He will not pretend the governor’s word was a suggestion.

Arminian pastoral: truth-telling here is costly honesty. Judah names the condition rather than managing the father with a softer lie.

Reformed

“Solemnly warned” is legal weight. Joseph’s face is the gate of life for this family, and he has locked it to Benjamin’s presence.

Reformed note: the unnamed “man” is the brother they do not recognize. Providence speaks through a warning they must take at face value.

Genesis 43:4

4If you will send our brother with us, we will go down and buy you food.

Arminian

If you send, we will go. Judah puts the decision in Israel’s hands without stealing it. Food is tied to a father’s yes.

Arminian emphasis: real condition, real obedience offered. They will not go as rebels; they wait for send-authority they can then own.

Reformed

The if-clause is household order. Judah will not freelance the heir of Rachel south.

Reformed accent: God will get Benjamin to Egypt, and He will do it through this father’s reluctant sending, not around it.

Genesis 43:5

5But if you will not send him, we will not go down, for the man said to us, ‘You shall not see my face, unless your brother is with you.’

Arminian

The negative is as clear as the offer. No send, no descent. Repeating the man’s word keeps the family from a doomed trip.

Classical Arminians honor this as responsible refusal. Going without Benjamin would be theater, not faith.

Reformed

Refusal to go is obedience to the governor’s oath, not mutiny against Jacob.

Reformed reading: the door Joseph shut is the door God is using. The brothers cannot work around the test.

Genesis 43:6

6Israel said, “Why did you treat me so badly as to tell the man that you had another brother?”

Arminian

Pain speaks as blame. Israel hears the disclosure as harm done to him. Grief still names Joseph “no more” in the background.

Arminian pastoral: wounded parents can accuse the messengers of providence. The question is unfair and human; grace will yet hold this house together.

Reformed

“Treat me so badly” locates the wound in the father’s chest. He cannot yet see that telling the truth was the only path to Simeon and to bread.

Reformed note: God is not the accused, but He is not absent. The badly-felt disclosure is how the dreams of chapter 37 keep coming true.

Genesis 43:7

7They replied, “The man questioned us carefully about ourselves and our kindred, saying, ‘Is your father still alive? Do you have another brother?’ What we told him was in answer to these questions. Could we in any way know that he would say, ‘Bring your brother down’?”

Arminian

They answer: we were questioned; we told the truth; we could not foresee the demand. Agency and ignorance sit together.

Arminian note: honest speech can still open a door you did not choose. They remain responsible for truth-telling, not for the governor’s next move.

Reformed

Careful questions from Joseph pull the family map into the open. They could not know—and God did.

Reformed accent: the unrecognized brother scripts the interrogation. Providence uses ordinary Q-and-A to fetch Benjamin without the brothers grasping why.

Genesis 43:8

8And Judah said to Israel his father, “Send the boy with me, and we will arise and go, that we may live and not die, both we and you and also our little ones.

Arminian

Judah takes the lead Reuben failed. Send the boy with me—that we may live, including little ones. The plea is for the whole house, not for Judah’s honor.

Classical Arminians hear a grace-enabled turn: the brother who once sold Joseph now stakes life on bringing Rachel’s other son home.

Reformed

Judah, not Reuben, becomes the speaker of life-and-death. “Little ones” widens the stake to the next generation of promise.

Reformed comfort: the lion-line begins to look like a pledge rather than a seller of brothers. God is reshaping Judah under famine.

Genesis 43:9

9I will be a pledge of his safety. From my hand you shall require him. If I do not bring him back to you and set him before you, then let me bear the blame forever.

Arminian

Surety language: from my hand, blame forever. Judah offers himself in Benjamin’s place if the boy is lost. This is not cheap talk; chapter 44 will spend it.

Arminian reading: substitution offered is a real moral act. Grace can turn a guilty man into a pledge. He must still keep the word.

Reformed

Pledge, require, blame forever: Judah becomes surety. The future king-tribe learns substitution under pressure.

Reformed instinct: this vow foreshadows a greater Brother who will not fail the Father’s charge. Here it is still a man’s oath; God will use it to break Joseph’s test.

Key contrast Both honor Judah’s surety as a turn from chapter 37. Arminians typically stress a responsible, grace-enabled pledge he must keep; Reformed readers typically stress God’s reshaping of Judah toward substitution that serves the larger preservation of the seed.

Genesis 43:10

10If we had not delayed, we would now have returned twice.”

Arminian

Delay has a cost: two trips lost. Judah names the father’s hesitation without sneering.

Arminian pastoral: waiting in fear is still a choice with consequences. Mercy remains; the calendar of hunger does not.

Reformed

Twice-returned is the road not taken. Delay was Jacob’s, and it has lengthened Simeon’s custody.

Reformed note: even wasted time sits inside the famine God timed. The second trip will still land on Joseph’s noon meal.

Genesis 43:11

11Then their father Israel said to them, “If it must be so, then do this: take some of the choice fruits of the land in your bags, and carry a present down to the man, a little balm and a little honey, gum, myrrh, pistachio nuts, and almonds.

Arminian

If it must be so: Israel yields. Gifts from a starving land—balm, honey, gum, myrrh, nuts—are the old Jacob, still trying to soften a face with presents.

Arminian note: yielding is not unbelief’s opposite. He sends what he can. The present is a father’s responsible attempt, not a bribe that replaces prayer (v. 14).

Reformed

Israel, the covenant name, now speaks. The present is thin luxury from a famine country—dignity offered to an unknown lord.

Reformed reading: God lets Jacob be Jacob, gifts and all, while the true provision is already stored in Egypt. The man will not be bought; he will weep.

Genesis 43:12

12Take double the money with you. Carry back with you the money that was returned in the mouth of your sacks. Perhaps it was an oversight.

Arminian

Double money, and the returned silver carried back. “Perhaps it was an oversight” is honesty hoping for a clean ledger.

Classical Arminians honor restitution. They will not keep what landed in the sacks. Integrity is a grace-enabled duty even when they did not steal.

Reformed

Oversight is Jacob’s best guess. The reader knows Joseph ordered the money back.

Reformed accent: they return silver God used to frighten them (42:28). Providence can hide a gift inside what feels like a trap.

Genesis 43:13

13Take also your brother, and arise, go again to the man.

Arminian

Take also your brother. The sentence Israel would not speak in 42:38 now comes out. Arise, go again.

Arminian pastoral: hard obedience can be late and still real. Sending Benjamin is trust under protest, not enthusiasm—and it is still a yes.

Reformed

The boy is released by the father’s mouth. “Go again to the man” aims them at Joseph unawares.

Reformed comfort: the heir of Rachel moves toward Egypt because Israel finally speaks the word God has been waiting to use.

Genesis 43:14

14May God Almighty grant you mercy before the man, and may he send back your other brother and Benjamin. And as for me, if I am bereaved of my children, I am bereaved.”

Arminian

El Shaddai is named: grant mercy before the man; send back Simeon and Benjamin. Then the wounded surrender—“if I am bereaved, I am bereaved.” Prayer and lament share a verse.

Classical Arminians hear a real petition. Mercy is asked because mercy can be granted. Israel does not fatalize God; he asks, then yields the children he cannot keep by clutching.

Reformed

God Almighty—the name of chapter 17—is invoked over an Egyptian doorway. Mercy before “the man” is the right request; the man is already Joseph.

Reformed reading: the bereaved clause is not unbelief’s last word but a saint’s emptying. God will answer beyond the prayer: not only two brothers, but Joseph himself.

Key contrast Both hear El Shaddai asked for mercy. Arminians typically stress a sincere petition that may be granted or refused; Reformed readers typically stress that the Almighty already has the mercy in hand, including the unrecognized brother who will show it.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Judah as surety, and Israel praying God Almighty for mercy—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 43:1–14.

Arminian Notable voices

“Judah's conscience had lately smitten him for what he had done a great while ago against Joseph; and as an evidence of the truth of his repentance, he is ready to undertake, as far as a man could do it, for Benjamin's security. He will not only not wrong him but will do all he can to protect him. This is such restitution as the case will admit: when he knew not how he could retrieve Joseph, he would make some amends for the irreparable injury he had done him, by doubling his care concerning Benjamin.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 43:9)

Reformed Notable voices

“It may, however, here be asked, how Jacob could have supported his family, even for a few days, with so small a quantity of corn: for, suppose it to be granted that several asses were conducted by each of the brethren, what was this to sustain three hundred persons? For, since Abraham had a much larger number of servants, and mention has been made above of the servants of Isaac; it is incredible that Jacob was so entirely destitute, as to have no servants left.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 43:1)
