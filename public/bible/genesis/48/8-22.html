Passage

Genesis 48

Verses 8–22 · ESV

Dim-eyed Israel blesses Joseph’s sons, crossing his hands so the right rests on Ephraim the younger. Joseph protests firstborn custom; Jacob refuses: “I know, my son, I know.” The blessing names God as shepherd and redeeming angel, sets Ephraim before Manasseh, and pledges return to the fathers’ land.

Genesis 48:8

8When Israel saw Joseph's sons, he said, "Who are these?"

Arminian

He sees yet asks, “Who are these?” Dim sight still wants names spoken.

Arminian pastoral: recognition can be a question. Grace lets the father hear Joseph identify the boys before he blesses them.

Reformed

Israel “saw” and still inquired. The blessing will not be anonymous.

Reformed note: God often has His servants ask what they partly see. The question opens the rite.

Genesis 48:9

9Joseph said to his father, "They are my sons, whom God has given me here." And he said, "Bring them to me, please, that I may bless them."

Arminian

“Whom God has given me here”—Egypt’s gift named as God’s. Jacob asks them brought for blessing.

Classical Arminians love the confession. Children are received, not seized; the blessing is requested, not presumed.

Reformed

Joseph traces the boys to God’s giving in this place. Jacob answers with a please and a purpose: bless them.

Reformed accent: fruit of the womb is gift. The patriarch will now spend what God deposited in him.

Genesis 48:10

10Now the eyes of Israel were dim with age, so that he could not see. So Joseph brought them near him, and he kissed them and embraced them.

Arminian

Eyes dim; kiss and embrace still find them. Nearness heals what sight cannot.

Arminian note: bodily weakness does not cancel love. Joseph helps; Jacob holds. Means and affection work together.

Reformed

Blindness like Isaac’s—yet this time the hands will not be fooled.

Reformed irony of mercy: dim eyes, clear blessing. God can guide crossed hands when sight fails.

Genesis 48:11

11And Israel said to Joseph, "I never expected to see your face; and behold, God has let me see your offspring also."

Arminian

He had not expected Joseph’s face; God has shown offspring too. Mercy outruns hope.

Arminian pastoral: grace often exceeds what we dared ask. The same God who restored a son now shows grandsons.

Reformed

“Behold, God has let me see.” The unexpected is credited to God, not to luck or Egypt.

Reformed comfort: providence overshot Jacob’s expectation. The lost son and the double seed are the Lord’s showing.

Genesis 48:12

12Then Joseph removed them from his knees, and he bowed himself with his face to the earth.

Arminian

From the knees, then Joseph bows to the earth. The vizier honors the dying shepherd.

Classical Arminians see fitting reverence, not groveling for spectacle. A son’s bow is owed; the next act is the blessing he came for.

Reformed

The boys leave Jacob’s lap; Joseph’s face meets the ground.

Reformed reading: Egypt’s lord becomes a worshiper at Israel’s bed. Rank yields to the prophet of the covenant.

Genesis 48:13

13And Joseph took them both, Ephraim in his right hand toward Israel's left hand, and Manasseh in his left hand toward Israel's right hand, and brought them near him.

Arminian

Joseph arranges them for custom: Manasseh to Jacob’s right, Ephraim to the left. He wants the firstborn under the better hand.

Arminian note: the setup is sincere and almost right. Nature’s order is not wicked; it is about to be overruled.

Reformed

Positions are carefully set so the right hand will find Manasseh. Joseph catechizes by choreography.

Reformed accent: human arranging is real—and not final. The next verse will cross what Joseph lined up.

Genesis 48:14

14And Israel stretched out his right hand and laid it on the head of Ephraim, who was the younger, and his left hand on the head of Manasseh, crossing his hands (for Manasseh was the firstborn).

Arminian

Right hand on the younger, hands crossed, firstborn named so we cannot miss it. Wesley: not mistake, humor, or partiality, but a spirit of prophecy.

Classical Arminians add Clarke’s caution: national and temporal preferments sit in God’s wise counsel; converting grace is still mercy offered with sufficiency to every man. The crossing is God’s pleasure, not a cartoon of a closed door.

Reformed

He guides his hands wittingly. Calvin: the Holy Spirit directed the act; Jacob was clearer-sighted in crossing arms than Joseph with open eyes.

Reformed confession: God is not bound to primogeniture. What He freely confers is of His own will; the crossed hands preach that liberty.

Key contrast Both deny accident and petty favoritism. Arminians typically hear prophetic insight and God’s freedom in temporal gifts, while insisting converting grace is sincerely offered; Reformed readers typically hear the Spirit directing a sign of free election not bound to nature’s order.

Genesis 48:15

15And he blessed Joseph and said, "The God before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac walked, the God who has been my shepherd all my life long to this day,

Arminian

The blessing of the boys is first a blessing of Joseph, and first a naming of God: walked-before, shepherd all my life.

Arminian warmth: a lifetime of being shepherded is testimony, not theory. Dying faith recites a God who can still be trusted for the lads.

Reformed

God of the fathers, shepherd of this man. The covenant Name is the fountain of the hands.

Reformed note: Jacob arrogates nothing. He blesses as a minister; the Shepherd is the source of the gift.

Genesis 48:16

16the angel who has redeemed me from all evil, bless the boys; and in them let my name be carried on, and the name of my fathers Abraham and Isaac; and let them grow into a multitude in the midst of the earth."

Arminian

The Angel who redeemed from all evil is asked to bless the boys. Clarke hears the Goel—Christ the redeeming Kinsman—invoked, not a mere creature.

Classical Arminians pray this over children with confidence: the Redeemer who kept Jacob can keep them. Name and multitude are sought from Him.

Reformed

Calvin: the Angel is joined to God as equal—Christ the Mediator, already the bond between God and man.

Reformed gospel: redemption from all evil is this Angel’s office. The name of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob is laid on the boys because God Himself blesses.

Genesis 48:17

17When Joseph saw that his father laid his right hand on the head of Ephraim, it displeased him, and he took his father's hand to move it from Ephraim's head to Manasseh's head.

Arminian

It displeased him; he takes the hand to move it. Love of the firstborn is understandable and still mistaken.

Arminian pastoral: good men can resist a strange mercy. Joseph is not mocked; he is corrected. Nature’s ranking is not the last word.

Reformed

Joseph binds grace to birth order and tries to fix the rite. Calvin: he errs in chaining God’s gift to nature’s custom.

Reformed reading: even holy affection can fight election’s sign. The hand will not stay where flesh prefers.

Genesis 48:18

18And Joseph said to his father, "Not this way, my father; since this one is the firstborn, put your right hand on his head."

Arminian

“Not this way”—the firstborn argument, spoken with honor. Joseph catechizes his father in the usual rule.

Classical Arminians grant the rule’s ordinary justice. Jacob will not deny Manasseh’s greatness; he will deny that custom owns God’s right hand.

Reformed

Firstborn is Joseph’s axiom. He would put the right hand back on Manasseh.

Reformed note: the protest is filial and fleshly at once. God often teaches by refusing the axiom.

Genesis 48:19

19But his father refused and said, "I know, my son, I know. He also shall become a people, and he also shall be great. Nevertheless, his younger brother shall be greater than he, and his offspring shall become a multitude of nations."

Arminian

“I know, my son, I know.” Manasseh will be a people and great; Ephraim greater still. Wesley: God often gives most to those least likely.

Arminian balance (Clarke): temporal and national preferment may rest in secret counsel; that is not the same as saying converting grace is withheld from some by an arbitrary shut door. Both boys are blessed; the difference is degree of public greatness.

Reformed

He refuses. Calvin: Jacob does not weigh worth; he pronounces what God decreed. Dignity is hidden in God’s counsel until vocation makes it plain.

Reformed confession: seeking other cisterns for adoption’s cause is vain. The primary source of election is God’s free good pleasure—and Manasseh is still called great, so envy is forbidden.

Key contrast Both hear a true prophetic refusal, not a slip. Arminians typically keep Manasseh’s real greatness and a sincere gospel offer in view while granting God’s freedom in public gifts; Reformed readers typically hear free election announced, causes of adoption located in God’s good pleasure alone.

Genesis 48:20

20So he blessed them that day, saying, "By you Israel will pronounce blessings, saying, 'God make you as Ephraim and as Manasseh.'" Thus he put Ephraim before Manasseh.

Arminian

The formula of later Israel: God make you as Ephraim and as Manasseh. Both names stay in the blessing; Ephraim is put first.

Arminian hope: the proverb is gift for the whole people. Preference is real; exclusion of Manasseh is not.

Reformed

That day he sets the order: Ephraim before Manasseh. Israel will bless by their names.

Reformed note: the public proverb seals what the hands did. Grace toward both is conspicuous so the lesser will not envy.

Genesis 48:21

21Then Israel said to Joseph, "Behold, I am about to die, but God will be with you and will bring you again to the land of your fathers."

Arminian

I die; God will be with you and bring you home. Presence outlasts the dying father.

Classical Arminians rest here pastorally: the God who shepherded Jacob still invites the family to trust the road back. The promise is sincere for those who go with Him.

Reformed

Death is named; God’s with-ness and the return are more certain than Jacob’s breath.

Reformed comfort: the covenant does not die with the patriarch. God will be with them and will bring them—the same Lord who crossed the hands.

Genesis 48:22

22Moreover, I have given to you rather than to your brothers one mountain slope that I took from the hand of the Amorites with my sword and with my bow."

Arminian

One extra portion (shekem) above the brothers—sword and bow. Joseph is honored without the others being unnamed in later blessings.

Arminian caution: extra portions can be just; they need not become a gospel of favorites. The land gift points home, not to Egypt’s palaces.

Reformed

A slope taken from Amorites is laid on Joseph. John 4 will still know the parcel.

Reformed reading: even the extra lot is under promise. Jacob gives what faith already counts as his in the land of the fathers.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jacob’s crossed-hand blessing of Ephraim and Manasseh—so each camp speaks in its own voice on grace, birth order, and why the younger is set first.

Arminian Notable voices

“Jacob gave him to understand that he knew what he did, and that he did it neither by mistake nor in a humour, nor from a partial affection to one more than the other, but from a spirit of prophecy. … God, in bestowing his blessings upon his people, gives more to some than to others. … Grace observes not the order of nature, nor doth God prefer those whom we think fittest to be preferred, but as it pleaseth him.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 48:16, 19)
“It is observable how God from the beginning has preferred the younger to the elder. … This is to be resolved entirely into the wise and secret counsel of God, so far as it regards temporal blessings and national privileges. … The grace that converts the soul certainly comes from the mere mercy of God, without any merit on man’s part; and a sufficiency of this is offered to every man. But it is not less certain that God loves those best who are most faithful to this grace.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Gen 48:14)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses says that he guided his hands wittingly, because he did not rashly put them forth. … Whence we gather that the Holy Spirit was the director of this act, who irradiated the mind of the holy man. … He errs in binding the grace of God to the accustomed order of nature: as if the Lord did not often purposely change the law of nature, to teach us that what he freely confers upon us is entirely the result of his own will.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 48:14, 17)
“Jacob does not dispute which of the youths shall be the more worthy; but only pronounces what God had decreed with himself, concerning each. … They are therefore altogether insane, who hew out dry and perforated cisterns, in seeking causes of divine adoption; whereas, everywhere, the Scripture defines in one word, that they are called to salvation whom God has chosen, and that the primary source of election is his free good pleasure.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 48:19)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
