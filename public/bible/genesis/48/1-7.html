Passage

Genesis 48

Verses 1–7 · ESV · Voices

Joseph hears that Jacob is ill and brings Manasseh and Ephraim. Israel sits up, recites El Shaddai’s Luz promise, and adopts the two Egyptian-born sons as his own—equal to Reuben and Simeon—then remembers Rachel’s burial on the way to Ephrath.

Genesis 48:1

1After this, Joseph was told, "Behold, your father is ill." So he took with him his two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim.

Arminian

News of illness sends Joseph with both sons. Filial duty and a father’s blessing meet on the same errand.

Arminian pastoral: grace does not skip ordinary love. Joseph acts; the boys are brought where the promise can be spoken over them.

Reformed

After this—sickness, then sons in tow. Joseph will not let Egypt keep the lads from Israel’s bed.

Reformed note: providence times the visit. The future tribes arrive while the patriarch can still sit up and speak.

Genesis 48:2

2And it was told to Jacob, "Your son Joseph has come to you." Then Israel summoned his strength and sat up in bed.

Arminian

Israel summons strength and sits up. Weak flesh still answers a son’s coming.

Classical Arminians honor responsible effort under mercy. He does what he can; God will put words of blessing in a raised body.

Reformed

Jacob becomes Israel in the verse: he strengthens himself for the office of blessing.

Reformed reading: the last acts of the covenant father are not leftover energy. God grants the sitting-up that the adoption requires.

Genesis 48:3

3And Jacob said to Joseph, "God Almighty appeared to me at Luz in the land of Canaan and blessed me,

Arminian

He begins not with Egypt’s honors but with Luz: God Almighty appeared and blessed. The old vision is still the ground.

Arminian note: a remembered appearance invites present trust. El Shaddai’s blessing is sincerely given and still claimed.

Reformed

El Shaddai at Luz (Bethel) is the charter. Jacob will adopt grandsons only from that word.

Reformed accent: the blessing is God’s appearing, not Jacob’s sentiment. What God said in Canaan now governs sons born in Egypt.

Genesis 48:4

4and said to me, 'Behold, I will make you fruitful and multiply you, and I will make of you a company of peoples and will give this land to your offspring after you for an everlasting possession.'

Arminian

Fruitful, a company of peoples, land as everlasting possession. The promise is large enough to include Joseph’s boys.

Classical Arminians hear a real pledge to offspring. Adoption in the next verse is how Jacob lets Egyptian-born sons inherit a Canaan word.

Reformed

Behold: God will make, multiply, give. The land remains the possession, even from an Egyptian sickbed.

Reformed comfort: everlasting here is God’s hold on the gift until Christ widens the inheritance. Jacob recites decree, then acts.

Genesis 48:5

5And now your two sons, who were born to you in the land of Egypt before I came to you in Egypt, are mine; Ephraim and Manasseh shall be mine, as Reuben and Simeon are.

Arminian

Born in Egypt, yet “mine”—as Reuben and Simeon. Adoption pulls them into Israel’s first rank.

Arminian pastoral: grace crosses a border. Place of birth does not bar the covenant; Jacob’s “are mine” is a real welcome they must still walk in.

Reformed

Two sons become two tribes by Jacob’s word under God’s promise. Ephraim is named before Manasseh already.

Reformed reading: this is insertion into the people of God, not a private fondness. The church’s future heads are claimed before the hands cross.

Key contrast Both receive the adoption as covenant mercy. Arminians typically stress a sincere welcome that Egyptian-born sons must still live in; Reformed readers typically stress God’s purpose inserting two tribes into Israel by the patriarch’s Spirit-led word.

Genesis 48:6

6And the children that you fathered after them shall be yours. They shall be called by the name of their brothers in their inheritance.

Arminian

Later children stay Joseph’s, named under the brothers’ inheritance. The double tribe is capped.

Arminian note: order in the household is pastoral wisdom. Grace makes room without erasing names or stirring a later contest.

Reformed

Only these two are lifted to tribal heads. Any further sons fold into Ephraim or Manasseh.

Reformed accent: the line is bounded as God wills. Inheritance follows the names Jacob has just claimed.

Genesis 48:7

7As for me, when I came from Paddan, to my sorrow Rachel died in the land of Canaan on the way, when there was still some distance to go to Ephrath, and I buried her there on the way to Ephrath (that is, Bethlehem)."

Arminian

Rachel’s death is still “to my sorrow,” buried on the way to Bethlehem. Love remembers at the edge of dying.

Classical Arminians hear grief that faith does not erase. Joseph’s mother is named so the sons know they belong to a story that includes loss, not only Egypt’s court.

Reformed

Paddan, sorrow, Ephrath, Bethlehem. The beloved wife’s grave marks Canaan as home even now.

Reformed note: providence kept the burial in the land of promise. Jacob’s ache sits inside the same God who will bless her grandsons.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on adopting Ephraim and Manasseh as Jacob’s own—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 48:1–7.

Arminian Notable voices

“God had promised him two things, a numerous issue, and Canaan for an inheritance. See how he blessed them by faith in that which God had said to him Heb 11:21.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 48:3)

Reformed Notable voices

“Jacob confers on his son the special privilege, that he, being one, should constitute two chiefs; that is, that his two sons should succeed to an equal right with their uncles, as if they had been heirs in the first degree. But what is this! that a decrepit old man assigns to his grandchildren, as a royal patrimony, a sixth part of the land in which he had entered as a stranger, and from which now again he is an exile!”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 48:5)
