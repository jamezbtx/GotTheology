Passage

Genesis 48

Verses 1–7 · WEB · Voices

Joseph hears that Jacob is ill and brings Manasseh and Ephraim. Israel sits up, recites El Shaddai’s Luz promise, and adopts the two Egyptian-born sons as his own—equal to Reuben and Simeon—then remembers Rachel’s burial on the way to Ephrath.

Genesis 48:1

1After these things, someone said to Joseph, “Behold, your father is sick.” He took with him his two sons, Manasseh and Ephraim.

Arminian

News of illness sends Joseph with both sons. Filial duty and a father’s blessing meet on the same errand.

Grace does not skip ordinary love. Joseph acts; the boys are brought where the promise can be spoken over them.

Reformed

After this—sickness, then sons in tow. Joseph will not let Egypt keep the lads from Israel’s bed.

God’s timing brings Joseph to his father’s bedside. The future tribes arrive while the patriarch can still sit up and speak.

Genesis 48:2

2Someone told Jacob, and said, “Behold, your son Joseph comes to you,” and Israel strengthened himself, and sat on the bed.

Arminian

Israel summons strength and sits up. Weak flesh still answers a son’s coming.

Arminians honor responsible effort under mercy. He does what he can; God will put words of blessing in a raised body.

Reformed

Jacob becomes Israel in the verse: he strengthens himself for the office of blessing.

The last acts of the covenant father are not leftover energy. God grants the sitting-up that the adoption requires.

Genesis 48:3

3Jacob said to Joseph, “God Almighty appeared to me at Luz in the land of Canaan, and blessed me,

Arminian

He begins not with Egypt’s honors but with Luz: God Almighty appeared and blessed. The old vision is still the ground.

A remembered appearance invites present trust. El Shaddai’s blessing is sincerely given and still claimed.

Reformed

El Shaddai at Luz (Bethel) is the charter. Jacob will adopt grandsons only from that word.

The blessing is God’s appearing, not Jacob’s sentiment. What God said in Canaan now governs sons born in Egypt.

Genesis 48:4

4and said to me, ‘Behold, I will make you fruitful, and multiply you, and I will make of you a company of peoples, and will give this land to your offspring after you for an everlasting possession.’

Arminian

The promise is large enough to include Joseph’s boys.

What comes through is a pledge to offspring. Adoption in the next verse is how Jacob lets Egyptian-born sons inherit a Canaan word.

Reformed

Behold: God will make, multiply, give. The land remains the possession, even from an Egyptian sickbed.

Everlasting here is God’s hold on the gift until Christ widens the inheritance. Jacob recites decree, then acts.

Genesis 48:5

5Now your two sons, who were born to you in the land of Egypt before I came to you into Egypt, are mine; Ephraim and Manasseh, even as Reuben and Simeon, will be mine.

Arminian

Born in Egypt, yet “mine”—as Reuben and Simeon. Adoption pulls them into Israel’s first rank.

Grace crosses a border. Place of birth does not bar the covenant; Jacob’s “are mine” is a welcome they must still walk in.

Reformed

Two sons become two tribes by Jacob’s word under God’s promise. Ephraim is named before Manasseh already.

This is insertion into the people of God, not a private fondness. The church’s future heads are claimed before the hands cross.

Key contrast Both receive the adoption as covenant mercy. Arminians typically stress a sincere welcome that Egyptian-born sons must still live in; Reformed interpreters stress God’s purpose inserting two tribes into Israel by the patriarch’s Spirit-led word.

Genesis 48:6

6Your offspring, whom you become the father of after them, will be yours. They will be called after the name of their brothers in their inheritance.

Arminian

Later children stay Joseph’s, named under the brothers’ inheritance. The double tribe is capped.

Order in the household is pastoral wisdom. Grace makes room without erasing names or stirring a later contest.

Reformed

Only these two are lifted to tribal heads. Any further sons fold into Ephraim or Manasseh.

The line is bounded as God wills. Inheritance follows the names Jacob has just claimed.

Genesis 48:7

7As for me, when I came from Paddan, Rachel died beside me in the land of Canaan on the way, when there was still some distance to come to Ephrath, and I buried her there on the way to Ephrath (also called Bethlehem).”

Arminian

Rachel’s death is still to my sorrow, buried on the way to Bethlehem. Love remembers at the edge of dying.

Wesley’s heirs hear grief that faith does not erase. Joseph’s mother is named so the sons know they belong to a story that includes loss, not only Egypt’s court.

Reformed

The beloved wife’s grave marks Canaan as home even now.

Providence kept the burial in the land of promise. Jacob’s ache sits inside the God who will bless her grandsons.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on adopting Ephraim and Manasseh as Jacob’s own, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“God had promised him two things, a numerous issue, and Canaan for an inheritance. See how he blessed them by faith in that which God had said to him Heb 11:21.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 48:3)

Reformed Notable voices

“Jacob confers on his son the special privilege, that he, being one, should constitute two chiefs; that is, that his two sons should succeed to an equal right with their uncles, as if they had been heirs in the first degree. But what is this! that a decrepit old man assigns to his grandchildren, as a royal patrimony, a sixth part of the land in which he had entered as a stranger, and from which now again he is an exile!”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 48:5)
