Passage

Genesis 34

Verses 1–31 · WEB · Voices

After Jacob’s return to the land (ch. 33), Dinah goes out among the daughters of the land. Shechem the Hivite seizes and humiliates her, then seeks marriage; Jacob’s sons answer with deceitful circumcision, and Simeon and Levi slaughter the city’s males. The chapter names outrage without baptizing either the assault or the massacre.

Genesis 34:1

1Dinah, the daughter of Leah, whom she bore to Jacob, went out to see the daughters of the land.

Arminian

Dinah is named as Leah’s daughter, Jacob’s child. Going out to see the daughters of the land is ordinary curiosity, not a crime.

Do not load this verse with blame for what Shechem will do. Grace treats her as a person, not a cautionary prop. The sin that follows is his.

Reformed

The narrator locates Dinah in the family of promise—Leah’s daughter, borne to Jacob—then lets her step into the land’s social world.

Providence records a going-out without making God the author of the violence ahead. The covenant household is in Canaan; holy purpose and human danger can share a scene.

Genesis 34:2

2Shechem the son of Hamor the Hivite, the prince of the land, saw her. He took her, lay with her, and humbled her.

Arminian

Prince of the land, yet the verbs are seize, lie with, humiliate. Power does not turn assault into courtship. Scripture refuses to pretty the deed.

Arminian interpreters name accountable evil. Shechem’s will acts; Dinah is not treated as consenting partner. Grace that later calls Canaan still condemns this outrage without remainder.

Reformed

The Hivite prince sees and takes. Royal status intensifies guilt: the man who should restrain the land becomes its predator.

This is Shechem’s sin, not God’s. Providence will still keep Jacob’s line, but the text first tells the truth about humiliation. God is not the author of the seizing.

Key contrast Both refuse to recast assault as romance. Arminians emphasize Shechem’s responsible, resistible violence under moral law; Reformed readers press that even this outrage cannot void God’s purpose for Jacob’s house, without excusing the prince.

Genesis 34:3

3His soul joined to Dinah, the daughter of Jacob, and he loved the young lady, and spoke kindly to the young lady.

Arminian

Drawn, loved, spoke tenderly—after humiliation. Tender speech cannot rewind seize-and-shame. Desire that follows violation is distinct from righteousness.

Feelings do not justify the prior act. Grace calls for repentance and justice, not a soft voice that tries to purchase what was taken.

Reformed

The narrator reports Shechem’s attachment without baptizing it. Love-language sits on top of defilement.

The heart can want what it has already wronged. Providence records his longing; holiness still names the humiliation of v. 2.

Genesis 34:4

4Shechem spoke to his father, Hamor, saying, “Get me this young lady as a wife.”

Arminian

He speaks to his father, not first to Dinah’s full dignity: Get me this girl. Marriage is treated as acquisition after the fact.

For the hearer: even a move toward marriage, if it skips confession and her voice, stays bent. Responsible love would begin with truth, not with “get me.”

Reformed

Hamor is enlisted to secure the girl. Household power is used to regularize what violence began.

Fathers and princes can become instruments of a son’s desire. The story will show how quickly “marriage” talk becomes a city’s bargain.

Genesis 34:5

5Now Jacob heard that he had defiled Dinah, his daughter; and his sons were with his livestock in the field. Jacob held his peace until they came.

Arminian

Jacob hears defilement and holds his peace until the sons return. Delay can be prudence or passivity; the chapter will test which.

A father’s silence is not automatically wisdom. Grace still summons him to protect Dinah. Waiting for the sons does not erase his own duty.

Reformed

News reaches Jacob: defiled. Livestock keep the sons away; he waits.

The patriarch of promise is quiet here. Election does not make him a flawless father. God will still carry the line; Jacob’s hush remains on the page.

Genesis 34:6

6Hamor the father of Shechem went out to Jacob to talk with him.

Arminian

Hamor goes out to speak—diplomacy after the crime. Negotiation arrives before repentance is shown.

Talking is not the same as making right. Grace still invites truth-telling; a father’s embassy cannot wash the humiliation.

Reformed

The Hivite father takes the road to Jacob. Politics steps in where holiness was violated.

God lets the scene be public and familial. The covenant household must now answer a city’s prince, not a private rumor.

Genesis 34:7

7The sons of Jacob came in from the field when they heard it. The men were grieved, and they were very angry, because he had done folly in Israel in lying with Jacob’s daughter, a thing that ought not to be done.

Arminian

Indignation is right: an outrageous thing in Israel, such a thing that ought not to be done. Anger at defilement can be moral, not merely tribal pride.

In practice: grief and anger may be holy; what they do next is still chosen. Grace does not require numbness, and it does not bless every sword that follows heat.

Reformed

The sons name the act as outrage in Israel—covenant language, not only family honor. “That ought not to be done” is moral law spoken in the land.

God-given conscience and emerging Israel-identity flare together. Their later deceit will not cancel that the outrage was no illusion.

Genesis 34:8

8Hamor talked with them, saying, “The soul of my son, Shechem, longs for your daughter. Please give her to him as a wife.

Arminian

Hamor pleads longing and marriage. Please give her—as if gift-language can cover seizure.

A father’s request does not create consent after the fact. Grace honors Dinah as more than a token in men’s talks.

Reformed

Longing is presented as reason enough. Hamor speaks to them (plural) about “your daughter.”

Diplomacy can be sincere and still unjust. Providence records the plea; the church must not confuse it with righteousness.

Genesis 34:9

9Make marriages with us. Give your daughters to us, and take our daughters for yourselves.

Arminian

The offer widens: intermarriage as policy. One crime is used to propose a people-merge.

Not every open door is a holy one. Abraham’s line was warned about being absorbed (24:3); responsible faith can refuse a merger that would dissolve calling.

Reformed

Hamor offers daughters both ways—assimilation by marriage.

The promise-people are being invited to become one with Hivite Shechem. God’s purpose for a distinct seed is at stake, not mere snobbery.

Genesis 34:10

10You shall dwell with us, and the land will be before you. Live and trade in it, and get possessions in it.”

Arminian

Land, trade, property—economic welcome after violation. Prosperity is the bait.

Goods are not evil; using them to bury justice is. Grace can bless sojourning without selling the family into a city’s terms.

Reformed

Open land and trade sound like Genesis hospitality, yet they ride on unrepented defilement.

The land was already promised by God (12:7). Hamor’s open offer would trade inheritance for Hivite security.

Genesis 34:11

11Shechem said to her father and to her brothers, “Let me find favor in your eyes, and whatever you will tell me I will give.

Arminian

Shechem himself bargains: favor, whatever you say. Generosity after humiliation can still be control.

Gifts cannot buy a clean conscience. True favor would start with confession of the seizing, not a blank check.

Reformed

The prince turns to father and brothers with payment language. Bride-price talk arrives late.

Wealth cannot atone. God will not be mocked by silver laid over blood and shame.

Genesis 34:12

12Ask me a great amount for a dowry, and I will give whatever you ask of me, but give me the young lady as a wife.”

Arminian

But give me the young woman—price is elastic; the demand is not. She is still spoken of, not with.

“Whatever you ask” can be a rich man’s way of skipping repentance. Marriage without justice is another taking.

Reformed

Bride-price and gift are offered at any height. The “only” reveals the will: obtain her.

The young woman remains object in his sentence. Covenant marriage is not this kind of purchase.

Genesis 34:13

13The sons of Jacob answered Shechem and Hamor his father with deceit when they spoke, because he had defiled Dinah their sister,

Arminian

The narrator says deceitfully—even while naming the because he had defiled Dinah their sister. Motive does not baptize method.

Arminian interpreters refuse a false choice. Outrage is not illusory; lying remains a chosen sin. Grace never needs deceit to do justice.

Reformed

Deceit is the sons’ answer. Scripture will not let readers call it holy strategy just because Dinah was wronged.

God can later judge Simeon and Levi (49:5–7) without denying Shechem’s guilt. Two evils do not make one righteousness.

Key contrast Both hold twin truths: defilement was not imaginary, and deceit is still sin. Arminians stress resistible, responsible lying under a duty to seek just means; Reformed readers underline that even this craft cannot overturn God’s larger purpose, and Genesis 49 will still condemn the violence it served.

Genesis 34:14

14and said to them, “We can’t do this thing, to give our sister to one who is uncircumcised; for that is a reproach to us.

Arminian

They cite circumcision and disgrace—true covenant markers used as cover. Holy words can be recruited for a trap.

Religious reasons may be spoken while the heart plots. Grace calls for integrity: if circumcision matters, it cannot be a ruse.

Reformed

Uncircumcised is named as disgrace. The sign of the covenant (ch. 17) is about to be weaponized.

Sacred signs can be handled by unclean hands. God’s ordinance is not at fault; their use of it is.

Genesis 34:15

15Only on this condition will we consent to you. If you will be as we are, that every male of you be circumcised,

Arminian

The condition: every male circumcised, become as we are. Identity language masks an ambush.

“Be as we are” is not evangelism here. A sign meant for faith is turned into a military advantage. Responsible religion refuses that conversion.

Reformed

Circumcision of every male is set as the price of peace. The covenant cut becomes a city’s vulnerability.

God gave the sign for belonging to Him, not for lulling neighbors before the sword.

Genesis 34:16

16then will we give our daughters to you; and we will take your daughters to us, and we will dwell with you, and we will become one people.

Arminian

They echo Hamor’s merger: one people. The promise they speak is the opposite of what they intend.

False peace-speech is still a lie. Grace builds honest neighbor-love, not assimilation theater that hides revenge.

Reformed

“One people” is the bait. Jacob’s sons promise the very absorption Hamor wanted.

The seed of Abraham is not meant to dissolve into Shechem. Their lie ironically names a danger even as they plot slaughter.

Genesis 34:17

17But if you will not listen to us and be circumcised, then we will take our sister, and we will be gone.”

Arminian

Take our daughter and be gone—an ultimatum. Dinah is still spoken of as movable property in the bargain.

Even a threat that sounds protective can treat her as a token. Justice would center her safety without using her as leverage in deceit.

Reformed

The alternative they name is departure with Dinah. Pressure is applied to the city’s gate.

“our sister” in their mouths is family claim; their method will soon stain the claim.

Genesis 34:18

18Their words pleased Hamor and Shechem, Hamor’s son.

Arminian

The words please them. Deceit works because it sounds like honor and belonging.

What pleases us can still be a snare. Grace trains discernment, not mere relief that a deal is offered.

Reformed

Pleasure at the terms sets the city’s fate in motion. They like what they hear.

God is not obliged to rescue those who swallow a lie; nor does He author the lie. Human gladness can be misjudged.

Genesis 34:19

19The young man didn’t wait to do this thing, because he had delight in Jacob’s daughter, and he was honored above all the house of his father.

Arminian

No delay: delight plus honor speeds the condition. Zeal in a wrong cause is still zeal.

For the hearer: honor in a house does not equal righteousness. Delight after humiliation remains morally tangled; haste is not holiness.

Reformed

Most honored, yet hurrying to the knife for delight’s sake. Status amplifies influence over other males.

Providence will use even this haste as the stage for what follows. Shechem’s honor does not cover his earlier seizing.

Genesis 34:20

20Hamor and Shechem, his son, came to the gate of their city, and talked with the men of their city, saying,

Arminian

Gate and civic speech: a private crime becomes public policy. The city’s men are drawn in.

Leaders remain responsible for what they pitch to a people. Grace does not dissolve corporate agency when princes speak.

Reformed

The gate is where judgments happen. Hamor and Shechem turn family desire into city counsel.

A whole male population will soon share a sign they do not understand. Influence is no pretense; so is coming ruin.

Genesis 34:21

21“These men are peaceful with us. Therefore let them live in the land and trade in it. For behold, the land is large enough for them. Let’s take their daughters to us for wives, and let’s give them our daughters.

Arminian

They announce peace and spacious land—half-truth. “Peaceful” hides the brothers’ deceit and the original outrage.

Peace-talk that skips justice is propaganda. Neighbors deserve honesty; daughters are not trade goods.

Reformed

Peace, trade, intermarriage: the civic version of Hamor’s offer. Land large enough becomes an argument for merger.

The land’s size was never the issue. Holy distinctness and unrepented violence are.

Genesis 34:22

22Only on this condition will the men consent to us to live with us, to become one people, if every male among us is circumcised, as they are circumcised.

Arminian

The city’s body is offered for a false unity.

A religious act without truth is danger. Circumcision here is not faith; it is a civic password. Grace never asks a people to be used this way.

Reformed

The sign of Abraham is proposed as the city’s ticket to one people.

Holy ordinance in pagan mouths, emptied of Yahweh, kept as a deal. God will not be a mascot for assimilation.

Genesis 34:23

23Won’t their livestock and their possessions and all their animals be ours? Only let’s give our consent to them, and they will dwell with us.”

Arminian

The mask slips: their livestock… be ours. Marriage-peace was also a grab.

Shechem’s house has mixed motives. Grace names greed beside the earlier assault. Two sins can share one speech.

Reformed

Property calculus sits under the piety of circumcision. Hamor sells the deal as profit.

They plan to absorb Jacob’s wealth; Jacob’s sons plan to empty theirs. Both sides are bargaining to exploit the other, and neither is keeping covenant.

Genesis 34:24

24All who went out of the gate of his city listened to Hamor, and to Shechem his son; and every male was circumcised, all who went out of the gate of his city.

Arminian

The gate listens; every male is circumcised. A city trusts its princes and takes a wound in good faith.

Followers are still people, not mere fodder—yet they consented to a rite they did not understand as worship. Leadership guilt is heavier; mass action is not nothing.

Reformed

All who went out of the gate: civic totality. The narrator repeats the phrase to stress completeness.

A people can be led into vulnerability in a day. God records it; He does not thereby bless the coming swords.

Genesis 34:25

25On the third day, when they were sore, two of Jacob’s sons, Simeon and Levi, Dinah’s brothers, each took his sword, came upon the unsuspecting city, and killed all the males.

Arminian

Third day, sore, secure: Simeon and Levi kill all the males. Dinah’s brothers avenge with a massacre, not a trial. The text reports, it does not applaud.

Classical Arminians keep both indignations. Shechem’s outrage was true; slaughter of a disabled city is chosen murder. Grace never needs the sword of deceit. Genesis 49 will remember this anger.

Reformed

Simeon and Levi take swords while the city feels secure. Kinship (“Dinah’s brothers”) explains heat, not holiness of method.

God can still preserve Israel after this and still curse their divided violence (49:5–7). Providence is not a rubber stamp on the massacre.

Key contrast Both refuse to choose only one villain. Arminians typically highlight Simeon and Levi’s accountable, resistible slaughter; Reformed readers add that Jacob’s God will judge this anger in due word without letting Canaan erase the promise.

Genesis 34:26

26They killed Hamor and Shechem, his son, with the edge of the sword, and took Dinah out of Shechem’s house, and went away.

Arminian

Hamor and Shechem die; Dinah is taken out of the house. Rescue is mixed with blood. She is retrieved, not consulted in the narrative.

Getting a sister out of a predator’s house can be right in aim and still married to wrong means. The church may honor her safety without blessing the method.

Reformed

Sword and extraction: the prince’s house is emptied of Dinah as it is emptied of life.

The narrator lets us see her brought out. God sees the victim; He also sees the killers. Neither glance is denied.

Genesis 34:27

27Jacob’s sons came on the dead, and plundered the city, because they had defiled their sister.

Arminian

The other sons join for plunder because of the defilement. “Because” again explains motive, not innocence.

Grief can become license to loot. Responsible love of a sister does not require stripping a city of its dead.

Reformed

Plunder follows slaughter. The because-clause keeps Dinah in view even as greed widens the crime.

Corporate family sin now, not only two brothers. The promise-people are capable of Canaanite methods.

Genesis 34:28

28They took their flocks, their herds, their donkeys, that which was in the city, that which was in the field,

Arminian

Flocks, herds, donkeys, whatever—thorough taking. The earlier Hivite fantasy (v. 23) is reversed in kind.

Mirroring a greedy speech with greedy hands is still theft. Grace trains a different justice than tit-for-tat spoil.

Reformed

City and field are stripped. The inventory is cold on purpose.

God can later enrich Israel by gift; this taking is not that gift. Holy history includes shameful pages.

Genesis 34:29

29and all their wealth. They took captive all their little ones and their wives, and took as plunder everything that was in the house.

Arminian

Little ones and wives captured. The circle of harm now includes those who did not seize Dinah.

Vengeance overflows onto the weak. A sincere ethic of love of neighbor cannot end here. Dinah’s dignity is not restored by new captives.

Reformed

Wealth, children, wives: the plunder list names a full sack of the houses.

Scripture tells on Jacob’s sons. The God of the promise remains Judge of this capture too.

Genesis 34:30

30Jacob said to Simeon and Levi, “You have troubled me, to make me odious to the inhabitants of the land, among the Canaanites and the Perizzites. I am few in number. They will gather themselves together against me and strike me, and I will be destroyed, I and my house.”

Arminian

Jacob’s rebuke centers trouble, stench, and survival—few numbers, fear of Canaanite revenge. He says little here about Dinah’s wound or the city’s dead.

A father may be right that the method was disastrous and still too self-referential. Grace would have him name both the sons’ sin and the daughter’s grief, not only his reputation.

Reformed

Jacob fears annihilation in the land of promise. His math is fewness; his theology in this verse is thin on God’s protection (cf. 32:12; 35:5).

The man who wrestled God now calculates destruction. Yet ch. 35 will show terror falling on the cities—the Lord still guards the household Jacob thinks is finished.

Genesis 34:31

31They said, “Should he deal with our sister as with a prostitute?”

Arminian

The last word is a question: Should he deal with our sister as with a prostitute? The question is morally piercing; it is not a full defense of massacre.

Classical Arminians let the question stand as indictment of Shechem and as incomplete ethics. Honor-language can be true about Dinah and still fail to justify killing every male. The church answers both: no, he should not; and no, this is not how brothers of the promise make it right.

Reformed

They end on sister-honor. Genesis will not forget the question—or the swords (49:5–7).

God hears the protest for Dinah and still writes judgment on Simeon and Levi’s violence. The promise moves to Bethel next, not because the brothers were clean, but because the Lord is faithful.

Key contrast Both refuse a tidy moral. Arminians typically keep Shechem’s outrage and the brothers’ responsible bloodshed in the same frame under a sincere moral law; Reformed interpreters usually add that God’s purpose for Jacob continues through—and over—this shame, with later prophetic verdict on the anger.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Arminian and Reformed writers of the past on Dinah, Shechem, and the brothers’ deceitful revenge, in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Dinah was then about fifteen or sixteen years of age when she went out to see the daughters of the land—Probably on some public day. She went to see; yet that was not all, she went to be seen too: she went to see the daughters of the land, but it may be with some thoughts of the sons of the land too.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 34:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses begins to relate the tragic issue of this history. Shechem, indeed, had acted wickedly and impiously; but it was far more atrocious and wicked that the sons of Jacob should murder a whole people, to avenge themselves of the private fault of one man. It was by no means fitting to seek a cruel compensation for the levity and rashness of one youth, by the slaughter of so many men.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 34:7)
