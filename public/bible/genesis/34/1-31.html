Passage

Genesis 34

Verses 1–31 · ESV · Voices

After Jacob’s return to the land (ch. 33), Dinah goes out among the women of the land. Shechem the Hivite seizes and humiliates her, then seeks marriage; Jacob’s sons answer with deceitful circumcision, and Simeon and Levi slaughter the city’s males. The chapter names outrage without baptizing either the assault or the massacre.

Genesis 34:1

1Now Dinah the daughter of Leah, whom she had borne to Jacob, went out to see the women of the land.

Arminian

Dinah is named as Leah’s daughter, Jacob’s child. Going out to see the women of the land is ordinary curiosity, not a crime.

Arminian pastoral: do not load this verse with blame for what Shechem will do. Grace treats her as a person, not a cautionary prop. The sin that follows is his.

Reformed

The narrator locates Dinah in the family of promise—Leah’s daughter, borne to Jacob—then lets her step into the land’s social world.

Reformed note: providence records a real going-out without making God the author of the violence ahead. The covenant household is in Canaan; holy purpose and human danger can share a scene.

Genesis 34:2

2And when Shechem the son of Hamor the Hivite, the prince of the land, saw her, he seized her and lay with her and humiliated her.

Arminian

Prince of the land, yet the verbs are seize, lie with, humiliate. Power does not turn assault into courtship. Scripture refuses to pretty the deed.

Classical Arminians name accountable evil. Shechem’s will acts; Dinah is not treated as consenting partner. Grace that later calls Canaan still condemns this outrage without remainder.

Reformed

The Hivite prince sees and takes. Royal status intensifies guilt: the man who should restrain the land becomes its predator.

Reformed holding: this is Shechem’s sin, not God’s. Providence will still keep Jacob’s line, but the text first tells the truth about humiliation. God is not the author of the seizing.

Key contrast Both refuse to recast assault as romance. Arminians typically stress Shechem’s responsible, resistible violence under real moral law; Reformed readers typically stress that even this outrage cannot void God’s purpose for Jacob’s house, without excusing the prince.

Genesis 34:3

3And his soul was drawn to Dinah the daughter of Jacob. He loved the young woman and spoke tenderly to her.

Arminian

Drawn, loved, spoke tenderly—after humiliation. Tender speech cannot rewind seize-and-shame. Desire that follows violation is not the same as righteousness.

Arminian warning: feelings do not justify the prior act. Grace calls for repentance and justice, not a soft voice that tries to purchase what was taken.

Reformed

The narrator reports Shechem’s attachment without baptizing it. Love-language sits on top of defilement.

Reformed sobriety: the heart can want what it has already wronged. Providence records his longing; holiness still names the humiliation of v. 2.

Genesis 34:4

4So Shechem spoke to his father Hamor, saying, “Get me this girl for my wife.”

Arminian

He speaks to his father, not first to Dinah’s full dignity: Get me this girl. Marriage is treated as acquisition after the fact.

Arminian pastoral: even a move toward marriage, if it skips confession and her voice, stays bent. Responsible love would begin with truth, not with “get me.”

Reformed

Hamor is enlisted to secure the girl. Household power is used to regularize what violence began.

Reformed note: fathers and princes can become instruments of a son’s desire. The story will show how quickly “marriage” talk becomes a city’s bargain.

Genesis 34:5

5Now Jacob heard that he had defiled his daughter Dinah. But his sons were with his livestock in the field, so Jacob held his peace until they came.

Arminian

Jacob hears defilement and holds his peace until the sons return. Delay can be prudence or passivity; the chapter will test which.

Arminian honesty: a father’s silence is not automatically wisdom. Grace still summons him to protect Dinah. Waiting for the sons does not erase his own duty.

Reformed

News reaches Jacob: defiled. Livestock keep the sons away; he waits.

Reformed reading: the patriarch of promise is quiet here. Election does not make him a flawless father. God will still carry the line; Jacob’s hush remains on the page.

Genesis 34:6

6And Hamor the father of Shechem went out to Jacob to speak with him.

Arminian

Hamor goes out to speak—diplomacy after the crime. Negotiation arrives before repentance is shown.

Arminian note: talking is not the same as making right. Grace still invites truth-telling; a father’s embassy cannot wash the humiliation.

Reformed

The Hivite father takes the road to Jacob. Politics steps in where holiness was violated.

Reformed accent: God lets the scene be public and familial. The covenant household must now answer a city’s prince, not a private rumor.

Genesis 34:7

7The sons of Jacob had come in from the field as soon as they heard of it, and the men were indignant and very angry, because he had done an outrageous thing in Israel by lying with Jacob's daughter, for such a thing must not be done.

Arminian

Indignation is right: an outrageous thing in Israel, such a thing must not be done. Anger at defilement can be moral, not merely tribal pride.

Arminian pastoral: grief and anger may be holy; what they do next is still chosen. Grace does not require numbness, and it does not bless every sword that follows heat.

Reformed

The sons name the act as outrage in Israel—covenant language, not only family honor. “Must not be done” is moral law spoken in the land.

Reformed note: God-given conscience and emerging Israel-identity flare together. Their later deceit will not cancel that the outrage was real.

Genesis 34:8

8But Hamor spoke with them, saying, “The soul of my son Shechem longs for your daughter. Please give her to him to be his wife.

Arminian

Hamor pleads longing and marriage. Please give her—as if gift-language can cover seizure.

Arminian edge: a father’s request does not create consent after the fact. Grace honors Dinah as more than a token in men’s talks.

Reformed

Longing is presented as reason enough. Hamor speaks to them (plural) about “your daughter.”

Reformed sobriety: diplomacy can be sincere and still unjust. Providence records the plea; the church must not confuse it with righteousness.

Genesis 34:9

9Make marriages with us. Give your daughters to us, and take our daughters for yourselves.

Arminian

The offer widens: intermarriage as policy. One crime is used to propose a people-merge.

Arminian caution: not every open door is a holy one. Abraham’s line was warned about being absorbed (24:3); responsible faith can refuse a merger that would dissolve calling.

Reformed

Hamor offers daughters both ways—assimilation by marriage.

Reformed reading: the promise-people are being invited to become one with Hivite Shechem. God’s purpose for a distinct seed is at stake, not mere snobbery.

Genesis 34:10

10You shall dwell with us, and the land shall be open to you. Dwell and trade in it, and get property in it.”

Arminian

Land, trade, property—economic welcome after violation. Prosperity is the bait.

Arminian application: goods are not evil; using them to bury justice is. Grace can bless sojourning without selling the family into a city’s terms.

Reformed

Open land and trade sound like Genesis hospitality, yet they ride on unrepented defilement.

Reformed note: the land was already promised by God (12:7). Hamor’s “open” offer would trade inheritance for Hivite security.

Genesis 34:11

11Shechem also said to her father and to her brothers, “Let me find favor in your eyes, and whatever you say to me I will give.

Arminian

Shechem himself bargains: favor, whatever you say. Generosity after humiliation can still be control.

Arminian pastoral: gifts cannot buy a clean conscience. True favor would start with confession of the seizing, not a blank check.

Reformed

The prince turns to father and brothers with payment language. Bride-price talk arrives late.

Reformed holding: wealth cannot atone. God will not be mocked by silver laid over blood and shame.

Genesis 34:12

12Ask me for as great a bride-price and gift as you will, and I will give whatever you say to me. Only give me the young woman to be my wife.”

Arminian

Only give me the young woman—price is elastic; the demand is not. She is still spoken of, not with.

Arminian warning: “whatever you say” can be a rich man’s way of skipping repentance. Marriage without justice is another taking.

Reformed

Bride-price and gift are offered at any height. The “only” reveals the will: obtain her.

Reformed note: the young woman remains object in his sentence. Covenant marriage is not this kind of purchase.

Genesis 34:13

13The sons of Jacob answered Shechem and his father Hamor deceitfully, because he had defiled their sister Dinah.

Arminian

The narrator says deceitfully—even while naming the because: he had defiled their sister. Motive does not baptize method.

Classical Arminians refuse a false choice. Outrage is real; lying remains a chosen sin. Grace never needs deceit to do justice.

Reformed

Deceit is the sons’ answer. Scripture will not let readers call it holy strategy just because Dinah was wronged.

Reformed accent: God can later judge Simeon and Levi (49:5–7) without denying Shechem’s guilt. Two evils do not make one righteousness.

Key contrast Both hold twin truths: defilement was real, and deceit is still sin. Arminians typically stress resistible, responsible lying under a real duty to seek just means; Reformed readers typically stress that even this craft cannot overturn God’s larger purpose, and Genesis 49 will still condemn the violence it served.

Genesis 34:14

14They said to them, “We cannot do this thing, to give our sister to one who is uncircumcised, for that would be a disgrace to us.

Arminian

They cite circumcision and disgrace—true covenant markers used as cover. Holy words can be recruited for a trap.

Arminian note: religious reasons may be spoken while the heart plots. Grace calls for integrity: if circumcision matters, it cannot be a ruse.

Reformed

Uncircumcised is named as disgrace. The sign of the covenant (ch. 17) is about to be weaponized.

Reformed grief: sacred signs can be handled by unclean hands. God’s ordinance is not at fault; their use of it is.

Genesis 34:15

15Only on this condition will we agree with you—that you will become as we are by every male among you being circumcised.

Arminian

The condition: every male circumcised, become as we are. Identity language masks an ambush.

Arminian warning: “become as we are” is not evangelism here. A sign meant for faith is turned into a military advantage. Responsible religion refuses that conversion.

Reformed

Circumcision of every male is set as the price of peace. The covenant cut becomes a city’s vulnerability.

Reformed sobriety: God gave the sign for belonging to Him, not for lulling neighbors before the sword.

Genesis 34:16

16Then we will give our daughters to you, and we will take your daughters to ourselves, and we will dwell with you and become one people.

Arminian

They echo Hamor’s merger: one people. The promise they speak is the opposite of what they intend.

Arminian pastoral: false peace-speech is still a lie. Grace builds honest neighbor-love, not assimilation theater that hides revenge.

Reformed

“One people” is the bait. Jacob’s sons promise the very absorption Hamor wanted.

Reformed note: the seed of Abraham is not meant to dissolve into Shechem. Their lie ironically names a real danger even as they plot slaughter.

Genesis 34:17

17But if you will not listen to us and be circumcised, then we will take our daughter, and we will be gone.”

Arminian

Take our daughter and be gone—an ultimatum. Dinah is still spoken of as movable property in the bargain.

Arminian edge: even a threat that sounds protective can treat her as a token. Justice would center her safety without using her as leverage in deceit.

Reformed

The alternative they name is departure with Dinah. Pressure is applied to the city’s gate.

Reformed reading: “our daughter” in their mouths is family claim; their method will soon stain the claim.

Genesis 34:18

18Their words pleased Hamor and Hamor's son Shechem.

Arminian

The words please them. Deceit works because it sounds like honor and belonging.

Arminian application: what pleases us can still be a snare. Grace trains discernment, not mere relief that a deal is offered.

Reformed

Pleasure at the terms sets the city’s fate in motion. They like what they hear.

Reformed note: God is not obliged to rescue those who swallow a lie; nor does He author the lie. Human gladness can be misjudged.

Genesis 34:19

19And the young man did not delay to do the thing, because he delighted in Jacob's daughter. Now he was the most honored of all his father's house.

Arminian

No delay: delight plus honor speeds the condition. Zeal in a wrong cause is still zeal.

Arminian pastoral: honor in a house does not equal righteousness. Delight after humiliation remains morally tangled; haste is not holiness.

Reformed

Most honored, yet hurrying to the knife for delight’s sake. Status amplifies influence over other males.

Reformed accent: providence will use even this haste as the stage for what follows. Shechem’s honor does not cover his earlier seizing.

Genesis 34:20

20So Hamor and his son Shechem came to the gate of their city and spoke to the men of their city, saying,

Arminian

Gate and civic speech: a private crime becomes public policy. The city’s men are drawn in.

Arminian note: leaders remain responsible for what they pitch to a people. Grace does not dissolve corporate agency when princes speak.

Reformed

The gate is where judgments happen. Hamor and Shechem turn family desire into city counsel.

Reformed holding: a whole male population will soon share a sign they do not understand. Influence is real; so is coming ruin.

Genesis 34:21

21“These men are at peace with us; let them dwell in the land and trade in it, for behold, the land is large enough for them. Let us take their daughters as wives, and let us give them our daughters.

Arminian

They announce peace and spacious land—half-truth. “At peace” hides the brothers’ deceit and the original outrage.

Arminian warning: peace-talk that skips justice is propaganda. Neighbors deserve honesty; daughters are not trade goods.

Reformed

Peace, trade, intermarriage: the civic version of Hamor’s offer. Land large enough becomes an argument for merger.

Reformed note: the land’s size was never the issue. Holy distinctness and unrepented violence are.

Genesis 34:22

22Only on this condition will the men agree to dwell with us to become one people—when every male among us is circumcised as they are circumcised.

Arminian

Only this condition: every male. The city’s body is offered for a false unity.

Arminian pastoral: a religious act without truth is danger. Circumcision here is not faith; it is a civic password. Grace never asks a people to be used this way.

Reformed

The sign of Abraham is proposed as the city’s ticket to one people.

Reformed grief: holy ordinance in pagan mouths, emptied of Yahweh, kept as a deal. God will not be a mascot for assimilation.

Genesis 34:23

23Will not their livestock, their property and all their beasts be ours? Only let us agree with them, and they will dwell with us.”

Arminian

The mask slips: their livestock… be ours. Marriage-peace was also a grab.

Arminian honesty: Shechem’s house has mixed motives. Grace names greed beside the earlier assault. Two sins can share one speech.

Reformed

Property calculus sits under the piety of circumcision. Hamor sells the deal as profit.

Reformed irony: they plan to absorb Jacob’s wealth; Jacob’s sons plan to empty theirs. Mutual exploitation, not covenant.

Genesis 34:24

24And all who went out of the gate of his city listened to Hamor and his son Shechem, and every male was circumcised, all who went out of the gate of his city.

Arminian

The gate listens; every male is circumcised. A city trusts its princes and takes a wound in good faith.

Arminian lament: followers are still people, not mere fodder—yet they consented to a rite they did not understand as worship. Leadership guilt is heavier; mass action is not nothing.

Reformed

All who went out of the gate: civic totality. The narrator repeats the phrase to stress completeness.

Reformed note: a people can be led into vulnerability in a day. God records it; He does not thereby bless the coming swords.

Genesis 34:25

25On the third day, when they were sore, two of the sons of Jacob, Simeon and Levi, Dinah's brothers, took their swords and came against the city while it felt secure and killed all the males.

Arminian

Third day, sore, secure: Simeon and Levi kill all the males. Dinah’s brothers avenge with a massacre, not a trial. The text reports, it does not applaud.

Classical Arminians keep both indignations. Shechem’s outrage was real; slaughter of a disabled city is chosen murder. Grace never needs the sword of deceit. Genesis 49 will remember this anger.

Reformed

Simeon and Levi take swords while the city feels secure. Kinship (“Dinah’s brothers”) explains heat, not holiness of method.

Reformed holding: God can still preserve Israel after this and still curse their divided violence (49:5–7). Providence is not a rubber stamp on the massacre.

Key contrast Both refuse to choose only one villain. Arminians typically emphasize Simeon and Levi’s accountable, resistible slaughter; Reformed readers typically add that Jacob’s God will judge this anger in due word without letting Canaan erase the promise.

Genesis 34:26

26They killed Hamor and his son Shechem with the sword and took Dinah out of Shechem's house and went away.

Arminian

Hamor and Shechem die; Dinah is taken out of the house. Rescue is mixed with blood. She is retrieved, not consulted in the narrative.

Arminian pastoral care: getting a sister out of a predator’s house can be right in aim and still married to wrong means. The church may honor her safety without blessing the method.

Reformed

Sword and extraction: the prince’s house is emptied of Dinah as it is emptied of life.

Reformed sobriety: the narrator lets us see her brought out. God sees the victim; He also sees the killers. Neither glance is denied.

Genesis 34:27

27The sons of Jacob came upon the slain and plundered the city, because they had defiled their sister.

Arminian

The other sons join for plunder because of the defilement. “Because” again explains motive, not innocence.

Arminian warning: grief can become license to loot. Responsible love of a sister does not require stripping a city of its dead.

Reformed

Plunder follows slaughter. The because-clause keeps Dinah in view even as greed widens the crime.

Reformed note: corporate family sin now, not only two brothers. The promise-people are capable of Canaanite methods.

Genesis 34:28

28They took their flocks and their herds, their donkeys, and whatever was in the city and in the field.

Arminian

Flocks, herds, donkeys, whatever—thorough taking. The earlier Hivite fantasy (v. 23) is reversed in kind.

Arminian application: mirroring a greedy speech with greedy hands is still theft. Grace trains a different justice than tit-for-tat spoil.

Reformed

City and field are stripped. The inventory is cold on purpose.

Reformed reading: God can later enrich Israel by gift; this taking is not that gift. Holy history includes shameful pages.

Genesis 34:29

29All their wealth, all their little ones and their wives, all that was in the houses, they captured and plundered.

Arminian

Little ones and wives captured. The circle of harm now includes those who did not seize Dinah.

Arminian grief: vengeance overflows onto the weak. A sincere ethic of love of neighbor cannot end here. Dinah’s dignity is not restored by new captives.

Reformed

Wealth, children, wives: the plunder list names a full sack of the houses.

Reformed horror without fatalism: Scripture tells on Jacob’s sons. The God of the promise remains Judge of this capture too.

Genesis 34:30

30Then Jacob said to Simeon and Levi, “You have brought trouble on me by making me stink to the inhabitants of the land, the Canaanites and the Perizzites. My numbers are few, and if they gather themselves against me and attack me, I shall be destroyed, both I and my household.”

Arminian

Jacob’s rebuke centers trouble, stench, and survival—few numbers, fear of Canaanite revenge. He says little here about Dinah’s wound or the city’s dead.

Arminian pastoral: a father may be right that the method was disastrous and still too self-referential. Grace would have him name both the sons’ sin and the daughter’s grief, not only his reputation.

Reformed

Jacob fears annihilation in the land of promise. His math is fewness; his theology in this verse is thin on God’s protection (cf. 32:12; 35:5).

Reformed irony: the man who wrestled God now calculates destruction. Yet ch. 35 will show terror falling on the cities—the Lord still guards the household Jacob thinks is finished.

Genesis 34:31

31But they said, “Should he treat our sister like a prostitute?”

Arminian

The last word is a question: Should he treat our sister like a prostitute? The question is morally piercing; it is not a full defense of massacre.

Classical Arminians let the question stand as indictment of Shechem and as incomplete ethics. Honor-language can be true about Dinah and still fail to justify killing every male. The church answers both: no, he should not; and no, this is not how brothers of the promise make it right.

Reformed

They end on sister-honor. Genesis will not forget the question—or the swords (49:5–7).

Reformed close: God hears the protest for Dinah and still writes judgment on Simeon and Levi’s violence. The promise moves to Bethel next, not because the brothers were clean, but because the Lord is faithful.

Key contrast Both refuse a tidy moral. Arminians typically keep Shechem’s outrage and the brothers’ responsible bloodshed in the same frame under a sincere moral law; Reformed readers typically add that God’s purpose for Jacob continues through—and over—this shame, with later prophetic verdict on the anger.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Dinah, Shechem, and the brothers’ deceitful revenge—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 34:1–31.

Arminian Notable voices

“Dinah was then about fifteen or sixteen years of age when she went out to see the daughters of the land—Probably on some public day. She went to see; yet that was not all, she went to be seen too: she went to see the daughters of the land, but it may be with some thoughts of the sons of the land too.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 34:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses begins to relate the tragic issue of this history. Shechem, indeed, had acted wickedly and impiously; but it was far more atrocious and wicked that the sons of Jacob should murder a whole people, to avenge themselves of the private fault of one man. It was by no means fitting to seek a cruel compensation for the levity and rashness of one youth, by the slaughter of so many men.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 34:7)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
