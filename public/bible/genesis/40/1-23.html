Passage

Genesis 40

Verses 1–23 · ESV · Voices

In prison Joseph is assigned to Pharaoh’s cupbearer and baker. Each dreams; Joseph says interpretations belong to God. The cupbearer will be restored in three days, the baker hanged. Joseph asks to be remembered. On Pharaoh’s birthday both words come true—and the cupbearer forgets him.

Genesis 40:1

1Some time after this, the cupbearer of the king of Egypt and his baker committed an offense against their lord the king of Egypt.

Arminian

Two officers fall by their own offense. Guilt is personal: they sinned against their lord. Prison will become a place of meeting, not because they were puppets, but because real wrongs have real consequences.

Reformed

Offense, then the king’s house. God is already steering Joseph’s path by other men’s failures. Providence uses even palace scandal without making God the author of their sin.

Genesis 40:2

2And Pharaoh was angry with his two officers, the chief cupbearer and the chief baker,

Arminian

Pharaoh’s anger is a human king’s choice. Rank does not cancel accountability: chief cupbearer and chief baker still answer to their lord—and to God who sees courts as well as pits.

Reformed

Royal wrath is real, and it is not outside the Lord’s hand. The same God who humbled Joseph by false charge now brings two chiefs down beside him for a purpose Joseph cannot yet see.

Genesis 40:3

3and he put them in custody in the house of the captain of the guard, in the prison where Joseph was confined.

Arminian

Same prison, same house. Their custody meets Joseph’s confinement. Location is not fate that erases agency; it is the setting where they will soon be asked to trust God’s word.

Reformed

The captain’s house is no accident. God parks the two dreams beside the interpreter He has already placed there. Confinement is the appointed waiting room of chapter 41.

Genesis 40:4

4The captain of the guard appointed Joseph to be with them, and he attended them. They continued for some time in custody.

Arminian

Joseph attends them—faithful service in a cell. Grace enables care for fellow prisoners without guaranteeing a quick exit. The “some time” is a real wait they must inhabit.

Reformed

Appointment from the captain, attendance from Joseph: means under providence. God trains His servant in hidden faithfulness while the clock of “some time” runs toward the dreams.

Genesis 40:5

5And one night they both dreamed—the cupbearer and the baker of the king of Egypt, who were confined in the prison—each his own dream, and each dream with its own interpretation.

Arminian

Two dreams, two meanings. God can speak in the night; the hearers remain responsible for what they do with the word. Distinct dreams refuse a one-size omen.

Reformed

Night visions in a cell are the Lord’s sending, not random sleep. Each dream carries its own interpretation because God authors both the riddle and the reading.

Genesis 40:6

6When Joseph came to them in the morning, he saw that they were troubled.

Arminian

He notices downcast faces. Compassion is a chosen attention: Joseph could have ignored fellow inmates. Grace trains the eyes to see trouble before it speaks.

Reformed

Morning mercy: the interpreter arrives already seeing. God who gave the dreams also gives Joseph sight for their trouble, so the word will not sit unasked.

Genesis 40:7

7So he asked Pharaoh’s officers who were with him in custody in his master’s house, “Why are your faces downcast today?”

Arminian

A simple question opens the door. Ministry often starts with honest inquiry. They are still officers, still in custody, still free to answer or stay shut.

Reformed

The question is pastoral and providential. Joseph’s “why” is the means by which God draws the dreams into the open for interpretation.

Genesis 40:8

8They said to him, “We have had dreams, and there is no one to interpret them.” And Joseph said to them, “Do not interpretations belong to God? Please tell them to me.”

Arminian

No court magician is at hand. Joseph points away from himself: interpretations belong to God—then invites them to tell. The gift is real; so is their choice to speak. God sincerely gives light; they must still open the dream.

Reformed

Joseph refuses a technician’s pride. Meaning is God’s property. “Please tell them to me” is the appointed means by which the Lord will interpret—not Joseph’s genius, but God’s word through His servant.

Key contrast Both deny that dreams are decoded by human craft. Arminians typically stress a sincere offer of God’s light that the officers may receive or withhold; Reformed readers typically stress that interpretation is God’s sovereign gift, effectually given through Joseph.

Genesis 40:9

9So the chief cupbearer told his dream to Joseph and said to him, “In my dream there was a vine before me,

Arminian

He tells the dream. Trusting Joseph with the night-vision is a responsible step toward the God Joseph named. Revelation meets a willing speaker.

Reformed

The cupbearer speaks because God is already moving the story. The vine will mean life restored—first heard in a prison, not a palace.

Genesis 40:10

10and on the vine there were three branches. As soon as it budded, its blossoms shot forth, and the clusters ripened into grapes.

Arminian

Three branches, sudden fruit: the dream is vivid and particular. God can use created images without turning nature into a fate-chart. The man must still hear the reading.

Reformed

Bud, blossom, grape in haste: the Lord packs three days into a vine. Details are His, not leftover decoration.

Genesis 40:11

11Pharaoh’s cup was in my hand, and I took the grapes and pressed them into Pharaoh’s cup and placed the cup in Pharaoh’s hand.”

Arminian

He dreams his old work: cup in hand, juice for Pharaoh. Hope of restoration is pictured before it is promised. The dream invites faith in the God who interprets, not in the cup itself.

Reformed

Office restored is shown before it happens. God previews what He will do on the third day. The cupbearer’s craft is the canvas; the Painter is the Lord.

Genesis 40:12

12Then Joseph said to him, “This is its interpretation: the three branches are three days.

Arminian

Joseph speaks as a servant of God’s meaning, not a freelancer. Three days is a clear word: grace gives understanding so the man can receive it. The interpretation is offered as truth to be believed.

Reformed

“This is its interpretation”—not a guess. God who owns the meaning now utters it by Joseph. The clock of three days is already set in heaven.

Genesis 40:13

13In three days Pharaoh will lift up your head and restore you to your office, and you shall place Pharaoh’s cup in his hand as formerly, when you were his cupbearer.

Arminian

Restoration is promised in time and task: head lifted, office returned, cup placed. Mercy to the cupbearer is particular. He will have to walk back into the job as a man who heard God’s word in prison.

Reformed

Pharaoh will lift the head because God has said so. Royal favor is the instrument; the decree is the Lord’s. Restoration “as formerly” is providence, not luck.

Genesis 40:14

14Only remember me, when it is well with you, and please do me the kindness to mention me to Pharaoh, and so get me out of this house.

Arminian

Joseph asks to be remembered—kindness, mention, release. The plea is lawful and human: grace does not forbid using means. The cupbearer remains free to keep or drop the request.

Reformed

A righteous ask sits beside a sure interpretation. God will use even a forgotten plea (v. 23) on His timetable. Joseph’s desire for the house to open is not unbelief; delay will still serve the promise.

Genesis 40:15

15For I was indeed stolen out of the land of the Hebrews, and here also I have done nothing that they should put me into the pit.”

Arminian

He names the truth: stolen, then jailed without cause. Innocent suffering is real; Joseph does not baptize the pit as his own sin. Asking for justice is compatible with trust in God.

Reformed

Two pits, neither earned. Providence allowed the theft and the false charge without making Joseph guilty. The Lord who records this innocence will also time the exit.

Genesis 40:16

16When the chief baker saw that the interpretation was favorable, he said to Joseph, “I also had a dream: there were three cake baskets on my head,

Arminian

He speaks because the other word was favorable—hope of a matching mercy. Motive is mixed and still responsible: he wants a good reading. God will not tailor truth to that want.

Reformed

Favor in the first dream lures the second telling. God still uses the baker’s desire to surface the baskets. The interpretation will not flatter.

Genesis 40:17

17and in the uppermost basket there were all sorts of baked food for Pharaoh, but the birds were eating it out of the basket on my head.”

Arminian

Food for Pharaoh, birds eating it: a picture he cannot control. The dream warns before Joseph speaks. Hearers are accountable to face a hard vision, not only a hopeful one.

Reformed

Birds at the basket are already the Lord’s sign. What looks like provision is marked for scavenging. God authors the grim detail as surely as the vine’s grapes.

Genesis 40:18

18And Joseph answered and said, “This is its interpretation: the three baskets are three days.

Arminian

Same three-day frame, different end. Joseph does not soften the word to keep a friend. Faithful interpretation serves God, not the hearer’s preference.

Reformed

Again, “this is its interpretation.” Same God, same clock, opposite verdict. Truth is not a technique Joseph adjusts; it is given.

Genesis 40:19

19In three days Pharaoh will lift up your head—from you!—and hang you on a tree. And the birds will eat the flesh from you.”

Arminian

The pun turns cruel: head lifted off. Joseph tells the baker the truth in time to meet God, not to entertain. Hard prophecy is still a sincere word; refusing to hear it would not make it less true.

Reformed

Judgment as well as restoration sits in Joseph’s mouth because both sit in God’s counsel. Pharaoh’s rope will fulfill what the Lord has shown. The birds finish the sign.

Key contrast Both refuse a God who only ever blesses. Arminians typically stress a true warning the baker is responsible to hear; Reformed readers typically stress that the hanging is as appointed as the cupbearer’s restoration.

Genesis 40:20

20On the third day, which was Pharaoh’s birthday, he made a feast for all his servants and lifted up the head of the chief cupbearer and the head of the chief baker among his servants.

Arminian

Third day, birthday feast, both heads lifted into public view. Joseph’s word meets the calendar. Court celebration does not cancel either man’s accountability before the king—or before God.

Reformed

Pharaoh’s feast is the stage God named in the dreams. Birthday and banquet serve the interpretation. The Lord times even a pagan holiday to keep His servant’s word.

Genesis 40:21

21He restored the chief cupbearer to his position, and he placed the cup in Pharaoh’s hand.

Arminian

Vine-dream kept: office and cup return. Mercy in history confirms the God Joseph confessed. The restored man now owes kindness he is still free to give or withhold.

Reformed

Restoration “as formerly” lands on schedule. God who promised the cup in Pharaoh’s hand now places it there. The word was not a lucky guess.

Genesis 40:22

22But he hanged the chief baker, as Joseph had interpreted to them.

Arminian

The baker dies as interpreted. Two hearers, two ends: the difference was not Joseph playing favorites, but God’s true word meeting two lives. Warning was not bluff.

Reformed

“As Joseph had interpreted” is the narrator’s seal. Hanging fulfills the baskets. Providence stands over both outcomes without confusing the judge with the sin that brought the baker down.

Genesis 40:23

23Yet the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph, but forgot him.

Arminian

Forgot: a real failure of kindness. Joseph’s plea was sincere; the cupbearer’s memory is responsible and drops. Delay is not proof God failed—it is a man neglecting a brother. Two years will test Joseph’s wait.

Reformed

Forgetting is sin and instrument. God will use the blank memory so Joseph rises at Pharaoh’s dream, not a servant’s convenience. The pit lasts until the Lord’s hour, not the cupbearer’s gratitude.

Key contrast Both take the forgetting as real neglect. Arminians typically stress the cupbearer’s accountable failure to keep a promised kindness; Reformed readers typically stress that God ordained the delay so Joseph would be remembered on God’s day, not man’s.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on dreams in prison, and interpretations that belong to God—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 40:1–23.

Arminian Notable voices

“We should not have had this story of Pharaoh's butler and baker recorded in scripture, if it had not been serviceable to Joseph's preferment. The world stands for the sake of the church, and is governed for its good. Observe, [1.] Two of the great officers of Pharaoh's court having offended the king are committed to prison.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 40:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“We have already seen, that when Joseph was in bonds, God cared for him. For whence arose the relaxation afforded him, but from the divine favor? Therefore, God, before he opened the door for his servant's deliverance, entered into the very prison to sustain him with his strength.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 40:1)
