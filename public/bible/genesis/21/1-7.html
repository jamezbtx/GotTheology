Passage

Genesis 21

Verses 1–7 · ESV · Voices

After the Abimelech episode in chapter 20, the long-delayed word arrives: the LORD visits Sarah as He had said. Isaac is born, named, and circumcised; Sarah’s laughter turns from disbelief to praise at the time God had spoken.

Genesis 21:1

1The LORD visited Sarah as he had said, and the LORD did to Sarah as he had promised.

Arminian

The chapter opens with fidelity, not surprise: the LORD visited Sarah “as he had said” and did “as he had promised.” Grace keeps its appointments.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere promise now kept in history. God had spoken; Sarah is not a prop. The visit is gift, and it summons the household to trust the Speaker who does what He says.

Reformed

Promise and performance sit in one sentence. The LORD both said and did; Sarah’s womb is the theater of His faithfulness.

Reformed comfort: the visit is God’s own initiative. What He promised in 17–18 He now effectually performs. Isaac’s arrival is covenant keeping, not chance timed to human effort.

Key contrast Both confess that God kept His word to Sarah. Arminians typically stress a sincere promise now fulfilled, inviting trust; Reformed readers typically stress that the same promise was God’s effectual “I will,” performed in His time.

Genesis 21:2

2And Sarah conceived and bore Abraham a son in his old age at the time of which God had spoken to him.

Arminian

Conception, birth, old age, and “the time of which God had spoken” pile up. The child is timed to God’s mouth, not to Abraham’s calendar.

Arminian pastoral reading: delay did not cancel the word. Hearers are called to wait under a real promise—grace that can be trusted even when the tent stays empty for years.

Reformed

Four clauses, one Author. Old age underlines impossibility; “the time” underlines sovereignty.

Reformed accent: God had spoken the hour as well as the son. Isaac is born on a schedule heaven set, so that faith would rest on the Promiser, not on Sarah’s strength.

Genesis 21:3

3Abraham called the name of his son who was born to him, whom Sarah bore him, Isaac.

Arminian

Abraham names him Isaac—“he laughs”—as God had commanded (17:19). The name remembers both Sarah’s earlier laugh and God’s last word over it.

Classical Arminians hear obedient faith putting God’s joke of grace on the child’s forehead. Naming is a responsible act: Abraham receives the son as the son God named first.

Reformed

The name is not Abraham’s invention. God named Isaac before the birth; Abraham now speaks it over the child Sarah bore.

Reformed note: identity is gift. The laughter built into the name is the LORD’s signature on the promised seed.

Genesis 21:4

4And Abraham circumcised his son Isaac when he was eight days old, as God had commanded him.

Arminian

Eight-day circumcision keeps the covenant sign of chapter 17. Faith that received the son also marks him as God commanded.

Arminian emphasis: grace does not skip obedience. Abraham’s responsibility is concrete and timely; the sign is received, not redesigned.

Reformed

The promised son is brought under the covenant cut at the commanded hour. Command and gift travel together.

Reformed reading: Isaac enters the visible people of God by the sign God appointed. Circumcision here is not human leverage on grace; it is the household’s yes to a word already kept.

Genesis 21:5

5Abraham was a hundred years old when his son Isaac was born to him.

Arminian

A hundred years old: Paul will linger here (Rom 4:19). The math is meant to humble flesh and magnify the promise.

Classical Arminians gladly say the deadness of the body did not stop faith from receiving what God offered. The age is a monument: salvation-history turns on gift, not on Abraham’s vitality.

Reformed

The narrator dates the miracle to Abraham’s hundredth year so no one can credit the father’s strength.

Reformed gospel: this is Romans 4 in narrative form. God gives life where the body is as good as dead, so that the seed rests on promise.

Genesis 21:6

6And Sarah said, “God has made laughter for me; everyone who hears will laugh over me.”

Arminian

Sarah interprets her own story: God has made laughter for me. The scoff of chapter 18 is remade into joy others will share.

Arminian pastoral word: God can turn incredulous laughter into praise. Grace invites the hearer to laugh with Sarah rather than at the promise—receiving the gift instead of remaining in the old unbelief.

Reformed

“God has made” is the whole theology of the verse. Laughter is manufactured by the LORD, then offered to “everyone who hears.”

Reformed comfort: the community’s joy is derivative. They will laugh because God first made laughter in Sarah.

Genesis 21:7

7And she said, “Who would have said to Abraham that Sarah would nurse children? Yet I have borne him a son in his old age.”

Arminian

Sarah marvels out loud: who would have said it? Nursing in old age is the proof in her arms. Wonder is the right response to kept promise.

Classical Arminians hear testimony meant to spread. The “yet” of this verse calls others to trust the God who does what no neighbor would have predicted.

Reformed

Rhetorical “who would have said” answers itself: God said it. Sarah now nurses what the LORD had named.

Reformed accent: the verse is doxology after decree. Old age remains; the son is there anyway, because God had spoken.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Isaac born as the LORD had said, and Sarah’s laughter turned to joy—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 21:1–7.

Arminian Notable voices

“God therefore, by promise, gave that strength. Abraham was old, and Sarah old, and both as good as dead, and then the word of God took place.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 21:2)

Reformed Notable voices

“Because all offspring, flows from the kindness of God, as it is in the psalm, The fruit of the womb is the gift of God;' ( Psalm 127:3 ;) therefore the Lord is said, not without reason, to visit those, to whom he gives children. For although the foetus seems to be produced naturally, each from its own kind; there is yet no fecundity in animals, except so far as the Lord puts forth his own power, to fulfill what he has said, Increase and multiply.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 21:1)
