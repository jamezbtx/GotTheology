Passage

Genesis 21

Verses 22–34 · WEB · Voices

After Ishmael’s wilderness rescue, Abimelech and Phicol approach Abraham, saying, God is with you. They swear a treaty, settle a seized well with seven ewe lambs, name the place Beersheba, and Abraham plants a tamarisk and calls on the LORD, the Everlasting God, remaining a sojourner in Philistine country.

Genesis 21:22

22At that time, Abimelech and Phicol the captain of his army spoke to Abraham, saying, “God is with you in all that you do.

Arminian

A foreign king and his commander read Abraham’s life out loud: God is with you in all that you do. Public fruit of private promise.

Classical Arminians find a testimony meant to humble and to invite. The with-ness of God is observable; neighbors may still respond—seeking peace rather than fighting the man God so plainly accompanies.

Reformed

“At that time” ties the treaty to Isaac’s birth and Ishmael’s sending. Even Philistine eyes can see the with-ness.

God with Abraham is not a private mood. Providence has become politically visible, preparing the oath that follows.

Genesis 21:23

23Now, therefore, swear to me here by God that you will not deal falsely with me, nor with my son, nor with my son’s son. But according to the kindness that I have done to you, you shall do to me, and to the land in which you have lived as a foreigner.”

Arminian

Abimelech wants an oath covering descendants and the land of sojourning, on the basis of prior kindness (ch. 20). Fear of God-with-Abraham becomes a request for covenant fairness.

Power asks for truth-telling. Grace that accompanies Abraham does not license false dealing; it obliges him to swear honestly with a neighbor who asks.

Reformed

The king seeks a God-backed oath because he has seen God with Abraham. Kindness received is leveraged into a generational pact.

Sojourning status is admitted. Abraham still owns no land, yet his God-with-him makes him a man kings must treat carefully.

Genesis 21:24

24Abraham said, “I will swear.”

Arminian

The answer is short and binding: I will swear. Abraham consents to truthful dealing under God’s name.

Arminian readers honor the responsible yes. The man of promise may swear when the oath serves peace and truth, not when it would bind him to sin.

Reformed

Four words close the request. The patriarch places himself under oath before the God Abimelech has already named.

The yes is as much worship as diplomacy. Abraham’s word is now publicly tied to the LORD’s name.

Genesis 21:25

25Abraham complained to Abimelech because of a water well, which Abimelech’s servants had violently taken away.

Arminian

Oath does not swallow justice: Abraham reproves the seized well before the ceremony proceeds. Peace is not silence about theft.

Grace-enabled courage tells the truth to power. The well matters because life in the south depends on it, and neighbors must not baptize seizure as kindness.

Reformed

Reproof interrupts the treaty. A seized well is named while the commanders are still present.

Covenant-making includes putting wrong on the table. The God who is “with” Abraham also cares about a well his servants lost.

Genesis 21:26

26Abimelech said, “I don’t know who has done this thing. You didn’t tell me, and I didn’t hear of it until today.”

Arminian

Abimelech pleads ignorance—three ways. Whether fully true or self-protective, the dispute is now in the open.

Arminians prefer open speech to stored grievance. Once told, the king can act; hidden injury would have poisoned the oath.

Reformed

The king’s defense is lack of knowledge. The narrator lets it stand without a verdict.

Providence uses the conversation. What was seized in the dark is now sworn in the light of an oath.

Genesis 21:27

27Abraham took sheep and cattle, and gave them to Abimelech. Those two made a covenant.

Arminian

Abraham funds the covenant with sheep and oxen. Gift seals the peace both men now name.

Abraham makes peace at his own cost. The blessed man gives; grace received in Isaac’s house overflows toward a Philistine treaty rather than a feud.

Reformed

Animals change hands; “Those two made a covenant.” Abraham, still a sojourner, treats a king as a covenant partner.

The livestock are not bribes but witnesses in a world where God is already the true Witness.

Genesis 21:28

28Abraham set seven ewe lambs of the flock by themselves.

Arminian

Seven ewe lambs are set apart—extra, specific, counted. Abraham will not let the well dissolve into vague goodwill.

Arminian readers see careful integrity. Grace does not make a man sloppy with property or memory; he marks the claim so the oath has a handle.

Reformed

Seven—later echoing in Beersheba’s name—are isolated from the general gift.

Symbols serve truth. The lambs will interpret the well so future generations cannot rewrite who dug it.

Genesis 21:29

29Abimelech said to Abraham, “What do these seven ewe lambs, which you have set by themselves, mean?”

Arminian

Abimelech asks for meaning. Signs without interpretation invite confusion; Abraham will explain.

It is good to ask what a rite means before you receive it. Neighborly covenants should be understood, not merely performed.

Reformed

The king’s question lets Abraham define the seven. Meaning is not left to later spin.

The dialogue itself is part of the covenant. Words will bind the lambs to the well.

Genesis 21:30

30He said, “You shall take these seven ewe lambs from my hand, that it may be a witness to me, that I have dug this well.”

Arminian

The lambs are a witness: I dug this well. Abraham claims honest labor, not conquest.

What comes through is a theology of truthful witness. Taking from his hand means Abimelech accepts the story. Peace includes acknowledging whose work watered the place.

Reformed

Witness language turns livestock into legal memory. The well is Abraham’s by digging, now recognized by oath.

God who promised land still lets His man hold a well by testimony in the meantime—sojourner rights under sworn witness.

Genesis 21:31

31Therefore he called that place Beersheba, because they both swore an oath there.

Arminian

Beersheba—“well of seven” or “well of the oath”—is named from what both men swore. Geography remembers speech.

Shared oaths can bless a place. Two parties answering God in truth leave a name that can still catechize later travelers.

Reformed

The narrator etymologizes in time: oath plus well equals Beersheba.

Naming is theological cartography. The map of the south now carries a memorial of sworn truth under Abraham’s God.

Genesis 21:32

32So they made a covenant at Beersheba. Abimelech rose up with Phicol, the captain of his army, and they returned into the land of the Philistines.

Arminian

Covenant made; the military escort goes home. Peace holds without occupation.

Classical Arminians see a good ending to a tense approach. God-with-Abraham produced a treaty, not a war—an invitation to deal kindly in the land of sojourning.

Reformed

The unit closes the diplomats’ visit: covenant at Beersheba, return to Philistine land.

Abraham remains; they leave. The man of promise is still a guest, yet the well and the oath stay.

Genesis 21:33

33Abraham planted a tamarisk tree in Beersheba, and there he called on the name of the LORD, the Everlasting God.

Arminian

A tamarisk—slow, long-lived—and a new name for God: El Olam, the Everlasting God. Worship, not only diplomacy, claims the well.

The God of the kept Isaac-promise is everlasting. Calling on His name is the right use of the place; hearers are invited to the same trust that outlasts Philistine treaties.

Reformed

Planting and invocation belong together. The Everlasting God is the title earned by chapters of kept word—Isaac included.

El Olam names the One whose purpose does not expire with a king’s generation. Beersheba becomes an altar-site in all but name.

Key contrast Both read this as worship after providence. Arminians stress Abraham’s responsible calling on the everlasting name, a pattern for trusting prayer; Calvinists typically stress that El Olam is the God whose everlasting purpose has just displayed itself in Isaac, Ishmael’s keeping, and this oath.

Genesis 21:34

34Abraham lived as a foreigner in the land of the Philistines many days.

Arminian

The chapter ends in sojourning, not settlement. Many days in Philistine land: promise still ahead, presence already here.

Arminian readers take the pilgrim note to heart. Faith lives long stretches as a guest, calling on the Everlasting God while waiting for more of what He promised.

Reformed

Sojourned many days refuses a premature conquest story. The well is his; the land is not yet.

The God who visited Sarah also parks Abraham in Philistine country for a long stay. Timing remains His. Chapter 22’s test will find a sojourner, not a king.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

The treaty at Beersheba and the well Abraham claimed: older Arminian and Reformed writers, each in their own words.

Arminian Notable voices

“Men should not only retain their goodness wherever they go, but do all they can to propagate it, and make others good. The everlasting God—Though God had made himself known to Abraham as his God in particular; yet he forgets not to give glory to him as the Lord of all, the everlasting God, who was before all worlds, and will be when time and days shall be no more.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 21:33)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses relates, that this covenant was entered into between Abraham and Abimelech, for the purpose of showing, that after various agitations, some repose was, at length, granted to the holy man. He had been constrained, as a wanderer, and without a fixed abode, to move his tent from place to place, during sixty years. But although God would have him to be a sojourner even unto death, yet, under king Abimelech, he granted him a quiet habitation.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 21:22)
