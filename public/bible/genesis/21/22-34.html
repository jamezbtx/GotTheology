Passage

Genesis 21

Verses 22–34 · ESV

After Ishmael’s wilderness rescue, Abimelech and Phicol approach Abraham: God is with you. They swear a treaty, settle a seized well with seven ewe lambs, name the place Beersheba, and Abraham plants a tamarisk and calls on the LORD, the Everlasting God, remaining a sojourner in Philistine country.

Genesis 21:22

22At that time Abimelech and Phicol the commander of his army said to Abraham, “God is with you in all that you do.

Arminian

A foreign king and his commander read Abraham’s life out loud: God is with you in all that you do. Public fruit of private promise.

Classical Arminians hear a testimony meant to humble and to invite. The with-ness of God is observable; neighbors may still respond—seeking peace rather than fighting the man God so plainly accompanies.

Reformed

“At that time” ties the treaty to Isaac’s birth and Ishmael’s sending. Even Philistine eyes can see the with-ness.

Reformed accent: God with Abraham is not a private mood. Providence has become politically visible, preparing the oath that follows.

Genesis 21:23

23Now therefore swear to me here by God that you will not deal falsely with me or with my descendants or with my posterity, but as I have dealt kindly with you, so you will deal with me and with the land where you have sojourned.”

Arminian

Abimelech wants an oath covering descendants and the land of sojourning, on the basis of prior kindness (ch. 20). Fear of God-with-Abraham becomes a request for covenant fairness.

Arminian note: power asks for truth-telling. Grace that accompanies Abraham does not license false dealing; it obliges him to swear honestly with a neighbor who asks.

Reformed

The king seeks a God-backed oath because he has seen God with Abraham. Kindness received is leveraged into a generational pact.

Reformed reading: sojourning status is admitted. Abraham still owns no land, yet his God-with-him makes him a man kings must treat carefully.

Genesis 21:24

24And Abraham said, “I will swear.”

Arminian

The answer is short and binding: I will swear. Abraham consents to truthful dealing under God’s name.

Classical Arminians honor the responsible yes. The man of promise may swear when the oath serves peace and truth, not when it would bind him to sin.

Reformed

Four words close the request. The patriarch places himself under oath before the God Abimelech has already named.

Reformed note: the yes is as much worship as diplomacy. Abraham’s word is now publicly tied to the LORD’s name.

Genesis 21:25

25When Abraham reproved Abimelech about a well of water that Abimelech's servants had seized,

Arminian

Oath does not swallow justice: Abraham reproves the seized well before the ceremony proceeds. Peace is not silence about theft.

Arminian pastoral reading: grace-enabled courage tells the truth to power. The well matters because life in the south depends on it, and neighbors must not baptize seizure as kindness.

Reformed

Reproof interrupts the treaty. A seized well is named while the commanders are still present.

Reformed accent: covenant-making includes putting wrong on the table. The God who is “with” Abraham also cares about a well his servants lost.

Genesis 21:26

26Abimelech said, “I do not know who has done this thing; you did not tell me, and I have not heard of it until today.”

Arminian

Abimelech pleads ignorance—three ways. Whether fully true or self-protective, the dispute is now in the open.

Classical Arminians prefer open speech to stored grievance. Once told, the king can act; hidden injury would have poisoned the oath.

Reformed

The king’s defense is lack of knowledge. The narrator lets it stand without a verdict.

Reformed note: providence uses the conversation. What was seized in the dark is now sworn in the light of an oath.

Genesis 21:27

27So Abraham took sheep and oxen and gave them to Abimelech, and the two men made a covenant.

Arminian

Abraham funds the covenant with sheep and oxen. Gift seals the peace both men now name.

Arminian emphasis: costly peacemaking. The blessed man gives; grace received in Isaac’s house overflows toward a Philistine treaty rather than a feud.

Reformed

Animals change hands; “the two men made a covenant.” Abraham, still a sojourner, treats a king as a covenant partner.

Reformed reading: the livestock are not bribes but witnesses in a world where God is already the true Witness.

Genesis 21:28

28Abraham set seven ewe lambs of the flock apart.

Arminian

Seven ewe lambs are set apart—extra, specific, counted. Abraham will not let the well dissolve into vague goodwill.

Classical Arminians see careful integrity. Grace does not make a man sloppy with property or memory; he marks the claim so the oath has a handle.

Reformed

Seven—later echoing in Beersheba’s name—are isolated from the general gift.

Reformed note: symbols serve truth. The lambs will interpret the well so future generations cannot rewrite who dug it.

Genesis 21:29

29And Abimelech said to Abraham, “What is the meaning of these seven ewe lambs that you have set apart?”

Arminian

Abimelech asks for meaning. Signs without interpretation invite confusion; Abraham will explain.

Arminian pastoral aside: it is good to ask what a rite means before you receive it. Neighborly covenants should be understood, not merely performed.

Reformed

The king’s question lets Abraham define the seven. Meaning is not left to later spin.

Reformed accent: the dialogue itself is part of the covenant. Words will bind the lambs to the well.

Genesis 21:30

30He said, “These seven ewe lambs you will take from my hand, that this may be a witness for me that I dug this well.”

Arminian

The lambs are a witness: I dug this well. Abraham claims honest labor, not conquest.

Classical Arminians hear a theology of truthful witness. Taking from his hand means Abimelech accepts the story. Peace includes acknowledging whose work watered the place.

Reformed

Witness language turns livestock into legal memory. The well is Abraham’s by digging, now recognized by oath.

Reformed comfort: God who promised land still lets His man hold a well by testimony in the meantime—sojourner rights under sworn witness.

Genesis 21:31

31Therefore that place was called Beersheba, because there both of them swore an oath.

Arminian

Beersheba—“well of seven” or “well of the oath”—is named from what both men swore. Geography remembers speech.

Arminian note: shared oaths can bless a place. Two parties answering God in truth leave a name that can still catechize later travelers.

Reformed

The narrator etymologizes in real time: oath plus well equals Beersheba.

Reformed reading: naming is theological cartography. The map of the south now carries a memorial of sworn truth under Abraham’s God.

Genesis 21:32

32So they made a covenant at Beersheba. Then Abimelech and Phicol the commander of his army rose up and returned to the land of the Philistines.

Arminian

Covenant made; the military escort goes home. Peace holds without occupation.

Classical Arminians see a good ending to a tense approach. God-with-Abraham produced a treaty, not a war—an invitation to deal kindly in the land of sojourning.

Reformed

The unit closes the diplomats’ visit: covenant at Beersheba, return to Philistine land.

Reformed accent: Abraham remains; they leave. The man of promise is still a guest, yet the well and the oath stay.

Genesis 21:33

33Abraham planted a tamarisk tree in Beersheba and called there on the name of the LORD, the Everlasting God.

Arminian

A tamarisk—slow, long-lived—and a new name for God: El Olam, the Everlasting God. Worship, not only diplomacy, claims the well.

Arminian doxology: the God of the kept Isaac-promise is everlasting. Calling on His name is the right use of the place; hearers are invited to the same trust that outlasts Philistine treaties.

Reformed

Planting and invocation belong together. The Everlasting God is the title earned by chapters of kept word—Isaac included.

Reformed comfort: El Olam names the One whose purpose does not expire with a king’s generation. Beersheba becomes an altar-site in all but name.

Key contrast Both read this as worship after providence. Arminians typically emphasize Abraham’s responsible calling on the everlasting name, a pattern for trusting prayer; Reformed readers typically emphasize that El Olam is the God whose everlasting purpose has just displayed itself in Isaac, Ishmael’s keeping, and this oath.

Genesis 21:34

34And Abraham sojourned many days in the land of the Philistines.

Arminian

The chapter ends in sojourning, not settlement. Many days in Philistine land: promise still ahead, presence already here.

Classical Arminians take the pilgrim note to heart. Faith lives long stretches as a guest, calling on the Everlasting God while waiting for more of what He promised.

Reformed

“Sojourned many days” refuses a premature conquest story. The well is his; the land is not yet.

Reformed close: the God who visited Sarah also parks Abraham in Philistine country for a long stay. Timing remains His. Chapter 22’s test will find a sojourner, not a king.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
