Passage

Genesis 21

Verses 8–21 · ESV · Voices

Isaac is weaned with a feast; Sarah sees Hagar’s son laughing and demands expulsion. God confirms that offspring will be named through Isaac, yet also promises a nation for the slave woman’s son. Hagar and the boy wander; God hears, opens her eyes to a well, and remains with Ishmael in Paran.

Genesis 21:8

8And the child grew and was weaned. And Abraham made a great feast on the day that Isaac was weaned.

Arminian

Growth and weaning are ordinary mercies after a miraculous birth. Abraham marks the day with a great feast—joy made public.

Arminian reading: gifts are to be celebrated, not hoarded. The household that received Isaac is called to gratitude; feasting is a fitting answer to grace.

Reformed

The promised child lives long enough to be weaned. Providence continues after the birth announcement.

Reformed note: the feast is Abraham’s, but the growth is the LORD’s. The seed is being kept, not merely born.

Genesis 21:9

9But Sarah saw the son of Hagar the Egyptian, whom she had borne to Abraham, laughing.

Arminian

The feast’s edge: Sarah sees Ishmael “laughing”—the same root as Isaac, now possibly mockery (Gal 4:29). Old household tension returns under new light.

Classical Arminians do not excuse cruelty, yet they take the scene honestly. Flesh and promise cannot share the inheritance as equals; the laugh exposes a real collision, and hearts must answer God, not merely protect pride.

Reformed

The narrator names him “the son of Hagar the Egyptian,” not “Abraham’s boy,” preparing the inheritance question.

Reformed reading (with Paul): the laughing may be persecution in seed form. Two sons in one camp make the contrast of flesh and promise visible.

Genesis 21:10

10So she said to Abraham, “Cast out this slave woman with her son, for the son of this slave woman shall not be heir with my son Isaac.”

Arminian

Sarah’s demand is harsh: cast them out; he shall not be heir with Isaac. Paul will cite this as Scripture’s own voice (Gal 4:30)—law-bondage cannot inherit with the child of promise.

Arminian pastoral care refuses to baptize Sarah’s tone while still hearing the theological line: salvation-inheritance comes by promise, received in faith, not by the slave-woman arrangement of chapter 16.

Reformed

Paul treats Sarah’s sentence as the voice of the passage: the slave woman’s son will not inherit with the son of the free woman.

Reformed accent: inheritance is by promise, not by Abraham’s earlier plan B. The line of the Seed is being fenced by God’s purpose, even through a painful household word.

Genesis 21:11

11And the thing was very displeasing to Abraham on account of his son.

Arminian

Abraham is not cold. The thing is “very displeasing… on account of his son.” Love for Ishmael is real; so is the coming command.

Classical Arminians honor this grief. Obedience will not require pretending the boy is nothing. Grace can hold a father’s ache while still ordering the house around the promise.

Reformed

The patriarch’s displeasure is recorded so we will not think the next verse is easy. God will speak into a wounded love.

Reformed comfort: providence is not stoicism. Abraham’s son-love is real; God’s purpose is still larger than that love.

Genesis 21:12

12But God said to Abraham, “Be not displeased because of the boy and because of your slave woman. Whatever Sarah says to you, do as she tells you, for through Isaac shall your offspring be named.

Arminian

God, not Sarah, settles the matter: do as she says, “for through Isaac shall your offspring be named.” Paul and Hebrews cite this as the definition of the promised seed (Rom 9:7; Heb 11:18).

Classical Arminians hear both comfort and call. God cares about Abraham’s displeasure and still directs him. The naming-through-Isaac is God’s chosen way of blessing the nations; faith is summoned to trust that way rather than the household’s older arrangement.

Reformed

The “for” is election in narrative: offspring will be named through Isaac. Romans 9 reads this as God’s freedom to name the children of promise.

Reformed confession: “Be not displeased” is pastoral sovereignty. God does not deny Ishmael’s existence; He locates the covenant name in Isaac according to purpose, not according to Abraham’s grief.

Key contrast Both receive this verse as the hinge of the promised seed (Rom 9:7; Gal 4). Arminians typically stress a real command Abraham must trustingly obey, with blessing still sincerely held out in the Isaac-line; Reformed readers typically stress God’s sovereign naming of offspring through Isaac, the children of promise according to election.

Genesis 21:13

13And I will make a nation of the son of the slave woman also, because he is your offspring.”

Arminian

Mercy is not erased: “I will make a nation of the son of the slave woman also.” Ishmael is still Abraham’s offspring, and God pledges a future.

Arminian hope: particular covenant-naming through Isaac does not mean God is stingy with Hagar’s son. Common kindness and national promise here are real gifts, sincerely spoken over a boy about to walk into the wilderness.

Reformed

“Also” and “because he is your offspring” show that expulsion is not annihilation. God remains generous outside the Isaac-line of naming.

Reformed note: two “I will make” speeches can stand together. Isaac carries the Seed; Ishmael still receives a nation by the same God’s word.

Genesis 21:14

14So Abraham rose early in the morning and took bread and a skin of water and gave it to Hagar, putting it on her shoulder, along with the child, and sent her away. And she departed and wandered in the wilderness of Beersheba.

Arminian

Early morning obedience: bread, water, the child on her shoulder, and she is sent away into Beersheba’s wilderness. The scene is spare and hard.

Classical Arminians do not romanticize it. Abraham obeys; Hagar wanders. Faithfulness to the word can still look like a skin of water and a road—while God remains responsible for the life He has promised to keep.

Reformed

Abraham rises early—the same haste as other costly obediences. Provision is small; the wilderness is named.

Reformed reading: the sending is under the word of vv. 12–13. Wander is not outside providence. The God who named a nation for the boy is already walking that desert.

Genesis 21:15

15When the water in the skin was gone, she put the child under one of the bushes.

Arminian

The skin runs dry. Hagar places the child under a bush—too spent to watch him die in her arms.

Arminian pastoral honesty: human resources end. The story now waits on God. Need this deep is not proof of abandonment; it is the place grace often speaks.

Reformed

Water gone, child under a bush: the narrator lets the crisis bottom out before heaven speaks.

Reformed accent: emptiness of the skin does not empty God’s oath. The boy under the bush is still the boy of v. 13.

Genesis 21:16

16Then she went and sat down opposite him a good way off, about the distance of a bowshot, for she said, “Let me not look on the death of the child.” And as she sat opposite him, she lifted up her voice and wept.

Arminian

A bowshot away, she refuses to watch him die, then weeps. Distance is love trying to survive grief.

Classical Arminians hear a mother’s cry that God will not ignore. Tears are not unbelief’s only language; they can be the last honest prayer when words fail.

Reformed

The geography of grief is measured: opposite him, a bowshot off. Her voice is lifted while the boy’s voice (v. 17) is what God names first.

Reformed comfort: Scripture records the weeping. The wilderness is not mute to heaven.

Genesis 21:17

17And God heard the voice of the boy, and the angel of God called to Hagar from heaven and said to her, “What troubles you, Hagar? Fear not, for God has heard the voice of the boy where he is.

Arminian

God heard the boy; the angel calls Hagar by name: Fear not. Hearing is the theology of this verse—the God who hears where the child actually is.

Arminian gospel note: Hagar is addressed again (cf. ch. 16). Grace finds her in the wilderness with a sincere “fear not,” summoning her to rise rather than resign.

Reformed

“God heard” governs the rescue. The angel from heaven knows her name and the boy’s location.

Reformed assurance: “where he is” is precise providence. The child of the slave woman is not off the map. Fear not rests on God’s hearing, not on Hagar’s remaining water.

Genesis 21:18

18Up! Lift up the boy, and hold him fast with your hand, for I will make him into a great nation.”

Arminian

Command and promise together: Up, lift, hold fast—for I will make him a great nation. Hagar must take hold of the boy God has already claimed.

Classical Arminians hear enabling summons. The “I will” is God’s; the lifting is hers under that word. Grace does not leave her sitting a bowshot away.

Reformed

“Up!” is resurrection language for a mother who had sat down to die. The “for” grounds her grip in God’s national “I will.”

Reformed reading: God repeats v. 13 to the woman herself. The great nation is His making; her hand is the appointed means.

Genesis 21:19

19Then God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water. And she went and filled the skin with water and gave the boy a drink.

Arminian

The well was there to be seen; God opened her eyes. She fills the skin and gives the boy a drink—mercy that uses her hands.

Arminian emphasis: illumination is gift, and the seeing leads to responsible care. Opened eyes still walk to the well; grace restores both sight and service.

Reformed

Opened eyes precede the well. Provision was not absent; perception was.

Reformed accent: God gives the seeing, then the drink follows. The boy lives because heaven opened what Hagar could not find.

Genesis 21:20

20And God was with the boy, and he grew up. He lived in the wilderness and became an expert with the bow.

Arminian

“God was with the boy” is the wilderness’s true climate. He grows, lives, and learns the bow. Presence is not only for Isaac’s tent.

Classical Arminians receive this as real accompanying kindness. The God who heard him stays with him; the boy’s future skill sits under that with-ness.

Reformed

With-ness, growth, wilderness, bow: four gifts of providence outside the promised-land plot.

Reformed note: “God was with” is the same kind of phrase later said of Isaac and Jacob. Ishmael is not the Seed, yet he is not God-forsaken.

Genesis 21:21

21He lived in the wilderness of Paran, and his mother took a wife for him from the land of Egypt.

Arminian

Paran becomes home; Hagar takes him an Egyptian wife. The line continues, tied to her land as well as to Abraham’s blood.

Arminian pastoral close: God kept the boy alive and let a household form. Mercy in the desert had a next chapter, even far from Isaac’s feast.

Reformed

Geography and marriage close the unit: Paran, a wife from Egypt. The nation-promise of vv. 13 and 18 has begun to take domestic shape.

Reformed reading: the narrator leaves Ishmael provided for, then returns to Abraham. God’s purposes for both sons run, without confusing which son carries the name.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Hagar and Ishmael sent out, and God hearing the boy’s voice—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 21:8–21.

Arminian Notable voices

“God heard the voice of the lad—We read not of a word be said; but his sighs and groans, cried loud in the ears of the God of mercy. An angel was sent to comfort Hagar, who assures her, God has heard the voice of the lad where he is—Though he be in the wilderness; for wherever we are, there is a way open heavenwards; therefore lift up the lad, and hold him in thy hand—God's readiness to help us when we are in trouble must not slacken, but quicken our endeavours to help ourselves.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 21:17)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses had said before that Hagar wept: how is it then, that, disregarding her tears, God only hears the voice of the lad ? If we should say, that the mother did not deserve to receive a favorable answer to her prayers; her son, certainly, was in no degree more worthy. For, as to the supposition of some, that they both were brought to repentance by this chastisement, it is but an uncertain conjecture.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 21:17)
