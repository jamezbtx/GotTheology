Passage

Genesis 35

Verses 1–15 · ESV · Voices

After Shechem’s violence, God sends Jacob back to Bethel: arise, dwell, make an altar to the God who appeared when he fled Esau. Jacob puts away foreign gods; a terror from God keeps the cities from pursuit. He builds an altar at El-bethel, buries Deborah at Allon-bacuth, and God appears again—renaming him Israel, promising fruitfulness, kings, and land—then raises a pillar, pours a drink offering, and names the place Bethel.

Genesis 35:1

1God said to Jacob, “Arise, go up to Bethel and dwell there. Make an altar there to the God who appeared to you when you fled from your brother Esau.”

Arminian

God’s word is a real summons: arise, go, dwell, make an altar. He points Jacob back to the God who met him in flight—sincere call after Shechem’s wreckage.

Classical Arminians hear enabling grace Jacob must obey. The God who was with him when he fled now calls him home; the response remains his, not forced.

Reformed

The command comes from God, not from Jacob’s piety. Bethel is the place of prior appearance; the Lord still governs the road after chapter 34.

Reformed accent: providence and covenant faithfulness. The God of the flight now orders the return so the promise line is not lost in Shechem’s fallout.

Key contrast Both hear a real command to Bethel. Arminians typically stress a sincere, resistible call Jacob must obey; Reformed readers typically stress that God effectually turns him back to the place of promise.

Genesis 35:2

2So Jacob said to his household and to all who were with him, “Put away the foreign gods that are among you and purify yourselves and change your garments.

Arminian

Jacob turns the call into household repentance: put away the gods, purify, change garments. Idols from the journey—and from Shechem—must actually be surrendered.

Arminian pastoral note: grace enables this putting-away; it does not happen by magic. A sincere summons to holiness still requires responsible response from the whole camp.

Reformed

Before the altar, the household is cleansed. Foreign gods cannot travel with the God of Bethel.

Reformed comfort: God keeps a people by also requiring their exclusive worship. Covenant faithfulness is not God ignoring idols; it is God reclaiming the line for Himself.

Key contrast Both refuse syncretism. Arminians typically stress a real, grace-enabled turning from idols the household might have kept; Reformed readers typically stress that the God of the promise will not share Jacob’s house with other gods.

Genesis 35:3

3Then let us arise and go up to Bethel, so that I may make there an altar to the God who answers me in the day of my distress and has been with me wherever I have gone.”

Arminian

Jacob names God as the One who answers in distress and has been with him wherever he has gone. The confession is personal: flight, Laban, Esau, Shechem—and still accompanied.

Classical Arminians love this: God’s presence in flight and return is faithful help, not coercion. Jacob now leads the camp to respond to the God who has already answered.

Reformed

“Who answers… and has been with me” is the pilgrim’s theology of providence. The same God of Genesis 28 now claims the return.

Reformed note: covenant presence is not a mood. God kept him on every road so that Bethel’s altar would be reached.

Genesis 35:4

4So they gave to Jacob all the foreign gods that they had, and the rings that were in their ears. Jacob hid them under the terebinth tree that was near Shechem.

Arminian

They actually give the gods and the rings; Jacob hides them. Repentance has a hole in the ground near Shechem—concrete, not theatrical.

Arminian emphasis: the household’s willing surrender matters. Enabling grace meets a responsible handing-over; buried idols are a real choice.

Reformed

The terebinth by Shechem becomes a grave for rivals. God has been bringing this camp to a clean road.

Reformed accent: providence uses Jacob’s act, but the deeper keeping is the Lord’s. The line will not carry Shechem’s gods into Bethel.

Genesis 35:5

5And as they journeyed, a terror from God fell upon the cities that were around them, so that they did not pursue the sons of Jacob.

Arminian

Cities around them might have hunted Jacob after Simeon and Levi’s swords. Instead a terror from God falls; they are not pursued.

Classical Arminians receive this as protecting mercy sincerely given to a threatened family—help they did not earn by the violence of chapter 34.

Reformed

“A terror from God” is the narrator’s verdict. Neighbor cities stay home because the Lord restrains them.

Reformed comfort: the promise line is kept by God’s hand on other people’s fear, not by Jacob’s numbers. Providence can halt a justly provoked pursuit.

Genesis 35:6

6And Jacob came to Luz (that is, Bethel), which is in the land of Canaan, he and all the people who were with him,

Arminian

Arrival is named twice: Luz, that is Bethel, in Canaan, with all the people. The call of verse 1 is met by a real coming.

Arminian reading: grace enabled the journey; they still had to travel. Obedience is geographical, not only inward.

Reformed

The old name and the theophany-name sit together in Canaan. God brings the whole camp to the spot He appointed.

Reformed note: getting there after Shechem is covenant faithfulness. The land of promise holds them because He said “dwell there.”

Genesis 35:7

7and there he built an altar and called the place El-bethel, because there God had revealed himself to him when he fled from his brother.

Arminian

He builds the altar and names the place El-bethel—“God of Bethel”—because God revealed Himself here in flight. Memory becomes worship.

Classical Arminians hear grateful response: the God who appeared in flight is honored on return. The name is Jacob’s responsible confession, not a forced script.

Reformed

El-bethel locates God, not Jacob, at the center of the place. The flight-revelation is the reason for the altar.

Reformed accent: the God who revealed Himself then now receives worship as the Keeper of that same man. Covenant memory is God’s gift before it is Jacob’s monument.

Genesis 35:8

8And Deborah, Rebekah’s nurse, died, and she was buried under an oak below Bethel. So he called its name Allon-bacuth.

Arminian

Rebekah’s nurse dies and is buried under an oak; the place is named Allon-bacuth, oak of weeping. Grief is allowed on the road of obedience.

Arminian pastoral note: returning to Bethel does not cancel tears. God is with the household in mourning as in flight; sorrow is a human, honest response.

Reformed

Even a nurse’s death is written into the promise’s itinerary. The oak of weeping stands below Bethel, not outside God’s care.

Reformed comfort: providence includes funerals. The God of the altar is not embarrassed by Allon-bacuth; He still holds the line while they bury the old home’s last tie.

Genesis 35:9

9God appeared to Jacob again, when he came from Paddan-aram, and blessed him.

Arminian

God appears again, after Paddan-aram, and blesses him. Appearance and blessing are gift, sincerely given to the returned pilgrim.

Classical Arminians receive blessing as God’s kind initiative that still calls for trust. The God of flight now meets him in the land as benefactor, not as fate.

Reformed

“Appeared again” ties this meeting to the first Bethel and to Paddan-aram’s long road. Blessing is God’s act.

Reformed accent: covenant faithfulness shows up as theophany. The same Lord who sent him out now blesses him at the return—election of Israel taking public form.

Genesis 35:10

10And God said to him, “Your name is Jacob; no longer shall your name be called Jacob, but Israel shall be your name.” So he called his name Israel.

Arminian

God names him Israel again, confirming Peniel. The old name is not the last word; a new identity is spoken and must be lived.

Arminian emphasis: the name is a sincere gift and a vocation. Jacob is called to walk as Israel; grace enables that walk without erasing responsible response.

Reformed

God speaks the name; God calls him Israel. Peniel’s wrestling is here publicly settled.

Reformed comfort: the people of God are named by God’s mouth. Election of Israel is not Jacob’s self-rebranding; it is the Lord’s claim on the promise line.

Key contrast Both hear a real renaming. Arminians typically stress a sincere new identity Jacob must live into by grace-enabled faith; Reformed readers typically stress that God effectually constitutes Israel by His own word.

Genesis 35:11

11And God said to him, “I am God Almighty: be fruitful and multiply. A nation and a company of nations shall come from you, and kings shall come from your own body.

Arminian

God Almighty commands fruitfulness and promises a nation, a company of nations, and kings from Jacob’s body. The word is as large as Abraham’s.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere, enabling promise: God can make this true, and Jacob is to receive it in trust, not treat it as a closed private fate.

Reformed

“I am God Almighty” grounds the seed-and-kings word. Fruitfulness is commanded because God has power to perform it.

Reformed accent: this is the Abrahamic oath restated. Kings from Jacob’s body are God’s covenant purpose, not a wish Jacob must independently fulfill.

Key contrast Both receive El Shaddai’s fruitfulness as grace. Arminians typically stress a sincere promise to be trusted and not resisted; Reformed readers typically stress God’s almighty “I am” that will surely raise nation, nations, and kings.

Genesis 35:12

12The land that I gave to Abraham and Isaac I will give to you, and I will give the land to your offspring after you.”

Arminian

The land given to Abraham and Isaac is now pledged to Jacob and his offspring. The gift is personal and generational, held out to be believed.

Arminian pastoral reading: God sincerely grants what He already swore. The heirs still sojourn; faith receives a land not yet possessed in full.

Reformed

The same land, the same Giver. “I will give” twice over—Jacob and the offspring after him.

Reformed note: covenant faithfulness is legal and locative. God keeps the promise line by deeding Canaan through the patriarchs; election of Israel includes soil, not only a name.

Key contrast Both treat the land as God’s gift, not Jacob’s spoils. Arminians typically hear a sincere grant the family must keep receiving in faith; Reformed readers typically hear a unilateral covenant transfer God Himself will perform.

Genesis 35:13

13Then God went up from him in the place where he had spoken with him.

Arminian

God goes up from the place of speech. The appearance ends; Jacob is left to obey what he heard.

Classical Arminians do not treat departure as abandonment. The sincere word remains; responsible worship must now answer a God who has spoken and withdrawn from sight.

Reformed

The going-up marks a completed theophany, as with Abraham. God is not a fixture Jacob can hold.

Reformed comfort: the Lord who withdraws still binds Himself by the words just spoken. Providence continues when vision ceases.

Genesis 35:14

14And Jacob set up a pillar in the place where he had spoken with him, a pillar of stone. He poured out a drink offering on it and poured oil on it.

Arminian

Jacob answers with a stone pillar, a drink offering, and oil. Worship is costly and physical—his responsible reply to the God who appeared.

Arminian note: grace that blessed him now enables this offering. The ritual is not payment for the name; it is grateful, uncoerced devotion.

Reformed

Pillar, libation, oil: the place of speech becomes a memorial. Jacob marks what God did.

Reformed accent: the offering is fruit of blessing already given. Covenant faithfulness on God’s side draws out worship; the promise does not wait on the pour.

Genesis 35:15

15So Jacob called the name of the place where God had spoken with him Bethel.

Arminian

He names the place Bethel—house of God—where God had spoken with him. The name matches the first night, now confirmed in daylight.

Classical Arminians hear a fitting, free confession: this ground is God’s house because God spoke. Naming is faith’s response, not a spell.

Reformed

Bethel is named because God spoke, not because Jacob felt. The house is God’s.

Reformed note: the promise’s geography is marked by God’s mouth. He keeps the line by tying this man to this place with a name that will catechize Israel.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on back to Bethel, foreign gods put away, and the name Israel—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 35:1–15.

Arminian Notable voices

“Arise go to Bethel—Here God minds Jacob of his vow at Beth-el, and sends him thither to perform it, Jacob had said in the day of his distress, If I come again in peace, this stone shall be God's house, Ge 28:22. God had performed his part, had given Jacob more than bread to eat, and raiment to put on; but it should seem he had forgotten his vow, or, at least, deferred the performance of it.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 35:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses relates that when Jacob had been reduced to the last extremity, God came to his help in the right time, and as at the critical juncture. We must, however, observe the order of his procedure; for God did not immediately appear to his servant, but suffered him first to be tormented by grief and excessive cares, that he might learn patience, deferring his consolation to the time of extreme necessity.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 35:1)
