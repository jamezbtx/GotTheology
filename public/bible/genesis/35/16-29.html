Passage

Genesis 35

Verses 16–29 · ESV · Voices

From Bethel the family travels toward Ephrath; Rachel dies in hard labor after naming Ben-oni, whom Jacob calls Benjamin, and she is buried on the way to Bethlehem. Israel camps beyond the tower of Eder; Reuben lies with Bilhah; the twelve sons are listed. Jacob reaches Isaac at Hebron, and Isaac dies at 180, gathered to his people—buried by Esau and Jacob.

Genesis 35:16

16Then they journeyed from Bethel. When they were still some distance from Ephrath, Rachel went into labor, and she had hard labor.

Arminian

The road from Bethel is not ease: still short of Ephrath, Rachel’s labor is hard. Obedience and pain can share a mile.

Arminian pastoral note: God’s enabling presence in flight and return does not cancel creaturely suffering. Rachel’s struggle is real; grace does not make her a prop.

Reformed

Promise-road and hard labor arrive together. Providence is not a cushion; it is God’s rule in the pain.

Reformed comfort: the line that will include Benjamin is being kept even here. God does not step out of the story because the labor is hard.

Genesis 35:17

17And when her labor was at its hardest, the midwife said to her, “Do not fear, for you have another son.”

Arminian

At the hardest point the midwife speaks courage: do not fear, you have another son. The word is human kindness and true news.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere comfort offered in crisis. Fear is met with a reason; Rachel is addressed as a person who can still receive hope.

Reformed

The second son of Rachel arrives under a midwife’s “do not fear.” God had opened this hope long before (30:24).

Reformed accent: providence uses an ordinary voice at the worst moment. The promise of another son is being kept while the mother is at the edge.

Genesis 35:18

18And as her soul was departing (for she was dying), she called his name Ben-oni; but his father called him Benjamin.

Arminian

As her soul departs she names him Ben-oni, son of my sorrow; the father names him Benjamin, son of the right hand. Both names tell the truth of the hour.

Arminian reading: Rachel’s dying word is hers, not overwritten as unreal. Jacob’s rename is a responsible act of hope—sorrow acknowledged, the child not left under a name of only grief.

Reformed

Two names, one boy: sorrow and right-hand strength. The father speaks the name that will stand in Israel.

Reformed note: God keeps the twelfth son even as the beloved wife dies. Election of the tribes does not require that God author the pain; He weaves Benjamin into the line through a death He did not need as a scheme of cruelty.

Key contrast Both honor both names. Arminians typically stress Rachel’s real dying agency and Jacob’s responsible hope; Reformed readers typically stress that God still gives the tribe of the right hand without making Himself the author of the mother’s death.

Genesis 35:19

19So Rachel died, and she was buried on the way to Ephrath (that is, Bethlehem),

Arminian

Rachel dies and is buried on the way—Ephrath, that is Bethlehem. The beloved is not carried home; the grave is a roadside fact.

Arminian pastoral note: faith does not deny the burial. God was with this family in flight and return, and He is not absent from this grave; grief remains a human, honest response.

Reformed

The narrator locates the tomb on the Bethlehem road. Promise geography now includes a grave.

Reformed comfort: covenant faithfulness can hold a burial. The line continues toward Hebron while Rachel rests where God, not chance, has placed this milestone.

Genesis 35:20

20and Jacob set up a pillar over her tomb. It is the pillar of Rachel’s tomb, which is there to this day.

Arminian

Jacob sets a pillar over her tomb, still known “to this day.” Love marks the place; memory is a responsible kindness.

Classical Arminians see no conflict between mourning and faith. The God who accompanied Jacob now meets him as a widower; the pillar is devotion, not unbelief.

Reformed

A second pillar in the chapter: one for Bethel’s speech, one for Rachel’s grave. God allows both to stand.

Reformed accent: providence writes a memorial into the land. The promise line will pass this pillar; God keeps Israel without erasing the cost along the road.

Genesis 35:21

21Israel journeyed on and pitched his tent beyond the tower of Eder.

Arminian

Israel journeys on and pitches beyond the tower of Eder. Grief does not freeze the camp; he still moves as a responsible shepherd of the household.

Arminian note: enabling grace for the next mile is real. The new name “Israel” is now the traveler’s name on a hard road, freely taken.

Reformed

The narrator calls him Israel on the far side of Rachel’s death. The flock moves because God still leads.

Reformed comfort: the promise does not camp forever at the tomb. Providence pushes the line on toward the next scene, tower of Eder included.

Genesis 35:22

22While Israel lived in that land, Reuben went and lay with Bilhah his father’s concubine. And Israel heard of it. Now the sons of Jacob were twelve.

Arminian

Reuben lies with Bilhah; Israel hears of it. Then, in the same verse, the sons are twelve. Sin is named as Reuben’s act, not as a necessity.

Classical Arminians refuse to make God the author of this incestuous grab. Responsible, resistible lust wounds the household; grace had enabled a better path Reuben did not take. The twelve are still counted.

Reformed

The crime is Reuben’s; the hearing is Israel’s; the twelve are still God’s. Birthright will later move (49:4; 1 Chr 5:1), but the people remain.

Reformed accent: election of Israel does not make God the author of Reuben’s sin. Providence can judge the firstborn’s outrage and still keep twelve tribes under the promise.

Key contrast Both condemn Reuben without blaming God. Arminians typically stress a resistible, responsible violation Reuben chose; Reformed readers typically stress that God keeps the twelve and later reorders the birthright without being the author of the sin.

Genesis 35:23

23The sons of Leah: Reuben (Jacob’s firstborn), Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, and Zebulun.

Arminian

Leah’s six are listed, Reuben still called firstborn. The family record is honest: status named, sin not erased from the previous verse.

Arminian reading: each son is a person under a sincere God, not a pawn. The household remains accountable as a real family, not a mere diagram of fate.

Reformed

The list begins with Leah’s sons, firstborn included. God counts the tribes He will keep.

Reformed note: covenant faithfulness writes names. The promise line is a people with mothers and an order—not an idea detached from these six.

Genesis 35:24

24The sons of Rachel: Joseph and Benjamin.

Arminian

Rachel’s two: Joseph and Benjamin. The dead mother’s sons are not dropped from the count. Gift and grief stand together.

Classical Arminians receive both boys as grace to be cherished. Joseph’s later story will still require responsible choices; Benjamin is already more than a sorrow-name.

Reformed

Rachel’s pair complete the beloved wife’s share. The future savior of the family (Joseph) sits beside the late-born son.

Reformed accent: God stocks the line through Rachel as through Leah. Providence has already placed Joseph in the list that famine and Egypt will need.

Genesis 35:25

25The sons of Bilhah, Rachel’s servant: Dan and Naphtali.

Arminian

Bilhah’s sons are named after the outrage against their mother. They are not reduced to Reuben’s crime.

Arminian pastoral note: God sincerely regards these boys as Jacob’s sons. Status as children of a servant does not cancel their belonging or their future accountability.

Reformed

Dan and Naphtali are counted inside Israel, not as an appendix. The promise takes in the sons of the concubine.

Reformed comfort: election of the tribes is wider than favored wives. God keeps Bilhah’s children in the twelve without endorsing how they were conceived in the rivalry of chapter 30.

Genesis 35:26

26The sons of Zilpah, Leah’s servant: Gad and Asher. These were the sons of Jacob who were born to him in Paddan-aram.

Arminian

Zilpah’s sons complete the twelve; the narrator sums them as born in Paddan-aram. The God who was with Jacob in the far country now numbers every son as fruit of that long stay.

Classical Arminians hear a family God accompanied in sojourn. Grace attended the eastern years; the return is not an empty camp.

Reformed

The Paddan-aram summary folds the household into one sentence. God brought a full twelve out of the sojourn.

Reformed note: covenant faithfulness counted sons in Laban’s house. The return is not empty; the promise line is already a people.

Genesis 35:27

27And Jacob came to his father Isaac at Mamre, or Kiriath-arba (that is, Hebron), where Abraham and Isaac had sojourned.

Arminian

The long road ends at Isaac’s tent in Hebron, where Abraham and Isaac sojourned. Reunion is gift after decades.

Arminian emphasis: Jacob actually comes—responsible return to the father he fled. The God who was with him in flight now lets him stand again in the ancestral camp.

Reformed

Mamre, Kiriath-arba, Hebron: the old sojourning ground. God brings the heir back to the heir.

Reformed comfort: providence closes a loop Abraham’s God opened. The promise line is physically reunited in the land, not left scattered in Paddan-aram.

Genesis 35:28

28Now the days of Isaac were 180 years.

Arminian

Isaac’s days are 180 years. A long life is numbered, not mythic. Years are a created gift to receive with thanks.

Classical Arminians read the number as God’s kindness in time, not a mechanical quota. Full years still leave the man a responsible sojourner to the end.

Reformed

The clock of the second patriarch is finished. One hundred eighty years under promise.

Reformed accent: God allots the span. Covenant faithfulness includes the length of Isaac’s life; the oath does not die with his breath still coming.

Genesis 35:29

29And Isaac breathed his last, and he died and was gathered to his people, old and full of days. And his sons Esau and Jacob buried him.

Arminian

Isaac dies, old and full of days, gathered to his people. Esau and Jacob bury him together—the last fraternal act in this chapter.

Arminian pastoral note: brothers who once split over a blessing now share a grave’s work. Grace that sought peace at Peniel still invites responsible honor of a father; neither son is a puppet at the burial.

Reformed

Gathered to his people: the narrator’s hope-language for the patriarch. Both sons stand at the grave.

Reformed comfort: the promise does not fail when Isaac is buried. God keeps the line through Jacob while Esau also does this good; providence can use both brothers at a funeral without confusing their callings.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Rachel’s death, Benjamin, and Isaac gathered to his people—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 35:16–29.

Arminian Notable voices

“But Jacob because he would not renew the sorrowful remembrance of his mother's death every time he called his son by name, changed his name, and called him Benjamin, the son of my right hand—That is, very dear to me; set on my right hand for a right hand blessing; the support of my age, like the staff in my right hand. Jacob buried her near the place where she died.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 35:18)

Reformed Notable voices

“We know that the ancients were very desirous of offspring, especially of male offspring. Since Rachel therefore does not accept this kind of consolation when offered, we infer that she was completely oppressed with pain. She therefore died in agonies, thinking of nothing but her sad childbirth and her own sorrows: from the feeling of which she gave a name to her son; but Jacob afterwards corrected the error.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 35:17)
