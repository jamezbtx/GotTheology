Passage

Genesis 2

Verses 1–3 · ESV

Genesis 2 opens by closing the creation week: heavens, earth, and their host are finished, and God rests, blesses, and makes holy the seventh day. Verses 1–3 are the Sabbath hinge between the six days of chapter 1 and the garden narrative that follows—rest after work, consecration after completion.

Genesis 2:1

1Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them.

Arminian

“Finished” and “host” close the week: heavens, earth, and all their array stand complete. Nothing is left half-made; the world is a furnished house, not a rough sketch still waiting for rival hands.

Classical Arminians gladly confess a completed creation given in goodness. The host is God’s, marshalled and sufficient; later Sabbath rest will be invitation to delight in a work already done, not a scramble to finish what He left undone.

Reformed

Moses sums the six days: heavens and earth finished, and all their host. The epilogue undercuts any notion that the world sprang up in an instant without ordered work, or that it remains an unfinished chaos.

Reformed accent: completion is God’s act. The world’s furniture—sun and stars, plants and living things—is His abundance. Providence will preserve what creation has now brought to its intended fullness.

Genesis 2:2

2And on the seventh day God finished his work that he had done, and he rested on the seventh day from all his work that he had done.

Arminian

On the seventh day God finished His work and rested from all He had done. Rest here is not fatigue; the Eternal is well-pleased, ceasing from making new kinds while He still upholds what He made.

Arminian pastoral note: the pattern is gift. Work six, rest one—not because God needed sleep, but so creatures might share His complacency in the finished world. Rest is for holiness and comfort, a means of grace later named Sabbath, sincerely held out to be received.

Reformed

God finished and rested on the seventh day from all His work. He did not withdraw His hand from preserving the world; He ceased from creating new kinds, putting the last touch so that nothing essential was wanting.

Reformed comfort: rest marks perfection of the fabric, not divine idleness. The same Lord who worked six days still governs; the seventh day’s rest is His example and appointment, a creation ordinance before Sinai ever spoke.

Genesis 2:3

3So God blessed the seventh day and made it holy, because on it God rested from all his work that he had done in creation.

Arminian

God blessed the seventh day and made it holy because He rested from all His work in creation. Blessing and sanctifying set this day apart: holy rest and holy work, as old as the world.

Classical Arminians, with Wesley, receive the Sabbath as an ordinance instituted in innocency for the church’s good. The day is honorably given so we may rest in the Creator and take complacency in Him; grace enables the keeping, and the invitation is as wide as the week God made.

Reformed

Blessing and making holy interpret the rest: God separates the seventh day from the common number and claims its meditations. He rested, then blessed that rest so that in all ages it might be held sacred among men.

Reformed reading: the day’s design is worship, not bare leisure. Calvin distinguished later ceremonial shadows fulfilled in Christ from the perpetual call to cease from our own works and dedicate a day to God—the Creator’s own example still tutoring the human race.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
