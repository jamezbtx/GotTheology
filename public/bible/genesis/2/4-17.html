Passage

Genesis 2

Verses 4–17 · ESV · Voices

After the seventh day, Moses retells humanity’s making with the covenant name LORD God. Verses 4–17 move from dusty ground to a planted garden: the man formed and enlivened, placed in Eden to work and keep it, given every tree but one, and warned that eating of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil means death. Lavish provision surrounds a real command.

Genesis 2:4

4These are the generations of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens.

Arminian

“These are the generations” retells origin: heavens and earth when they were created, in the day the LORD God made earth and heavens. The covenant name now joins Elohim—Yahweh God as Maker.

Classical Arminians hear a personal God, not an anonymous force. The world has a beginning and an Author; remembering that origin directs praise to Him who freely made, rather than to the creature treating itself as self-born.

Reformed

Generations here means origin, not offspring. Moses inculcates that the world existed only from the time it was created, under the name LORD God.

Reformed confession: this repetition guards God’s glory against those who would feign an eternal world. Heaven and earth, by synecdoche the whole, come from the self-existent LORD who made them—history starts with His making.

Genesis 2:5

5When no bush of the field was yet in the land and no small plant of the field had yet sprung up—for the LORD God had not caused it to rain on the land, and there was no man to work the ground,

Arminian

No bush or small plant of the field had yet sprung up: the LORD God had not caused rain, and there was no man to work the ground. Field-growth is tied to God’s gift of rain and to human vocation.

Arminian note: means are real. Rain and tilling will matter, yet both wait on the LORD who opens the heavens and who is about to form a worker. Dependence and calling arrive together, not as rivals.

Reformed

Plants of the field had not yet sprung, because God had not caused rain and man was not yet there to work the ground. Moses explains a manner of production different from later seed and sweat.

Reformed accent: God shuts and opens the rain; culture is assistant, not lord. The ground’s fruitfulness is His to give, and the missing worker is a creature He is about to make—not a gap nature fills on its own.

Genesis 2:6

6and a mist was going up from the land and was watering the whole face of the ground—

Arminian

A mist was going up from the land, watering the whole face of the ground. Before ordinary rain and human irrigation, God still watered what He would plant.

Classical Arminians see kindness in the mist: the earth is not left parched while man is yet unformed. Providence cares for the ground that will soon feed a living creature.

Reformed

Mist (or spring) waters the whole face of the ground. God supplies moisture without the later pattern of rain and plow.

Reformed comfort: even the watering is His. Secondary means will come; this clause shows the land already under His hand, prepared for the man He is about to form from its dust.

Genesis 2:7

7then the LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature.

Arminian

The LORD God formed the man of dust from the ground, breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living creature. Body from earth, life from God’s breath: a little world of heaven and earth joined.

Classical Arminians, with Wesley, hear both humility and dignity. Dust forbids pride; the breath that makes a living soul is God’s immediate gift, fitting a creature who can know and answer Him—formed with care, not flung into being as the beasts were.

Reformed

Formed of dust, then inbreathed: the man becomes a living creature. Calvin marked gradations—clay, vital breath, and the image already named in chapter 1—so that no one exults in flesh, and no one denies the soul’s rank.

Reformed note: origin in dust lays us under obligation and under lowliness. Life is not an intrinsic spark we own; it proceeds from the LORD who breathed. The later fall will not erase that we were made this way, only how far we have declined from original integrity.

Genesis 2:8

8And the LORD God planted a garden in Eden, in the east, and there he put the man whom he had formed.

Arminian

The LORD God planted a garden in Eden, in the east, and put there the man He had formed. Residence is gift: a real place of delight, not a mist of allegory, appointed for this firstborn of humankind.

Arminian pastoral weight: God places before He commands. The man does not find or seize Eden; he is put in a planted garden. Grace in creation looks like a home prepared, which the man may receive with gratitude or later forfeit by unbelief.

Reformed

God planted and put: Eden in the east, the formed man set in it. Moses speaks of a region on the earth, selected and embellished, not a fable in the air.

Reformed reading: placement is providence. The Lord assigns the first man his habitation as a token of special favor; dominion over the earth will be learned first in a garden God Himself planted.

Genesis 2:9

9And out of the ground the LORD God made to spring up every tree that is pleasant to the sight and good for food. The tree of life was in the midst of the garden, and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil.

Arminian

Every tree pleasant to the sight and good for food springs up, with two extraordinary trees in the midst: the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. Beauty, provision, and a revealed test stand together.

Classical Arminians, with Wesley, take the tree of life as a sign of continued life upon perseverance in innocency, and the prohibited tree as a positive law by which Adam might know good and evil. Superfluity of good gifts left no excuse for reaching what God had named off-limits.

Reformed

Out of the ground God made every delightful and nourishing tree spring, and named two by their effect: life, and the knowledge of good and evil.

Reformed confession: the tree of life is sacrament-like memorial of life received from God, not a pagan elixir; the second tree is prohibited so that man might not, trusting his own understanding, cast off God’s yoke. Lavish plenty makes the later transgression the more inexcusable.

Genesis 2:10

10A river flowed out of Eden to water the garden, and there it divided and became four rivers.

Arminian

A river flowed out of Eden to water the garden, then divided into four. The place of delight is well-watered; fruitfulness has a source upstream of human skill.

Arminian note: God waters what He plants. The splitting streams serve pleasantness and fertility; readers are not required to map every ancient channel in order to receive the point—Eden’s life is gift from the LORD’s geography of kindness.

Reformed

One river waters the garden and becomes four heads. Moses describes a real, well-watered tract, not a mythic fog.

Reformed accent: the garden’s irrigation is arranged, not accidental. Whatever later debate attends Pishon and Gihon, the theological line is plain: the man’s first home sat where God made water serve the planted place.

Genesis 2:11

11The name of the first is the Pishon. It is the one that flowed around the whole land of Havilah, where there is gold.

Arminian

The first river is the Pishon, flowing around Havilah, where there is gold. Named lands and precious metal locate Eden in a world of real places, not a never-never.

Classical Arminians can leave cartography modest and still hear the lesson: gold is noted, yet it is not the garden’s true treasure. Communion with God and the tree of life outvalue Havilah’s ore.

Reformed

Pishon and Havilah, with gold, specify a region. Moses writes as a historian of origins, naming what his first hearers could place on a map.

Reformed instinct: particularity honors providence. God did not lodge man in a generic “somewhere”; He set him in a watered land whose neighbors had gold—good gifts in their place, never rivals to the Giver.

Genesis 2:12

12And the gold of that land is good; bdellium and onyx stone are there.

Arminian

The gold of that land is good; bdellium and onyx are there. Moses lingers on excellence of materials around Eden’s rivers.

Arminian pastoral reading: created goods are truly good. The later fall will twist greed, not prove that gold and stone were snares from the start; the garden’s wealth is meant for grateful use under God.

Reformed

Good gold, bdellium, and onyx: the surrounding land is rich. Moses records quality, not a command to hoard.

Reformed note: the Creator stocks the earth with things of beauty and worth. Such gifts display His bounty; they do not compete with Him, and they cannot replace the life signified in the garden’s midst.

Genesis 2:13

13The name of the second river is the Gihon. It is the one that flowed around the whole land of Cush.

Arminian

The second river is the Gihon, flowing around the land of Cush. Another named stream, another named land: the garden sits in a networked world.

Classical Arminians hear a Creator of nations’ later homes already tracing waters. Geography is not a distraction from theology; it is theology in soil and stream—the LORD God of a real earth.

Reformed

Gihon and Cush continue the fourfold division. The text keeps Eden earthly and locatable in principle, even where modern identification of every name is debated.

Reformed comfort: Scripture’s God is not the author of a parable without a place. He waters lands He will later call, judge, and bless; this river is already His.

Genesis 2:14

14And the name of the third river is the Tigris, which flows east of Assyria. And the fourth river is the Euphrates.

Arminian

The third river is the Tigris, east of Assyria; the fourth is the Euphrates. Two names still known fasten the narrative to Mesopotamia’s world.

Arminian note: faith is not asked to float Eden off the map. The same God who named these rivers will later call Abram from a related country; history of redemption roots in the earth He made.

Reformed

Tigris and Euphrates close the four heads—rivers no reader can treat as mere symbols. Moses ties paradise to known watercourses.

Reformed reading: particular rivers serve a particular man under a particular Lord. Providence that named the Euphrates here will later steer empires along its banks; the garden’s waters already belong to His government.

Genesis 2:15

15The LORD God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to work it and keep it.

Arminian

The LORD God took the man and put him in Eden to work it and keep it. Vocation precedes the fall: labor is not punishment yet, but pleasant charge—cultivate and guard.

Classical Arminians receive work as original goodness. Man is made for fruitful activity under God, not idle consumption; the garden is held in trust, a stewardship that can be faithfully kept or later neglected when the command is despised.

Reformed

Took and put, to work and keep: office as well as privilege. Calvin heard here a condemnation of indolent repose—man created to be occupied, a steward of what God committed to his hand.

Reformed accent: possession is conditional on care. We hold the earth as God’s stewards, to hand it on uninjured; the garden’s culture is the first liturgy of daily obedience, before any thorn has sprung.

Genesis 2:16

16And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, “You may surely eat of every tree of the garden,

Arminian

The LORD God commanded the man: “You may surely eat of every tree of the garden.” Law begins with largesse; the prohibition of verse 17 will sit inside permission this wide.

Arminian pastoral weight: God is generous Lawgiver. The man in innocency is addressed as one who can hear and obey; the command is real speech to a responsible creature, not a trap sprung on a puppet. Liberty to eat is the first word.

Reformed

Commanded, saying he may surely eat of every tree. Subjection to God is marked by a law, and the law opens with profusion.

Reformed confession: man governs the world only as one himself governed. The wide permission commends God’s liberality so that the one reserve which follows is unmistakably a test of obedience, not stinginess.

Genesis 2:17

17but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die.”

Arminian

One tree is fenced: of the knowledge of good and evil he shall not eat, or in that day he shall surely die. Wesley heard loss of happiness, liability to death, and spiritual death seizing at once—not a hollow threat.

Classical Arminians read a genuine probation. Innocency included power to obey; the warning is sincere. Death is the stated wage of transgression, and the man’s later eating will be responsible disobedience, not the unveiling of a secret decree that made the command unreal.

Reformed

The prohibition and the death-sentence form the garden’s test. Reformed theology has named this the covenant of works: life hung on obedience to a clear command, with death as the threatened sanction.

Reformed note: “Surely die” covers the miseries of soul and body that follow alienation from God, even if bodily collapse was not instant. The Lawgiver’s authority is the point; Adam stood as public person, and the command was not given because God needed fruit left on a tree, but that man might know he had a Lord.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the LORD God forming man, the garden, and the first command—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 2:4–17.

Arminian Notable voices

“Man consisting of body and soul, a body made out of the earth, and a rational immortal soul, we have in these verses the provision that was made for the happiness of both. That part of man, which is allied to the world of sense, was made happy, for he was put in the paradise of God; that part which is allied to the world of spirits was well provided for, for he was taken into covenant with God.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 2:8)

Reformed Notable voices

“He had said that he was formed after the image of God. This is incomparably the highest nobility; and, lest men should use it as an occasion of pride, their first origin is placed immediately before them; whence they may learn that this advantage was adventitious; for Moses relates that man had been, in the beginning, dust of the earth. Let foolish men now go and boast of the excellency of their nature!”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 2:7)
