Passage

Genesis 6

Verses 1–8 · ESV · Voices

As humanity multiplies, boundaries blur and violence ripens. The Lord limits His Spirit’s striving with flesh, names the earth’s great wickedness—every intention only evil continually—and grieves to His heart. Judgment is announced: He will blot out man and creature from the land. Yet the dark paragraph ends with a door: Noah found favor in the eyes of the Lord.

Genesis 6:1

1When man began to multiply on the face of the land and daughters were born to them,

Arminian

The line from Eden to this chapter is fruitfulness under a groaning world. Multiplication is still God’s gift; the trouble is what fallen people do with life together.

Arminian reading starts with shared humanity, not a secret caste. Grace will soon single out Noah, but the story first shows ordinary increase—and how quickly increase can become a stage for disordered desire.

Reformed

Genesis marks a new scene by population, not by a new creator. The God who blessed fruitfulness now watches that blessing unfold on a land already marked by sin.

Reformed framing: providence governs even the census. What multiplies here is not only people but the conditions in which the Lord will soon display both judgment and mercy.

Genesis 6:2

2the sons of God saw that the daughters of man were attractive. And they took as their wives any they chose.

Arminian

Whatever one concludes about “sons of God” (heavenly beings or a privileged human line), the moral grain is clear: seeing, desiring, taking “any they chose.” Attraction becomes appetite without restraint.

Classical Arminians hear a warning about resistible light. Beauty is not sin; grabbing without regard for God’s order is. Grace still calls people to honor marriage and neighbor rather than treat others as spoils.

Reformed

The narrator stresses sight and seizure. Holy lineage language (“sons of God”) is set against “daughters of man,” and the verb is taking, not covenanting in the fear of the Lord.

Reformed readers keep the identity debate secondary to the theology: left to themselves, even impressive people choose by appetite. The heart’s want, not merely bad examples, is already in view.

Genesis 6:3

3Then the Lord said, “My Spirit shall not abide in man forever, for he is flesh: his days shall be 120 years.”

Arminian

God speaks before He floods. The Spirit will not strive with flesh forever; a numbered patience (often read as a grace period before the waters) stands between warning and wipeout.

Arminian pastoral weight: divine forbearance is real and time-bound. God sincerely warns; flesh can persist in resistance until the window closes. Today remains a day to yield to the Spirit who still contends.

Reformed

The Lord sets a limit: His Spirit will not abide with mortal flesh without end. “120 years” is God’s clock, not humanity’s negotiation.

Reformed accent: patience is personal to God, not weakness. He remains free to withdraw striving and to appoint an end. Judgment, when it comes, will not be hasty caprice but decreed holiness.

Key contrast Both hear a real limit on God’s patience with flesh. Arminians typically stress a genuine period in which people may yet respond to the Spirit’s striving; Reformed readers typically stress that the timetable and the outcome rest in God’s sovereign determination.

Genesis 6:4

4The Nephilim were on the earth in those days, and also afterward, when the sons of God came in to the daughters of man and they bore children to them. These were the mighty men who were of old, the men of renown.

Arminian

Mighty men and men of renown fill the earth’s memory—and still the chapter is about wickedness, not celebrity. Fame is no proof of favor.

Arminian caution: cultures still baptize strength as virtue. The gospel measures people by walking with God, not by how loudly their names are remembered. Renown can coexist with a heart only evil continually.

Reformed

The text refuses to let legend outrun theology. Nephilim and mighty men are noted, then the narrative drives on to what the Lord saw in the heart.

Reformed note: human greatness does not revise God’s verdict. The Lord is not impressed by ancient heroes when the earth is filling with violence; His seeing, not their reputation, will decide the chapter.

Genesis 6:5

5The Lord saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every intention of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually.

Arminian

This is Scripture’s severe X-ray: not a few bad deeds but great wickedness, every intention, only evil, continually. The problem is the heart, not a scheduling error.

Classical Arminians confess this depth of fallenness without sliding into Pelagius. Man cannot, of himself, think or will the true good; regenerating grace is necessary. They still insist that God may truly strive and that many resist that grace.

Reformed

The Lord’s seeing is exhaustive: earth-wide wickedness sourced in the heart’s continual intent. Calvin heard here a fountain of iniquity, not a surface stain.

Reformed confession: we are wholly inclined to evil apart from grace. Genesis 6:5 is not hyperbole to scare children; it is the Bible’s anatomy of the fallen race, which only God’s mercy can interrupt.

Key contrast Both refuse a mild view of sin. Arminians typically join total inability to grace that enables a real response some still refuse; Reformed readers typically conclude that only God’s effectual mercy can reverse a heart whose every intention is only evil.

Genesis 6:6

6And the Lord regretted that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him to his heart.

Arminian

God is not an ice statue. He regrets and grieves—holy sorrow over a world He made good and now sees ruined. Anthropomorphic language still tells the truth: sin hurts the heart of God.

Arminian preaching leans into this grief as sincere. The Judge who will flood is the Maker who laments. That grief underwrites both warning and the later offer of mercy in the gospel.

Reformed

“Regretted” does not mean God discovered a mistake in His decree. It means He truly opposes and sorrows over sin in the world He governs.

Reformed care: hold two truths—God’s counsel stands, and God’s heart is not indifferent. The grief is real; the plan to save a people through judgment is not a divine accident.

Genesis 6:7

7So the Lord said, “I will blot out man whom I have created from the face of the land, man and animals and creeping things and birds of the heavens, for I am sorry that I have made them.”

Arminian

The Creator claims the right to unmake. Blotting out is judicial, not random, and it sweeps the land’s living web because human violence has spoiled the garden’s tenants.

Arminian gravity: judgment is deserved and announced. God is sorry over what sin has done, yet He still acts as righteous Lord. The same holiness later makes the cross necessary—and still leaves room to flee to the ark He provides.

Reformed

Speech precedes the waters: “I will blot out.” Creation’s Lord remains Judge of His creatures, including the animals caught in humanity’s ruin.

Reformed weight: justice is not the opposite of God’s character; it is God being God. Sorrow and sentence arrive together. Salvation, when it appears in v. 8, will shine against this dark, rightful verdict.

Genesis 6:8

8But Noah found favor in the eyes of the Lord.

Arminian

The chapter’s hinge is grace: “but Noah.” Favor (hen) is not wages for a self-made saint; it is the Lord’s kind regard that will become ark, covenant, and life.

Classical Arminians gladly ascribe the start of Noah’s deliverance to unearned favor. They also note that Noah will walk, build, and obey—grace that truly enables a living response rather than leaving him inert.

Reformed

Before Noah is called righteous in v. 9, he finds favor. Mercy is the headline; character is the fruit that follows, not the purchase price of God’s eyes.

Reformed comfort: in a world under blot-out, God freely sets His favor on a man and, through him, on a household. Election’s pattern is already pastoral—unearned kindness in the midst of judgment.

Key contrast Shared ground: Noah’s hope is the Lord’s favor, not human heroics. Arminians typically describe that favor as grace that enables Noah’s subsequent walk and obedience; Reformed readers typically describe it as God’s sovereign, unearned choice from which righteousness and the ark-work flow.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on human sin and God’s holy judgment—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 6:1–8.

Arminian Notable voices

“In his lapsed and sinful state, man is not capable, of and by himself, either to think, to will, or to do that which is really good… it is necessary for him to be regenerated and renewed in his intellect, affections or will, and in all his powers, by God in Christ through the Holy Spirit.”
Jacobus Arminius · Public Disputation 11, “On the Free Will of Man and Its Powers”
“Is man by nature filled with all manner of evil? Is he void of all good? Is he wholly fallen? Is his soul totally corrupted? Allow this, and you give up the whole cause of the Pelagians… If we take Scripture for our guide, we must answer, Yes.”
John Wesley · Sermon 44, “Original Sin”

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses traces the overflowing fountain of iniquity to the heart of man… He does not say that men were led into sin by some sudden impulse, but that they were devoted to it… so that nothing pure or sincere remained.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on 6:5)
“From this original corruption, whereby we are utterly indisposed, disabled, and made opposite to all good, and wholly inclined to all evil, do proceed all actual transgressions.”
Westminster Confession of Faith · 6.4 (1647)
