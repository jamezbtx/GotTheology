Passage

Genesis 16

Verses 1–6 · ESV · Voices

Ten years in Canaan after the promise, Sarai is still barren. She gives Hagar, her Egyptian servant, to Abram as a wife to obtain children by her. Conception brings contempt, blame, and harshness; Hagar flees.

Genesis 16:1

1Now Sarai, Abram’s wife, had borne him no children. She had a female Egyptian servant whose name was Hagar.

Arminian

The narrator names both the ache and the person: Sarai barren, Hagar an Egyptian servant with a name. Classical Arminians refuse to treat Hagar as a prop. God will soon address her as a woman He sees.

Reformed

Childlessness sits beside the promises of chapters 12 and 15. Reformed readers hear delay under providence, not a cancelled oath. Hagar’s naming already marks her as a person inside the Lord’s story, not an accident.

Genesis 16:2

2And Sarai said to Abram, “Behold now, the LORD has prevented me from bearing children. Go in to my servant; it may be that I shall obtain children by her.” And Abram listened to the voice of Sarai.

Arminian

Sarai tells the truth about the LORD’s hand, then proposes a workaround. Arminians hear a real, responsible choice: the promise was given, yet they try to fulfill it by household custom. Abram “listened to the voice of Sarai”—faith can yield to a plan God did not command.

Reformed

She attributes barrenness to the LORD, then reaches for a surrogate anyway. Reformed accent: waiting on the promise is itself obedience. Abram’s listening recalls earlier failures to wait; the scheme cannot rewrite what God has sworn.

Key contrast Both treat this as unbelief dressed as piety. Arminians typically stress a resistible, responsible detour from a sincere promise; Reformed readers typically stress that God’s timing cannot be forced, and this plan sits outside the oath of chapter 15.

Genesis 16:3

3So, after Abram had lived ten years in the land of Canaan, Sarai, Abram’s wife, took Hagar the Egyptian, her servant, and gave her to Abram her husband as a wife.

Arminian

Ten years in the land, then Sarai takes and gives. The verbs make the cost plain: Hagar is handed over. Arminian pastoral reading names the injustice without sneering. Custom does not erase the wound of using a servant to “build” a house.

Reformed

The clock is theological: a decade after entering Canaan. Reformed readers see the wait as the Lord’s, and this marriage as human impatience. Giving Hagar “as a wife” does not make the detour into the promised seed.

Genesis 16:4

4And he went in to Hagar, and she conceived. And when she saw that she had conceived, she looked with contempt on her mistress.

Arminian

Conception happens; contempt follows. Hagar’s look is sin, not mere psychology. Arminians hold both women responsible: Sarai’s scheme and Hagar’s scorn each wound the other. Grace will still find the one who flees.

Reformed

The plan “works” biologically and immediately sours. Reformed note: fruitfulness without the Lord’s appointed way breeds rivalry. Providence permits the pregnancy; it does not baptize the household’s contempt.

Genesis 16:5

5And Sarai said to Abram, “May the wrong done to me be on you! I gave my servant to your embrace, and when she saw that she had conceived, she looked on me with contempt. May the LORD judge between you and me!”

Arminian

Sarai blames Abram for the wrong she initiated and calls the LORD to judge. Arminian reading: invoking God is not the same as repenting. Real moral agency means she can name the wound and still refuse her own part in it.

Reformed

She appeals to the LORD as judge while remaining inside her own plan. Reformed readers hear the irony: the God she summons will soon speak—not first to her, but to Hagar in the wilderness.

Genesis 16:6

6But Abram said to Sarai, “Behold, your servant is in your power; do to her as you please.” Then Sarai dealt harshly with her, and she fled from her.

Arminian

Abram abdicates; Sarai deals harshly; Hagar flees. Classical Arminians do not excuse any of the three. Power used to crush is sin, and flight is the cry of a mistreated woman. The next scene will show that running from Sarai is not running beyond the LORD.

Reformed

Passivity is not innocence. Abram hands Hagar back into harm; Sarai’s harshness drives her out. Reformed comfort is not in the household’s virtue but in the God who will find the fugitive. The promise still stands; this cruelty is not its method.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Hagar given, conception, and contempt in Sarai’s house—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 16:1–6.

Arminian Notable voices

“We have here the marriage of Abram to Hagar, who was his secondary wife. Herein, though he may be excused, he cannot be justified; for from the beginning it was not so: and when it was so, it seems to have proceeded from an irregular desire to build up their families, for the speedier peopling of the world. Christ has reduced this matter to the first institution, and makes the marriage union to be between one man and one woman only.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 16:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“She saw that she was barren, and had passed the age of bearing. Moses expressly relates, that the design of marrying a second wife did not originate with Abram himself, but with Sarai, to teach us that the holy man was not impelled by lust to these nuptials; but that when he was thinking of no such thing, he was induced to engage in them, by the exhortation of his wife.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 16:1)
