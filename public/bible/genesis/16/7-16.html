Passage

Genesis 16

Verses 7–16 · ESV · Voices

The angel of the LORD finds Hagar by a spring on the way to Shur, names her, sends her back, and promises countless offspring. Ishmael is named because the LORD has heard her affliction; she names the LORD “a God of seeing.” She bears Abram a son; Abram is eighty-six.

Genesis 16:7

7The angel of the LORD found her by a spring of water in the wilderness, the spring on the way to Shur.

Arminian

The first “angel of the LORD” in Scripture finds a fleeing servant, not a patriarch at an altar. Arminians hear mercy that seeks. Hagar is not hunted to be discarded; she is found at water on the road home to Egypt.

Reformed

“Found her” is the Lord’s initiative in the wilderness. Reformed readers take the angel of the LORD as God coming near. Providence meets Hagar on the Shur road before she disappears from the promise’s household.

Genesis 16:8

8And he said, “Hagar, servant of Sarai, where have you come from and where are you going?” She said, “I am fleeing from my mistress Sarai.”

Arminian

He speaks her name and her station, then asks origin and destination. The questions are pastoral, not ignorant. She answers honestly: she is fleeing. Grace starts with truth-telling.

Reformed

Named, located, questioned. God addresses Hagar as Hagar. Reformed accent: omniscience still asks, so the woman may speak. Her flight is heard; it is not hidden from the Lord who finds.

Genesis 16:9

9The angel of the LORD said to her, “Return to your mistress and submit to her.”

Arminian

Return and submit is a hard word, not a shrug at abuse. Arminian pastoral care holds both: God does not bless contempt, and He also does not abandon Hagar to the desert. The command comes with the promises that follow; she is sent back under His eye.

Reformed

The Lord directs her path as surely as He found her. Reformed reading: this return keeps Ishmael inside Abram’s house until the later dismissal of chapter 21. Hard providence is still providence; she is not told to vanish.

Genesis 16:10

10The angel of the LORD also said to her, “I will surely multiply your offspring so that they cannot be numbered for multitude.”

Arminian

“I will surely multiply” is spoken to Hagar, not only about her. Classical Arminians hear a sincere promise of multitude. The God who sees the afflicted also pledges a future; she may trust the word and return.

Reformed

The angel speaks in the first person with Abrahamic echo: unnumbered offspring. Reformed accent: this is the LORD’s own “I will.” Ishmael’s line is gift, distinct from the covenant seed yet truly promised.

Key contrast Both hear a real promise to Hagar. Arminians typically stress a sincere word she is invited to trust; Reformed readers typically stress the LORD’s sovereign “I will” that will make a nation even from this wilderness meeting.

Genesis 16:11

11And the angel of the LORD said to her, “Behold, you are pregnant and shall bear a son. You shall call his name Ishmael, because the LORD has listened to your affliction.

Arminian

Ishmael means God hears. The LORD has listened to her affliction, not merely noticed a pregnancy. Arminian comfort: the cry of the mistreated is not beneath Him. Naming the boy writes that hearing into the family.

Reformed

Pregnancy announced, name assigned, reason given. Reformed confession: God hears because God is God, not because Hagar climbed to Him. The affliction is real; the listening is covenant kindness toward a servant.

Genesis 16:12

12He shall be a wild donkey of a man, his hand against everyone and everyone’s hand against him, and he shall dwell over against all his kinsmen.”

Arminian

The oracle is frank, not flattering: a wild-donkey freedom, conflict, and a life over against his kin. Arminians refuse to romanticize it. God tells the truth about the son’s way even as He blesses his future.

Reformed

Prophecy here is realism under sovereignty. Reformed readers hear neither curse-only nor sentimental blessing: Ishmael will live, multiply, and live in tension near his brothers. The Lord names the future He will govern.

Genesis 16:13

13So she called the name of the LORD who spoke to her, “You are a God of seeing,” for she said, “Truly here I have seen him who looks after me.”

Arminian

Hagar names the LORD: El Roi, a God of seeing. A servant theologian. Classical Arminians treasure this confession—the afflicted may know God truly. She has seen the One who looks after her; seeing is mutual mercy, not speculation.

Reformed

She names “the LORD who spoke to her,” identifying the angel with YHWH. Reformed reading: theophany, then doxology. “Looks after me” is providence in a servant’s mouth—God’s care spoken from the wilderness.

Key contrast Both honor Hagar’s confession. Arminians typically stress that the God who sees sincerely looks after the afflicted who call on Him; Reformed readers typically stress that this seeing is the LORD’s own coming-near, a theophany that names and keeps her.

Genesis 16:14

14Therefore the well was called Beer-lahai-roi; it lies between Kadesh and Bered.

Arminian

The well gets a name so the meeting can be found on a map. Mercy leaves geography. Arminian pastoral: remember the place God looked after you; testimony has coordinates.

Reformed

Beer-lahai-roi—“well of the Living One who sees me”—fixes the vision in the land. Reformed note: even wilderness springs sit inside the Lord’s ordering, between Kadesh and Bered.

Genesis 16:15

15And Hagar bore Abram a son, and Abram called the name of his son, whom Hagar bore, Ishmael.

Arminian

She bears; Abram names the boy Ishmael—accepting the name God gave her. The household now has to live with the God-who-hears in a child’s name. Faith here is at least this: he does not rename the hearing.

Reformed

Birth fulfills the wilderness word. Abram’s naming is obedience to the oracle Hagar received. Reformed comfort: the Lord who heard her affliction writes that hearing into Abram’s line, even before Isaac.

Genesis 16:16

16Abram was eighty-six years old when Hagar bore Ishmael to Abram.

Arminian

Eighty-six: still not ninety-nine, still not Isaac. Arminian reading lets the number preach patience. Human schemes produce a son; they do not hurry the son of promise. Wait remains the call.

Reformed

The narrator clocks the detour. Reformed accent: Ishmael’s birth is dated so Isaac’s later birth will show the difference. God keeps time; the covenant seed will still come by promise, not by this year.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the God who sees Hagar, and Ishmael named because the LORD heard—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 16:7–16.

Arminian Notable voices

“And she called the name of the Lord that spake unto her—That is, thus she made confession of his name, Thou God seest me—This should be with her, his name for ever, and this his memorial, by which she will know him, and remember him while she lives, Thou God seest me. Thou seest my sorrow and affliction. This Hagar especially refers to: when we have brought ourselves into distress by our own folly, yet God has not forsaken us.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 16:13)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses, I have no doubt, implies that Hagar, after she was admonished by the angel, changed her mind: and being thus subdued, retook herself to prayer; unless, perhaps, here the confession of the tongue, rather than change of mind, is denoted. I rather incline, however, to the opinion, that Hagar, who had before been of a wild and intractable temper, begins now at length to acknowledge the providence of God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 16:13)
