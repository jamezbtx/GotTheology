Passage

Genesis 16

Verses 7–16 · WEB · Voices

The angel of the LORD finds Hagar by a fountain on the way to Shur, names her, sends her back, and promises countless offspring. Ishmael is named because the LORD has heard her affliction; she names the LORD “a God who sees.” She bears Abram a son; Abram is eighty-six.

Genesis 16:7

7The LORD’s angel found her by a fountain of water in the wilderness, by the fountain on the way to Shur.

Arminian

The first “angel” in Scripture finds a fleeing servant, not a patriarch at an altar. Arminians hear mercy that seeks. Hagar is not hunted to be discarded; she is found at water on the road home to Egypt.

Reformed

“Found her” is the Lord’s initiative in the wilderness. Reformed readers take the angel of the LORD as God coming near. Providence meets Hagar on the Shur road before she disappears from the promise’s household.

Genesis 16:8

8He said, “Hagar, Sarai’s servant, where did you come from? Where are you going?” She said, “I am fleeing from the face of my mistress Sarai.”

Arminian

He speaks her name and her station, then asks origin and destination. The questions are pastoral, not ignorant. She answers honestly: she is fleeing. Grace starts with truth-telling.

Reformed

The angel names Hagar, places her, and questions her. God addresses Hagar as Hagar. Omniscience still asks, so the woman may speak. Her flight is heard; it is not hidden from the Lord who finds.

Genesis 16:9

9The LORD’s angel said to her, “Return to your mistress, and submit yourself under her hands.”

Arminian

Return and submit is a hard word, not a shrug at abuse. Arminian pastoral care holds both: God does not bless contempt, and He also does not abandon Hagar to the desert. The command comes with the promises that follow; she is sent back under His eye.

Reformed

The Lord directs her path as surely as He found her. This return keeps Ishmael inside Abram’s house until the later dismissal of chapter 21. Hard providence is still providence; she is not told to vanish.

Genesis 16:10

10The LORD’s angel said to her, “I will greatly multiply your offspring, that they will not be counted for multitude.”

Arminian

“I will greatly multiply” is spoken to Hagar, not only about her. Arminian readers hear a sincere promise of multitude. The God who sees the afflicted also pledges a future; she may trust the word and return.

Reformed

The angel speaks in the first person with Abrahamic echo: unnumbered offspring. This is the LORD’s own “I will.” Ishmael’s line is gift, distinct from the covenant seed yet truly promised.

Key contrast Both hear an earnest promise to Hagar. Arminians typically stress a sincere word she is invited to trust; Calvinists typically press the LORD’s sovereign “I will” that will make a nation even from this wilderness meeting.

Genesis 16:11

11The LORD’s angel said to her, “Behold, you are with child, and will bear a son. You shall call his name Ishmael, because the LORD has heard your affliction.

Arminian

Ishmael means God hears. The LORD has listened to her affliction, not merely noticed a pregnancy. The cry of the mistreated is not beneath Him. Naming the boy writes that hearing into the family.

Reformed

Pregnancy announced, name assigned, reason given. God hears because God is God, not because Hagar climbed to Him. The affliction is authentic; the listening is covenant kindness toward a servant.

Genesis 16:12

12He will be like a wild donkey among men. His hand will be against every man, and every man’s hand against him. He will live opposed to all of his brothers.”

Arminian

The oracle is frank, not flattering: a wild-donkey freedom, conflict, and a life over against his kin. Arminians refuse to romanticize it. God tells the truth about the son’s way even as He blesses his future.

Reformed

Prophecy here is realism under sovereignty. Reformed readers hear neither curse-only nor sentimental blessing: Ishmael will live, multiply, and live in tension near his brothers. The Lord names the future He will govern.

Genesis 16:13

13She called the name of the LORD who spoke to her, “You are a God who sees,” for she said, “Have I even stayed alive after seeing him?”

Arminian

Hagar names the LORD: El Roi, a God of seeing. A servant theologian. Arminians treasure this confession—the afflicted may know God truly. She has seen the One who looks after her; seeing is mutual mercy, not speculation.

Reformed

She names “the LORD who spoke to her,” identifying the angel with YHWH. The encounter with God leads straight into praise. Looks after me is providence in a servant’s mouth—God’s care spoken from the wilderness.

Key contrast Both honor Hagar’s confession. Arminians underline that the God who sees sincerely looks after the afflicted who call on Him; Reformed readers emphasize that this seeing is the LORD’s own coming-near, a theophany that names and keeps her.

Genesis 16:14

14Therefore the well was called Beer Lahai Roi. Behold, it is between Kadesh and Bered.

Arminian

The well gets a name so the meeting can be found on a map. Mercy leaves geography. Remember the place God looked after you; testimony has coordinates.

Reformed

Beer-lahai-roi—“well of the Living One who sees me”—fixes the vision in the land. Even wilderness springs sit inside the Lord’s ordering, between Kadesh and Bered.

Genesis 16:15

15Hagar bore a son for Abram. Abram called the name of his son, whom Hagar bore, Ishmael.

Arminian

She bears; Abram names the boy Ishmael—accepting the name God gave her. The household now has to live with the God-who-hears in a child’s name. Faith here is at least this: he does not rename the hearing.

Reformed

Birth fulfills the wilderness word. Abram’s naming is obedience to the oracle Hagar received. The Lord who heard her affliction writes that hearing into Abram’s line, even before Isaac.

Genesis 16:16

16Abram was eighty-six years old when Hagar bore Ishmael to Abram.

Arminian

Eighty-six: still not ninety-nine, still not Isaac. Arminian reading lets the number preach patience. Human schemes produce a son; they do not hurry the son of promise. Wait remains the call.

Reformed

The narrator clocks the detour. Ishmael’s birth is dated so Isaac’s later birth will show the difference. God keeps time; the covenant seed will still come by promise, not by this year.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

On the God who sees Hagar, and Ishmael named because the LORD heard, a few classic voices from each tradition.

Arminian Notable voices

“And she called the name of the Lord that spake unto her—That is, thus she made confession of his name, Thou God seest me—This should be with her, his name for ever, and this his memorial, by which she will know him, and remember him while she lives, Thou God seest me. Thou seest my sorrow and affliction. This Hagar especially refers to: when we have brought ourselves into distress by our own folly, yet God has not forsaken us.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 16:13)

Reformed Notable voices

“Moses, I have no doubt, implies that Hagar, after she was admonished by the angel, changed her mind: and being thus subdued, retook herself to prayer; unless, perhaps, here the confession of the tongue, rather than change of mind, is denoted. I rather incline, however, to the opinion, that Hagar, who had before been of a wild and intractable temper, begins now at length to acknowledge the providence of God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 16:13)
