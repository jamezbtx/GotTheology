Passage

Genesis 15

Verses 7–21 · WEB · Voices

The same night continues: God identifies Himself as the One who brought Abram from Ur to give him the land. Abram asks how he will know; the LORD answers with a covenant rite. Animals are divided; Abram drives off birds; a deep sleep and dreadful darkness fall; then a smoking furnace and flaming torch pass between the pieces while the promise of sojourn, affliction, exodus, and land is sworn.

Genesis 15:7

7He said to Abram, “I am the LORD who brought you out of Ur of the Chaldees, to give you this land to inherit it.”

Arminian

Self-identification first: I am the LORD who brought you out of Ur—to give this land. Rescue already happened; possession is the stated purpose.

Past grace grounds present trust. The God who actually led Abram out now pledges the land. Faith is invited to rest on a history of kindness, not on a new guess.

Reformed

The covenant Name and the exodus-from-Ur are one sentence. Land is gift because the Giver already claimed the man.

“I am the LORD who brought you out” is gospel grammar later heard at Sinai. Initiative is entirely His; possession will be His giving, not Abram’s seizure.

Genesis 15:8

8He said, “Lord GOD, how will I know that I will inherit it?”

Arminian

A second honest question: How will I know? Faith that has just been counted righteous still asks for confirmation of the land.

Arminians do not scold the question. Grace allows seeking assurance. Abram wants to know; God will answer with a rite rather than a rebuke.

Reformed

“How will I know” seeks a pledge, not a loophole. The man justified in verse 6 still needs the land promise sealed.

God stoops to oath. Assurance is His gift by covenant ceremony, not Abram’s right to audit the Almighty.

Genesis 15:9

9He said to him, “Bring me a heifer three years old, a female goat three years old, a ram three years old, a turtledove, and a young pigeon.”

Arminian

The answer is liturgical: fetch these animals. Knowing will come through a rite Abram must prepare.

God involves Abram. He is told to bring. Assurance is not magic; it is a commanded participation under grace.

Reformed

The inventory is precise—three-year beasts and two birds. God specifies the means of the oath.

Even the materials of confirmation are appointed. Abram does not invent a ceremony; he obeys a prescription.

Genesis 15:10

10He brought him all these, and divided them in the middle, and laid each half opposite the other; but he didn’t divide the birds.

Arminian

Abram obeys the ancient covenant form: halves facing halves, birds left whole. A path of death is laid for the oath-walkers.

Arminian readers see faith acting. The man who believed now cuts. Trust becomes costly preparation for God’s confirming word.

Reformed

Divided carcasses are a self-maledictory aisle: so may it be done to the one who breaks the oath. The uncut birds follow later priestly pattern.

The rite means death for covenant-breakers. What happens next will show who walks the path.

Genesis 15:11

11The birds of prey came down on the carcasses, and Abram drove them away.

Arminian

Vultures land on the pieces; Abram drives them off. Promise is threatened even before it is sworn, and the man keeps watch.

Faith guards what God has set in place. Grace does not cancel the work of shooing scavengers until the LORD arrives.

Reformed

Prey-birds on a covenant sacrifice are a small picture of hostility against the promise. Abram’s driving them off is not imaginary, and still preliminary.

The pieces are not yet walked. Providence lets scavengers test the scene; the oath will not be stolen by them.

Genesis 15:12

12When the sun was going down, a deep sleep fell on Abram. Now terror and great darkness fell on him.

Arminian

Sunset, deep sleep, dreadful darkness. Abram is taken out of the action as God prepares to speak and to walk. Awe, not casual ritual, fills the hour.

Arminians hear both gift and gravity. Sleep is from God; the dread is honest. The man who asked “how shall I know?” now cannot manage the answer—he can only receive it.

Reformed

Deep sleep recalls Adam (Gen 2:21): God works while the man is laid still. Dreadful darkness is the Lord’s theater, not Abram’s technique.

Justification’s God now binds Himself, and Abram is a spectator. The ceremony’s center will not be human walking.

Genesis 15:13

13He said to Abram, “Know for sure that your offspring will live as foreigners in a land that is not theirs, and will serve them. They will afflict them four hundred years.

Arminian

“Know for sure” answers verse 8—and the knowledge includes sojourn, slavery, and four hundred years of affliction. Assurance is not a shortcut around suffering.

God tells the truth about the path. Faith can receive a hard future without calling God unfaithful. The certainty includes the pain.

Reformed

Certainty is granted, and it includes affliction. Offspring will be sojourners and servants on a timetable God names.

Comfort: even Egypt is scheduled. Four hundred years are not outside the oath; they are inside the “know for sure.”

Genesis 15:14

14I will also judge that nation, whom they will serve. Afterward they will come out with great wealth;

Arminian

Judgment on the oppressor, then exodus with great possessions. The story of Israel is told in advance as God’s doing.

Arminians find a just God who sees servants and will act. The promise of land survives slavery because the Judge has already spoken.

Reformed

“I will also judge that” and “they will come out” are the LORD’s future-tense gospel. Possessions will reverse the affliction.

Exodus is not Israel’s improvisation. God pre-writes plagues and plunder as covenant faithfulness to Abram.

Genesis 15:15

15but you will go to your fathers in peace. You will be buried at a good old age.

Arminian

Abram himself will not walk the four hundred years. Peace and a good old age are promised to him personally.

God’s care is individual as well as national. The man who asked for knowledge receives a peaceful death as part of the answer.

Reformed

Peace and burial at a good old age are decreed. Abram will die before possession, yet not before the oath.

The promise outlives the patriarch by design. He can go to his fathers in peace because God will keep the rest.

Genesis 15:16

16In the fourth generation they will come here again, for the iniquity of the Amorite is not yet full.”

Arminian

Return in the fourth generation; Amorite iniquity is not yet complete. Delay is moral, not forgetful. God waits on a people’s sin to fill its measure.

This is patience that is also judgment. Nations remain accountable; the timetable treats Amorite evil as accumulating, not as theater.

Reformed

The land grant waits on Amorite iniquity’s fullness. God’s justice has a clock as well as a map.

Election of Abram’s seed does not make Canaan’s judgment arbitrary. Providence times both gift and dispossession.

Key contrast Both refuse to call the delay caprice. Arminians typically stress moral accountability as iniquity fills up; Reformed Christians typically emphasize God’s sovereign timing of judgment and gift together.

Genesis 15:17

17It came to pass that, when the sun went down, and it was dark, behold, a smoking furnace and a flaming torch passed between these pieces.

Arminian

In the dark, a smoking furnace and flaming torch pass between the pieces—God’s own presence walking the death-path. Abram sleeps; the LORD binds Himself.

This is assurance given, not earned. The God who invited trust now stakes His name on the promise. Faith’s “how shall I know?” is answered by God bearing the oath’s risk.

Reformed

Only the fire pot and torch walk the aisle. In ANE covenant form, the walker invokes the carcass-curse on himself. God takes it alone.

Unilateral grace in ritual form. Abram does not pass through. The LORD swears by Himself, and the later cross will show what it costs when God keeps such an oath.

Key contrast Both see God, not Abram, walking between the pieces. Arminians emphasize gracious assurance that invites ongoing trust; Reformed Christians typically stress a unilateral, self-maledictory oath—God binding Himself while the patriarch sleeps.

Genesis 15:18

18In that day the LORD made a covenant with Abram, saying, “I have given this land to your offspring, from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates:

Arminian

“In that day” the LORD made a covenant. Land from Egypt’s river to the Euphrates is granted to Abram’s offspring as gift-speech.

Classical Arminians receive a covenant that calls for continued faith in the Giver. The borders are specific; the Giver is faithful; the heirs must still trust the promise through the sojourn just foretold.

Reformed

Covenant is cut this day: I give. The geography is vast because the Giver is not small.

The oath does not wait on Abram’s later obedience to become true. God made a covenant; the land is given in His speech, to be possessed in His time.

Genesis 15:19

19the land of the Kenites, the Kenizzites, the Kadmonites,

Arminian

Named peoples, not a vague horizon. Kenites, Kenizzites, Kadmonites—the promise has addresses.

God’s gifts are concrete. Faith trusts a mapped mercy, not a slogan.

Reformed

The list begins. Nations occupy the gift. Promise is as particular as a gazetteer.

The LORD names what He will dispossess in time. Nothing here is abstract.

Genesis 15:20

20the Hittites, the Perizzites, the Rephaim,

Arminian

Hittites, Perizzites, Rephaim—some feared, some obscure. The oath covers the intimidating and the forgotten alike.

Faith will later meet these names in Joshua; here they are already inside the covenant sentence.

Reformed

The catalog continues under the same I give. Even Rephaim—names that later spell dread—are already allotted in the dark.

Giants in the land do not rewrite the oath spoken while Abram slept.

Genesis 15:21

21the Amorites, the Canaanites, the Girgashites, and the Jebusites.”

Arminian

The list closes with Amorites, Canaanites, Girgashites, and Jebusites—the peoples whose iniquity is filling up (v. 16). Promise and patience share one map.

Classical Arminians hold both: God truly gives the land, and He truly waits to judge. The ceremony ends with names; the story will take centuries. Faith is summoned to live in the meantime.

Reformed

The last names include those whose sin is “not yet full. It came to pass that.” The covenant already includes their dispossession on God’s clock.

The smoking pot has passed; the list is spoken. What God swore in the dark, He will keep in history—down to Jebus, later Jerusalem.

Key contrast Both take the land grant as God’s sure word over occupied territory. Arminians typically join the gift to a long, morally timed history that faith must endure; Reformed readers commonly join it to the unilateral oath of verses 17–18, sure because God walked the pieces.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Arminian Notable voices

“When the sun was gone down the sign was given—The smoaking furnace signified the affliction of his seed in Egypt: they were there in the furnace of affliction, and labouring in the very fire. They were there in the smoke, their eyes darkened that they could not see to the end of their troubles. 2. The burning lamp speaks comfort in this affliction; and this God shewed Abram at the same time with the smoaking furnace.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 15:17)

Reformed Notable voices

“Again a new vision was added, to confirm his faith in the oracle. At first, Abram was horror-struck with the thick darkness; now, in the midst of a smoking furnace, he sees a burning lamp. Many suppose that a sacrifice was consumed with this fire; but I rather interpret it as a symbol of future deliverance, which would well agree with the fact itself.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 15:17)
