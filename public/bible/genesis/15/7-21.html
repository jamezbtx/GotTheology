Passage

Genesis 15

Verses 7–21 · ESV

The same night continues: God identifies Himself as the One who brought Abram from Ur to give him the land. Abram asks how he will know; the LORD answers with a covenant rite. Animals are divided; Abram drives off birds; a deep sleep and dreadful darkness fall; then a smoking fire pot and flaming torch pass between the pieces while the promise of sojourn, affliction, exodus, and land is sworn.

Genesis 15:7

7And he said to him, “I am the LORD who brought you out from Ur of the Chaldeans to give you this land to possess.”

Arminian

Self-identification first: I am the LORD who brought you out from Ur—to give this land. Rescue already happened; possession is the stated purpose.

Arminian hearing: past grace grounds present trust. The God who actually led Abram out now pledges the land. Faith is invited to rest on a history of kindness, not on a new guess.

Reformed

The covenant Name and the exodus-from-Ur are one sentence. Land is gift because the Giver already claimed the man.

Reformed accent: “I am the LORD who brought you out” is gospel grammar later heard at Sinai. Initiative is entirely His; possession will be His giving, not Abram’s seizure.

Genesis 15:8

8But he said, “O Lord GOD, how am I to know that I shall possess it?”

Arminian

A second honest question: How am I to know? Faith that has just been counted righteous still asks for confirmation of the land.

Classical Arminians do not scold the question. Grace allows seeking assurance. Abram wants to know; God will answer with a rite rather than a rebuke.

Reformed

“How am I to know” seeks a pledge, not a loophole. The man justified in verse 6 still needs the land promise sealed.

Reformed pastoral note: God stoops to oath. Assurance is His gift by covenant ceremony, not Abram’s right to audit the Almighty.

Genesis 15:9

9He said to him, “Bring me a heifer three years old, a female goat three years old, a ram three years old, a turtledove, and a young pigeon.”

Arminian

The answer is liturgical: fetch these animals. Knowing will come through a rite Abram must prepare.

Arminian note: God involves Abram. He is told to bring. Assurance is not magic; it is a commanded participation under grace.

Reformed

The inventory is precise—three-year beasts and two birds. God specifies the means of the oath.

Reformed reading: even the materials of confirmation are appointed. Abram does not invent a ceremony; he obeys a prescription.

Genesis 15:10

10And he brought him all these, cut them in half, and laid each half over against the other. But he did not cut the birds in half.

Arminian

Abram obeys the ancient covenant form: halves facing halves, birds left whole. A path of death is laid for the oath-walkers.

Classical Arminians see faith acting. The man who believed now cuts. Trust becomes costly preparation for God’s confirming word.

Reformed

Divided carcasses are a self-maledictory aisle: so may it be done to the one who breaks the oath. The uncut birds follow later priestly pattern.

Reformed gravity: the rite means death for covenant-breakers. What happens next will show who walks the path.

Genesis 15:11

11And when birds of prey came down on the carcasses, Abram drove them away.

Arminian

Vultures land on the pieces; Abram drives them off. Promise is threatened even before it is sworn, and the man keeps watch.

Arminian pastoral image: faith guards what God has set in place. Grace does not cancel the work of shooing scavengers until the LORD arrives.

Reformed

Prey-birds on a covenant sacrifice are a small picture of hostility against the promise. Abram’s driving them off is real, and still preliminary.

Reformed note: the pieces are not yet walked. Providence lets scavengers test the scene; the oath will not be stolen by them.

Genesis 15:12

12As the sun was going down, a deep sleep fell on Abram. And behold, dreadful and great darkness fell upon him.

Arminian

Sunset, deep sleep, dreadful darkness. Abram is taken out of the action as God prepares to speak and to walk. Awe, not casual ritual, fills the hour.

Classical Arminians hear both gift and gravity. Sleep is from God; the dread is honest. The man who asked “how shall I know?” now cannot manage the answer—he can only receive it.

Reformed

Deep sleep recalls Adam (Gen 2:21): God works while the man is laid still. Dreadful darkness is the Lord’s theater, not Abram’s technique.

Reformed accent: justification’s God now binds Himself, and Abram is a spectator. The ceremony’s center will not be human walking.

Genesis 15:13

13Then the LORD said to Abram, “Know for certain that your offspring will be sojourners in a land that is not theirs and will be servants there, and they will be afflicted for four hundred years.

Arminian

“Know for certain” answers verse 8—and the knowledge includes sojourn, slavery, and four hundred years of affliction. Assurance is not a shortcut around suffering.

Arminian pastoral word: God tells the truth about the path. Faith can receive a hard future without calling God unfaithful. The certainty includes the pain.

Reformed

Certainty is granted, and it includes affliction. Offspring will be sojourners and servants on a timetable God names.

Reformed comfort: even Egypt is scheduled. Four hundred years are not outside the oath; they are inside the “know for certain.”

Genesis 15:14

14But I will bring judgment on the nation that they serve, and afterward they shall come out with great possessions.

Arminian

Judgment on the oppressor, then exodus with great possessions. The story of Israel is told in advance as God’s doing.

Classical Arminians hear a just God who sees servants and will act. The promise of land survives slavery because the Judge has already spoken.

Reformed

“I will bring judgment” and “they shall come out” are the LORD’s future-tense gospel. Possessions will reverse the affliction.

Reformed reading: Exodus is not Israel’s improvisation. God pre-writes plagues and plunder as covenant faithfulness to Abram.

Genesis 15:15

15As for you, you shall go to your fathers in peace; you shall be buried in a good old age.

Arminian

Abram himself will not walk the four hundred years. Peace and a good old age are promised to him personally.

Arminian comfort: God’s care is individual as well as national. The man who asked for knowledge receives a peaceful death as part of the answer.

Reformed

Peace and burial in a good old age are decreed. Abram will die before possession, yet not before the oath.

Reformed note: the promise outlives the patriarch by design. He can go to his fathers in peace because God will keep the rest.

Genesis 15:16

16And they shall come back here in the fourth generation, for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete.”

Arminian

Return in the fourth generation; Amorite iniquity is not yet complete. Delay is moral, not forgetful. God waits on a people’s sin to fill its measure.

Classical Arminians hear patience that is also judgment. Nations remain accountable; the timetable treats Amorite evil as real and accumulating, not as theater.

Reformed

The land grant waits on Amorite iniquity’s fullness. God’s justice has a clock as well as a map.

Reformed accent: election of Abram’s seed does not make Canaan’s judgment arbitrary. Providence times both gift and dispossession.

Key contrast Both refuse to call the delay caprice. Arminians typically stress moral accountability as iniquity really fills up; Reformed readers typically stress God’s sovereign timing of judgment and gift together.

Genesis 15:17

17When the sun had gone down and it was dark, behold, a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch passed between these pieces.

Arminian

In the dark, a smoking fire pot and flaming torch pass between the pieces—God’s own presence walking the death-path. Abram sleeps; the LORD binds Himself.

Arminian gospel: this is assurance given, not earned. The God who invited trust now stakes His name on the promise. Faith’s “how shall I know?” is answered by God bearing the oath’s risk.

Reformed

Only the fire pot and torch walk the aisle. In ANE covenant form, the walker invokes the carcass-curse on himself. God takes it alone.

Reformed wonder: unilateral grace in ritual form. Abram does not pass through. The LORD swears by Himself, and the later cross will show what it costs when God keeps such an oath.

Key contrast Both see God, not Abram, walking between the pieces. Arminians typically emphasize gracious assurance that invites ongoing trust; Reformed readers typically emphasize a unilateral, self-maledictory oath—God binding Himself while the patriarch sleeps.

Genesis 15:18

18On that day the LORD made a covenant with Abram, saying, “To your offspring I give this land, from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates,

Arminian

“On that day” the LORD made a covenant. Land from Egypt’s river to the Euphrates is granted to Abram’s offspring as gift-speech.

Classical Arminians receive a real covenant that calls for continued faith in the Giver. The borders are specific; the Giver is faithful; the heirs must still trust the promise through the sojourn just foretold.

Reformed

Covenant is cut this day: “I give.” The geography is vast because the Giver is not small.

Reformed accent: the oath does not wait on Abram’s later obedience to become true. God made a covenant; the land is given in His speech, to be possessed in His time.

Genesis 15:19

19the land of the Kenites, the Kenizzites, the Kadmonites,

Arminian

Named peoples, not a vague horizon. Kenites, Kenizzites, Kadmonites—the promise has addresses.

Arminian note: God’s gifts are concrete. Faith trusts a mapped mercy, not a slogan.

Reformed

The list begins. Real nations occupy the gift. Promise is as particular as a gazetteer.

Reformed reading: the LORD names what He will dispossess in time. Nothing here is abstract.

Genesis 15:20

20the Hittites, the Perizzites, the Rephaim,

Arminian

Hittites, Perizzites, Rephaim—some feared, some obscure. The oath covers the intimidating and the forgotten alike.

Faith will later meet these names in Joshua; here they are already inside the covenant sentence.

Reformed

The catalog continues under the same “I give.” Even Rephaim—names that later spell dread—are already allotted in the dark.

Reformed comfort: giants in the land do not rewrite the oath spoken while Abram slept.

Genesis 15:21

21the Amorites, the Canaanites, the Girgashites and the Jebusites.”

Arminian

The list closes with Amorites, Canaanites, Girgashites, and Jebusites—the peoples whose iniquity is filling up (v. 16). Promise and patience share one map.

Classical Arminians hold both: God truly gives the land, and He truly waits to judge. The ceremony ends with names; the story will take centuries. Faith is summoned to live in the meantime.

Reformed

The last names include those whose sin is “not yet complete.” The covenant already includes their dispossession on God’s clock.

Reformed close: the smoking pot has passed; the list is spoken. What God swore in the dark, He will keep in history—down to Jebus, later Jerusalem.

Key contrast Both take the land grant as God’s sure word over occupied territory. Arminians typically join the gift to a long, morally timed history that faith must endure; Reformed readers typically join it to the unilateral oath of verses 17–18, sure because God walked the pieces.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
