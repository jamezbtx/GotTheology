Passage

Genesis 28

Verses 1–9 · ESV · Voices

After the stolen blessing of chapter 27, Isaac now knowingly sends Jacob to Paddan-aram: no Canaanite wife, and the Abrahamic blessing spoken on purpose. Esau watches, then tries a cosmetic fix by marrying Mahalath of Ishmael’s line.

Genesis 28:1

1Then Isaac called Jacob and blessed him and directed him, “You must not take a wife from the Canaanite women.

Arminian

Isaac now calls Jacob on purpose and blesses him. The stolen scene is not left hanging; the father speaks the blessing with open eyes and adds a charge: do not take a Canaanite wife.

Classical Arminians hear a real parental word under grace. Jacob must go and obey. The blessing is God’s gift, and the direction is a responsible path, not a charm that works without a walk.

Reformed

Isaac blesses knowingly, confirming what God had already assigned. The charge about marriage guards the covenant line, not mere ethnic taste.

Reformed comfort: human fraud did not cancel the promise. The father now speaks as steward of a blessing God had bound to Jacob.

Genesis 28:2

2Arise, go to Paddan-aram to the house of Bethuel your mother’s father, and take as your wife from there one of the daughters of Laban your mother’s brother.

Arminian

“Arise, go” is concrete: Paddan-aram, Bethuel’s house, a wife from Laban’s daughters. Flight becomes vocation. The same road that escapes Esau is the road of a commanded marriage.

Arminian pastoral note: grace often looks like packing. Jacob is summoned to act; enabling mercy does not replace the going.

Reformed

Exile is placed under command. The destination is the mother’s kin, where Abraham’s servant once found Rebekah.

Reformed accent: providence uses Rebekah’s family again. Jacob’s path is not random flight but the Lord’s ordering of the promised seed’s household.

Genesis 28:3

3God Almighty bless you and make you fruitful and multiply you, that you may become a company of peoples.

Arminian

El Shaddai is asked to bless, make fruitful, and multiply Jacob into a company of peoples. The wording echoes creation and Abraham: fruitfulness as gift aimed at a people.

Classical Arminians gladly confess God’s initiative in fruitfulness. The blessing invites Jacob to trust the God who can make a fleeing son into a people—grace that calls for living faith on the road.

Reformed

“God Almighty” (El Shaddai) names the God who is sufficient for the promise. Fruitfulness and a company of peoples are God’s making, not Jacob’s craft.

Reformed reading: Isaac hands on what God pledged. The future multitude sits inside divine power, even while Jacob walks out nearly alone.

Genesis 28:4

4May he give the blessing of Abraham to you and to your offspring with you, that you may take possession of the land of your sojournings that God gave to Abraham!”

Arminian

The blessing of Abraham is named and aimed: land of sojournings, given to Abraham, now spoken over Jacob and his offspring. Promise is personal and missional—possession still future, faith required now.

Arminian emphasis: the gift is sincerely extended to Jacob. He must receive it as Abraham did, by trust, while still a sojourner rather than a settler.

Reformed

Isaac does not invent a new covenant; he transmits Abraham’s. Land is “given,” yet still “sojournings”—promise ahead of possession.

Reformed comfort: Jacob is constituted heir of the Abrahamic grant. The Lord’s gift, not Jacob’s trick, is the title to the land.

Key contrast Both hear Abraham’s blessing transferred to Jacob. Arminians typically stress a sincere grant that Jacob must receive in sojourning faith; Reformed readers typically stress that Isaac is handing on a covenant God had already bound to this son.

Genesis 28:5

5Thus Isaac sent Jacob away. And he went to Paddan-aram, to Laban, the son of Bethuel the Aramean, the brother of Rebekah, Jacob’s and Esau’s mother.

Arminian

Isaac sends; Jacob goes. The genealogy is spelled out so the reader sees the family web: Laban, Bethuel, Rebekah, both sons. Obedience is underway.

Arminian pastoral reading: the blessed man actually leaves. Grace enabled the step; the step still had to be taken.

Reformed

Sending and going close the scene. Moses lingers on kinship so the next chapters will not feel accidental.

Reformed note: even the road to Laban is covenant geography. The God of Abraham is moving the heir toward the household from which the next generation will come.

Genesis 28:6

6Now Esau saw that Isaac had blessed Jacob and sent him away to Paddan-aram to take a wife from there, and that as he blessed him he directed him, “You must not take a wife from the Canaanite women,”

Arminian

Esau “saw” the blessing, the sending, and the Canaanite prohibition. Light reached him. He is not ignorant of what pleased his father and what the blessing involved.

Classical Arminians stress that seeing is already a kind of summons. Esau could have turned; instead he will manage appearances. Prior light raises responsibility.

Reformed

Esau observes the whole package: blessing, departure, marriage charge. Knowledge without repentance is Moses’ diagnosis.

Reformed accent: the reprobate can see the value of the blessing and still miss its heart. Sight here is not saving illumination.

Genesis 28:7

7and that Jacob had obeyed his father and his mother and gone to Paddan-aram.

Arminian

Jacob’s obedience is public enough for Esau to notice. The contrast is already moral, not only legal: one son goes as told.

Arminian note: obedience can be seen and still not imitated. Grace that produced Jacob’s going also left Esau without excuse for remaining unmoved.

Reformed

Moses names Jacob’s obedience as part of what Esau saw. The heir acts; the other watches.

Reformed reading: God distinguishes the brothers not only by oracle but by a visible walk. Jacob’s going is fruit of the blessing’s hold on him.

Genesis 28:8

8So when Esau saw that the Canaanite women did not please Isaac his father,

Arminian

Esau’s concern is Isaac’s displeasure, not the Lord’s will. He reads the family politics more carefully than the covenant.

Pastoral Arminian warning: you can notice what displeases a parent and still not repent toward God. Partial reform aimed at human approval is not conversion.

Reformed

The motive is exposed: Canaanite wives “did not please Isaac.” Esau aims at his father’s face, not at God’s call.

Reformed diagnosis: fear of a father’s frown is not fear of the LORD. Hypocrisy can be domestically shrewd and spiritually dead.

Genesis 28:9

9Esau went to Ishmael and took as his wife, besides the wives he had, Mahalath the daughter of Ishmael, Abraham’s son, the sister of Nebaioth.

Arminian

Esau adds Mahalath from Ishmael—Abraham’s line, but not the promised line—while keeping the wives already hated. A third marriage is meant to look like obedience.

Classical Arminians lament cosmetic religion: extra ritual without putting away the sin. Grace still called Esau to true turning; he chose a patch.

Reformed

Ishmael’s daughter is Abrahamic by blood and outside the covenant by promise. Adding her “besides the wives he had” leaves the old disobedience in place.

Reformed sobriety: false repair cannot buy the blessing. Esau remains in the land with a new wife; Jacob leaves with the promise.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat Esau’s new marriage as repentance. Arminians typically stress a real turning he should have made under known light; Reformed readers typically stress a hypocrite’s patch that never reaches the heart God had not renewed.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Isaac sending Jacob with the Abrahamic blessing—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 28:1–9.

Arminian Notable voices

“Two great promises Abraham was blessed with, and Isaac here entails them both upon Jacob. The promise of heirs, God make thee fruitful and multiply thee. Through his loins that people should descend from Abraham which should be numerous as the stars of heaven.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 28:3)

Reformed Notable voices

“Here follows the form of benediction, which slightly differs in words from the former, but nevertheless tends to the same end. First, he desires that Jacob should be blessed by God; that is, that he should be so increased and amplified in his own offspring, as to grow into a multitude of nations; or, in other words, that he should produce many people who might combine into one body under the same head; as if he had said, Let there arise from thee many tribes, who shall constitute one people.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 28:3)
