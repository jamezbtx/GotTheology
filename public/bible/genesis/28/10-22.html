Passage

Genesis 28

Verses 10–22 · ESV · Voices

Jacob leaves Beersheba toward Haran, sleeps with a stone for a pillow, and dreams of a ladder between earth and heaven. The LORD restates Abraham’s promise, pledges His presence, and Jacob wakes to name the place Bethel—then vows oil, house, and a tenth.

Genesis 28:10

10Jacob left Beersheba and went toward Haran.

Arminian

Beersheba to Haran: the blessed heir is on the road, not on a throne. Departure is obedience under pressure.

Arminian pastoral note: God can meet a man in transit. The journey is already a response to Isaac’s charge; grace will meet him farther on.

Reformed

The heir of promise exits the land of sojourning toward the east. Moses states the fact without ornament.

Reformed comfort: leaving is not loss of the covenant. The God of Beersheba will prove He is not bound to one well.

Genesis 28:11

11And he came to a certain place and stayed there that night, because the sun had set. Taking one of the stones of the place, he put it under his head and lay down in that place to sleep.

Arminian

Sunset, a stone, sleep: no inn, no retinue. Need becomes the setting for revelation. Jacob lies down because he must, not because the place looks holy yet.

Classical Arminians notice that God often draws near where we did not plan a sanctuary. Weariness is no barrier to grace that still seeks the fugitive.

Reformed

“A certain place” is unnamed until God names it. Providence times the halt to sundown and a stone pillow.

Reformed accent: the heir’s poverty is not evidence of divine absence. The Lord has appointed this hard bed for a vision.

Genesis 28:12

12And he dreamed, and behold, there was a ladder set up on the earth, and the top of it reached to heaven. And behold, the angels of God were ascending and descending on it!

Arminian

Earth and heaven are joined: a ladder, angels going both ways. The fugitive is shown that traffic with God has not been cut off by his exile—or his recent sin.

Arminian reading, with Wesley: the ladder images Christ’s mediation, the way of access sincerely opened. Favors come down and services go up by this way, offered to the one who will trust it.

Reformed

The dream is revelation, not indigestion. Angels move on a ladder whose top is heaven; God is about to speak from above it.

Reformed confession, with Calvin: Christ alone connects heaven and earth. The covenant is founded in the Mediator; Bethel already preaches the One who fills the gap sin made.

Key contrast Both read the ladder as more than scenery, and both end at Christ. Arminians typically stress an opened way of intercourse with God, sincerely given to the wanderer; Reformed readers typically stress that the covenant’s traffic runs only through the appointed Mediator, shown here in figure.

Genesis 28:13

13And behold, the Lord stood above it and said, “I am the Lord, the God of Abraham your father and the God of Isaac. The land on which you lie I will give to you and to your offspring.

Arminian

The LORD stands above the ladder and names Himself: God of Abraham and Isaac. Then He gives the land under Jacob’s body to him and his offspring. Speech interprets the vision.

Classical Arminians hear a personal address. The God of the fathers sincerely pledges land to this son; the gift calls for trust from a man who does not yet hold a deed.

Reformed

Mute vision is not left mute. “I am the LORD” anchors Jacob in the God of the fathers; land is granted by oath-speech while he still lies on it as a stranger.

Reformed comfort: God Himself constitutes Jacob heir. The father’s blessing is now the LORD’s own mouth.

Genesis 28:14

14Your offspring shall be like the dust of the earth, and you shall spread abroad to the west and to the east and to the north and to the south, and in you and your offspring shall all the families of the earth be blessed.

Arminian

Dust-many offspring, four-direction spread, and blessing for all families: Abraham’s gospel is restated over a man with a stone pillow. The horizon is the nations.

Arminian emphasis: “all the families of the earth” is a sincere worldwide aim. In Jacob’s offspring—ultimately the Seed—God opens blessing to be received by faith, not a closed clan prize.

Reformed

The Abrahamic formula returns in full: numberless seed, geographic spread, global blessing. God binds the nations’ good to this man’s line.

Reformed accent: the promise is as sure as the Speaker. All families will be blessed in the Seed God has appointed; Jacob’s emptiness tonight cannot shrink the oath.

Key contrast Both hear Genesis 12 restated at Bethel. Arminians typically stress a sincere blessing aimed at all families, to be received by faith; Reformed readers typically stress that the same worldwide word is God’s effectual covenant in the Seed He will not fail to send.

Genesis 28:15

15Behold, I am with you and will keep you wherever you go, and will bring you back to this land. For I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised you.”

Arminian

Presence, keeping, homecoming, and a “until” tied to promise: God pledges Himself to a traveling sinner. “Wherever you go” covers Haran, Laban, and the long way back.

Classical Arminians treasure the companionship. Grace goes with Jacob and still calls him to walk in that keeping—presence that comforts and claims.

Reformed

God anticipates the fear of exile: I am with you; I will keep you; I will bring you back; I will not leave you until the promise is done.

Reformed assurance: the Keeper’s “until” is not a threat of abandonment but a pledge of completion. Jacob’s safety rests on God’s fidelity to His own word.

Key contrast Both cling to “I am with you.” Arminians typically hear a real accompanying grace that Jacob must continue to trust and not treat as automatic; Reformed readers typically hear an effectual keeper-promise that will not stop until God has done all He said.

Genesis 28:16

16Then Jacob awoke from his sleep and said, “Surely the Lord is in this place, and I did not know it.”

Arminian

Waking confession: the LORD was here, and Jacob had not known. Revelation uncovers unnoticed presence. Ignorance was real; so is the new recognition.

Arminian pastoral hope: we sometimes meet God where we did not ask. Grace can surprise a dull traveler and invite him to live awake.

Reformed

Jacob names both the fact and his prior blindness. God was in the place before Jacob named it holy.

Reformed note: the initiative remains the LORD’s appearing. Jacob’s knowledge is gift; the place did not become God’s house because Jacob guessed well.

Genesis 28:17

17And he was afraid and said, “How awesome is this place! This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven.”

Arminian

Fear and awe, not casual warmth: house of God, gate of heaven. Nearness humbles. Access is real, and it is not casual.

Classical Arminians keep both: the gate is open in mercy, and the holiness of the place still bows the sinner who stumbles into it.

Reformed

Dread and comfort meet. “Gate of heaven” is Jacob’s name for the opened access the ladder showed.

Reformed instinct: majesty that would terrify also, in the Mediator, admits. The house is God’s before it is Jacob’s shrine.

Genesis 28:18

18So early in the morning Jacob took the stone that he had put under his head and set it up for a pillar and poured oil on top of it.

Arminian

Dawn turns the pillow into a pillar; oil marks it. Memory becomes worship. Jacob answers revelation with a concrete act.

Arminian reading: grace received seeks a response. The stone is not a rival god; it is a witness that this man will not pretend the night was only a dream.

Reformed

Early rising, pillar, oil: consecration of a memorial, not the carving of an idol. The same stone that served sleep now serves testimony.

Reformed note: worship here is response to the word already spoken. Jacob does not create the presence; he marks where God has spoken.

Genesis 28:19

19He called the name of that place Bethel, but the name of the city was Luz at the first.

Arminian

Bethel—“house of God”—overwrites Luz in Jacob’s mouth. Naming is theology: this spot is interpreted by the night, not by the town’s old label.

Arminian pastoral cue: grace teaches us to rename ordinary ground. What was merely Luz can become, in encounter, a house of God for the one who received the vision.

Reformed

Two names stand: Luz in the map, Bethel in the revelation. Moses records both so later readers will know the city did not yet live by Jacob’s title.

Reformed accent: God names by appearing. The house is His; Jacob’s word only confesses what the dream disclosed.

Genesis 28:20

20Then Jacob made a vow, saying, “If God will be with me and will keep me in this way that I go, and will give me bread to eat and clothing to wear,

Arminian

The vow begins with “If”—not haggling so much as taking God at the night’s word: presence, keeping, bread, clothes. Need is confessed without shame.

Classical Arminians hear a man binding himself to gratitude if the promised help arrives. The conditions track God’s own pledges; faith answers with a vowed life.

Reformed

Jacob’s “if” restates the promise God just made: with you, keep you, provide. The vow is appendage to the oracle, not a bribe to make God kind.

Reformed caution and charity: the grammar sounds conditional, yet the content is borrowed from verse 15. The heir clings to the Keeper’s terms.

Genesis 28:21

21so that I come again to my father’s house in peace, then the Lord shall be my God,

Arminian

Home in peace, then “the LORD shall be my God.” Jacob aims the vow at belonging, not at a mere safe trip. The God of the fathers is to be his God.

Arminian emphasis: this is a responsible yielding. Grace has spoken; Jacob pledges personal allegiance if he is brought home—a real “my God,” not inherited slogan only.

Reformed

Return in peace is the promise’s earthly shape; “the LORD shall be my God” is covenant language. Jacob takes the God of Abraham as his own.

Reformed reading: God will be Jacob’s God because God has already named Himself so. The vow confesses what electing mercy has begun.

Key contrast Both refuse to reduce the vow to a bargain for groceries. Arminians typically hear Jacob’s responsible “then… my God” as grace-enabled allegiance; Reformed readers typically hear the vow as faith clinging to a God who has already claimed him.

Genesis 28:22

22and this stone, which I have set up for a pillar, shall be God’s house. And of all that you give me I will give a full tenth to you.”

Arminian

Pillar as God’s house, and a tenth of all God gives: worship and open-handed return. Jacob plans a future at this stone and a habit of giving.

Arminian pastoral close: received mercy trains generosity. The tenth is response, not purchase. What God gives can be given back in thanks.

Reformed

The stone will be God’s house; the tenth will acknowledge the Giver of “all that you give me.” Even the tithe assumes God’s prior giving.

Reformed close: Jacob’s religion at Bethel is built on gift. House and tenth are signs that the Keeper who promised will also supply what He is owed in gratitude.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Jacob’s ladder and the God of Bethel—so each camp speaks in its own voice on the Mediator who joins heaven and earth.

Arminian Notable voices

“The mediation of Christ. He is this ladder: the foot on earth in his human nature, the top in heaven in his divine nature… All the intercourse between heaven and earth since the fall is by this ladder. Christ is the way: all God’s favours come to us, and all our services come to him, by Christ.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 28:12)
“It was probably a type of Christ, in whom both worlds meet, and in whom the Divine and human nature are conjoined. The Ladder was set up on the Earth, and the Top of it reached to Heaven; for God was manifested in the Flesh, and in him dwelt all the fullness of the Godhead bodily. … Jesus Christ is the grand connecting medium between heaven and earth, and between God and man. By him God comes down to man; through him man ascends to God.”
Adam Clarke · Commentary on the Bible (on Gen 28:12)

Reformed Notable voices

“It is Christ alone, therefore, who connects heaven and earth: he is the only Mediator who reaches from heaven down to earth: he is the medium through which the fullness of all celestial blessings flows down to us, and through which we, in turn, ascend to God.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 28:12)
“That ladder … was a picture of Christ, the way of access between man and God. You will never perceive God in nature, until you have learned to see God in grace. … You must first of all perceive God incarnate in the flesh of Christ, before you will perceive God in the creation which he has made. … When I see Christ, I see God’s new and living way, between my soul and my God, most clear and pleasant.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · Sermon 401, “Jacob’s Waking Exclamation,” on Gen 28:16 (21 July 1861)

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
