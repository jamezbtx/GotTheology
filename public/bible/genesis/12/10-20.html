Passage

Genesis 12

Verses 10–20 · ESV

Famine drives Abram down to Egypt. Fear for his life leads him to pass Sarai off as his sister; she is taken into Pharaoh’s house, Abram is enriched, and the LORD strikes Pharaoh’s household. The pagan king rebukes the patriarch and sends them away—promise threatened, then preserved by God, not by Abram’s scheme.

Genesis 12:10

10Now there was a famine in the land. So Abram went down to Egypt to sojourn there, for the famine was severe in the land.

Arminian

Severe famine meets the land just promised. Abram goes down to Egypt to sojourn—survival, not a change of gods.

Classical Arminians do not pretend the choice is simple. Need is real; going down can be prudence or unbelief. The text will soon show how fear, not hunger alone, shapes what he does with Sarai.

Reformed

The land of promise can starve. Providence includes famine that pushes the chosen man off the map he just entered.

Reformed reading: “went down” is geographically and theologically downward. God is not absent from the scarcity; the next verses will prove He still guards the promise Abram is about to endanger.

Genesis 12:11

11When he was about to enter Egypt, he said to Sarai his wife, “I know that you are a woman beautiful in appearance,

Arminian

At the border, beauty becomes a threat in Abram’s mind. He addresses Sarai as wife even as he prepares to hide that fact.

Arminian pastoral honesty: fear talks before it prays here. The man who built altars now calculates. Grace does not make the patriarch infallible; it will have to rescue what his plan puts at risk.

Reformed

“I know” introduces a scheme, not a prayer. Sarai’s beauty is real; Abram’s reading of Egypt is anxious.

Reformed note: the father of faith is still a sinner. Election and promise do not require a flawless hero; they require a faithful God about to be needed.

Genesis 12:12

12and when the Egyptians see you, they will say, ‘This is his wife.’ Then they will kill me, but they will let you live.

Arminian

Abram scripts the worst: they will kill me and spare you. Self-preservation imagines Sarai as the expendable shield.

Classical Arminians name this as real moral failure, not a clever ruse. The call to trust the God of verse 3 (“I will curse him who dishonors you”) is set aside for a story Abram tells himself about Egypt.

Reformed

Projected murder organizes the lie. The man of promise treats God’s protection as less certain than Egyptian violence.

Reformed accent: unbelief here is not theoretical. It is a husband bargaining his wife’s safety against his own—yet the covenant God will still act.

Genesis 12:13

13Say you are my sister, that it may go well with me because of you, and that my life may be spared for your sake.”

Arminian

The request is plain: lie, so that it may go well with me and my life be spared for your sake. Half-truth (she is kin) is still a use of Sarai.

Arminian emphasis: Abram’s will is active and culpable. He asks her to collude. The passage will not baptize the tactic; later Pharaoh will speak the moral sentence Abram would not.

Reformed

“That it may go well with me” is the tell. Blessing was God’s to give (vv. 2–3); Abram now seeks a private well-being through deceit.

Reformed reading: the scheme inverts the promise’s direction. Instead of being a blessing, he would be spared “for your sake”—using the very wife through whom offspring must come.

Key contrast Both refuse to excuse the lie. Arminians typically stress Abram’s responsible, resistible unbelief under a still-sincere promise; Reformed readers typically stress that the same failure cannot void God’s election—the Lord will keep the line despite the patriarch.

Genesis 12:14

14When Abram entered Egypt, the Egyptians saw that the woman was very beautiful.

Arminian

What he feared happens: they see her beauty. The plan “works” at the level of notice, which is not the same as wisdom.

Arminian note: consequences unfold along the path chosen. Beauty is not sin; hiding the marriage is. The story lets the reader feel the cost landing on Sarai.

Reformed

The narrator confirms the beauty without endorsing the scheme. Egypt sees what Abram used as leverage.

Reformed instinct: providence is already in the seeing. The threat to the promised wife is now public, and the Lord will not leave the covenant hanging on Abram’s cover story.

Genesis 12:15

15And when the princes of Pharaoh saw her, they praised her to Pharaoh. And the woman was taken into Pharaoh’s house.

Arminian

Praise to Pharaoh, then taking: Sarai is moved into the palace. Abram’s strategy delivers her into another man’s house.

Classical Arminians stay with her in this verse. The matriarch of promise is treated as a prize. Human schemes can place holy things in danger; they cannot force God to abandon His word.

Reformed

Taken into Pharaoh’s house is the crisis of the seed. If Sarai is lost to Pharaoh, the offspring of verse 7 is humanly impossible.

Reformed comfort begins in the horror: God has let the danger become acute so that His own rescue, not Abram’s cunning, will be the reason the promise survives.

Genesis 12:16

16And for her sake he dealt well with Abram; and he had sheep, oxen, male donkeys, male servants, female servants, female donkeys, and camels.

Arminian

Abram is enriched “for her sake.” The livestock list is success that tastes like betrayal. Going well (v. 13) arrives as plunder on a silenced wife.

Arminian pastoral warning: prosperity can ride on a compromised conscience. Wealth here is not proof of approval; the next verse is.

Reformed

The inventory is ironic blessing. Goods flow to Abram while Sarai is in the palace—the wrong well-being at the wrong price.

Reformed note: even this enrichment sits under providence that will soon reverse Pharaoh. God can let a bad plan “work” without making it righteous.

Genesis 12:17

17But the LORD afflicted Pharaoh and his house with great plagues because of Sarai, Abram’s wife.

Arminian

“But the LORD” is the turning. Great plagues fall because of Sarai, named again as Abram’s wife. God defends her when her husband would not.

Arminian reading: the Lord remains free and just. He can afflict a household to protect a woman and a promise. Grace here is intervention, not indulgence of Abram’s tactic.

Reformed

The covenant Name acts. Pharaoh’s house is struck for the sake of “Sarai, Abram’s wife”—the title Abram hid.

Reformed accent: preservation of the promised line is God’s work. Plague in Egypt this early already hints that the Lord, not the patriarch’s wisdom, guards the family He chose.

Genesis 12:18

18So Pharaoh called Abram and said, “What is this you have done to me? Why did you not tell me that she was your wife?

Arminian

The pagan king becomes prophet of sorts: What have you done? Why did you not tell me? Moral clarity comes from the palatial victim of the lie.

Classical Arminians let the rebuke stand. Abram is accountable. God’s plagues do not erase the need for truth-telling; they expose it.

Reformed

Pharaoh’s questions are just. The chosen man is catechized by a ruler who feared to take another man’s wife once he knew.

Reformed humility: common-grace conscience in Pharaoh outruns Abram’s fear. Election is no shield against deserved shame.

Genesis 12:19

19Why did you say, ‘She is my sister,’ so that I took her for my wife? Now then, here is your wife; take her, and go.”

Arminian

Pharaoh names the lie and its fruit: I took her for my wife. Then he restores her—“take her, and go.” Restoration comes through a rebuke.

Arminian pastoral close of the scheme: confession is extracted by circumstance. Abram must receive his wife back as gift after failure, and go—still under the original call.

Reformed

“Here is your wife” is mercy in a king’s mouth, after God has made the truth costly. Sarai is returned; the offspring promise can proceed.

Reformed comfort: God uses even Pharaoh’s dismissal to put the family back on the road. The covenant does not travel by Abram’s cleverness.

Genesis 12:20

20And Pharaoh gave men orders concerning him, and they sent him away with his wife and all that he had.

Arminian

Escorted out with wife and goods: Egypt expels the sojourner. He leaves richer and shamed, Sarai beside him again.

Arminian hope: failure is not the last word. The God who called in verses 1–3 still has them. Repentance will have to deepen (the sister-lie will return in ch. 20); for now, going on is itself mercy.

Reformed

Orders and an escort: providence marches them out. Wife and possessions leave together because the Lord preserved both.

Reformed close: the promise survives Egypt by God’s plague and a pagan’s command. Abram is sent away still Abram, still chosen—grace that keeps the unimpressive father of many nations.

Key contrast Both see God saving the promise from Abram’s fear. Arminians typically stress the moral exposure and a mercy that still calls the patriarch to truer trust; Reformed readers typically stress God’s invincible preservation of the chosen line despite the chosen man’s collapse.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

From the tradition

Voices from both camps

Historic words from Arminian and Reformed teachers—so you hear each side in its own voice, not a caricature.

Arminian Notable voices

“I ascribe to grace the commencement, the continuance and the consummation of all good… The controversy… relates solely to the mode of operation, whether it be irresistible or not… I believe, according to the scriptures, that many persons resist the Holy Spirit and reject the grace that is offered.”
Jacobus Arminius · Declaration of Sentiments (1608)
“The grace or love of God, whence cometh our salvation, is FREE IN ALL, and FREE FOR ALL.”
John Wesley · Sermon 128, “Free Grace” (1739)
“That this grace of God is the beginning, continuance, and accomplishment of all good… But as respects the mode of the operation of this grace, it is not irresistible, inasmuch as it is written concerning many, that they have resisted the Holy Spirit.”
The Remonstrance · Article IV (1610)

Reformed Notable voices

“We call predestination God’s eternal decree, by which he determined with himself what he willed to become of each man. For all are not created in equal condition; rather, eternal life is foreordained for some, eternal damnation for others.”
John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5
“I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite certain that, if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards.”
Charles H. Spurgeon · “A Defense of Calvinism”
“But now that election operates according to grace, there is hope even for the most wretched. If work and reward were the standard of admission into the kingdom of heaven, its gates would be opened for no one.”
Herman Bavinck · Reformed Dogmatics, vol. 2

Quotations lightly abridged for length; wording follows standard English translations of the sources named.
