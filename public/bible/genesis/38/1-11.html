Passage

Genesis 38

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

While Joseph’s story pauses, Judah goes down from his brothers, marries a Canaanite, and fathers Er, Onan, and Shelah. Er and Onan die under the LORD’s judgment; Judah withholds Shelah from Tamar and sends her home as a widow.

Genesis 38:1

1It happened at that time that Judah went down from his brothers and turned aside to a certain Adullamite, whose name was Hirah.

Arminian

“At that time” drops Judah out of the brothers’ circle toward Hirah of Adullam. Descent is geographic and moral: he turns aside.

Classical Arminians see a real, responsible separation. Grace had placed Judah in Jacob’s house; leaving that company is a choice that will shape the next ten verses.

Reformed

Genesis interrupts Joseph with Judah’s detour. Providence is not absent from the aside; the messianic line will run through this very chapter.

Reformed framing: “went down” and “turned aside” sit under God’s larger purpose, even as Judah’s companionship with Hirah is his own.

Genesis 38:2

2There Judah saw the daughter of a certain Canaanite whose name was Shua. He took her and went in to her,

Arminian

Seeing, taking, going in: Judah marries Shua’s Canaanite daughter without seeking the family’s counsel. Desire leads the household decision.

Arminian pastoral caution: attraction is not vocation. The patriarchs were warned about Canaanite marriage; Judah’s will is active here, and the fruit will be costly.

Reformed

The verbs are abrupt—saw, took, went in. Judah’s house is planted in Canaanite soil.

Reformed note: God can still write promise through compromised unions. The line is not protected by Judah’s wisdom, but by the LORD who overrules.

Genesis 38:3

3and she conceived and bore a son, and he called his name Er.

Arminian

A firstborn is named Er. Fruitfulness follows the union; naming claims the child as Judah’s.

Arminian reading: life is gift even when the marriage was unwise. What comes next will show that birth is not the same as blessing.

Reformed

Conception and naming open Judah’s line. Er will be firstborn—and first to fall.

Reformed gravity: God gives the son. The LORD’s later judgment on Er will prove that gift does not cancel holiness.

Genesis 38:4

4She conceived again and bore a son, and she called his name Onan.

Arminian

The mother names Onan. A second son arrives in the same pattern of ordinary household growth.

Classical Arminians honor the mother’s voice in the naming. Ordinary births still sit under God’s moral government, as Onan’s later wickedness will show.

Reformed

A second son, a mother’s name. The family expands on Canaanite ground.

Reformed note: providence fills the house that will soon be emptied of two sons. The story is already under the LORD’s sight.

Genesis 38:5

5Yet again she bore a son, and she called his name Shelah. Judah was in Chezib when she bore him.

Arminian

Shelah is born while Judah is away in Chezib. Absence at a birth is a small sign of distance in the home.

Arminian observation: fatherhood can be geographically near and spiritually far. The son he will later withhold from Tamar is already born at a distance.

Reformed

Three sons complete the first generation. Judah’s location at Shelah’s birth is remembered.

Reformed framing: even the aside about Chezib is not filler. The third son will become the hinge of Judah’s fear and Tamar’s future.

Genesis 38:6

6And Judah took a wife for Er his firstborn, and her name was Tamar.

Arminian

Judah takes a wife for Er: Tamar enters the story as a named woman, not a prop. The firstborn’s marriage is the father’s arrangement.

Arminian dignity: Tamar’s name matters. She will bear more righteousness than Judah, but here she is simply given—and will be owed justice.

Reformed

Tamar is chosen for the firstborn. The future of Judah’s seed now includes this woman.

Reformed comfort: God already knows the matriarch of Perez. Human arranging sits inside a purpose larger than Judah’s household plan.

Genesis 38:7

7But Er, Judah's firstborn, was wicked in the sight of the Lord, and the Lord put him to death.

Arminian

Er’s wickedness is “in the sight of the LORD,” and the LORD puts him to death. Moral evil is seen and judged, not ignored.

Classical Arminians refuse to soften the sentence. Wickedness is culpable; death is God’s holy response. Firstborn status does not shield a man who lives against the LORD.

Reformed

The narrator gives no catalogue of Er’s deeds—only the verdict and the act: wicked, put to death by the LORD.

Reformed confession: God is judge of Judah’s house. Election of a tribe does not license the firstborn’s evil; holiness still kills.

Key contrast Both take Er’s death as divine judgment, not accident. Arminians typically stress culpable wickedness under a holy God who responds to real evil; Reformed readers typically stress the LORD’s sovereign right to put to death those He judges wicked.

Genesis 38:8

8Then Judah said to Onan, “Go in to your brother's wife and perform the duty of a brother-in-law to her, and raise up offspring for your brother.”

Arminian

Judah commands levirate duty: Onan must raise offspring for Er. The widow is not to be discarded; the dead brother’s name is to live.

Arminian justice: Tamar has a claim. Family duty is a real moral obligation, not a suggestion Onan may privately gut.

Reformed

The brother-in-law duty is Judah’s word and, later, Torah’s pattern. Offspring for the dead brother is the point.

Reformed note: God cares about name, seed, and the vulnerable widow. The command sets the stage for Onan’s refusal of that care.

Genesis 38:9

9But Onan knew that the offspring would not be his. So whenever he went in to his brother's wife he would waste the semen on the ground, so as not to give offspring to his brother.

Arminian

Onan knows the child would not be his, so he uses Tamar while refusing the duty. The act is calculated, repeated (“whenever”), and aimed against his brother’s name.

Arminian clarity: this is exploitation plus injustice, not a puzzle about contraception in the abstract. He takes the pleasure and withholds the offspring he owed. That willful refusal is wicked.

Reformed

Motive is named: the offspring would not be his. He goes in, then wastes the seed so Er will have no name.

Reformed diagnosis: Onan sins against brother, widow, and the LORD’s concern for the line. The text locates wickedness in the knowing refusal to give offspring.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat Onan’s act as a minor private habit. Arminians typically emphasize responsible, repeated injustice against Tamar and Er; Reformed readers typically emphasize wickedness in the LORD’s sight that He will judge with death.

Genesis 38:10

10And what he did was wicked in the sight of the Lord, and he put him to death also.

Arminian

Again: wicked in the LORD’s sight, put to death also. Onan is not a victim of bad luck; he is judged for what he did.

Classical Arminians hear a severe mercy to Tamar and a warning to the living. God’s sight still defines wickedness when families hide it.

Reformed

The same formula as Er: sight of the LORD, death by the LORD. Two sons, one verdict.

Reformed gravity: God will not let Judah’s line be advanced by theft of a brother’s name. Judgment is His, and it is just.

Genesis 38:11

11Then Judah said to Tamar his daughter-in-law, “Remain a widow in your father's house, till Shelah my son grows up”—for he feared that he would die, like his brothers. So Tamar went and remained in her father's house.

Arminian

Judah delays Shelah under a pious-sounding “till he grows up,” but the narrator exposes fear: Shelah might die too. Tamar is sent home, owed a husband she will not receive.

Arminian pastoral honesty: fear can wear the mask of care. Tamar obeys and waits; Judah’s withheld promise is a real wrong that later he himself will confess.

Reformed

The parenthetical is the truth: Judah feared. Widowhood in her father’s house is the stall, not the solution.

Reformed reading: God sees the fear and the fraud. Tamar’s waiting will become the means by which the promise-line is forced into the open.

Key contrast Both name Judah’s delay as fearful injustice, not prudence. Arminians typically stress his responsible breach of duty to Tamar; Reformed readers typically stress that God will still keep the line through the widow Judah tried to sideline.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Judah, Tamar withheld, and the LORD’s judgment on Er and Onan—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Genesis 38:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“Judah went down from his brethren—Withdrew for a time from his father's family, and got intimately acquainted with one Hirah an Adullamite. When young people that have been well educated begin to change their company, they will soon change their manners, and lose their good education. They that go down from their brethren, that forsake the society of the seed of Israel, and pick up Canaanites for their companions, are going down the hill apace.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 38:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“Before Moses proceeds in relating the history of Joseph, he inserts the genealogy of Judah, to which he devotes more labor, because the Redeemer was thence to derive his origin; for the continuous history of that tribe, from which salvation was to be bought, could not remain unknown, without loss. What is here related, so far from inflating the minds of the sons of Judah, ought rather to cover them with shame.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 38:1)
