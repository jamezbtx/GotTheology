Passage

Genesis 38

Verses 1–11 · WEB · Voices

While Joseph’s story pauses, Judah goes down from his brothers, marries a Canaanite, and fathers Er, Onan, and Shelah. Er and Onan die under the LORD’s judgment; Judah withholds Shelah from Tamar and sends her home as a widow.

Genesis 38:1

1At that time, Judah went down from his brothers, and visited a certain Adullamite, whose name was Hirah.

Arminian

“At that time” drops Judah out of the brothers’ circle toward Hirah of Adullam. Descent is geographic and moral: he turns aside.

Wesleyan Arminians see a responsible separation. Grace had placed Judah in Jacob’s house; leaving that company is a choice that will shape the next ten verses.

Reformed

Genesis interrupts Joseph with Judah’s detour. Providence is not absent from the aside; the messianic line will run through this very chapter.

“Went down” and turned aside sit under God’s larger purpose, even as Judah’s companionship with Hirah is his own.

Genesis 38:2

2There, Judah saw the daughter of a certain Canaanite man named Shua. He took her, and went in to her.

Arminian

Seeing, taking, going in: Judah marries Shua’s Canaanite daughter without seeking the family’s counsel. Desire leads the household decision.

Attraction is not vocation. The patriarchs were warned about Canaanite marriage; Judah’s will is active here, and the fruit will be costly.

Reformed

The verbs are abrupt—saw, took, went in. Judah’s house is planted in Canaanite soil.

God can still write promise through compromised unions. The line is not protected by Judah’s wisdom, but by the LORD who overrules.

Genesis 38:3

3She conceived, and bore a son; and he named him Er.

Arminian

A firstborn is named Er. Fruitfulness follows the union; naming claims the child as Judah’s.

Life is gift even when the marriage was unwise. What comes next will show that birth is not the same as blessing.

Reformed

Conception and naming open Judah’s line. Er will be firstborn—and first to fall.

God gives the son. The LORD’s later judgment on Er will prove that gift does not annul holiness.

Genesis 38:4

4She conceived again, and bore a son; and she named him Onan.

Arminian

The mother names Onan. A second son arrives in the same pattern of ordinary household growth.

Classical Arminians honor the mother’s voice in the naming. Ordinary births still sit under God’s moral government, as Onan’s later wickedness will show.

Reformed

A second son, a mother’s name. The family expands on Canaanite ground.

Providence fills the house that will soon be emptied of two sons. The story is already under the LORD’s sight.

Genesis 38:5

5She yet again bore a son, and named him Shelah. He was at Chezib when she bore him.

Arminian

Shelah is born while Judah is away in Chezib. Absence at a birth is a small sign of distance in the home.

Fatherhood can be geographically near and spiritually far. The son he will later withhold from Tamar is already born at a distance.

Reformed

Three sons complete the first generation. Judah’s location at Shelah’s birth is remembered.

Even the aside about Chezib is not filler. The third son will become the hinge of Judah’s fear and Tamar’s future.

Genesis 38:6

6Judah took a wife for Er, his firstborn, and her name was Tamar.

Arminian

Judah takes a wife for Er: Tamar enters the story as a named woman, not a prop. The firstborn’s marriage is the father’s arrangement.

Tamar is named, and her name will matter. She will bear more righteousness than Judah, but here she is simply given—and will be owed justice.

Reformed

Tamar is chosen for the firstborn. The future of Judah’s seed now includes this woman.

God already knows the matriarch of Perez. Human arranging sits inside a purpose larger than Judah’s household plan.

Genesis 38:7

7Er, Judah’s firstborn, was wicked in the LORD’s sight. So the LORD killed him.

Arminian

Er’s wickedness is “in the LORD’s sight. So the LORD,” and the LORD puts him to death. Moral evil is seen and judged, not ignored.

Arminians refuse to soften the sentence. Wickedness is culpable; death is God’s holy response. Firstborn status does not shield a man who lives against the LORD.

Reformed

The narrator gives no catalogue of Er’s deeds—only the verdict and the act: wicked, put to death by the LORD.

God is judge of Judah’s house. Election of a tribe does not license the firstborn’s evil; holiness still kills.

Key contrast Both take Er’s death as divine judgment, not accident. Arminians typically emphasize culpable wickedness under a holy God who responds to evil; Calvinists underline the LORD’s sovereign right to put to death those He judges wicked.

Genesis 38:8

8Judah said to Onan, “Go in to your brother’s wife, and perform the duty of a husband’s brother to her, and raise up offspring for your brother.”

Arminian

Judah commands levirate duty: Onan must raise offspring for Er. The widow is not to be discarded; the dead brother’s name is to live.

Tamar has a claim. Family duty is a moral obligation, not a suggestion Onan may privately gut.

Reformed

The brother-in-law duty is Judah’s word and, later, Torah’s pattern. Offspring for the dead brother is the point.

God cares about name, seed, and the vulnerable widow. The command sets the stage for Onan’s refusal of that care.

Genesis 38:9

9Onan knew that the offspring wouldn’t be his; and when he went in to his brother’s wife, he spilled his semen on the ground, lest he should give offspring to his brother.

Arminian

Onan knows the child would not be his, so he uses Tamar while refusing the duty. The act is calculated, repeated (“when”), and aimed against his brother’s name.

This is exploitation plus injustice, not a puzzle about contraception in the abstract. He takes the pleasure and withholds the offspring he owed. That willful refusal is wicked.

Reformed

Motive is named: the offspring wouldn’t be his. He goes in, then wastes the seed so Er will have no name.

Onan sins against brother, widow, and the LORD’s concern for the line. The text locates wickedness in the knowing refusal to give offspring.

Key contrast Both refuse to treat Onan’s act as a minor private habit. Arminians emphasize responsible, repeated injustice against Tamar and Er; Calvinists emphasize wickedness in the LORD’s sight that He will judge with death.

Genesis 38:10

10The thing which he did was evil in the LORD’s sight, and he killed him also.

Arminian

Onan is not a victim of bad luck; he is judged for what he did.

The passage carries a severe mercy to Tamar and a warning to the living. God’s sight still defines wickedness when families hide it.

Reformed

The same formula as Er: sight of the LORD, death by the LORD. Two sons, one verdict.

God will not let Judah’s line be advanced by theft of a brother’s name. Judgment is His, and it is just.

Genesis 38:11

11Then Judah said to Tamar, his daughter-in-law, “Remain a widow in your father’s house, until Shelah, my son, is grown up;” for he said, “Lest he also die, like his brothers.” Tamar went and lived in her father’s house.

Arminian

Judah delays Shelah under a pious-sounding “till he grows up,” but the narrator exposes fear: Shelah might die too. Tamar is sent home, owed a husband she will not receive.

Fear can wear the mask of care. Tamar obeys and waits; Judah’s withheld promise is a wrong that later he himself will confess.

Reformed

The parenthetical is the truth: Judah feared. Widowhood in her father’s house is the stall, not the solution.

God sees the fear and the fraud. Tamar’s waiting will become the means by which the promise-line is forced into the open.

Key contrast Both name Judah’s delay as fearful injustice, not prudence. Arminians stress his responsible breach of duty to Tamar; Reformed readers underline that God will still keep the line through the widow Judah tried to sideline.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What older interpreters in each camp said about Judah, Tamar withheld, and the LORD’s judgment on Er and Onan.

Arminian Notable voices

“Judah went down from his brethren—Withdrew for a time from his father's family, and got intimately acquainted with one Hirah an Adullamite. When young people that have been well educated begin to change their company, they will soon change their manners, and lose their good education. They that go down from their brethren, that forsake the society of the seed of Israel, and pick up Canaanites for their companions, are going down the hill apace.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the Old Testament (on Gen 38:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“Before Moses proceeds in relating the history of Joseph, he inserts the genealogy of Judah, to which he devotes more labor, because the Redeemer was thence to derive his origin; for the continuous history of that tribe, from which salvation was to be bought, could not remain unknown, without loss. What is here related, so far from inflating the minds of the sons of Judah, ought rather to cover them with shame.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Genesis (on Gen 38:1)
