Passage

Romans 5

Verses 12–21 · WEB

Paul compares Adam and Christ. Sin and death entered the world through one man, and through one man’s obedience grace and righteousness abound to many. The passage underlies the doctrine of original sin and the representative work of Christ. Arminian and Reformed readers share much of this, while differing on how Adam’s sin passes to his descendants and on the scope of “all” in verse 18.

Romans 5:12

12Therefore, as sin entered into the world through one man, and death through sin, so death passed to all men because all sinned.

Arminian

Sin entered the world through one man, and death spread to all because all sinned. Arminian readers affirm original sin. Wesley held that all people inherit a corrupt nature from Adam and are born under guilt, though many later Arminians emphasize inherited corruption more than inherited guilt.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Adam as the representative head of humanity. Many Reformed theologians teach that Adam’s guilt is imputed to his descendants, so that “all sinned” in him.

Key contrast Both affirm that Adam’s sin brought sin and death to all. Reformed readers commonly teach the imputation of Adam’s guilt through his headship. Arminians affirm inherited corruption, and Wesley also affirmed inherited guilt, while many later Arminians emphasize corruption more than guilt.

Romans 5:13

13For until the law, sin was in the world; but sin is not charged when there is no law.

Arminian

Sin was in the world before the law, though it is not counted where there is no law. Arminian readers see Paul explaining that death reigned even before Moses.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this as evidence that death came through Adam’s sin rather than through each person’s violation of the law of Moses.

Romans 5:14

14Nevertheless death reigned from Adam until Moses, even over those whose sins weren’t like Adam’s disobedience, who is a foreshadowing of him who was to come.

Arminian

Death reigned from Adam to Moses even over those who did not sin as Adam did. Adam is a type of the one to come. Both traditions see Adam and Christ as heads of two humanities.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the parallel of representative headship, which grounds both condemnation in Adam and justification in Christ.

Romans 5:15

15But the free gift isn’t like the trespass. For if by the trespass of the one the many died, much more did the grace of God and the gift by the grace of the one man, Jesus Christ, abound to the many.

Arminian

The gift is not like the trespass. If many died through one man’s trespass, much more has God’s grace abounded to the many. Arminian readers stress the abundance of grace, which surpasses the damage of sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s work as greater than Adam’s failure, secured for those who belong to him.

Romans 5:16

16The gift is not as through one who sinned; for the judgment came by one to condemnation, but the free gift followed many trespasses to justification.

Arminian

Judgment followed one trespass and brought condemnation; the gift followed many trespasses and brought justification. Both traditions see grace overcoming far more than Adam’s sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see justification as God’s answer to the accumulated guilt of many sins.

Romans 5:17

17For if by the trespass of the one, death reigned through the one; so much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness reign in life through the one, Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Those who receive the abundance of grace and the gift of righteousness reign in life through Christ. Arminian readers note the word “receive”: the benefits of Christ come to those who accept them by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gift of righteousness as imputed to those united to Christ, who will certainly reign in life.

Romans 5:18

18So then as through one trespass, all men were condemned; even so through one act of righteousness, all men were justified to life.

Arminian

“Through one act of righteousness, all men were justified to life.” Arminian readers see the universal scope of Christ’s work: his righteousness provides justification for all, received by those who believe. Many speak of a universal benefit of the atonement, including the removal of the guilt of original sin for infants.

Reformed

Reformed readers read “all” in light of verse 17, which speaks of those who receive grace. All who are in Adam are condemned, and all who are in Christ are justified. The parallel describes two representatives and their people, not universal salvation.

Key contrast Arminian readers see Christ’s work providing justification for all, effective for those who believe. Reformed readers read “all” as all who are in Christ, as verse 17’s “those who receive” suggests. Neither tradition reads the verse as teaching universal salvation.

Romans 5:19

19For as through the one man’s disobedience many were made sinners, even so through the obedience of the one, many will be made righteous.

Arminian

Through one man’s disobedience many were made sinners, and through one man’s obedience many will be made righteous. Arminian readers see Christ’s obedience as the ground of righteousness for believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s obedience, both his life and death, credited to his people, as Adam’s disobedience was credited to his.

Romans 5:20

20The law came in that the trespass might abound; but where sin abounded, grace abounded more exceedingly,

Arminian

The law came in so that trespass might increase, but where sin increased, grace abounded all the more. Both traditions see the law exposing sin and grace overcoming it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law as a means of revealing sin and driving sinners to Christ.

Romans 5:21

21that as sin reigned in death, even so grace might reign through righteousness to eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.

Arminian

Grace reigns through righteousness to eternal life through Christ. Arminian readers see grace triumphant over sin and death for all who receive it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace reigning as a king, sure to accomplish its purpose in God’s people.
