Passage

Romans 11

Verses 1–10 · ESV · Voices

Paul asks whether God has rejected his people: by no means—he himself is an Israelite; God has not rejected the people he foreknew; as in Elijah’s day a remnant remains, chosen by grace and not by works; Israel failed to obtain what it sought, the elect obtained it, and the rest were hardened.

Romans 11:1

1I ask, then, has God rejected his people? By no means! For I myself am an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, a member of the tribe of Benjamin.

Arminian

Wesley: Hath God rejected his whole people—all Israel? In no wise. Paul’s first proof is himself: an Israelite, Abraham’s descendant, of Benjamin. If God had cast off the nation as such, the apostle to the Gentiles would be a contradiction.

Classical Arminians hear a national “no.” Chapter 10 ended with held-out hands and a contrary people; chapter 11 opens by refusing total rejection. There is now a remnant who believe, Wesley adds, and hereafter all Israel will be saved. The people are not discarded. Unbelief has not become a finished, absolute reprobation of Jacob’s line.

Reformed

Has God rejected his people? By no means. Paul himself is the first exhibit: Israelite, of Abraham, of Benjamin. Calvin will rest the “no” on the people God foreknew (v. 2), not on ethnic identity alone.

Reformed accent: the question is real after 10:21’s contrary people. God has not cast away His people—meaning, Calvin will say, those He foreknew with electing good pleasure. Paul’s pedigree proves a remnant exists now. “By no means” is covenant fidelity, not a promise that every Israelite is saved. The nation is not abandoned; not every branch still stands.

Romans 11:2

2God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew. Do you not know what the Scripture says of Elijah, how he appeals to God against Israel?

Arminian

God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew. Wesley’s “in no wise” still governs: the people known of old are not cast off. Foreknowledge here, for classical Arminians, is God’s knowing of His people—and, as in 8:29, is not Calvin’s bare good pleasure irrespective of faith. The remnant who believe (v. 5) are the present tense of that knowing.

Elijah’s appeal against Israel is the next proof: even in a night of national apostasy God had a people. Arminian reading: He had not rejected those who had not bowed to Baal. The foreknown people are the believing people, now and—Wesley—hereafter, when all Israel will be saved. Do not turn “whom he foreknew” into an eternal pick that made unbelief inevitable for the rest.

Reformed

God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew. Calvin: by the verb foreknow is not a foresight of what sort of being any one will be, but that good pleasure according to which he has chosen those as sons who, being not yet born, could not have procured for themselves his favor.

Reformed hinge: foreknowledge is electing love, matching 8:29, not inspection of future faith. The people not rejected are the people so known. Elijah is summoned because remnant logic is old: God’s purpose held a people when the prophet thought he was alone. Foresight of belief is exactly what Calvin denies here. Good pleasure, not foreseen worth, names who is not cast away.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take “whom he foreknew” as God’s knowing of His people, the remnant who believe (Wesley), not an unconditional decree that fixed the rest in unbelief. Reformed readings, with Calvin, take foreknow as electing good pleasure, not foresight of what sort of being any one will be: those chosen as sons before they were born are the people God has not rejected.

Romans 11:3

3“Lord, they have killed your prophets, they have demolished your altars, and I alone am left, and they seek my life.”

Arminian

Elijah’s complaint: they have killed your prophets, demolished your altars; I alone am left, and they seek my life. The prophet reads the nation as finished and himself as the last believer. He is wrong about the count, not about the violence.

Arminian pastoral: despair that “I alone am left” is a temptation even in a true servant. God’s answer (v. 4) will name thousands who had not bowed. Wilful prejudice (Wesley on v. 7) explains the killing of prophets; it does not mean God had reprobated the whole people. The remnant who believe were there while Elijah could not see them.

Reformed

Elijah appeals against Israel as if the covenant were extinct. Prophets killed, altars down, one man left, his life sought. Scripture records the plea so that God’s reply, not the prophet’s census, will be the last word.

Reformed reading: the complaint is how unbelief looks from inside a collapse. Calvin’s remnant according to election is the answer waiting in v. 4. Elijah did not create the seven thousand by noticing them; God had kept them. The prophet’s solitude is real feeling and false theology. God’s people are not measured by the visible ruin.

Romans 11:4

4But what is God’s reply to him? “I have kept for myself seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal.”

Arminian

God’s reply: I have kept for myself seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal. Wesley’s remnant who believe stands here in Old Testament dress. They had not bowed—their faith was real, their refusal of Baal was theirs.

Classical Arminians hear both the “I have kept” and the “who have not bowed.” God preserved a people; the people actually would not kneel to the idol. Keeping is grace; not bowing is believing. The seven thousand are not a roll that made bowing impossible for everyone else so much as the living evidence that God had not rejected His people, and that faith remained possible in a dark day.

Reformed

What is God’s reply? I have kept for myself seven thousand who have not bowed to Baal. Calvin’s accent falls on the keeper: God reserved them for Himself by electing good pleasure, not because He foresaw they would be a certain sort of being.

Reformed reading: “I have kept for myself” is sovereign reservation. The seven thousand’s not-bowing is the fruit of that keeping, not its ground. Elijah is told what he could not see: a people God had already held. Present remnant (v. 5) will match this pattern—chosen by grace, not produced by the prophet’s accuracy or by works.

Romans 11:5

5So too at the present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace.

Arminian

So too at the present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace. Wesley: according to the election of grace—according to that gracious purpose of God, He that believeth shall be saved. The remnant is defined by believing, as Elijah’s seven thousand were defined by not bowing.

Classical Arminians take “chosen by grace” as God’s gracious purpose to save believers, not an unconditional selection of persons irrespective of faith. Now there is a remnant who believe; hereafter all Israel will be saved. Grace chooses the way—faith, not works (v. 6)—and the people found in that way. Do not turn the remnant into a hint that the rest never could have believed.

Reformed

At the present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace. Calvin: a remnant according to the election of grace. The parallel with Elijah is exact: God has a people now as He had seven thousand then, and the choosing is grace, not Israel’s zeal (10:2).

Reformed hinge: remnant is election’s present tense. “Chosen by grace” is personal and prior, the good pleasure of v. 2, not foresight of who would believe. A remnant means most of visible Israel is not that remnant. Grace explains why any remain. Verse 6 will lock this against works; v. 7 will name the elect who obtained and the rest who were hardened.

Key contrast Arminian readings, with Wesley, take the remnant chosen by grace as that gracious purpose of God, He that believeth shall be saved: the remnant who believe now, not an unconditional roster. Reformed readings, with Calvin, take the remnant according to the election of grace as God’s electing good pleasure in persons—kept for Himself, as the seven thousand were—not foresight of faith.

Romans 11:6

6But if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace.

Arminian

If by grace, no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace. Wesley: whether ceremonial or moral. The very nature of grace is lost if you mix it with a debt paid to works. He that believeth shall be saved is itself gracious: faith is not a work that earns the remnant-place.

Classical Arminians agree that remnant standing is not wages. The fork is what grace chooses: Wesley’s gracious purpose that believers be saved, not a selection of persons irrespective of faith. Verse 6 closes the door on Israel’s law-zeal (10:2–3). It does not, for this camp, close the door on the rest as if they could never believe.

Reformed

If through grace, it is no more by works. Calvin: if any place is given to works, so much is taken away from grace. He is not here only on justification’s means; he has ascended to why God, before the foundation of the world, chose only some and passed by others—His own good pleasure.

Reformed hinge, matching Eph 1:3–6: blend foreknowledge of works with election and grace cannot reign alone. Future works and past works are both excluded. The remnant of v. 5 is grace rather than works because election is gratuitous. Verse 7 will name who obtained and who did not.

Key contrast Arminian readings, with Wesley, take “if by grace, no longer of works” as the nature of salvation: He that believeth shall be saved is gift, not debt, without turning the remnant into an unconditional roster. Reformed readings, with Calvin, take the same sentence as locking election against all works, even foreseen: grace leaves nothing to works, or it is no longer grace.

Romans 11:7

7What then? Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. The elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened,

Arminian

Israel failed to obtain what it was seeking. Wesley: Israel in general hath not obtained justification; but those of them only who believe. The elect obtained it: the remnant who believe, according to that gracious purpose, He that believeth shall be saved. The rest were blinded—by their own wilful prejudice.

Do not flatten “hardened” into a decree that made faith impossible from the womb. Wesley’s rest are blinded by prejudice they would not drop: they sought as if by works (9:32; 10:3) and would not submit. The elect here are the believing remnant, not a hidden list that explains why the rest never could. Hardening is judicial on unbelief, pointing toward 11:11—they have not stumbled so as to fall beyond recovery.

Reformed

What then? Israel failed to obtain; the elect obtained it; the rest were hardened. Calvin: they are not those who strive by relying on merits, but those whose salvation depends on the gratuitous election of God. As the elect alone are delivered by God’s grace from destruction, so all who are not elected must necessarily remain blinded.

Reformed hinge: “the elect” and “the rest” are God’s distinction, the remnant of grace versus those hardened. Obtaining is not finally explained by who decided to believe; believing is how the elect obtain, and hardening is what becomes of the rest. Verse 6’s grace-not-works is the reason the split is not Israel’s seeking. Match Eph 1: faith is fruit, not cause.

Key contrast Arminian readings, with Wesley, take “the elect obtained it, but the rest were hardened” as those who believe versus the rest blinded by their own wilful prejudice—not a decree that made faith impossible. Reformed readings, with Calvin, take the elect as those whose salvation depends on gratuitous election, and the rest as necessarily remaining blinded: obtaining and hardening sit under God’s distinction, not Israel’s last word.

Romans 11:8

8as it is written, “God gave them a spirit of stupor, eyes that would not see and ears that would not hear, down to this very day.”

Arminian

As it is written: God gave them a spirit of stupor, eyes that would not see, ears that would not hear, down to this very day. Wesley: God hath at length withdrawn his Spirit, and so given them up to a spirit of slumber, which is fulfilled unto this day.

Classical Arminians hear judicial giving-up, not original reprobation. Stupor is what unbelief becomes when God withdraws after resisted light (10:18–21). Eyes that would not see are the fruit of wilful prejudice (v. 7), now handed over. “Down to this very day” names a present hardness, which 11:11 will refuse to treat as total, final fall. God gave them up; they had already refused to see.

Reformed

God gave them a spirit of stupor. Calvin reads the citations as reaching the fountain: brutal stupor seizes the senses after they are given up, and he will not let this be only a late punishment detachable from eternal reprobation’s fruit. The Prophet was bidden to harden; Paul names the secret judgment by which the reprobate are so demented they cannot form a judgment.

Reformed reading: “God gave” is not a metaphor for their self-blinding only. Eyes that would not see describe a people God has given over. “To this very day” means the blindness was not a one-day Old Testament episode now expired. The remnant obtained; this stupor is the rest. Chapter 11 will still promise mercy’s larger story—without making this giving-up unreal.

Romans 11:9

9And David says, “Let their table become a snare and a trap, a stumbling block and a retribution for them;

Arminian

David says: let their table become a snare and a trap, a stumbling block and a retribution. Wesley: in that prophetic imprecation applicable to them as well as to Judas. A recompence of their preceding wickedness. So sin is punished by sin; the gospel which should have fed them is become a means of destroying them.

Arminian hearing: retribution answers prior unbelief. The table—blessing, Scripture, covenant privilege—becomes a trap because they would not believe. This is not God baiting the unelect with a gospel never meant for them. It is judgment on a people who had a table and used it against the Christ it pointed to. Wilful prejudice (v. 7) is the preceding wickedness Wesley names.

Reformed

David’s imprecation: table as snare, trap, stumbling block, retribution. Calvin: whatever is desirable and happy in life might turn out to the ruin of the ungodly. The gospel is the savor of death unto death where it is not received in faith.

Reformed accent: the citation fits a people who had the table of covenant privilege and stumbled. Retribution is real; it also belongs to the “rest” of v. 7, the hardened as distinct from the elect who obtained. Privilege without electing grace becomes snare. Paul is not merely describing bad luck at a meal; he is reading Israel’s present stumble as Scripture already knew it.

Romans 11:10

10let their eyes be darkened so that they cannot see, and bend their backs forever.”

Arminian

Let their eyes be darkened so that they cannot see, and bend their backs forever. Wesley continues the prophetic imprecation: blindness and bowed backs as the gospel’s terrible reverse when it is refused. “Forever” in David’s psalm is the language of judgment, which this chapter will not let become Israel’s last chapter (vv. 11, 23–26).

Classical Arminians refuse to stop the book at darkened eyes. Verse 10 is real judicial darkening on wilful prejudice. Verse 11 will ask whether they stumbled so as to fall—and answer no. Darkened eyes can still meet mercy if they do not continue in unbelief (v. 23). Do not read “forever” here as Wesley’s “totally and finally” for the whole people; he has already said in no wise, and hereafter all Israel will be saved.

Reformed

Darkened eyes, bowed backs. Calvin: blindness of spirit and weakening of strength. The imprecation remains for the adversaries of Christ. Paul applies it so the blindness of the majority might not appear new or unusual.

Reformed reading: this is the rest who were hardened (v. 7), not a temporary mood. Yet the same chapter will deny that the nation has so fallen that no hope of repentance remains (v. 11) and will speak of grafting in again (v. 23). Individual hardening and national future are not the same sentence. Darkened eyes name the present majority; they do not cancel the remnant of grace or the God who hardens and has mercy.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take darkened eyes and bowed backs as judicial judgment on wilful unbelief, not the last word over Israel (Wesley: in no wise rejected; they have not fallen totally and finally). Reformed readings typically take the same darkening as the rest who were hardened under God’s distinction from the elect who obtained—real, scriptural, and still held inside the larger remnant-and-mercy story of the chapter.
