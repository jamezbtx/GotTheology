Passage

Romans 11

Verses 25–36 · WEB

Paul reveals a mystery: a partial hardening has come on Israel until the fullness of the Gentiles comes in, and so all Israel will be saved. As regards the gospel, Israel are enemies; as regards election, they are beloved for the fathers’ sake, because God’s gifts and calling are irrevocable. God has bound all to disobedience so that he may have mercy on all. The section ends with a doxology to the depth of God’s wisdom. Both traditions debate the meaning of “all Israel”; their main difference here concerns how mercy on “all” relates to election.

Romans 11:25

25For I don’t desire you to be ignorant, brothers, of this mystery, so that you won’t be wise in your own conceits, that a partial hardening has happened to Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in,

Arminian

A partial hardening has come on Israel until the fullness of the Gentiles comes in. Arminian readers see hardening as temporary and partial.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s plan unfolding according to his purpose, with the fullness of the Gentiles determined by God.

Romans 11:26

26and so all Israel will be saved. Even as it is written, “There will come out of Zion the Deliverer, and he will turn away ungodliness from Jacob.

Arminian

“And so all Israel will be saved.” Interpreters in both traditions differ: some see a future mass conversion of ethnic Israel, others the elect of Israel throughout history, others the whole church of Jews and Gentiles.

Reformed

Reformed readers have held each of these views. Calvin read “all Israel” as the whole people of God, Jews and Gentiles; many later Reformed interpreters expect a future conversion of Jewish people.

Romans 11:27

27This is my covenant with them, when I will take away their sins.”

Arminian

God’s covenant will take away their sins. Both traditions see the new covenant promise of forgiveness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s covenant faithfulness guaranteeing forgiveness.

Romans 11:28

28Concerning the Good News, they are enemies for your sake. But concerning the election, they are beloved for the fathers’ sake.

Arminian

As regards the gospel, they are enemies; as regards election, they are beloved for the fathers’ sake. Arminian readers see corporate election of Israel as a nation, which does not guarantee the salvation of each Israelite.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s electing love for Israel continuing, even while many are hardened.

Romans 11:29

29For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable.

Arminian

God’s gifts and calling are irrevocable. Arminian readers apply this to God’s faithfulness to Israel as a people. Many also caution against using it to prove that individuals cannot fall away, since its context is national.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a principle that applies to God’s saving purposes generally: what God gives in effectual calling he does not take back.

Key contrast Both see God’s faithfulness to Israel in verse 29. Reformed readers often extend the principle to the security of the effectually called. Arminian readers keep it tied to Israel’s national calling.

Romans 11:30

30For as you in time past were disobedient to God, but now have obtained mercy by their disobedience,

Arminian

Gentiles once disobedient have received mercy through Israel’s disobedience. Both traditions see the interplay of mercy among the nations.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s wise plan working through human disobedience.

Romans 11:31

31even so these also have now been disobedient, that by the mercy shown to you they may also obtain mercy.

Arminian

Israel has been disobedient so that they too may receive mercy. Arminian readers see God’s goal as mercy, not destruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see mercy planned for Israel in God’s purpose.

Romans 11:32

32For God has bound all to disobedience, that he might have mercy on all.

Arminian

God has bound all to disobedience so that he may have mercy on all. Arminian readers see the universal scope of God’s merciful intention, which supports a universal offer of grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers usually read “all” as Jews and Gentiles alike, not every individual. God has shut up both groups in disobedience so that mercy might come to both.

Key contrast Arminian readers see God’s merciful intention toward all people. Reformed readers read “all” as both Jews and Gentiles, with mercy shown to a people from both.

Romans 11:33

33Oh the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and the knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past tracing out!

Arminian

“Oh the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and the knowledge of God!” Both traditions end the discussion of Romans 9–11 in worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that God’s ways surpass human understanding, which calls for humility in discussing election.

Romans 11:34

34“For who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor?”

Arminian

No one has known the Lord’s mind or been his counselor. Both traditions affirm God’s incomprehensible wisdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s freedom in his counsel.

Romans 11:35

35“Or who has first given to him, and it will be repaid to him again?”

Arminian

No one has given to God first so as to be repaid. Arminian readers agree that grace is never owed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the verse ruling out any claim on God’s mercy.

Romans 11:36

36For of him and through him and to him are all things. To him be the glory for ever! Amen.

Arminian

From him, through him, and to him are all things. To him be the glory forever. Both traditions agree that salvation ends in God’s glory.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the comprehensive sovereignty of God that frames the whole discussion.
