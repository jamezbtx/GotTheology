Passage

Romans 7

Verses 1–6 · WEB

Paul uses the analogy of marriage to explain the believer’s release from the law. Just as death ends a marriage bond, believers have died to the law through the body of Christ so that they belong to another, the risen Christ, and bear fruit for God. They now serve in the new way of the Spirit, not the old way of the written code. Both traditions read this as the believer’s freedom from the law’s condemnation. They differ on the continuing role of the moral law.

Romans 7:1

1Or don’t you know, brothers (for I speak to men who know the law), that the law has dominion over a man for as long as he lives?

Arminian

The law has authority over a person only while that person lives. Both traditions see the principle that death ends legal obligation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law’s binding power as lasting as long as a person lives under it. That sets up Paul’s point that only death, here union with Christ in his death, can release someone from its condemnation.

Romans 7:2

2For the woman that has a husband is bound by law to the husband while he lives, but if the husband dies, she is discharged from the law of the husband.

Arminian

A married woman is bound to her husband while he lives. Both traditions see an illustration, not a full teaching on marriage.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the marriage as an analogy, not a full teaching on marriage. They note that Paul’s point concerns the change of covenant relationship, not divorce law.

Romans 7:3

3So then if, while the husband lives, she is joined to another man, she would be called an adulteress. But if the husband dies, she is free from the law, so that she is no adulteress, though she is joined to another man.

Arminian

If the husband dies, she is free to marry another. Arminian readers draw from this that freedom from the law is a real change of status. It opens a new relationship that the believer is called to live out faithfully.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the point of the analogy in the word “free.” The believer’s release from the law is as complete as a widow’s release from her marriage, so there is no lingering condemnation.

Romans 7:4

4Therefore, my brothers, you also were made dead to the law through the body of Christ, that you would be joined to another, to him who was raised from the dead, that we might produce fruit to God.

Arminian

You died to the law through the body of Christ, so that you may belong to another. Arminian readers see freedom from the law as a covenant of works.

Reformed

Reformed readers see release from the law’s curse and condemnation, while the moral law remains a guide for the believer’s life.

Key contrast Both agree that believers are released from the law’s condemnation. Reformed readers keep the moral law as a rule of life, often called its third use. Wesleyan Arminians also affirm the moral law as a guide to holy living.

Romans 7:5

5For when we were in the flesh, the sinful passions which were through the law worked in our members to bring out fruit to death.

Arminian

In the flesh, sinful passions aroused by the law bore fruit for death. Both traditions see the law stirring up sin in fallen people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law as unable to give life to people in the flesh. Rather than restraining sin, the commandment provoked it, which shows that the problem lies in the human heart, not in the law.

Romans 7:6

6But now we have been discharged from the law, having died to that in which we were held; so that we serve in newness of the spirit, and not in oldness of the letter.

Arminian

Now we serve in the new way of the Spirit. Arminian readers, especially Wesleyans, draw from this that the Spirit gives real power for obedience. Holiness is not just required but possible by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit writing the law on the heart, as Jeremiah 31 promised. Obedience now flows from a renewed nature, not from external pressure of the written code.
