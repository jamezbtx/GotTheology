Passage

Romans 7

Verses 7–25 · WEB

Paul defends the law: it is holy, righteous, and good, but sin used the commandment to deceive and kill. He then describes in the first person a struggle in which he wants to do good but does evil, delighting in God’s law inwardly while another law wars in his members. The passage ends with a cry for deliverance and thanks to God through Jesus Christ. Interpreters have long disputed who the “I” of verses 14–25 is. Wesley read it as an awakened person not yet freed by grace. Many Reformed readers, following Augustine’s later view and Calvin, read it as the regenerate Paul. The debate does not fall neatly along party lines.

Romans 7:7

7What shall we say then? Is the law sin? May it never be! However, I wouldn’t have known sin except through the law. For I wouldn’t have known coveting unless the law had said, “You shall not covet.”

Arminian

Is the law sin? No, but through the law I came to know sin. Arminian readers draw from this that the law has a preparing role. It awakens people to their need, which Wesley saw as the usual first step toward seeking Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law exposing covetousness in the heart. The tenth commandment reaches past outward acts to desire itself, which convinces even moral people that they are sinners.

Romans 7:8

8But sin, finding occasion through the commandment, produced in me all kinds of coveting. For apart from the law, sin is dead.

Arminian

Sin, seizing the commandment, produced every kind of coveting. Arminian readers see sin as an enslaving power that a person under the law cannot overcome alone, so deliverance must come from grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the power of indwelling sin, which turns even God’s good commandment into an occasion for rebellion. Sin is not just wrong acts but an active force in fallen human nature.

Romans 7:9

9I was alive apart from the law once, but when the commandment came, sin revived and I died.

Arminian

When the commandment came, sin revived and I died. Arminian readers see the law awakening a sense of guilt. Wesley described this as passing from a careless state to an awakened one.

Reformed

Reformed readers see conviction of sin. Many read “I was alive” as Paul’s former self-confidence, which collapsed when the commandment exposed his heart.

Romans 7:10

10The commandment which was for life, this I found to be for death;

Arminian

The commandment meant for life proved to be death. Arminian readers stress that the fault lies not in God’s command but in the sinner who cannot keep it without grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant of works broken. The law promised life to those who keep it, but since no fallen person can keep it, it brings death instead.

Romans 7:11

11for sin, finding occasion through the commandment, deceived me, and through it killed me.

Arminian

Sin deceived me through the commandment and killed me. Arminian readers see deception as one of sin’s main weapons. Grace opens people’s eyes so they can see sin for what it is.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress sin’s deceit: it promises life and freedom while bringing death. They see this as an echo of the serpent’s deception in Eden, which used God’s own command.

Romans 7:12

12Therefore the law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy, righteous, and good.

Arminian

The law is holy, and the commandment holy, righteous, and good. Arminian readers, especially Wesleyans, value the moral law as a guide to the holiness that grace makes possible.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law as a reflection of God’s holy character. Because it is good, it remains a guide for believers, even though it cannot justify them.

Romans 7:13

13Did then that which is good become death to me? May it never be! But sin, that it might be shown to be sin, was producing death in me through that which is good; that through the commandment sin might become exceedingly sinful.

Arminian

Sin, through what is good, brought death so that sin might be shown to be sinful. Both traditions see the law exposing sin’s character.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s purpose in letting sin work through the law. Sin is unmasked as utterly sinful, which drives sinners away from self-reliance and toward Christ.

Romans 7:14

14For we know that the law is spiritual, but I am fleshly, sold under sin.

Arminian

The law is spiritual, but I am fleshly, sold under sin. Wesley and many Arminian readers take this as describing a person under conviction of the law but not yet set free by the Spirit, since Romans 6 says believers are freed from sin’s slavery.

Reformed

Many Reformed readers take it as describing the believer’s ongoing struggle with remaining sin, since the speaker delights in God’s law (v22).

Calvin read the passage this way.

Key contrast Both see a real struggle with sin. Wesley and many Arminians read verses 14–25 as an awakened person not yet freed by the Spirit. Many Reformed readers, with Calvin, read them as the regenerate believer’s continuing conflict. Some scholars in each tradition take the other view.

Romans 7:15

15For I don’t understand what I am doing. For I don’t practice what I desire to do; but what I hate, that I do.

Arminian

I do not do what I want, but what I hate. Arminian readers who follow Wesley see a person who approves the good but lacks the power to do it. That is the condition of someone awakened by the law before the Spirit sets them free.

Reformed

Reformed readers who follow Calvin see the believer’s experience of indwelling sin. Only a renewed heart truly hates sin, so the hatred itself is evidence of grace.

Romans 7:16

16But if what I don’t desire, that I do, I consent to the law that it is good.

Arminian

If I do what I do not want, I agree that the law is good. Arminian readers draw from this that conscience, aided by prevenient grace, can recognize the law’s goodness even before conversion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the renewed mind’s approval of the law. Agreement with the law despite failure shows that the will has been changed at its root.

Romans 7:17

17So now it is no more I that do it, but sin which dwells in me.

Arminian

It is no longer I who do it, but sin dwelling in me. Arminian readers note that this is not an excuse. Paul describes how sin enslaves a person, which is exactly what Romans 8 says the Spirit breaks.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the distinction between the believer’s true self and remaining sin. The believer’s deepest identity is now aligned with God, even while sin still dwells within.

Romans 7:18

18For I know that in me, that is, in my flesh, dwells no good thing. For desire is present with me, but I don’t find it doing that which is good.

Arminian

Nothing good dwells in my flesh. Arminian readers affirm the corruption of fallen human nature. They hold that any good a person does flows from grace, not from the flesh.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the persistence of the sinful nature even in believers. “Flesh” refers to the fallen nature that remains until glorification.

Romans 7:19

19For the good which I desire, I don’t do; but the evil which I don’t desire, that I practice.

Arminian

I do not do the good I want but the evil I do not want. Arminian readers see the failure of human resolve without the Spirit. Wesley stressed that this is not the normal Christian life described in Romans 6 and 8.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an ongoing struggle in sanctification. Mature believers often feel this conflict more sharply, because they see sin more clearly.

Romans 7:20

20But if what I don’t desire, that I do, it is no more I that do it, but sin which dwells in me.

Arminian

If I do what I do not want, it is sin dwelling in me. Arminian readers see Paul restating the bondage of a person under the law. The repetition makes the cry of verse 24 all the more urgent.

Reformed

Reformed readers read Paul’s repetition as emphasis on how deeply sin is rooted. They see it as a warning against any claim to have outgrown the struggle in this life.

Romans 7:21

21I find then the law that, while I desire to do good, evil is present.

Arminian

When I want to do good, evil lies close at hand. Arminian readers see a “law” in the sense of a fixed pattern that human effort cannot break. Only the “law of the Spirit of life” in 8:2 overcomes it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer’s warfare. Every desire for good meets resistance from remaining sin, so the Christian life is a fight to the end.

Romans 7:22

22For I delight in God’s law after the inward person,

Arminian

I delight in God’s law in my inner being. Arminian readers note that an awakened conscience can approve and even delight in the law before it has the power to obey. Prevenient grace makes this possible.

Reformed

Reformed readers see delight in God’s law as possible only for the regenerate. The unregenerate mind is hostile to God (8:7), so Calvin took this delight as proof that the speaker is a believer.

Romans 7:23

23but I see a different law in my members, warring against the law of my mind, and bringing me into captivity under the law of sin which is in my members.

Arminian

Another law in my members wars against the law of my mind. Arminian readers see someone held captive, which matches being “sold under sin” in verse 14 rather than the freedom of 6:18.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the flesh warring against the Spirit, as in Galatians 5:17. Captivity here describes the believer’s frequent defeats, not total slavery.

Romans 7:24

24What a wretched man I am! Who will deliver me out of the body of this death?

Arminian

Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death? Arminian readers see the cry of someone seeking deliverance that Romans 8 provides.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer longing for final deliverance from sin at the resurrection. “This body of death” points to the redemption of the body that Paul describes in 8:23.

Romans 7:25

25I thank God through Jesus Christ, our Lord! So then with the mind, I myself serve God’s law, but with the flesh, sin’s law.

Arminian

Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord! Arminian readers see the thanksgiving as the turn from bondage toward the freedom described in Romans 8. Christ is the answer to the struggle.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer serving God with the mind while the flesh remains. The thanksgiving comes before the final summary, so the struggle continues even for those who already know deliverance in Christ.
