Passage

Romans 16

Verses 1–16 · WEB · Voices

Paul commends Phoebe, servant of the assembly that is at Cenchreae and bearer of the letter, then greets coworkers and saints at Rome: Prisca and Aquila, Andronicus and Junia, household gatherings, and many named by love and labor. The list ends with a holy kiss and greeting from all the churches of Christ.

Romans 16:1

1I commend to you Phoebe, our sister, who is a servant of the assembly that is at Cenchreae,

Arminian

Phoebe is sister, servant of the Cenchrean church, and—Wesley notes—the bearer of this letter. The Greek may read deaconess: in the apostolic age grave women visited the sick and ministered to temporal and spiritual need, not as public teachers.

Arminians honor women’s labor in the gospel. Grace forms servants the church must receive; commendation is not flattery but a charge to treat her ministry as holy work.

Reformed

Calvin: Paul commends Phoebe as the letter’s bearer, first for her office—a most honorable and most holy function—and then because she had been a helper to all the godly.

God staffs His church with named servants. Phoebe’s commendation is apostolic authorization; the Romans honor Christ by receiving the woman He sent with this epistle.

Romans 16:2

2that you receive her in the Lord in a way worthy of the saints, and that you assist her in whatever matter she may need from you, for she herself also has been a helper of many, and of my own self.

Arminian

Wesley: “in the Lord” means for the Lord’s sake, and in a Christian manner. Welcome worthy of the saints is practical: help her, because she herself also has been a helper of many—including Paul.

Hospitality is grace-enabled duty, not optional polish. Patronage is holy generosity the church should match; saints repay kindness without embarrassment.

Reformed

Calvin: as it is meet for saints, it would be unbecoming Christ’s servants not to show her honor and kindness; those who perform a public office should be especially loved; courtesy requires that one naturally given to kindness not be forsaken when in need.

God often funds the gospel through patrons He has made. Receiving Phoebe is receiving both the letter and the Lord who sent her.

Romans 16:3

3Greet Prisca and Aquila, my fellow workers in Christ Jesus,

Arminian

Husband and wife are named fellow workers in Christ Jesus, Prisca first. Their marriage is mission; the church greets laborers, not spectators.

Grace makes coworkers. Shared work in Christ is human partnership under assisting grace, and the greeting treats that labor as the church’s joy.

Reformed

Calvin: a singular honor, especially with regard to a woman. Paul’s modesty shines: he disdained not to have a woman as his associate in the Lord’s work, nor was he ashamed to confess it. She was Aquila’s wife; Luke calls her Priscilla.

God raises households as fellow workers. Their worth is located in Christ Jesus, not in Roman rank.

Romans 16:4

4who risked their own necks for my life, to whom not only I give thanks, but also all the assemblies of the Gentiles.

Arminian

Wesley: they exposed themselves to the utmost danger for Paul’s life; even Rome gives thanks for preserving so valuable a life. Love spends life.

Enabling grace makes costly courage possible. The churches’ thanks is a moral debt, not a slogan—Gentile congregations live because these two risked their necks.

Reformed

Calvin: they had not spared their life to preserve Paul’s; he is individually thankful, and all the churches of Christ give thanks. Deservedly dear was such a man’s life to the Gentiles, an incomparable treasure.

God kept the apostle through their risk. Thanksgiving lands on the preservers because the Lord used their necks to keep the missionary alive.

Romans 16:5

5Greet the assembly that is in their house. Greet Epaenetus, my beloved, who is the first fruits of Achaia to Christ.

Arminian

Wesley: Aquila, once driven from Rome under Claudius, now hosts the church as Gaius did at Corinth; where a Christian had a large house, they assembled. Epaenetus is the firstfruit of proconsular Asia. House church and first convert are grace at street level.

First faith is to be honored and continued. A living room can be a true church; a first convert is beloved, not a statistic.

Reformed

Calvin: Paul could not have more splendidly adorned their household than by giving it the title of Church—the sacred name, not a mere congregation. Epaenetus as first-fruit: those first called have honor if the end corresponds with the beginning.

God plants churches in homes and claims firstfruits. The title Church is His gift; first faith is a prerogative to be finished, not a relic.

Romans 16:6

6Greet Mary, who labored much for us.

Arminian

Mary is greeted for hard work toward the Romans. Labor is named; a woman’s toil in the gospel is not anonymous to Paul.

Grace-enabled work is true and should be received as such. The church that lives by the letter must also honor those who have already spent themselves for its good.

Reformed

Calvin: he commemorates these praises in order to recommend those whom he praised to the Romans.

God records labor. Hard work for the church is a mark He is pleased to honor through the apostle’s greeting; commendation is pastoral providence, not padding.

Romans 16:7

7Greet Andronicus and Junia, my relatives and my fellow prisoners, who are notable among the apostles, who were also in Christ before me.

Arminian

Kinsmen, fellow prisoners, well known to the apostles (or of note among them), in Christ before Paul. Wesley: they seem some of the most early converts; fellow prisoners for the gospel’s sake.

Early faith and shared chains are honors the gospel actually produces. Junia is greeted as a coworker in the first generation; those in Christ before Paul are not lesser because they are not Paul.

Reformed

Calvin: relationship is a lesser eulogy; fellow-prisoners carry more weight—bonds are not the least honor in Christ’s warfare. He calls them apostles in a wider sense: those who spend labor promulgating the gospel everywhere, not only the Twelve. Because they embraced the gospel before him, he sets them before himself.

God’s timeline of faith is His to write. Prior conversion is honor, not rivalry; gospel work and chains measure the labor.

Romans 16:8

8Greet Amplias, my beloved in the Lord.

Arminian

Ampliatus is simply “beloved in the Lord.” Affection in Christ is enough to name a person in a canonical letter.

Grace creates friendship. Being beloved in the Lord is living union, not a title purchased by rank; the church should greet obscure saints with the same gospel love.

Reformed

A single line of affection locates Ampliatus in the Lord. Love here is covenant kinship, not flattery.

God writes unknown names into the church’s memory. Belovedness in the Lord is the apostle’s true commendation—identity given in Christ.

Romans 16:9

9Greet Urbanus, our fellow worker in Christ, and Stachys, my beloved.

Arminian

Wesley: Urbanus is our fellow laborer—Paul’s and Timothy’s (v. 21). Stachys is beloved. Work and affection stand side by side.

Shared labor in Christ is a calling people actually take up. The church thrives when coworkers are named and beloved, not used and forgotten.

Reformed

Fellow worker in Christ and beloved: two honors, one Lord. Urbanus’s work is located in Christ; Stachys’s dearness is Paul’s.

God supplies both laborers and loves. Greetings are how the apostle binds distant saints into one work under Christ.

Romans 16:10

10Greet Apelles, the approved in Christ. Greet those who are of the household of Aristobulus.

Arminian

Apelles is approved in Christ—tested, not merely named. The household of Aristobulus is greeted as a group; Wesley later notes that only part of such families were converted.

Approval is lived faithfulness under grace. A household greeting does not baptize every relative; those in Christ are greeted as those in Christ.

Reformed

Approved in Christ is a weighty word: tried and found genuine. The family of Aristobulus is greeted without inflating the name into a Christian dynasty.

God approves in Christ, not in pedigree. Households appear in the list because some there belong to the Lord, not because Rome’s great houses confer grace.

Romans 16:11

11Greet Herodion, my kinsman. Greet them of the household of Narcissus, who are in the Lord.

Arminian

Wesley: of Aristobulus’s and Narcissus’s families, only part were converted; some Paul knew only by character. Faith does not create moroseness, but courtesy, which even an apostle’s gravity did not hinder.

“them of the household of Narcissus, who are in the Lord” is the line. Kinship and household are not saving; living faith is. Courtesy still greets the saints inside mixed houses.

Reformed

Calvin: Narcissus was likely Claudius’s notorious freedman; the more wonderful that God’s goodness penetrated that impure house. Not that Narcissus himself was converted; the faithful were a few. Servants need not wait for masters; each should follow Christ for himself.

Grace visits unlikely houses and divides families. Election is not a class trait; “in the Lord” marks those God has actually claimed.

Romans 16:12

12Greet Tryphaena and Tryphosa, who labor in the Lord. Greet Persis, the beloved, who labored much in the Lord.

Arminian

Wesley: Tryphaena and Tryphosa were probably sisters. Three women are honored as workers; Persis has worked hard in the Lord. Labor, again, is the greeting’s measure.

Grace enables toil that the apostle will not hide. Women’s work in the Lord is church work; the letter trains the Romans to say so out loud.

Reformed

Workers in the Lord, beloved, hard-worked: the titles stay in Christ. God records women’s labor beside men’s without a lesser gospel.

The Lord owns both the workers and the work. Persis’s hard labor is providence made visible; greeting her is obedience, not gallantry.

Romans 16:13

13Greet Rufus, the chosen in the Lord, and his mother and mine.

Arminian

Wesley: perhaps the Rufus of Mark 15:21; “his mother and mine” may denote the tender care she had taken of Paul. “Chosen in the Lord” names a man standing in living union with Christ—an honorable, tried Christian, not a spectator.

Arminian readers read the phrase as the standing of one who is in Christ by faith. Election-language here is in the Lord: Rufus is choice because he belongs to Jesus, and his mother’s care is the gospel’s ordinary kindness.

Reformed

“Chosen in the Lord” is not casual praise. Reformed readers hear election’s fruit in a named saint—God’s choice made public in Rufus—and providence in a mother who mothered Paul as well as her son.

God elects people, then clothes that choice in households and hospitality. The mother is not a footnote to the decree; she is how the chosen apostle was tended.

Key contrast Both honor Rufus as a true Christian and his mother as a mother in the faith. Arminians typically read “chosen in the Lord” as the honorable standing of one in Christ by living faith; Reformed readers commonly read it as election made visible—God’s choice displayed in a saint He has claimed.

Romans 16:14

14Greet Asyncritus, Phlegon, Hermes, Patrobas, Hermas, and the brothers who are with them.

Arminian

Wesley: he seems to join those joined by kindred, nearness of habitation, or other circumstance. It could not but encourage the poor to be saluted by name, who perhaps did not know the apostle had ever heard of them.

Grace notices the unnamed cluster around named saints. Being greeted is not a prize for the eminent; the gospel makes room for ordinary brothers.

Reformed

A string of names plus “the brothers with them” sketches house-sized fellowships God already knows. Obscurity in Rome is not obscurity in Christ.

Providence writes small names into Scripture. The church is larger than its famous workers; the Lord binds unknown households by an apostle’s greeting.

Romans 16:15

15Greet Philologus and Julia, Nereus and his sister, and Olympas, and all the saints who are with them.

Arminian

Another household cluster, ending with “all the saints who are with them.” Wesley: had Peter been then at Rome, Paul would doubtless have saluted him by name, since none in this catalogue matched his eminence.

“All the saints” keeps the greeting from becoming a celebrity list. The gospel’s courtesy runs to every holy one in the circle, named or not.

Reformed

Calvin: it would have been unbecoming to pass by Peter in so long a catalogue if he were then at Rome; no impartial reader is persuaded he was. The names here are the obscure and ignoble at Rome—no magnificent titles.

God builds His church from people the city does not count. Apostolic greeting confers the only honor that matters: they are saints in Christ.

Romans 16:16

16Greet one another with a holy kiss. The assemblies of Christ greet you.

Arminian

Wesley: termed by Peter the kiss of love (1 Pet 5:14). So the ancient Christians concluded their solemn offices—the men saluting the men, and the women the women. The custom continued for some ages in all Christian churches.

Holiness qualifies the greeting. Love is to be shown, not merely felt; the assemblies of Christ greet Rome as one family under a sincere gospel.

Reformed

Calvin: a kiss was a Jewish symbol of friendship; Paul seems not to enjoin a ceremony but to exhort brotherly love, distinguished from the world’s disguised friendships. By sending salutations from the churches he binds all the members of Christ by the mutual bond of love.

The holy kiss is love regulated, not a rite to copy blindly. God knits churches together; Rome is not an island, and greeting is covenant affection.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

What earlier interpreters on each side wrote about Romans 16:1–16.

Arminian Notable voices

“I commend unto you Phebe—The bearer of this letter. A servant—The Greek word is a deaconness.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Rom 16:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“He first commends to them Phoebe, to whom he gave this Epistle to be brought to them; and, in the first place, he commends her on account of her office, for she performed a most honorable and a most holy function in the Church; and then he adduces another reason why they ought to receive her and to show her every kindness, for she had always been a helper to all the godly. It is a singular honor which he ascribes here to Prisca and Aquila, especially with regard to a woman; for he disdained not to have a woman as his associate in the work of the Lord, nor was he ashamed to confess this.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Romans (on Rom 16:1–4)
