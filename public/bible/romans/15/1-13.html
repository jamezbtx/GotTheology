Passage

Romans 15

Verses 1–13 · ESV · Voices

Paul turns from the weak and the strong to Christ's own pattern: those who are strong have an obligation to bear with the failings of the weak, not to please themselves. Christ did not please himself; the Scriptures were written so that through endurance and encouragement we might have hope. He prays for harmony, then summons Jew and Gentile to welcome one another as Christ has welcomed them, that the God of hope may fill them with joy and peace in believing, and they may abound in hope by the power of the Holy Spirit.

Romans 15:1

1We who are strong have an obligation to bear with the failings of the weak, and not to please ourselves.

Arminian

The strong—Wesley says those of a clearer judgment, free from the scruples of Romans 14—are not excused from the table. They have an obligation: bear with the failings of the weak, and do not please yourselves. Strength is for carrying, not for winning the argument.

Arminian pastoral reading: Christlike liberty is liberty to stoop. The able remain responsible; this duty can be taken up or refused. A church is healthy when the strong actually take the weight, not when they lecture the weak from a height.

Reformed

Calvin: this strength was bestowed so that they might sustain the weak and prevent them from falling. Gifts are for the body of Christ. The stronger anyone is in Christ, the more bound he is to bear with the weak—not merely to tolerate them, but to help carry what they cannot carry.

Reformed instinct: obligation here is a trust, not a suggestion. God has committed the strong to the weak as surely as He committed knowledge to those who must teach. Strength is not a private comfort.

Key contrast Both bind the strong to carry the weak rather than please themselves. Arminians typically hear a real obligation the strong can obey or refuse. Reformed readers typically hear strength itself as a gift bestowed for this duty—the stronger in Christ, the more bound.

Romans 15:2

2Let each of us please his neighbor for his good, to build him up.

Arminian

Please the neighbor—not to flatter, but for his good, to build him up. Wesley: edification is one species of good. The aim is the other's growth in Christ, not a smoother evening.

Classical Arminians hear a wide, practical love: each of us is addressed. Building up is work we can do, and work we can neglect. The neighbor's good is a real charge, not a mood.

Reformed

Calvin: we are under obligations to others, and ought to accommodate ourselves to our brethren according to God's word, to their edification. Two things: do not rest in your own judgment, and do not please people toward their ruin.

Reformed caution: most people cannot be pleased except you indulge their humor. The command is not popularity. Please the neighbor for his good—his building-up in the gospel—not for his applause.

Romans 15:3

3For Christ did not please himself, but as it is written, “The reproaches of those who reproached you fell on me.”

Arminian

The pattern is not a proverb; it is Christ. He did not please himself. Wesley: He bore not only the infirmities but the reproaches of His brethren, and so fulfilled Psalm 69. The strong are not asked to do what their Lord refused.

Arminian pastoral hope: if Christ stooped, no one in the church is too free to stoop. Reproach that falls on a weak brother is not beneath the strong. The verse invites imitation, not a shrug that only Jesus could live this way.

Reformed

Calvin: it would be very strange in us to wish exemption from a duty to which Christ, our Lord and King, submitted Himself. Having no regard for Himself, He gave Himself wholly to this service. Zeal for God's glory absorbed Him.

Reformed reading: the servant does not refuse what the Lord has undertaken. Christ's self-emptying is the church's law because He reigns in His people. If He reigns in us, what derogates from God's glory will grieve us as if it struck our own name.

Romans 15:4

4For whatever was written in former days was written for our instruction, that through endurance and through the encouragement of the Scriptures we might have hope.

Arminian

The Old Testament was not a closed file. Wesley: aforetime, in the Old Testament—and through the consolation God gives us by these writings we may have patience and a joyful hope. Scripture is for the living church, not only for the first readers.

Classical Arminians take this as a sincere means of grace: God instructs, encourages, and holds out hope in the Word. Those who attend to the Scriptures are summoned to endure and to hope; the page is not a locked decree but a living comfort.

Reformed

Calvin: there is nothing vain in the oracles of God. The Scriptures raise those prepared by patience and strengthened by consolation to the hope of eternal life. God alone is the author of patience and consolation; He uses His Word as the instrument.

Reformed comfort: hope is not a mood we manufacture from old stories. The Spirit plants what the Word teaches. Former days were written for our salvation, and fanatics who discard the Old Testament rob the church of what God appointed for her.

Romans 15:5

5May the God of endurance and encouragement grant you to live in such harmony with one another, in accord with Christ Jesus,

Arminian

Paul does not merely command harmony; he asks the God of endurance and encouragement to grant it—in accord with Christ Jesus, not in accord with the stronger party's taste. Wesley hears this according to the power of Christ Jesus.

Arminian reading: God grants what the church must still live. Prayer and responsibility sit together. Harmony is a gift to be received, not a trophy of the party that won Romans 14.

Reformed

Calvin: after exhorting them, Paul turns to pray, for he fully understood that to speak of duty was to no purpose except God inwardly effected by His Spirit what he spoke by the mouth of man. God is the author of patience and consolation; the Word is His instrument.

Reformed instinct: the God who is named from what He produces is asked to produce like-mindedness. Unity according to Christ is not a human truce. God must bring minds to real unanimity, or the exhortation dies on the page.

Key contrast Both pray that God grant harmony in accord with Christ, not that the strong simply win. Arminians typically hear a real gift that the church must still receive and live. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin's point: duty-talk is pointless unless God inwardly effects by His Spirit what the apostle speaks.

Romans 15:6

6that together you may with one voice glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

The end of harmony is not a quieter church calendar. It is one voice—Wesley: Jews and Gentiles believing with one mind and confessing with one mouth—glorifying the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian pastoral reading: shared praise is the test. If the strong and the weak cannot sing the Father's glory together, the previous verses have not yet landed. Doxology is the point of the patience.

Reformed

Calvin: God is not truly glorified by us unless the hearts of all agree in giving Him praise, and their tongues join in harmony. He so esteems the unity of His servants that He will not have His glory sounded forth amid discords.

Reformed warning: no one may boast that he will glorify God after his own manner. This one thought ought to check the rage for contention. The Father of our Lord Jesus Christ is praised when the church is one mouth, not a contest of mouths.

Romans 15:7

7Therefore welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God.

Arminian

Therefore—because Christ is the pattern and the Scriptures give hope—welcome one another. Wesley: weak and strong, with mutual love. The measure is not, Welcome those who have caught up to you, but, As Christ has welcomed you.

Arminian pastoral hope: Christ's welcome is sincere and wide enough for this mixed room. If He received you when you were not yet strong, you may not freeze out a brother whose conscience still trembles. Glory to God is at stake in the greeting.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ, having received not one or two of us but all together, has connected us so that we ought to cherish one another if we would continue in His bosom. Only thus do we confirm our calling: if we separate not ourselves from those the Lord has bound together.

Reformed reading: welcome is covenant practice. Christ has actually received these people for the glory of God. To refuse a brother He has taken is to tear what He joined. The glory of the Father is made known in that reception.

Key contrast Both command a practical welcome because of Christ, for God's glory. Arminians typically hear Christ's welcome as the sincere pattern and offer that gathered this mixed church, so the strong and weak must receive one another without a hidden inner circle. Reformed readers typically hear that Christ has actually received these people into His bosom—those He bound together—so refusing a brother is tearing what Christ joined.

Romans 15:8

8For I tell you that Christ became a servant to the circumcised to show God's truthfulness, in order to confirm the promises given to the patriarchs,

Arminian

Paul now shows how Christ received us. Wesley: He was a servant of His Father, of the circumcision, for the salvation of the Jews, to manifest the truth and fidelity of God. The promises to the patriarchs were not a feint; Christ confirmed them.

Classical Arminians gladly confess that God's truthfulness stands: what He promised to the fathers He performed in the Son. Gentile believers do not erase Israel; they meet a Christ who first became a servant to the circumcised.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ embraced us all, yet in the first place He was promised to the Jewish nation and in a manner peculiarly destined for them. He became servant of the circumcision to accomplish the truth of God by performing the promises given to the fathers. The old covenant was in reality spiritual.

Reformed confession: this is no common honor—the Lord of heaven put on flesh to procure salvation for that people. Salvation in Christ belonged by covenant to the Jews; His coming fulfilled what the Father had promised Abraham. Truthfulness here is promise kept.

Romans 15:9

9and in order that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy. As it is written, “Therefore I will praise you among the Gentiles, and sing to your name.”

Arminian

Christ's servanthood has a second aim: that the Gentiles might glorify God for His mercy. Wesley hears Psalm 18 as Jews and Gentiles joining in the worship of the God of Israel. Mercy is sung, not merely noted.

Arminian reading: the nations are sincerely summoned into praise. Mercy to the Gentiles is not an afterthought God might withhold from those who would come; it is written into the psalm so that outsiders may actually sing His name.

Reformed

Calvin: this is the second point, and Paul dwells on it longer because it was not so evident. Mercy named the Gentiles' share, as truth named the Jews'—promise kept to the fathers, mercy extended to those who had no such distinct pledge. God's praises are declared in the assembly of those who can hear them.

Reformed instinct: the calling of the Gentiles is a fulfilled prophecy, not a human expansion of the franchise. They glorify God for mercy because God has brought them into communion with His people so that His name is known among them.

Romans 15:10

10And again it says, “Rejoice, O Gentiles, with his people.”

Arminian

Again: Rejoice, O Gentiles, with His people. Wesley points to Deuteronomy 32. The nations are not a tolerated gallery; they are called to rejoice with Israel, not instead of Israel.

Arminian pastoral reading: the gospel's table is wide. Gentile joy is commanded joy, a real invitation into the people's song. No one in Rome may treat the other half of the church as a guest who should stay quiet.

Reformed

The Scripture says it again so no one can call Gentile inclusion a Pauline novelty. Gentiles rejoice with His people—joined, not annexed as a rival people of God.

Reformed comfort: God had already written the mixed choir. What Paul requires in the Roman church is what Moses and the psalms already sang. Unity here is obedience to Scripture, not a modern experiment.

Romans 15:11

11And again, “Praise the Lord, all you Gentiles, and let all the peoples extol him.”

Arminian

Psalm 117: all you Gentiles, all the peoples. Wesley simply marks the text. The smallest psalm opens the largest door: praise is asked of the nations, not only of Zion.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere call. Peoples are summoned to extol the Lord; the summons is not theater. Those who hear may praise, and the church that believes this will not hoard the song.

Reformed

Calvin: how can they who know not God's greatness praise Him? They could no more do this than call on His name when unknown. It is a prophecy most suitable to prove the calling of the Gentiles.

Reformed reading: the psalm is not a wish that never lands. God calls the nations because He will have them praise Him. Extolling Him is the fruit of being brought to know His name, not a compliment paid from remaining darkness.

Romans 15:12

12And again Isaiah says, “The root of Jesse will come, even he who arises to rule the Gentiles; in him will the Gentiles hope.”

Arminian

Isaiah 11: the root of Jesse—Wesley notes that kings and the Messiah were promised to Jesse even before David. In Him shall the Gentiles hope, who before had been without hope (Ephesians 2:12).

Arminian hope: the nations are not left as spectators of Israel's Messiah. They may actually hope in Him. The root of Jesse arises to rule and to be trusted; former hopelessness is not the last word for those who now look to Christ.

Reformed

Calvin: this prophecy is the most illustrious of them all. From the dry trunk of David's family a shoot would arise for the Gentiles' salvation. Hope in Christ is an evidence of His divinity. Twice the calling of the Gentiles is confirmed: He is raised as a sign, and they shall hope in Him—which cannot take place without the preaching of the word and illumination of the Spirit.

Reformed confession: Gentile hope is Christ's gift, not a native religious talent. He arises to rule; they hope because the Word is preached and the Spirit gives light.

Romans 15:13

13May the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, so that by the power of the Holy Spirit you may abound in hope.

Arminian

The section closes as it must: a prayer. Wesley: the God of hope—a glorious title, till now unknown to the heathens, whose goddess Hope was nothing. He fills with all joy and peace in believing, that we may abound in hope through the Spirit's power.

Arminian pastoral reading: filling is God's, and believing is the appointed way. Hope overflows where people actually trust Him. The Spirit's power is real help, not a substitute for faith; the church is invited to abound, not to wait and see whether hope was assigned.

Reformed

Calvin: Paul desires the Lord to give what He had commanded. The Lord does not measure His precepts according to our strength or the power of free-will; He commands what requires the aid of His grace, that He may stimulate us to prayer. Through the power of the Holy Spirit all these are gifts of divine bounty—that wonderful energy by which the Spirit works in us faith, hope, joy, and peace.

Reformed comfort: abounding hope is not the extra mile of hardy souls. The God in whom we hope fills; the Spirit's power does the work. Prayer is the right response because the command outruns us.

Key contrast Both ascribe overflowing hope to the God of hope and the Spirit's power, not to native cheer. Arminians typically hear “in believing” as the appointed way God fills those who trust Him—real filling joined to real faith. Reformed readers typically hear the Spirit's power as the energy that actually works faith, hope, joy, and peace; God does not command hope as if free-will were equal to it.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the strong bearing with the weak, Christ who did not please himself, and the God of hope who fills Jew and Gentile together—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Romans 15:1–13.

Arminian Notable voices

“We who are strong—Of a clearer judgment, and free from these scruples. And not to please ourselves—Without any regard to others. Christ bore not only the infirmities, but reproaches, of his brethren. That through the consolation which God gives us by the Scriptures, we may have patience and a joyful hope. Receive ye one another—Weak and strong, with mutual love. Christ was a servant of his Father, of the circumcision, for the salvation of the Jews, to manifest the truth and fidelity of God. In him shall the Gentiles hope—who before had been without hope. Now the God of hope—A glorious title of God, but till now unknown to the heathens.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Rom 15:1–13)

Reformed Notable voices

“The stronger then any one is in Christ, the more bound he is to bear with the weak. Since it is not right that a servant should refuse what his lord has himself undertaken, it would be very strange in us to wish an exemption from the duty of bearing the infirmities of others, to which Christ submitted himself; for he having no regard for himself, gave up himself wholly to this service. He, having received not one or two of us, but all together, has thus connected us, that we ought to cherish one another if we would continue in his bosom. It hence appears that the Lord does in no degree measure his precepts according to our strength or the power of free-will; he commands those things which require the aid of his grace, that he may stimulate us in our attention to prayer.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Romans (on Rom 15:1–13)
