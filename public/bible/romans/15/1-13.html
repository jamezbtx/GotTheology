Passage

Romans 15

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Paul finishes his appeal to the strong and the weak by pointing to Christ, who did not please himself. Scripture was written to give endurance and hope, and Paul prays that the church will glorify God with one voice. Believers are to welcome one another as Christ welcomed them, because Christ became a servant to Israel to confirm God’s promises and so that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy. A chain of Old Testament quotations celebrates the nations praising God, and Paul closes with a prayer to the God of hope. Both traditions read the passage as a vision of one people of God drawn from every nation, with different emphases on how that people is gathered.

Romans 15:1

1Now we who are strong ought to bear the weaknesses of the weak, and not to please ourselves.

Arminian

The strong ought to bear with the failings of the weak and not please themselves. Arminian readers see love that helps weaker believers keep going in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see strength given by grace for the service of others. Christian maturity shows itself in patient care for the weak.

Romans 15:2

2Let each one of us please his neighbor for that which is good, to be building him up.

Arminian

Each should please his neighbor for his good, to build him up. Arminian readers stress the community’s role in strengthening believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see edification as the purpose of Christian liberty and the goal of all church life.

Romans 15:3

3For even Christ didn’t please himself. But, as it is written, “The reproaches of those who reproached you fell on me.”

Arminian

Christ did not please himself; he bore reproach. Arminian readers see Christ as the model of self-giving love that believers are called and enabled to follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ first as the substitute who bore reproach for his people, and then as their example. Imitation rests on redemption.

Romans 15:4

4For whatever things were written before were written for our learning, that through perseverance and through encouragement of the Scriptures we might have hope.

Arminian

What was written in former days was written for our instruction, that we might have hope. Arminian readers see Scripture as a means of grace that sustains faith and hope.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the unity of Scripture. The Old Testament speaks directly to Christians, and God uses it to produce endurance.

Romans 15:5

5Now the God of perseverance and of encouragement grant you to be of the same mind with one another according to Christ Jesus,

Arminian

May the God of endurance and encouragement grant you to live in harmony. Arminian readers see God giving grace in answer to prayer, which believers receive and use.

Reformed

Reformed readers see unity as God’s gift, prayed for because only he can give it.

Romans 15:6

6that with one accord you may with one mouth glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Together with one voice glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. Arminian readers see united worship as the goal of mutual acceptance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s glory as the final aim of the church’s unity, as of all things.

Romans 15:7

7Therefore accept one another, even as Christ also accepted you, to the glory of God.

Arminian

Welcome one another as Christ welcomed you, for the glory of God. Arminian readers draw from this that Christ’s welcome extends to all who come to him in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s welcome of his people as the ground and pattern of their welcome of one another.

Romans 15:8

8Now I say that Christ has been made a servant of the circumcision for the truth of God, that he might confirm the promises given to the fathers,

Arminian

Christ became a servant to the circumcised to confirm the promises given to the patriarchs. Arminian readers see God’s faithfulness to Israel as part of his plan to bless the world.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ fulfilling the covenant promises to Abraham. The continuity of God’s covenant is central to Reformed theology.

Romans 15:9

9and that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy. As it is written, “Therefore I will give praise to you among the Gentiles and sing to your name.”

Arminian

The Gentiles glorify God for his mercy. Arminian readers draw from this that God’s mercy is meant for all nations without restriction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s mercy reaching his elect among the Gentiles, as promised from the beginning.

Key contrast Both celebrate the Gentiles glorifying God for his mercy. Arminian readers see mercy offered to all people. Reformed readers see mercy reaching the elect from every nation.

Romans 15:10

10Again he says, “Rejoice, you Gentiles, with his people.”

Arminian

“Rejoice, you Gentiles, with his people.” Arminian readers see Jews and Gentiles joined in one worshiping community through faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the one people of God, with Gentiles grafted into Israel’s blessings (11:17).

Romans 15:11

11Again, “Praise the Lord, all you Gentiles! Let all the peoples praise him.”

Arminian

“Praise the Lord, all you Gentiles.” Arminian readers see the universal scope of the gospel reflected in the Psalms.

Reformed

Reformed readers see worship from all peoples as the goal of God’s redemptive plan.

Romans 15:12

12Again, Isaiah says, “There will be the root of Jesse, he who arises to rule over the Gentiles; in him the Gentiles will hope.”

Arminian

The root of Jesse will rise to rule the Gentiles; in him they will hope. Arminian readers see Christ as the hope offered to every nation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Messiah’s kingly rule gathering the nations under him, fulfilling Isaiah 11.

Romans 15:13

13Now may the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, that you may abound in hope in the power of the Holy Spirit.

Arminian

May the God of hope fill you with joy and peace in believing, that you may abound in hope by the power of the Holy Spirit. Arminian readers see joy and peace as the experience of faith, sustained by the Spirit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see hope, joy, and peace as gifts of God through the Spirit’s power. Believers abound in hope because God is its source.
