Passage

Romans 13

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Paul instructs believers to submit to governing authorities, because there is no authority except from God. Rulers are God’s servants to restrain evil and commend good, so believers should obey for conscience’s sake, pay taxes, and give honor and respect where due. The passage has shaped Christian political thought for centuries. The questions it raises, such as the limits of obedience and the right to resist tyranny, cut across both traditions rather than dividing them. Each still brings its own emphasis.

Romans 13:1

1Let every soul be in subjection to the higher authorities, for there is no authority except from God, and those who exist are ordained by God.

Arminian

Everyone must submit to governing authorities, since all authority comes from God. Arminian readers see God establishing government as an institution for human good, without endorsing every ruler’s actions.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence in the appointment of rulers. Calvin taught that even unjust rulers hold office under God’s sovereignty.

Romans 13:2

2Therefore he who resists the authority withstands the ordinance of God; and those who withstand will receive to themselves judgment.

Arminian

Whoever resists the authority resists what God has appointed. Arminian readers note that this describes government acting within its purpose, and that the apostles disobeyed when told to stop preaching (Acts 5:29).

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the duty of submission while developing a doctrine of lawful resistance. Later Reformed writers argued that lower magistrates may resist tyranny.

Romans 13:3

3For rulers are not a terror to the good work, but to the evil. Do you desire to have no fear of the authority? Do that which is good, and you will have praise from the authority,

Arminian

Rulers are a terror to evil conduct, not to good. Arminian readers see Paul describing government’s proper purpose, which provides a standard by which rulers can be judged.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the restraint of evil as one of God’s gifts of common grace to the world, preserving order so the gospel can spread.

Romans 13:4

4for he is a servant of God to you for good. But if you do that which is evil, be afraid, for he doesn’t bear the sword in vain; for he is a servant of God, an avenger for wrath to him who does evil.

Arminian

The ruler is God’s servant for your good and bears the sword to punish wrongdoing. Arminian readers see government as an agent of God’s justice in a fallen world.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the magistrate as a minister of God. The Westminster Confession grounds the civil authority’s right to punish evildoers partly in this verse.

Romans 13:5

5Therefore you need to be in subjection, not only because of the wrath, but also for conscience’ sake.

Arminian

Submit not only to avoid punishment but for conscience’s sake. Arminian readers stress the believer’s free, conscientious obedience as part of living a holy life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see conscience bound by God’s ordinance. Obedience to rulers becomes part of obedience to God.

Romans 13:6

6For this reason you also pay taxes, for they are servants of God’s service, continually doing this very thing.

Arminian

Pay taxes, because authorities are God’s servants. Arminian readers draw from this that ordinary civic duties are part of faithful Christian living.

Reformed

Reformed readers see paying taxes as a way of honoring God’s order. It echoes Jesus’ teaching about giving Caesar what belongs to Caesar.

Romans 13:7

7Therefore give everyone what you owe: if you owe taxes, pay taxes; if customs, then customs; if respect, then respect; if honor, then honor.

Arminian

Give everyone what you owe: taxes, revenue, respect, and honor. Arminian readers see Christian integrity reaching every relationship, including those with the state.

Reformed

Reformed readers see each person’s due as part of God’s ordered world. The verse leads into Paul’s point that the one debt never paid off is love (13:8).
