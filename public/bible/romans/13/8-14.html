Passage

Romans 13

Verses 8–14 · ESV · Voices

From civic dues Paul passes to an unpaid debt: love one another. The commandments against harming a neighbor are summed up in loving the neighbor as oneself; love does no wrong, and so fulfills the law. The hour has come to wake from sleep—salvation nearer now than when we first believed. Cast off the works of darkness, put on the armor of light, walk properly as in the daytime, and put on the Lord Jesus Christ, making no provision for the flesh.

Romans 13:8

8Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law.

Arminian

From dues to magistrates Paul passes to general duties (Wesley). “Owe no one anything, except to love each other”—an eternal debt “which can never be sufficiently discharged; but yet if this be rightly performed, it discharges all the rest.” He that loves another as he ought “hath fulfilled the whole law toward his neighbour.”

Arminian pastoral: love is not a mood that replaces the commandments; it is the living way they are kept. The debt remains open on purpose. A church that has paid Caesar can still default on the neighbor. Classical Arminians hear a real fulfillment: love, rightly performed, actually does the law’s neighbor-work—not a fiction, and not a ladder of merit, but the life grace trains.

Reformed

Calvin would rather take the words simply: the precept about magistrates belongs to the law of love. “He then who introduces anarchy, violates love; for what immediately follows anarchy, is the confusion of all things.” He who loves another has fulfilled the law—hypothetically. “Paul declares not what men do or do not, but he speaks hypothetically of that which you will find nowhere accomplished.” Keeping the law would be true righteousness; “as no one performs it, and never has performed it… the only refuge is in the grace of Christ.”

Reformed pastoral: love is the real summary of neighbor-duty, including civic order. It is not a second justification. We owe love daily; we do not finish the bill and then rest in our performance. Refuge remains Christ.

Key contrast Both take the unpaid debt of love as real and as the summary of duty to neighbor. Arminians typically hear Wesley: if love is rightly performed it discharges the rest and fulfills the law toward the neighbor. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: the fulfillment is hypothetical, nowhere accomplished, so this verse cannot fund justification by works; the only refuge is the grace of Christ.

Romans 13:9

9For the commandments, “You shall not commit adultery, You shall not murder, You shall not steal, You shall not covet,” and any other commandment, are summed up in this word: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”

Arminian

The particular commandments—adultery, murder, theft, coveting, and any other toward the neighbor—are summed up in this word: You shall love your neighbor as yourself. Wesley: if you were not even thinking of a given command, “yet if your heart was full of love, you would fulfil it.”

Arminian pastoral: love is not vagueness; it is the heart from which the second table actually gets kept. A full heart of love would not need a separate reminder not to steal. The gospel that trains love does not cancel the commandments; it writes them on the neighbor.

Reformed

Calvin: Paul does not here give a complete list of the second table; he omits even parents, and adds “any other commandment.” The point is that “God intended nothing else by all his commandments than to teach us the duty of love.” Obedience to magistrates is “not the least thing which tends to nourish peace, to preserve brotherly love.”

Reformed pastoral: the commandments named are samples of neighbor-love, not a new law of our own making. Love toward man flows from love of God and is its evidence. Civic peace is one of love’s public forms.

Romans 13:10

10Love does no wrong to a neighbor; therefore love is the fulfilling of the law.

Arminian

“Love does no wrong to a neighbor; therefore love is the fulfilling of the law.” Wesley: “the same love which restrains from all evil, incites us to all good.” Fulfillment is both No and Yes.

Arminian pastoral: a church can stop short at “do no harm” and call it love. Wesley will not allow it. The debt of v. 8 is active. Love that never wrongs and never helps is not the love that fulfills.

Reformed

Calvin: true love “will never entertain the thought of injuring others.” What else does the whole second table forbid? Apply it: magistrates are “guardians of peace and justice”; those who want every person secured from wrong “ought to defend, as far as he can, the power of magistrates.” “The enemies of government show a disposition to do harm.” He speaks only of the second table; the first table is not in view.

Reformed pastoral: love’s first public test is whether we wish our neighbor protected. Anarchy is not a more spiritual love. Fulfilling the law here still does not mean anyone has arrived at justifying righteousness.

Romans 13:11

11Besides this you know the time, that the hour has come for you to wake from sleep. For salvation is nearer to us now than when we first believed.

Arminian

“Do this”—fulfill the law of love—“knowing the season, full of grace, but hasting away” (Wesley). It is high time to awake. Wesley carries the metaphor: this life a night; the resurrection the day; the gospel shining on the heart the dawn. “Final salvation, glory, is nearer to us now, than when we first believed—It is continually advancing, flying forward upon the swiftest wings of time.”

Arminian pastoral: wake because the time is grace, not because panic is holy. Sleep here is the believer’s drift. Salvation nearer is glory nearer. The same people who love the neighbor must not doze through the little moment left.

Reformed

Calvin: another exhortation. The seasonable time has come to awake from sleep. “Nearer is salvation now to us than at that time when we began to believe.” He refuses to confine this to Jews under the law; it is said to the Roman church. The comparison of night and day looks toward the coming brightness.

Reformed pastoral: calling and the day of visitation require a new life. Sleep is torpor in those already shone on. Salvation nearer is the approaching fullness, not a hint that we must finish justifying ourselves before dawn. Wake because Christ is near.

Romans 13:12

12The night is far gone; the day is at hand. So then let us cast off the works of darkness and put on the armor of light.

Arminian

“The night is far gone; the day is at hand.” Wesley: rise, throw away night-clothes fit only for darkness, put on new, and, being soldiers, arm—we are encompassed with so many enemies. “The day dawns when we receive faith, and then sleep gives place. Then it is time to rise, to arm, to walk, to work, lest sleep steal upon us again.”

Arminian pastoral: casting off works of darkness is a real act of those who have seen dawn. Armor of light is for walking now. Grace that shone can be slept through; the warning exists because they might.

Reformed

Calvin: the night has advanced; we are not in the thick darkness of the unbelieving. The gospel sets resurrection hope before our eyes. “Day” here is the glorious brightness of celestial life, whose beginnings we already see. Cast off shameful works; put on armor, “because we are to carry on a warfare for the Lord.”

Reformed pastoral: look forward to the coming of Christ as people who already see dawn. The armor is given for a fight God appointed. Works of darkness are to be thrown off because the day is at hand, not because we dress ourselves for justification.

Romans 13:13

13Let us walk properly as in the daytime, not in orgies and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and sensuality, not in quarreling and jealousy.

Arminian

Walk properly as in the daytime: not orgies and drunkenness, not sexual immorality and sensuality, not quarreling and jealousy. Wesley hears “banqueting” as “luxurious, elegant feasts”—not only the alley but the polished table.

Arminian pastoral: the three pairs are public and private, flesh and faction. Quarreling sits beside drunkenness on purpose. A church can stay technically chaste and still walk in the night by jealousy. Daytime living is ordinary holiness, the same courtesy Titus 3:2 names toward all people.

Reformed

Calvin names three kinds, each with two names: excess in living, carnal lust, and envy-and-contention. “Strifes and contests arise from this fountain”: seeking to excel, envying, ambition. Even carnal men are ashamed to do these before witnesses; we who stand in God’s light should abstain even withdrawn from human eyes.

Reformed pastoral: the daytime is not a costume for public respectability. God and angels see. Ambition is as much darkness as reveling. Walk as those already in the light of Christ, not as those performing for the street.

Romans 13:14

14But put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, to gratify its desires.

Arminian

“Put on the Lord Jesus Christ.” Wesley: “Herein is contained the whole of our salvation. It is a strong and beautiful expression for the most intimate union with him, and being clothed with all the graces which were in him.” Paul does not say, Put on purity and sobriety; “he says all this and a thousand times more at once, in saying, Put on Christ.” “Make not provision—To raise foolish desires, or, when they are raised already, to satisfy them.”

Arminian pastoral: union with Christ is the dress, not a list of virtues we stitch together. The whole of salvation is in Him—graces included. Provision for the flesh is planned consent. Those united to Christ can still make that provision; the command exists because they might. Put Him on, and the nighttime list of v. 13 is already answered.

Reformed

Calvin: to put on Christ here is “to be on every side fortified by the power of his Spirit, and be thereby prepared to discharge all the duties of holiness; for thus is the image of God renewed in us.” God, “by adopting us, unites us to the body of his only-begotten Son,” that we, “renouncing our former life, may become new men in him.” Provide for the flesh’s wants as pilgrims; do not indulge its lusts.

Reformed pastoral: this is the holiness of the adopted, Spirit-armor for new men in the Son—not a human costume. No provision means a bridle on desire, not hatred of the body. The day is at hand; dress for it in Him.

Key contrast Both hear a real putting on of Christ as new life, not a slogan. Arminians typically hear Wesley: here is “the whole of our salvation”—intimate union, clothed with all His graces at once. Reformed readers typically hear Calvin: the adopted are fortified by the Spirit to holiness, the image of God renewed as they become new men in the Son.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on love as the fulfilling of the law, waking from sleep, and putting on the Lord Jesus Christ—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Romans 13:8–14.

Arminian Notable voices

“But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ—Herein is contained the whole of our salvation. It is a strong and beautiful expression for the most intimate union with him, and being clothed with all the graces which were in him. The apostle does not say, Put on purity and sobriety, peacefulness and benevolence; but he says all this and a thousand times more at once, in saying, Put on Christ. And make not provision—To raise foolish desires, or, when they are raised already, to satisfy them.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Rom 13:14)

Reformed Notable voices

“Now to put on Christ, means here to be on every side fortified by the power of his Spirit, and be thereby prepared to discharge all the duties of holiness; for thus is the image of God renewed in us, which is the only true ornament of the soul. For Paul had in view the end of our calling; inasmuch as God, by adopting us, unites us to the body of his only-begotten Son, and for this purpose,—that we, renouncing our former life, may become new men in him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Romans (on Rom 13:14)
