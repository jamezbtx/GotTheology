Passage

Romans 13

Verses 8–14 · WEB

Paul sums up the law in love. Believers should owe nothing except the continuing debt of love, because love fulfills the commandments. He then adds urgency: the night is nearly over and the day of salvation is near, so believers should wake up, put off the works of darkness, and put on the Lord Jesus Christ. Augustine’s conversion turned on verses 13–14. Both traditions embrace the passage, with different emphases on holiness and on how the law relates to love.

Romans 13:8

8Owe no one anything, except to love one another; for he who loves his neighbor has fulfilled the law.

Arminian

Owe no one anything except to love one another, for love fulfills the law. Wesleyan readers see love as the substance of holiness. Wesley described entire sanctification as being filled with love for God and neighbor.

Reformed

Reformed readers see love as the summary of the moral law, not its replacement. The commandments still describe what love looks like in practice.

Romans 13:9

9For the commandments, “You shall not commit adultery,” “You shall not murder,” “You shall not steal,” “You shall not covet,” and whatever other commandments there are, are all summed up in this saying, namely, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”

Arminian

The commandments are summed up in “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” Arminian readers see love as the motive that makes obedience joyful rather than burdensome.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the second table of the law summed up but not abolished. Each commandment remains a specific expression of love.

Romans 13:10

10Love doesn’t harm a neighbor. Love therefore is the fulfillment of the law.

Arminian

Love does no wrong to a neighbor, so love is the fulfilling of the law. Arminian readers, especially Wesleyans, see love as the goal toward which grace leads believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law fulfilled through love in believers, as Christ first fulfilled it perfectly on their behalf.

Romans 13:11

11Do this, knowing the time, that it is already time for you to awaken out of sleep, for salvation is now nearer to us than when we first believed.

Arminian

Salvation is nearer now than when we first believed. Arminian readers see a call to vigilance, since believers must stay awake and remain faithful until the end.

Reformed

Reformed readers see salvation here as its final completion at Christ’s return, which is certain for those who believe. The certainty motivates watchfulness.

Romans 13:12

12The night is far gone, and the day is near. Let’s therefore throw off the deeds of darkness, and let’s put on the armor of light.

Arminian

The night is far gone and the day is near; cast off the works of darkness and put on the armor of light. Arminian readers stress the believer’s active part in putting away sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the imperative resting on what God has already done. Believers belong to the day, so they must live as children of light.

Romans 13:13

13Let’s walk properly, as in the day; not in reveling and drunkenness, not in sexual promiscuity and lustful acts, and not in strife and jealousy.

Arminian

Walk properly, not in revelry, drunkenness, sexual immorality, or quarreling. Arminian readers see a description of the holy life that is truly possible by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the list includes quarreling and jealousy as well as gross sins. Sanctification reaches every area of life.

Romans 13:14

14But put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, for its lusts.

Arminian

Put on the Lord Jesus Christ and make no provision for the flesh. Arminian readers see a daily choice to clothe oneself with Christ and refuse sin. Augustine read this verse at his conversion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see believers putting on in practice the Christ they have already been clothed with in baptism (Galatians 3:27). Augustine’s conversion through this verse is treasured in both traditions.
