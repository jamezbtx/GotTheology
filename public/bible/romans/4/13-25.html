Passage

Romans 4

Verses 13–25 · ESV · Voices

Paul has shown that Abraham was counted righteous by faith before circumcision. Now he presses the promise itself: heir of the world came not through the law but through the righteousness of faith, so that it might rest on grace and be guaranteed to all Abraham’s offspring. Abraham believed the God who gives life to the dead; the same counting is for us who believe in him who raised Jesus, delivered for our trespasses and raised for our justification.

Romans 4:13

13For the promise to Abraham and his offspring that he would be heir of the world did not come through the law but through the righteousness of faith.

Arminian

The promise to Abraham and his offspring that he would be heir of the world did not come through the law but through the righteousness of faith. Not Sinai but Genesis 15; not works but the counting already named. Heir of the world is gift-language.

Classical Arminians hear the righteousness of faith as faith graciously counted, Christ remaining the meritorious cause. The promise is sincerely for Abraham’s offspring who believe—not a wage the law could have paid if they had tried harder.

Reformed

Through the righteousness of faith, not through the law. Calvin will soon say: if heirship comes by works, faith vanishes and the promise is abolished. The world’s heirship is based on gift-righteousness.

Reformed confession: the righteousness of faith is imputative—Christ’s, received by the instrument of faith. The promise did not come through the law because the law is not the source of this heirship. Mercy alone founds it.

Romans 4:14

14For if it is the adherents of the law who are to be the heirs, faith is null and the promise is void.

Arminian

If the adherents of the law are the heirs, faith is null and the promise is void. Mix the promise with law-heirship and you empty both. Wesley’s later word: it is of faith that it might be of grace, firm to those who follow Abraham’s faith.

Classical Arminians refuse a hybrid: you cannot keep faith as a living condition and add the law as a second title to the inheritance. The promise stands for those who believe, not for those who would make the law their claim.

Reformed

Calvin: if the heirship of salvation comes by works, faith vanishes, the promise is abolished; both must be sure and certain. Hence it comes by faith, stability based on the goodness of God alone.

Reformed accent: voided promise is the cost of works-heirship. Faith as instrument cannot share the title with the law. God guarantees the inheritance by taking it off the wage-system entirely.

Romans 4:15

15For the law brings wrath, but where there is no law there is no transgression.

Arminian

The law brings wrath; where there is no law there is no transgression. Paul is not slandering the law; he is telling the truth about what it does in this argument. Wrath is what law-heirship would deliver, because the law names transgression.

Classical Arminians honor the law’s verdict without making it a ladder into the promise. The law is holy; it is not the source of the world’s heirship. Grace must give what law cannot—righteousness counted to those who believe.

Reformed

Law brings wrath. That is why it cannot carry the promise. Calvin’s point stands: the curse and the inheritance do not flow from the same fountain as a title to the world.

Reformed reading: transgression is law’s exposure of sinners, not a small deficit faith can top up with effort. The promise must rest elsewhere—on grace, through the righteousness of faith—or wrath is all the law can pay.

Romans 4:16

16That is why it depends on faith, in order that the promise may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his offspring—not only to the adherent of the law but also to the one who shares the faith of Abraham, who is the father of us all,

Arminian

That is why it depends on faith, in order that the promise may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all his offspring—not only to the adherent of the law but also to the one who shares the faith of Abraham, who is the father of us all. Wesley: the blessing is of faith, that it might be of grace—that it might appear to flow from the free love of God, and that the promise might be firm, sure, and effectual to all the spiritual seed; Jews and gentiles if they follow his faith.

Classical Arminians hear faith as living trust: grace is the fountain, faith the condition so that the promise can actually meet Jews and Gentiles who believe. “Guaranteed to all his offspring” is the spiritual seed who share Abraham’s faith. Both camps say grace, not works; the fork is whether faith is that living following, graciously enabled, or only a link God forges in a closed chain.

Reformed

Calvin: it is of faith, that it might be by grace, to the end the promise might be sure to all the seed. If heirship comes by works, faith vanishes and the promise is abolished; hence it comes by faith, so that its stability, based on the goodness of God alone, may be secured.

Reformed confession: faith is the gift-in-the-chain, the Spirit-given instrument, so that grace remains grace and the promise remains sure. Guarantee is God’s goodness, not the quality of our following. Abraham is father of us all because God made the inheritance rest on mercy—faith receiving, not contributing.

Key contrast Both confess that the promise depends on faith so that it may rest on grace and be guaranteed to all Abraham’s offspring, Jew and Gentile—grace, not works. Arminians typically hear faith as living trust: the blessing is of faith that it might be of grace, sure to all who follow Abraham’s faith. Reformed readers typically hear faith as the gift-in-the-chain: the promise is guaranteed because its stability is based on the goodness of God alone, faith His instrument rather than an independent condition that makes the mercy take.

Romans 4:17

17as it is written, “I have made you the father of many nations”—in the presence of the God in whom he believed, who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist.

Arminian

I have made you the father of many nations—in the presence of the God in whom he believed, who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist. Wesley: the dead are not dead to him; even the things that are not, are before God; he summons them to rise into being. The seed did not then exist; yet God said, So shall thy seed be.

Classical Arminians hear a God whose promise is sincere and creative: he calls, and faith receives what does not yet appear. Abraham believed this God, not his own body. Living trust rests on the Promiser who quickens the dead.

Reformed

Calvin: whom he believed, who quickens the dead, and calls those things which be not as though they were. A seed was promised as though he were in vigor; he was as it were dead. He had to raise his thoughts to the power of God, by which the dead are quickened.

Reformed wonder: the God of this verse creates what he commands. Nations from a dead body are fiat grace. Faith is not Abraham’s contribution of vitality; it is the instrument that looks at God’s power and rests.

Romans 4:18

18In hope he believed against hope, that he should become the father of many nations, as he had been told, “So shall your offspring be.”

Arminian

In hope he believed against hope, that he should become the father of many nations, as he had been told, So shall your offspring be. Hope against hope is not wishful thinking; it is trust in a word when the body says no. The stars of Genesis 15:5 are still the text.

Classical Arminians honor this as living faith: Abraham really believed. Grace enabled the trust; he still believed against appearances. The promise was sincerely pledged; faith laid hold of it when hope, humanly speaking, was gone.

Reformed

Against hope he believed in hope, because the speaker was the God who quickens the dead. Calvin’s deadness is still on the page: the promise did not wait for Abraham to feel possible.

Reformed pastoral: “against hope” proves the object of faith is God’s word, not Abraham’s prospects. The same power that calls into existence what does not exist held him when natural hope died.

Romans 4:19

19He did not weaken in faith when he considered his own body, which was as good as dead (since he was about a hundred years old), or when he considered the barrenness of Sarah’s womb.

Arminian

He did not weaken in faith when he considered his own body, as good as dead, or the barrenness of Sarah’s womb. He looked at the facts and did not let them outvote the promise. Faith is not blindness; it is trust that God is greater than deadness.

Classical Arminians see a real consideration and a real not-weakening. Grace does not forbid looking at the empty tent; it forbids concluding that God cannot fill it. Living trust considers—and still believes.

Reformed

Calvin: he was as it were dead; it was necessary to raise his thoughts to the power of God. Considering the dead body did not become unbelief, because the consideration was carried up to the Quickener.

Reformed accent: deadness is the point, not a hurdle Abraham’s willpower cleared. The faith that did not weaken is faith in God’s ability, not faith in remaining human possibility. Gift-faith looks at death and names God.

Romans 4:20

20No unbelief made him waver concerning the promise of God, but he grew strong in his faith as he gave glory to God,

Arminian

No unbelief made him waver concerning the promise of God; he grew strong in his faith as he gave glory to God. Growing strong is the life of trust: not a single static ticket, but faith that gives God glory under delay.

Classical Arminians hear faith that can grow. Giving glory is the believer’s living response, enabled by grace. Unbelief is refused, not because Abraham became his own promise, but because he kept trusting the Promiser.

Reformed

He grew strong in faith, giving glory to God. Strength here is not self-manufacture; it is the patriarch held by the promise. Calvin’s whole argument still stands: stability based on the goodness of God alone.

Reformed reading: wavering is shut out because God is able, not because Abraham found a new inner resource. The Spirit’s instrument can be strengthened; the glory goes to God, not to the quality of the act as merit.

Romans 4:21

21fully convinced that God was able to do what he had promised.

Arminian

Fully convinced that God was able to do what he had promised. The conviction is about God’s ability, not Abraham’s. Wesley’s God who quickens the dead is the one Abraham was sure of.

Classical Arminians preach this as the shape of saving trust: persuasion that God can and will do what he said. Faith is living conviction, graciously enabled, the condition on which the counting meets the sinner—not a work that supplies what God lacked.

Reformed

Fully convinced: faith’s fullness is fullness of trust in God’s power to perform his own word. Calvin: he raised his thoughts to the power of God by which the dead are quickened.

Reformed confession: assurance here is theodicy in miniature—God is able. The conviction is itself of grace. Faith as instrument is full when it is empty of self-ability and full of the Promiser.

Romans 4:22

22That is why his faith was “counted to him as righteousness.”

Arminian

That is why his faith was counted to him as righteousness. Paul returns to Genesis 15:6 after showing what that faith actually was: trust in the God who raises the dead. Wesley: God accepted him as if he had been altogether righteous.

Classical Arminians hear the “why”: this living faith, not works, is graciously counted. Christ remains the meritorious cause; faith is the sole condition. The same fork as 4:3 and Genesis 15:6, now filled with dead-body trust.

Reformed

That is why: the faith just described—empty of works, full of God’s power—was the instrument of the counting. Calvin: faith brings righteousness not as a meritorious act but because it obtains God’s favor and adorns us with another’s righteousness.

Reformed confession: “that is why” does not mean the quality of Abraham’s act became his righteousness. It means this is the faith that receives the imputed righteousness of Christ. The reason is still God’s counting, not Abraham’s vigor.

Romans 4:23

23But the words “it was counted to him” were not written for his sake alone,

Arminian

The words “it was counted to him” were not written for his sake alone. Moses wrote more than a patriarch’s diary. The accounting is canonical gospel: it aims at readers who will need the same counting.

Classical Arminians take the “not for his sake alone” as a sincere wideness. Scripture means to meet us. What was counted to Abraham is offered as pattern to those who will believe—not a closed story about one elect man we may only admire.

Reformed

Not for his sake alone: Scripture’s purpose is larger than Abraham’s biography. God had us in mind when he had Moses write the counting.

Reformed reading: the word is for the people God will justify the same way—by imputation, received by faith. “Not for his sake alone” is comfort that the gospel is public in Scripture, not a hint that every reader is already justified.

Romans 4:24

24but for ours also. It will be counted to us who believe in him who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord,

Arminian

But for ours also. It will be counted to us who believe in him who raised from the dead Jesus our Lord. The same God, the same counting, now named on the far side of Easter. We believe in the raiser of Jesus; Abraham believed in the quickener of the dead.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere counting to all who believe. Faith is living trust in the God who raised Jesus, the graciously enabled condition. “For ours also” is as wide as the need: those who believe are counted righteous, Christ remaining the meritorious cause.

Reformed

For ours also: counted to us who believe in him who raised Jesus. Calvin’s imputative righteousness is not Abraham-only. The God who quickens the dead has now publicly raised our Lord.

Reformed confession: “us who believe” names those in whom the Spirit makes faith the instrument. The counting will be done; it is God’s. Faith is not an independent lever that makes Easter take. It receives the Christ God has already delivered and raised.

Key contrast Both say the counting was written for us also, who believe in him who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead. Arminians typically hear a sincere counting to all who believe—living trust in the God who raised Jesus, faith the graciously enabled condition. Reformed readers typically hear the same gospel preached to us, with faith the Spirit-given instrument by which the elect receive the counted righteousness—not an independent lever.

Romans 4:25

25who was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification.

Arminian

Who was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification. Wesley: delivered to death for our offences as an atonement; raised for our justification—to empower us to receive that atonement by faith. Death answers the trespasses; resurrection answers our receiving.

Classical Arminians glory in a real atonement sincerely set forth. The risen Christ empowers us to receive by faith what his death accomplished. Both are grace, not works. Living trust lays hold of the delivered and raised Lord; faith remains the condition, not a merit that adds to the blood.

Reformed

Calvin: our sins were expiated by the death of Christ; by his resurrection was obtained our righteousness. Satisfaction was given on the cross so that Christ might restore us to the Father’s favor; the completeness of that favor appears more clear by his coming to life again.

Reformed confession: delivered and raised are God’s accomplished gospel. Justification is obtained, not merely made possible. Faith is the Spirit-given instrument that receives this righteousness—empty-handed, not a co-cause. The ungodly are justified because Christ died and lives, not because their believing completed a half-finished work.

Key contrast Both glory in Jesus delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification, atonement and verdict by grace, not works. Arminians typically hear the resurrection as empowering us to receive that atonement by faith—living trust laying hold of the delivered and risen Lord. Reformed readers typically hear the resurrection as the obtaining of our righteousness: sins expiated at the cross, justification secured by his coming to life again, faith the instrument that receives what Christ accomplished.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the promise through the righteousness of faith, the God who gives life to the dead, and Jesus delivered for our trespasses and raised for our justification—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Romans 4:13–25.

Arminian Notable voices

“Therefore it—The blessing. Is of faith, that it might be of grace—That it might appear to flow from the free love of God, and that the promise might be firm, sure, and effectual, to all the spiritual seed of Abraham; not only Jews, but gentiles also, if they follow his faith. As quickening the dead—The dead are not dead to him, and even the things that are not, are before God. And calling the things that are not—Summoning them to rise into being, and appear before him. The seed of Abraham did not then exist; yet God said, So shall thy seed be. Who was delivered—To death. For our offences—As an atonement for them. And raised for our justification—To empower us to receive that atonement by faith.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Romans 4:16–17, 25)

Reformed Notable voices

“It is therefore of faith, that it might be by grace; to the end the promise might be sure to all the seed. If the heirship of salvation comes to us by works, then faith in it vanishes, the promise of it is abolished; but it is necessary that both these should be sure and certain; hence it comes to us by faith, so that its stability being based on the goodness of God alone, may be secured. Whom he believed, who quickens the dead, and calls those things which be not as though they were. A seed was promised to him as though he was in vigor and strength; but he was as it were dead. It was hence necessary for him to raise up his thoughts to the power of God, by which the dead are quickened. Who was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification. He says, first, that our sins were expiated by the death of Christ, and secondly, that by his resurrection was obtained our righteousness. Satisfaction for our sins was given on the cross; the completeness of his favor appears more clear by his coming to life again.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Romans (on Romans 4:16–17, 25)
