Passage

Romans 1

Verses 8–15 · WEB

Before laying out his gospel, Paul tells the Roman church how he prays for them and why he wants to visit. He thanks God that their faith is reported throughout the world, longs to share spiritual blessing with them, and explains that he has often been prevented from coming. He sees himself as a debtor to all kinds of people and is eager to preach the gospel in Rome too. These verses are pastoral common ground, though each tradition draws out its own emphases.

Romans 1:8

8First, I thank my God through Jesus Christ for all of you, that your faith is proclaimed throughout the whole world.

Arminian

Paul thanks God that the Romans’ faith is reported throughout the world. Arminian readers note that he thanks God for their faith while praising it as theirs, which reflects both divine grace and genuine human response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul thanking God, not the Romans, for their faith. This fits the Reformed view that faith itself is God’s gift.

Romans 1:9

9For God is my witness, whom I serve in my spirit in the Good News of his Son, how unceasingly I make mention of you always in my prayers,

Arminian

God is Paul’s witness that he prays for them without ceasing. Arminian readers draw from this that intercession truly matters to the spiritual growth of others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see prayer as one of the means God has ordained to accomplish his purposes. God’s sovereignty does not make prayer pointless but gives it its confidence.

Romans 1:10

10requesting, if by any means now at last I may be prospered by the will of God to come to you.

Arminian

Paul asks that by God’s will he may finally come to them. Arminian readers see Paul’s plans submitted to God’s guidance, with God responding to the prayers of his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul submitting his plans to God’s providence. Whether he comes is in God’s hands, and Paul accepts that.

Romans 1:11

11For I long to see you, that I may impart to you some spiritual gift, to the end that you may be established;

Arminian

Paul longs to impart some spiritual gift to strengthen them. Arminian readers see the strengthening of believers as part of what helps them continue in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God using ministers to build up his people. Strengthening the saints is one way he preserves them to the end.

Romans 1:12

12that is, that I with you may be encouraged in you, each of us by the other’s faith, both yours and mine.

Arminian

Paul and the Romans will be encouraged by one another’s faith. Arminian readers stress mutual encouragement as a real support for perseverance.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Paul’s humility. Even an apostle receives encouragement from ordinary believers, so the church grows through shared faith.

Romans 1:13

13Now I don’t desire to have you unaware, brothers, that I often planned to come to you (and was hindered so far), that I might have some fruit among you also, even as among the rest of the Gentiles.

Arminian

Paul often planned to come but was prevented. Arminian readers see real obstacles in a world where God works through circumstances that are not all determined in advance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence directing even hindrances. Paul’s delays served God’s purpose, including the writing of this letter.

Romans 1:14

14I am debtor both to Greeks and to foreigners, both to the wise and to the foolish.

Arminian

Paul is a debtor to Greeks and barbarians, wise and foolish. Arminian readers draw from this that the gospel is owed to every person, which fits their view that Christ died for all.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an obligation to preach to all kinds of people. Since no one knows who the elect are, the gospel must go to everyone.

Romans 1:15

15So as much as is in me, I am eager to preach the Good News to you also who are in Rome.

Arminian

Paul is eager to preach the gospel in Rome. Both traditions draw a missionary zeal from this verse. Arminian readers see eagerness rooted in God’s desire that all be saved.

Reformed

Reformed readers see eagerness rooted in confidence that God will save his people through preaching. They point to the great Reformed missionaries as an example of this confidence.
