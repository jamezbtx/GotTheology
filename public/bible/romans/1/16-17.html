Passage

Romans 1

Verses 16–17 · WEB

These two verses state the theme of Romans. Paul is not ashamed of the gospel, because it is God’s power to save everyone who believes, Jew first and also Greek, and in it God’s righteousness is revealed by faith. Luther’s rediscovery of verse 17 helped launch the Reformation, and both Arminian and Reformed Protestants stand on it. Their difference lies in how the gospel’s power relates to the faith of those who hear it.

Romans 1:16

16For I am not ashamed of the Good News of Christ, because it is the power of God for salvation for everyone who believes, for the Jew first, and also for the Greek.

Arminian

Paul writes to Christians in the capital of the empire, where a crucified Messiah looked foolish. He refuses to be ashamed, because the gospel is God’s power for salvation. Wesley called it the great means God uses to save all who accept salvation in his way. The order “Jew first, and also Greek” reflects the history of the promise; it does not make the offer narrower for anyone.

Arminian readers take “everyone who believes” as the condition under which the gospel’s power saves. The power is offered sincerely to all who hear, grace enables them to believe, and those who refuse are not excluded by any hidden decree. The verse describes a gospel meant for every neighbor.

Reformed

Calvin noted that God puts forth his saving power through the preaching of the gospel, not through a private revelation. The message is offered to all for their salvation. He added that its power does not take effect in everyone, but only where the Spirit, as the inward teacher, opens the heart.

Reformed readers therefore hear in “everyone who believes” the people in whom the Spirit makes the word effective. The offer goes to every nation without distinction, and salvation is entirely God’s power. Those who reject the same gospel find in it a witness against them.

Key contrast Both traditions glory in a gospel that is God’s power to save Jew and Greek who believe. Arminian readers see power offered to all, with grace-enabled faith as the condition of receiving it. Reformed readers see the same wide offer, with the gospel’s saving power made effective where the Spirit gives faith.

Romans 1:17

17For in it is revealed God’s righteousness from faith to faith. As it is written, “But the righteous shall live by faith.”

Arminian

In the gospel “God’s righteousness” is revealed. Wesley understood this as the righteousness that comes by faith, called God’s because he prepares it, reveals it, and gives it. He read “from faith to faith” as a progression of clearer promises and continuing trust, and Habakkuk’s line as teaching that the righteous obtain God’s favor by believing and continue in it by believing.

Arminian readers stress that this righteousness is received by living trust, not earned as a wage. Faith is the condition God has set, enabled by grace and never a meritorious work. The believer begins and continues in God’s favor by faith.

Reformed

Calvin taught that anyone who seeks life with God must first seek righteousness, by which we are reconciled to him. That righteousness, approved at God’s judgment seat, is offered in the gospel and received by faith. He read “from faith to faith” as faith growing, so that the believer’s hold on God’s righteousness is confirmed over time.

Reformed readers emphasize that the righteousness revealed is God’s, credited to the believer rather than produced in him. Faith is the instrument that receives it, and faith itself depends wholly on God’s mercy. Habakkuk shows that life before God has always been by faith, not by works of the law.

Key contrast Both confess that the gospel reveals God’s righteousness and that the righteous live by faith. Arminian readers describe faith as the grace-enabled condition by which believers receive and remain in God’s favor. Reformed readers describe an imputed righteousness received by faith as the Spirit-given instrument, so that justification depends entirely on mercy.
