Passage

Romans 1

Verses 18–32 · WEB

Having announced the gospel, Paul shows why it is needed. God’s wrath is revealed against human ungodliness, because people suppress the truth about God that creation makes plain. Refusing to honor him, they turned to idols, and God gave them over to the desires, passions, and corrupt thinking they chose. Both traditions read the passage as a portrait of fallen humanity. They differ mainly on what general revelation means for human ability and on how to describe God’s “giving them up.”

Romans 1:18

18For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men who suppress the truth in unrighteousness,

Arminian

God’s wrath is revealed against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of those who suppress the truth. Arminian readers see a holy God responding to deliberate rejection of what people know.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s righteous anger against sin, revealed now in the consequences of sin and finally at the judgment.

Romans 1:19

19because that which is known of God is revealed in them, for God revealed it to them.

Arminian

What can be known about God is plain to people, because God has shown it to them. Arminian readers often connect this general revelation with prevenient grace, which gives every person real light.

Reformed

Reformed readers see general revelation as sufficient to leave people without excuse, though not sufficient to save.

Key contrast Both affirm that God reveals himself to all through creation. Arminian readers link this light with prevenient grace given to every person. Reformed readers see it as leaving people without excuse while insufficient for salvation apart from special grace.

Romans 1:20

20For the invisible things of him since the creation of the world are clearly seen, being perceived through the things that are made, even his everlasting power and divinity, that they may be without excuse.

Arminian

God’s eternal power and divine nature are clearly seen in creation, so people are without excuse. Both traditions affirm human accountability before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that no one can claim ignorance of God as a defense at the judgment.

Romans 1:21

21Because knowing God, they didn’t glorify him as God, and didn’t give thanks, but became vain in their reasoning, and their senseless heart was darkened.

Arminian

Knowing God, they did not glorify him or give thanks, and their thinking became futile. Arminian readers see a refusal that people are responsible for.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the root of sin as ingratitude and refusal to worship, which darkens the mind.

Romans 1:22

22Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools,

Arminian

Claiming to be wise, they became fools. Both traditions see the irony of human pride.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the noetic effects of sin, in which rebellion distorts human reasoning.

Romans 1:23

23and traded the glory of the incorruptible God for the likeness of an image of corruptible man, and of birds, four-footed animals, and creeping things.

Arminian

They exchanged the glory of God for images. Both traditions see idolatry as the central human sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see idolatry as the substitution of the creature for the Creator.

Romans 1:24

24Therefore God also gave them up in the lusts of their hearts to uncleanness, that their bodies should be dishonored among themselves;

Arminian

“God gave them up” to impurity in the lusts of their hearts. Arminian readers see God withdrawing restraint and allowing people the consequences of their own choices. The giving up follows their rejection of him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a judicial act in which God hands people over to the sin they love. It is both punishment and the outworking of their own desires.

Key contrast Both read “God gave them up” as judgment that follows human rejection. Arminian readers stress God withdrawing restraint from people who chose this path. Reformed readers stress the judicial character of the act, in which God actively hands people over to the sin they desire.

Romans 1:25

25who exchanged the truth of God for a lie, and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed forever. Amen.

Arminian

They exchanged the truth of God for a lie and worshiped the creature. Both traditions see the deep exchange at the heart of idolatry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see worship of the creature as the source of every other disorder.

Romans 1:26

26For this reason, God gave them up to vile passions. For their women changed the natural function into that which is against nature.

Arminian

God gave them up to dishonorable passions. Both traditions read Paul’s examples as illustrations of disordered desire flowing from idolatry. Christians debate how these verses apply today, but the debate does not divide along Arminian and Reformed lines.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the examples as signs of a world turned from its Creator.

Romans 1:27

27Likewise also the men, leaving the natural function of the woman, burned in their lust toward one another, men doing what is inappropriate with men, and receiving in themselves the due penalty of their error.

Arminian

Paul continues the description. Both traditions see sin bringing its own penalty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the consequences of sin as part of God’s judgment.

Romans 1:28

28Even as they refused to have God in their knowledge, God gave them up to a reprobate mind, to do those things which are not fitting;

Arminian

Since they did not see fit to acknowledge God, God gave them up to a debased mind. Arminian readers see the mind darkened as a result of refusing God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the mind’s corruption as a judgment that confirms rebellion.

Romans 1:29

29being filled with all unrighteousness, sexual immorality, wickedness, covetousness, malice; full of envy, murder, strife, deceit, evil habits, secret slanderers,

Arminian

Paul lists the sins that fill a society that has turned from God. Both traditions see sin spreading into every area of life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the pervasiveness of sin.

Romans 1:30

30backbiters, hateful to God, insolent, arrogant, boastful, inventors of evil things, disobedient to parents,

Arminian

The list continues with slander, arrogance, and disobedience to parents. Both traditions see ordinary sins alongside grosser ones.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that all these sins are expressions of the same rebellion.

Romans 1:31

31without understanding, covenant breakers, without natural affection, unforgiving, unmerciful;

Arminian

They are without understanding, faithless, heartless, and ruthless. Both traditions see the loss of natural affection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the breakdown of human relationships as the fruit of broken relationship with God.

Romans 1:32

32who, knowing the ordinance of God, that those who practice such things are worthy of death, not only do the same, but also approve of those who practice them.

Arminian

They know God’s decree and still practice these things and approve of others who do. Arminian readers see conscious rebellion against known truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the guilt of approving sin, which shows how deeply sin has taken hold.
