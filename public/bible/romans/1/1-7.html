Passage

Romans 1

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Paul opens his longest letter by introducing himself and his gospel to a church he has not yet visited. He is a servant of Christ, called as an apostle and set apart for the good news God promised through the prophets. That good news concerns God’s Son, descended from David and declared Son of God in power by the resurrection. Paul greets the Roman believers as people called to belong to Jesus Christ and called to be saints. Both traditions share most of this ground. The word “called,” used three times, is where they begin to differ.

Romans 1:1

1Paul, a servant of Jesus Christ, called to be an apostle, set apart for the Good News of God,

Arminian

Paul is a servant of Christ, called to be an apostle, and set apart for the gospel. Arminian readers see a calling to a particular office and task. They note that this verse concerns vocation to ministry, not the question of who is saved.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul’s apostleship as resting entirely on God’s initiative. Paul elsewhere says God set him apart from his mother’s womb (Galatians 1:15), which Reformed readers connect with God’s sovereign purpose.

Romans 1:2

2which he promised before through his prophets in the holy Scriptures,

Arminian

God promised the gospel beforehand through the prophets. Arminian readers draw from this that the gospel is God’s long-announced plan to bless all nations, in continuity with his promise to Abraham.

Reformed

Reformed readers see one covenant of grace running through both testaments. The gospel is not a new idea but the fulfillment of what God promised from the beginning.

Romans 1:3

3concerning his Son, who was born of the offspring of David according to the flesh,

Arminian

The gospel concerns God’s Son, descended from David according to the flesh. Arminian readers stress the true humanity of Christ, which makes him a fitting Savior for all who share that humanity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ fulfilling the covenant with David. His human descent places him as the promised king who will reign forever.

Romans 1:4

4who was declared to be the Son of God with power according to the Spirit of holiness, by the resurrection from the dead, Jesus Christ our Lord,

Arminian

He was declared Son of God with power by the resurrection. Both traditions affirm the full deity of Christ. Arminian readers draw from the resurrection the assurance that Christ’s saving work is effective for all who believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the resurrection as God’s public declaration of Christ’s identity and his vindication of the Son’s finished work. It marks the beginning of his reign as the exalted Messiah.

Romans 1:5

5through whom we received grace and apostleship for obedience of faith among all the nations for his name’s sake;

Arminian

Through Christ Paul received grace and apostleship to bring about the obedience of faith among all nations. Arminian readers see faith as a real response of obedience to the gospel, offered among all the nations.

Reformed

Reformed readers read “obedience of faith” as the obedience that consists in faith and flows from it. They see God gathering his people from every nation through Paul’s preaching.

Romans 1:6

6among whom you are also called to belong to Jesus Christ;

Arminian

The Romans are among those called to belong to Jesus Christ. Arminian readers see “called” here as describing those who have heard and answered the gospel invitation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the effectual call, in which God draws his elect to himself. Paul uses “called” in this sense in Romans 8:30, where those called are also justified.

Key contrast Both read “called” as linking believers to Christ. Arminian readers see the call of the gospel answered by faith. Reformed readers see the effectual call that God gives to the elect, which always results in faith.

Romans 1:7

7to all who are in Rome, beloved of God, called to be saints: Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

Arminian

The letter goes to all in Rome who are beloved of God and called to be saints, with grace and peace. Arminian readers see all believers as holy by calling and summoned to live out that holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see “saints” as describing everyone set apart by God in Christ, not a special class. Grace and peace sum up the blessings that flow from God’s initiative.
