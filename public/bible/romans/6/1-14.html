Passage

Romans 6

Verses 1–14 · WEB

Paul anticipates an objection: if grace abounds where sin increased, should believers keep sinning? He answers that those united to Christ in baptism have died to sin and been raised to new life. They are to consider themselves dead to sin and alive to God, and not let sin reign in their bodies, because they are not under law but under grace. Both traditions treat this as central to the Christian life. Arminian readers, following Wesley, often see in it the hope of deliverance from the power of willful sin. Reformed readers see the definitive break with sin that begins a lifelong process of sanctification.

Romans 6:1

1What shall we say then? Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound?

Arminian

Should we continue in sin so that grace may abound? Both traditions reject this inference.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the objection shows Paul taught grace freely enough to be misunderstood.

Romans 6:2

2May it never be! We who died to sin, how could we live in it any longer?

Arminian

“May it never be! We who died to sin, how could we live in it any longer?” Arminian readers see a real change that makes continuing in sin a contradiction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer’s death to sin as a settled fact of union with Christ.

Romans 6:3

3Or don’t you know that all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death?

Arminian

All who were baptized into Christ were baptized into his death. Both traditions see baptism as a sign of union with Christ, though they differ among themselves on its mode and subjects.

Reformed

Reformed readers see baptism as a sign and seal of union with Christ.

Romans 6:4

4We were buried therefore with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we also might walk in newness of life.

Arminian

Buried with him, we are raised to walk in newness of life. Both traditions see new life as the purpose of union with Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see regeneration as producing a new walk.

Romans 6:5

5For if we have become united with him in the likeness of his death, we will also be part of his resurrection;

Arminian

If united with him in his death, we will be united in his resurrection. Both traditions see the certainty of resurrection life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see union with Christ as the source of every saving benefit.

Romans 6:6

6knowing this, that our old man was crucified with him, that the body of sin might be done away with, so that we would no longer be in bondage to sin.

Arminian

Our old self was crucified with him so that the body of sin might be done away with and we would no longer be enslaved to sin. Wesley read this as a real deliverance from the power of sin, opening the way to entire sanctification, in which a believer does not commit willful sin.

Arminian readers do not claim sinless perfection in the sense of freedom from all mistakes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the old self crucified once for all, breaking sin’s dominion. Indwelling sin remains, however, and the believer fights it throughout life.

The Westminster Confession describes sanctification as real but imperfect in this life.

Key contrast Both agree that sin’s dominion is broken in Christ. Wesleyan Arminians hold out the hope of entire sanctification in this life. Reformed readers see a real but incomplete sanctification, with a continuing struggle against remaining sin.

Romans 6:7

7For he who has died has been freed from sin.

Arminian

One who has died has been set free from sin. Both traditions see freedom from sin’s rule.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a legal and relational release from sin’s claim.

Romans 6:8

8But if we died with Christ, we believe that we will also live with him,

Arminian

If we died with Christ, we believe we will also live with him. Both traditions see the hope of resurrection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer’s future secured in Christ.

Romans 6:9

9knowing that Christ, being raised from the dead, dies no more. Death no longer has dominion over him!

Arminian

Christ, raised from the dead, will never die again. Both traditions see Christ’s victory over death.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the finality of Christ’s work.

Romans 6:10

10For the death that he died, he died to sin one time; but the life that he lives, he lives to God.

Arminian

He died to sin once for all and lives to God. Both traditions see Christ’s death as complete.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the once-for-all character of the atonement.

Romans 6:11

11Thus consider yourselves also to be dead to sin, but alive to God in Christ Jesus our Lord.

Arminian

Consider yourselves dead to sin and alive to God. Arminian readers see faith claiming what God has done.

Reformed

Reformed readers see believers reckoning on their union with Christ.

Romans 6:12

12Therefore don’t let sin reign in your mortal body, that you should obey it in its lusts.

Arminian

Do not let sin reign in your mortal body. Arminian readers see a real responsibility that believers can fulfill by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the imperative grounded in the indicative: because sin’s dominion is broken, believers must resist it.

Romans 6:13

13Also, do not present your members to sin as instruments of unrighteousness, but present yourselves to God as alive from the dead, and your members as instruments of righteousness to God.

Arminian

Present yourselves to God as alive from the dead. Both traditions see the call to active obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sanctification as active cooperation with grace.

Romans 6:14

14For sin will not have dominion over you, for you are not under law, but under grace.

Arminian

Sin will not have dominion over you, because you are not under law but under grace. Arminian readers read this as both promise and encouragement for those who walk in grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a promise that sin will never regain mastery over a true believer.

Key contrast Both read the verse as a promise grounded in grace. Arminian readers see a promise to those who continue walking in grace. Reformed readers see a guarantee that sin will never again rule a true believer.
