Passage

Romans 14

Verses 13–23 · WEB

Paul turns from the weak to the strong. Though no food is unclean in itself, believers should never use their liberty in a way that causes a brother to stumble or destroys someone for whom Christ died. The kingdom of God is about righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit, not food and drink. Whatever is not from faith is sin. Both traditions draw ethical guidance from the passage. Verse 15, which warns against destroying a brother for whom Christ died, is a point of real debate between them.

Romans 14:13

13Therefore let’s not judge one another any more, but judge this rather, that no man put a stumbling block in his brother’s way, or an occasion for falling.

Arminian

Do not put a stumbling block or hindrance in a brother’s way. Arminian readers see a real danger that one believer’s actions can harm another’s faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see love limiting liberty. Believers must consider how their freedom affects others.

Romans 14:14

14I know and am persuaded in the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean of itself; except that to him who considers anything to be unclean, to him it is unclean.

Arminian

Nothing is unclean in itself, but it is unclean to anyone who thinks it is. Arminian readers draw from this the importance of acting in line with conscience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see food laws fulfilled in Christ, while conscience must still be respected. Violating conscience is wrong even when the act itself is permitted.

Romans 14:15

15Yet if because of food your brother is grieved, you walk no longer in love. Don’t destroy with your food him for whom Christ died.

Arminian

Do not destroy by your food the one for whom Christ died. Arminian readers see this as evidence that a believer for whom Christ died can truly be destroyed, meaning finally lost. They also see it as evidence that Christ’s death was for people who may perish.

Reformed

Reformed readers usually read “destroy” as serious spiritual harm, not final loss of salvation. They also read the warning as one of the means God uses to preserve his people.

Key contrast Both take the warning seriously. Arminian readers see a real possibility that a brother for whom Christ died could finally perish. Reformed readers see grievous harm to faith that God’s warnings are designed to prevent, without implying the elect can be lost.

Romans 14:16

16Then don’t let your good be slandered,

Arminian

Do not let what you regard as good be spoken of as evil. Arminian readers see liberty used unwisely as a cause of reproach to the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christian freedom as a good gift that must be guarded by love, so that it is not discredited.

Romans 14:17

17for God’s Kingdom is not eating and drinking, but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit.

Arminian

The kingdom of God is not eating and drinking but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit. Arminian readers, especially Wesleyans, see the kingdom as present holiness and joy worked by the Spirit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the kingdom’s blessings flowing from Christ’s righteousness and the Spirit’s work. Secondary matters must not crowd out what is central.

Romans 14:18

18For he who serves Christ in these things is acceptable to God and approved by men.

Arminian

Whoever serves Christ this way is acceptable to God and approved by people. Arminian readers see faithful service as pleasing to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see service flowing from grace, which God graciously accepts for Christ’s sake.

Romans 14:19

19So then, let’s follow after things which make for peace, and things by which we may build one another up.

Arminian

Pursue what makes for peace and mutual building up. Arminian readers see the community’s role in helping believers persevere.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the edification of the church as the goal of Christian liberty.

Romans 14:20

20Don’t overthrow God’s work for food’s sake. All things indeed are clean, however it is evil for that man who creates a stumbling block by eating.

Arminian

Do not destroy the work of God for food. Arminian readers see a fellow believer as God’s work that can be damaged by careless behavior.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer as God’s workmanship. Tearing down a brother works against what God is building.

Romans 14:21

21It is good to not eat meat, drink wine, nor do anything by which your brother stumbles, is offended, or is made weak.

Arminian

It is good not to eat meat or drink wine or do anything that causes your brother to stumble. Arminian readers see self-denial in love as part of holy living.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the voluntary limitation of liberty for the sake of others. Freedom is used best in serving love.

Romans 14:22

22Do you have faith? Have it to yourself before God. Happy is he who doesn’t judge himself in that which he approves.

Arminian

Keep your faith about these things between yourself and God. Blessed is the one who does not condemn himself by what he approves. Arminian readers stress the integrity of personal conviction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a clear conscience before God as a blessing. Liberty should be enjoyed privately when its public use would harm others.

Romans 14:23

23But he who doubts is condemned if he eats, because it isn’t of faith; and whatever is not of faith is sin.

Arminian

Whoever doubts is condemned if he eats, because whatever is not from faith is sin. Arminian readers see the necessity of acting from conviction before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers often apply this principle broadly, holding that even good acts done apart from faith do not please God (Hebrews 11:6). Augustine and the Reformers drew on this verse in that way.
