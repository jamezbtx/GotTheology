Passage

Romans 14

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Paul addresses tensions between the “weak,” who keep food laws and special days out of conscience, and the “strong,” who feel free from them. Neither group should despise or judge the other, because each lives and dies to the Lord, and each will give account to God. Both traditions read the passage as a guide to Christian liberty and charity on secondary matters. Each draws on it in its own way, Arminian readers stressing individual accountability and Reformed readers stressing the lordship of Christ over conscience.

Romans 14:1

1Now accept one who is weak in faith, but not for disputes over opinions.

Arminian

Welcome the one who is weak in faith, but not to quarrel over opinions. Arminian readers see a call to patience with believers at different stages of growth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the principle of liberty of conscience in matters Scripture leaves free. The Westminster Confession teaches that God alone is Lord of the conscience.

Romans 14:2

2One man has faith to eat all things, but he who is weak eats only vegetables.

Arminian

One person believes he may eat anything, while the weak person eats only vegetables. Arminian readers see sincere believers reaching different conclusions on non-essential matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers see adiaphora, matters neither commanded nor forbidden. Believers must not bind one another where God has not.

Romans 14:3

3Don’t let him who eats despise him who doesn’t eat. Don’t let him who doesn’t eat judge him who eats, for God has accepted him.

Arminian

The one who eats must not despise the one who abstains, and the abstainer must not judge the eater, for God has welcomed him. Arminian readers draw from this that God’s acceptance of a believer rests on faith, not on these practices.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s welcome as the ground for mutual acceptance. Since God has received both, neither may reject the other.

Romans 14:4

4Who are you who judge another’s servant? To his own lord he stands or falls. Yes, he will be made to stand, for God has power to make him stand.

Arminian

Who are you to judge another’s servant? He will stand, because the Lord is able to make him stand. Arminian readers see the Lord’s power to uphold the believer who keeps trusting him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Lord’s power to make his servants stand as an assurance of preservation. The verse is often cited in support of perseverance.

Key contrast Both read “the Lord is able to make him stand” as encouragement. Arminian readers see God upholding those who continue in faith. Reformed readers see an assurance that God will preserve his people to the end.

Romans 14:5

5One man esteems one day as more important. Another esteems every day alike. Let each man be fully assured in his own mind.

Arminian

One person esteems one day above another; another esteems all days alike. Each should be fully convinced in his own mind. Arminian readers stress personal conviction and responsibility before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers apply this mainly to Jewish festivals. Many Reformed churches still hold the Lord’s Day as a continuing creation ordinance, which they distinguish from these days.

Romans 14:6

6He who observes the day, observes it to the Lord; and he who does not observe the day, to the Lord he does not observe it. He who eats, eats to the Lord, for he gives God thanks. He who doesn’t eat, to the Lord he doesn’t eat, and gives God thanks.

Arminian

The one who observes the day does so to the Lord, and the one who eats does so to the Lord, giving thanks. Arminian readers see sincere devotion honored by God in either practice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see thanksgiving as the mark of genuine faith in both groups. The motive of honoring God matters more than the practice.

Romans 14:7

7For none of us lives to himself, and none dies to himself.

Arminian

None of us lives to himself, and none dies to himself. Arminian readers see the believer’s whole life belonging to the Lord, which is the root of holy living.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the believer’s life and death as belonging to Christ. The Heidelberg Catechism’s first answer echoes this: I belong to my faithful Savior, body and soul, in life and in death.

Romans 14:8

8For if we live, we live to the Lord. Or if we die, we die to the Lord. If therefore we live or die, we are the Lord’s.

Arminian

Whether we live or die, we are the Lord’s. Arminian readers draw comfort and accountability together: believers belong to the Lord and live for him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see deep assurance here. Nothing, not even death, changes the believer’s belonging to Christ.

Romans 14:9

9For to this end Christ died, rose, and lived again, that he might be Lord of both the dead and the living.

Arminian

Christ died and lived again to be Lord of both the dead and the living. Both traditions see Christ’s lordship grounded in his death and resurrection. Arminian readers stress that this lordship calls for personal submission.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s universal lordship won by his work, which extends over his people in life and death.

Romans 14:10

10But you, why do you judge your brother? Or you again, why do you despise your brother? For we will all stand before the judgment seat of Christ.

Arminian

Why do you judge or despise your brother? We will all stand before God’s judgment seat. Arminian readers see personal accountability before God as a reason to leave judgment to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the judgment seat as the great leveler. Believers will give account, though not to be condemned, since there is no condemnation for those in Christ (8:1).

Romans 14:11

11For it is written, “‘As I live,’ says the Lord, ‘to me every knee will bow. Every tongue will confess to God.’”

Arminian

Every knee will bow and every tongue confess to God. Arminian readers see God’s universal sovereignty and the accountability of every person.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the certainty of God’s final triumph. Paul applies Isaiah 45:23 to the judgment of believers.

Romans 14:12

12So then each one of us will give account of himself to God.

Arminian

Each of us will give account of himself to God. Arminian readers stress individual responsibility for one’s own choices and faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that believers will give account, and see this as motivating diligence. Their acceptance with God rests on Christ, while their works are evaluated for reward.
