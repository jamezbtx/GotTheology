Passage

Romans 8

Verses 31–39 · ESV · Voices

After the golden chain, Paul asks who can be against us, who will bring a charge, who will separate us from the love of Christ: tribulation, death, life, angels, nothing in all creation.

Romans 8:31

31What then shall we say to these things? If God is for us, who can be against us?

Arminian

“What then shall we say to these things?” follows the golden chain. Paul glories: if God is for us, who can be against us? Both camps take the question as triumph, not shrug.

Classical Arminians name the “us”: those in Christ who love God (8:28) and continue in His goodness (Rom 11:22). God is truly for that people, so no opponent can finally unseat them while they remain. The verse is not a lock irrespective of faith; it is the believer’s boast under a God who is for those who stay in His Son.

Reformed

After 8:29–30, the “these things” are the chain itself. If God is for us, who can be against us? The question expects no successful foe.

Reformed reading names the “us”: the predestined, called, justified, glorified. Calvin: this is the chief and only support in every temptation—not that nothing will oppose, but that victory is promised. God being “for” the people of the chain means no enemy can break what He has already spoken in glory’s past tense.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take the “us” as those in Christ who love God (8:28) and continue in His goodness (Rom 11:22): God is for believers, so no opponent prevails while they remain. Reformed readings typically take the “us” as the predestined, called, justified, glorified of 8:29–30: if God is for that people, no enemy can break the chain.

Romans 8:32

32He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all, how will he not also with him graciously give us all things?

Arminian

The greater gift proves the lesser: He did not spare His own Son but gave Him up for us all; how will He not also, with Him, graciously give us all things? The cross is the measure of the Father’s generosity toward those in His Son.

Arminian reading: “us all” is the believing people for whom the Son was given—those who love God and remain (8:28; 11:22). “All things” are the needful gifts that follow justification (Wesley: freely, for all that follows justification is a free gift also). The a fortiori comforts those who continue in Christ; it does not make perseverance automatic apart from remaining in His goodness.

Reformed

He who did not spare His own Son but gave Him up for us all will, with Him, graciously give us all things. Calvin: an argument from the greater to the less—having nothing dearer than His Son, He will neglect nothing profitable to us.

Reformed accent: “us all” is the same “us” as v. 31—the elect of the chain. The gift of the Son is particular, and “all things” include every grace now and glory hereafter. If the costliest gift is already given for that people, the remaining gifts—including holding them to the end—cannot be withheld.

Romans 8:33

33Who shall bring any charge against God’s elect? It is God who justifies.

Arminian

Who shall bring any charge against God’s elect? It is God who justifies. The courtroom is closed while the Judge has absolved.

Wesley on “God’s elect”: the title once belonged to Israel; in the New Testament it is applied to the members of the invisible church, and nothing could cut them off from these privileges but their own wilful apostasy. Classical Arminians hear a real elect—those in Christ by faith—against whom no charge sticks while God justifies them. The fork is not whether God justifies; it is that the elect here are the believing people who must continue (Rom 11:22), not an unconditional list that cannot be forsaken.

Reformed

Who shall bring any charge against God’s elect? God is the one who justifies. Calvin: there is but one God at whose tribunal we stand; there is no room for accusation when He justifies. The devil, the law, and conscience may accuse; they prevail nothing with this Judge.

Reformed reading: “God’s elect” are those He predestined, called, justified, glorified (8:29–30). Calvin applies the title as Paul did—not by private revelation, but as what every godly person may take to himself by examining the calling. The charge cannot land because the Judge has already counted this people just.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take “God’s elect” as the believing people—NT members of the invisible church (Wesley)—justified while they continue; nothing cuts them off but wilful apostasy. Reformed readings typically take “God’s elect” as those God chose in the chain of 8:29–30: the Judge has justified them, so no charge can stand and the people so named cannot finally be un-elected.

Romans 8:34

34Who is to condemn? Christ Jesus is the one who died—more than that, who was raised—who is at the right hand of God, who indeed is interceding for us.

Arminian

Who is to condemn? Four facts answer: Christ died, was raised, sits at God’s right hand, and intercedes for us. Wesley: our faith should not stop at His death but be exercised farther on His resurrection, kingdom, second coming; He presents there His obedience, sufferings, prayers, and ours sanctified through Him.

Arminian pastoral: this intercession is for the “us” in Christ—those who love God and remain. The risen Advocate is real help against condemnation; He does not make remaining optional. Nothing external can unseat His people; they may still neglect the goodness in which they stand (Rom 11:22).

Reformed

Who is to condemn? Christ died, was raised, sits at the right hand, and intercedes. Calvin: whoever would condemn us must bring Christ back to death; He has not only died but come forth as death’s conqueror, and He sits to be a perpetual advocate securing our salvation.

Reformed comfort: the “us” of the intercession are the elect of vv. 29–33. Christ’s death and session close the court. Calvin called the scholastic uncertainty of salvation pestilent: our faith is nothing unless we feel assured that Christ is ours. The intercession does not wait on our grip; it is the grip.

Romans 8:35

35Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or danger, or sword?

Arminian

Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? The list is tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, nakedness, danger, sword—outward and inward hardships, not unbelief. Wesley: the love of Christ toward us; can any of these separate us from His protection in it, and, if He sees good, deliverance from it?

Classical Arminians glory here with the Reformed: nothing in this catalogue can tear the believer from Christ’s love. The fork is the “us” and the kind of separation denied. “Us” = those in Christ who love God (8:28). No creaturely trouble can separate; we may still forsake. Paul names swords, not apostasy.

Reformed

Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Calvin: whatever happens, we ought to stand firm in this faith—that God, who once in His love embraced us, never ceases to care for us. The love is Christ’s toward us, opened in Him by the Father, not first our fervor toward God.

Reformed reading: the “us” are the elect of the chain. Tribulation through sword cannot divorce that people from participating in His love. The question is not whether hardship happens—it will—but whether any of it can unmake those He has embraced. It cannot.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically hear that no external hardship—tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, nakedness, danger, sword—can separate those in Christ who love God from Christ’s love; we may still neglect or forsake. Reformed readings typically hear that nothing can separate the elect (the “us” of 8:29–30) from Christ’s love toward them: suffering cannot undo the people the chain has named.

Romans 8:36

36As it is written, “For your sake we are being killed all the day long; we are regarded as sheep to be slaughtered.”

Arminian

Psalm 44:22: for Your sake we are being killed all the day long; we are regarded as sheep to be slaughtered. Suffering is the expected lot of God’s people, not proof that His love has failed.

Arminian note: the “we” who are slaughtered sheep are the same “us”—believers who love God. Daily dying does not mean God is against us (v. 31). The psalm trains the church not to read hardship as separation; it does not turn the people into an unconditional list that cannot walk away from the goodness in which they stand.

Reformed

As it is written: killed all the day long, regarded as sheep to be slaughtered. Calvin: the dread of death is so far from a reason for falling away that it has been almost ever the lot of God’s servants to have death present before their eyes.

Reformed accent: the citation weighs the list of v. 35. The elect are not spared the slaughter-house of this age; they are spared separation. Being counted as sheep is not abandonment of the people God justified (v. 33). The chain runs through the slaughter, not around it.

Romans 8:37

37No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us.

Arminian

No: in all these things we are more than conquerors through Him who loved us. Wesley: we are not only no losers, but abundant gainers, by all these trials—this period seems to describe the full assurance of hope.

Arminian reading: the victory is “through Him who loved us,” not through stoic grit. The “us” are those He loved in Christ, who love God and continue. Super-conquest is real for believers in tribulation; it is not a promise that no one can neglect the love that won it. The conquerors are the people still in the One who loved them.

Reformed

In all these things we are more than conquerors through Him who loved us. Calvin: we always struggle and emerge; the issue given them is that they obtain the victory—and he repeats that this invincible power proceeds from the paternal kindness of God and of Christ toward us.

Reformed comfort: “who loved us” locates the cause in His love, not ours. The elect conquer because He loved them. The aorist of that love, with the chain of 8:29–30, means the victory is as sure as the Lover. Super-conquest is the present tense of a people already embraced.

Romans 8:38

38For I am sure that neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers,

Arminian

“I am sure”: neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers. Wesley infers this from v. 34 in order: death cannot hurt, for Christ is dead; life cannot, for He is risen; angels, principalities, powers, present and to come cannot, for He is at the right hand.

Classical Arminians share Paul’s persuasion about every named power. The catalogue is creaturely—death, life, angels, time, authorities. No such thing can separate those who believe. The assurance is of believers concerning what is beneath the Almighty (v. 39), not a denial that we may still forsake the love we stand in.

Reformed

Paul is sure. Death, life, angels, rulers, present, future, powers: Calvin hears hyperbolic reach so that we might be more fully confirmed. Whatever seems capable of tearing us away shall effect nothing; even angels, were they to attempt it, shall do no harm.

Reformed reading: this persuasion is the certainty of the elect, against the schoolmen who said no one is sure of final perseverance without rare special revelation. Calvin: such a dogma destroys faith, which must extend to death and beyond. He who began the good work will carry it on. The “us” of this surety are the chain’s people.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take Paul’s “I am sure” as the believer’s persuasion that no listed creaturely power can separate those who believe and continue; the catalogue is external. Reformed readings typically take the same persuasion as certainty of final perseverance for the elect of 8:29–30 (Calvin against scholastic uncertainty): the chain cannot lose a link to death, life, angels, or time.

Romans 8:39

39nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord.

Arminian

Nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord. Wesley: nor any creature—nothing beneath the Almighty; visible enemies he does not even deign to name. Shall be able, either by force or by any legal claim, to separate us from the love of God in Christ—which will surely save, protect, deliver us who believe.

Both camps glory: nothing in creation can unmake that love. The Arminian fork is the “us” and the kind of threat denied. “Us” = those in Christ who love God (8:28) and continue in His goodness (Rom 11:22). No creature can separate; apostasy is not a creature acting from outside—we may still neglect or forsake. The love of God is in Christ; remain in Him.

Reformed

Nothing in all creation will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord. Calvin: if we are through Him united to God, we may be assured of the immutable and unfailing kindness of God toward us; the fountain of love is in the Father and flows to us from Christ.

Reformed reading names the “us”: the predestined, called, justified, glorified of 8:29–30. The chain cannot lose a link. Christ’s death and intercession secure this people; height, depth, and every created thing fail against God’s love in Christ. This is not generic comfort. It is the perseverance of the elect He has already glorified in hope.

Key contrast KEY perseverance. Both camps glory: nothing in all creation separates from God’s love in Christ. The fork is who the “us” is. Arminian readings typically take “us” as those in Christ who love God (8:28) and continue in His goodness (Rom 11:22): no creature can separate (Wesley: the love of God is in Christ, delivering us who believe); we may still neglect or forsake. Reformed readings typically take “us” as the predestined, called, justified, glorified of 8:29–30: the chain cannot lose a link; Christ’s death and intercession secure the elect, and nothing in creation can separate them from God’s love in Christ. Do not flatten both into mere comfort.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on “if God is for us” and nothing that can separate us from the love of Christ—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Romans 8:31–39.

Arminian Notable voices

“If God be for us, who can be against us? The same appellations which once belonged to the Jewish nation were now given to the gentile Christians also, together with which they were invested with all the privileges of the chosen people of God; and nothing could cut them off from these but their own wilful apostasy. Nor any creature—nothing beneath the Almighty. Shall be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ—which will surely save, protect, deliver us who believe in, and through, and from, them all.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Rom 8:31–39)

Reformed Notable voices

“If God be for us: this is the chief and the only support which can sustain us in every temptation. He does not mean that nothing shall oppose us; but he promises a victory over all kinds of enemies. No adversary can shake or endanger our salvation. God, who once in his love embraced us, never ceases to care for us. This declaration is clearly against the schoolmen, who say that no one is certain of final perseverance: we ought to feel confident that he who has begun in us a good work will carry it on until the day of the Lord Jesus.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Romans (on Rom 8:31–39)
