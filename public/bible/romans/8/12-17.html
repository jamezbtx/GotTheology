Passage

Romans 8

Verses 12–17 · WEB

Paul draws a practical conclusion from the Spirit’s work in Romans 8:1–11. Believers owe the flesh nothing; those who put sin to death by the Spirit will live, and those led by the Spirit are God’s children. The Spirit of adoption replaces fear with the cry “Abba! Father!” and testifies with our spirit that we are heirs with Christ. Both traditions find assurance here, and both notice the conditions: “if you live after the flesh, you must die” and “if indeed we suffer with him.”

Romans 8:12

12So then, brothers, we are debtors, not to the flesh, to live after the flesh.

Arminian

“So then” draws a conclusion from what the Spirit has done. Believers are debtors, but not to the flesh; they owe their lives to God who gave them the Spirit. Arminian readers hear a call to the will: the obligation is real and must be acted on.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the indicative grounding the imperative. Because the Spirit dwells in believers (vv. 9–11), they are no longer obligated to the flesh. Holiness flows from union with Christ and is pursued in dependence on the Spirit.

Romans 8:13

13For if you live after the flesh, you must die; but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live.

Arminian

Paul warns that living according to the flesh leads to death, while putting sin to death by the Spirit leads to life. He is writing to believers. Wesley and the Arminian tradition take the warning at face value: a Christian who returns to a life ruled by the flesh is in real danger of death.

The remedy is not self-effort alone but mortification “by the Spirit.” Believers cooperate with grace in putting sin to death, and life is promised to those who do.

Reformed

Reformed readers also take the warning seriously and apply it to everyone in the church. Calvin taught that believers mortify sin by the Spirit’s power, never by their own strength. The warning is one of the means by which God keeps his people from the path that leads to death.

On the Reformed reading, those who are truly the Spirit’s will in fact put sin to death, and that ongoing struggle is a mark of genuine faith. Those who live by the flesh without repentance show that they do not belong to Christ (v. 9).

Key contrast Both read verse 13 as addressed to believers and both insist on mortification by the Spirit. Arminian readers see a real possibility of a believer returning to the flesh and dying. Reformed readers see a warning God uses to keep true believers putting sin to death, which they will do.

Romans 8:14

14For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, these are children of God.

Arminian

Being led by the Spirit is the mark of God’s children. Arminian readers see a living relationship in which believers follow the Spirit’s leading and can also grieve or resist him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit’s leading as the sure sign of adoption. Those whom the Spirit leads are God’s children, and his leading is effective in their lives.

Romans 8:15

15For you didn’t receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry, “Abba! Father!”

Arminian

The Spirit of adoption replaces the fear of a slave with the trust of a child. “Abba! Father!” is the cry of intimate confidence. Wesley gave great weight to this verse in his teaching on assurance: a believer can know that he is God’s child.

Reformed

Reformed readers see adoption as a legal and relational act of God that gives believers a new status. The cry of “Abba” arises from the Spirit’s work, not from human confidence. Fear gives way to filial trust.

Romans 8:16

16The Spirit himself testifies with our spirit that we are children of God;

Arminian

The Spirit testifies with our spirit that we are God’s children. Wesley built his doctrine of the witness of the Spirit on this verse: there is a direct inward testimony of the Spirit, joined with the testimony of our own spirit seen in the fruit of a changed life. Assurance is a normal privilege of believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the Spirit’s witness and connect it with the promises of the gospel and the evidence of grace in the believer’s life. The Westminster Confession speaks of assurance as resting on God’s promises, the inward evidence of grace, and the Spirit’s testimony. Assurance can be shaken but is grounded in God’s faithfulness.

Key contrast Both traditions teach that believers can be assured of their adoption through the Spirit’s witness. Wesley stressed a direct inward testimony of the Spirit alongside the evidence of a changed life. Reformed teaching ties the Spirit’s witness closely to God’s promises and to the marks of grace.

Romans 8:17

17and if children, then heirs—heirs of God and joint heirs with Christ, if indeed we suffer with him, that we may also be glorified with him.

Arminian

Children are heirs, heirs of God and joint heirs with Christ, “if indeed we suffer with him.” Arminian readers note the condition: sharing in glory is tied to sharing in Christ’s sufferings, and believers are called to endure.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same condition as describing the path all true heirs walk. Suffering with Christ is not a payment for the inheritance but the shape of the life that leads to it. God’s heirs will share in Christ’s glory as surely as they share his sufferings.
