Passage

Romans 8

Verses 18–30 · WEB

Paul sets present suffering against future glory. Creation groans, believers groan, and the Spirit groans in prayer, all waiting for the redemption of the body. The passage ends with two of the most debated verses in the debate over election: God works all things for good for those called according to his purpose, and those he foreknew he predestined, called, justified, and glorified. Arminian and Reformed readers share the hope of these verses and divide over the meaning of “foreknew.”

Romans 8:18

18For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which will be revealed toward us.

Arminian

Paul weighs present suffering against coming glory and finds no comparison. Arminian readers see encouragement for believers to persevere, since the glory ahead outweighs every cost.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the certainty of future glory as the ground for patient endurance. The glory “will be revealed,” and God’s purpose guarantees it.

Romans 8:19

19For the creation waits with eager expectation for the children of God to be revealed.

Arminian

Creation itself waits eagerly for the revealing of God’s children. Both traditions see the hope of the gospel extending to the whole created order.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the renewal of creation as part of God’s comprehensive plan of redemption, tied to the glorification of his people.

Romans 8:20

20For the creation was subjected to vanity, not of its own will, but because of him who subjected it, in hope

Arminian

Creation was subjected to futility, not by its own choice, but by God, in hope. Most interpreters see a reference to the curse after Adam’s sin in Genesis 3. Arminian readers note that the subjection was given “in hope,” so even the curse aims at restoration.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign hand in the curse and in the hope attached to it. The one who subjected creation to futility is also the one who will set it free.

Romans 8:21

21that the creation itself also will be delivered from the bondage of decay into the liberty of the glory of the children of God.

Arminian

Creation will be freed from its bondage to decay into the glorious freedom of God’s children. Both traditions see a renewed creation, not an escape from the material world.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that redemption reaches as far as the curse, so the new creation is this creation set free.

Romans 8:22

22For we know that the whole creation groans and travails in pain together until now.

Arminian

The whole creation groans like a woman in labor. The image suggests pain that leads to new life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the groaning as anticipation of the birth of the new creation.

Romans 8:23

23Not only so, but ourselves also, who have the first fruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for adoption, the redemption of our body.

Arminian

Believers who have the firstfruits of the Spirit also groan as they wait for the redemption of the body. Arminian readers see the Spirit as a foretaste of what is to come, and the waiting as part of the Christian life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the firstfruits as a pledge that guarantees the full harvest. The adoption already received will be completed when the body is raised.

Romans 8:24

24For we were saved in hope, but hope that is seen is not hope. For who hopes for that which he sees?

Arminian

We were saved in hope, and hope by definition looks to what is not yet seen. Arminian readers see salvation as having a future dimension that believers must hold onto in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see salvation as already secured but not yet fully revealed. Hope rests on God’s promise, not on what can be seen.

Romans 8:25

25But if we hope for that which we don’t see, we wait for it with patience.

Arminian

Hope for what is unseen produces patient waiting. Both traditions see perseverance as the natural expression of hope.

Reformed

Reformed readers see patience as the fruit of hope grounded in God’s certain purpose.

Romans 8:26

26In the same way, the Spirit also helps our weaknesses, for we don’t know how to pray as we ought. But the Spirit himself makes intercession for us with groanings which can’t be uttered.

Arminian

The Spirit helps believers in their weakness, interceding with groanings too deep for words when they do not know how to pray. Arminian readers find comfort in the Spirit’s help for weak and struggling believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit’s intercession as one of the ways God secures the salvation of his people. Even when believers cannot pray rightly, the Spirit prays for them.

Romans 8:27

27He who searches the hearts knows what is on the Spirit’s mind, because he makes intercession for the saints according to God.

Arminian

God, who searches hearts, knows the Spirit’s mind, because the Spirit intercedes according to God’s will. Both traditions see the harmony of the Father and the Spirit in the believer’s prayer life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit’s intercession as always in line with God’s purpose, so it is always effective for the saints.

Romans 8:28

28We know that all things work together for good for those who love God, for those who are called according to his purpose.

Arminian

God works all things together for good for those who love him and are called according to his purpose. Arminian readers stress that the promise is given to those who love God, and they understand the calling as God’s gracious summons in the gospel, which people respond to in faith and love.

The promise is a comfort in suffering, not a guarantee that every event is good in itself. God works within and through all things for the good of those who love him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see “called according to his purpose” as effectual calling, the calling that always results in faith because it flows from God’s eternal purpose. Calvin noted that love for God is not the cause of the calling but the evidence of it. The promise is certain because it rests on God’s purpose.

The verse is the bridge to verses 29–30, which explain the purpose behind the calling.

Key contrast Both read verse 28 as a promise to those who love God. Arminian readers understand the calling as the gospel summons that believers have answered. Reformed readers understand it as effectual calling grounded in God’s eternal purpose, which the next verses unfold.

Romans 8:29

29For whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he might be the firstborn among many brothers.

Arminian

“Whom he foreknew, he also predestined.” Arminians, following Arminius and Wesley, read foreknowledge as God’s knowing in advance who would believe. God predestines those he foreknows as believers to be conformed to the image of his Son. Predestination here concerns the destiny of believers, Christlikeness, rather than who will become a believer.

Some Arminians read the foreknowledge corporately, as God’s prior knowledge of his people as a body. Either way, they hold that faith is not caused by an unconditional decree.

Reformed

Reformed readers argue that “foreknew” means more than knowing facts in advance. In Scripture God’s knowing often means his choosing love, as in Amos 3:2 or Jeremiah 1:5. The verse says God foreknew persons (“whom”), not their faith. Those whom God set his love on beforehand he predestined to be conformed to Christ.

On this reading, foreknowledge is the root of the whole chain of salvation that follows, and it rests on God’s initiative alone.

Key contrast The verse turns on the meaning of “foreknew.” Arminian readers understand it as God’s foreknowledge of those who would believe, so that predestination follows foreseen faith. Reformed readers understand it as God’s prior choosing love for persons, so that foreknowledge is itself an act of election.

Romans 8:30

30Whom he predestined, those he also called. Whom he called, those he also justified. Whom he justified, those he also glorified.

Arminian

Paul links predestination, calling, justification, and glorification in a chain. Arminian readers see a description of God’s purpose for believers, those foreknown as believing, and note that the chain describes God’s side of salvation. They hold that other passages warn that believers must continue in faith, so the chain is not a guarantee that no one who begins can fall away.

The past tense “glorified” expresses the certainty of God’s plan for those who are his.

Reformed

Reformed readers call this the golden chain of salvation. Every link includes the same people: all whom God predestined he called, all whom he called he justified, and all whom he justified he glorified. No one drops out. The calling here must be effectual, since all who are called are justified.

The past tense “glorified” shows that the outcome is so certain it can be spoken of as already done. This verse is a central text for both unconditional election and the perseverance of the saints.

Key contrast Both see a chain that describes God’s saving work and its certainty for his people. Reformed readers see the same people at every link, which supports effectual calling and the perseverance of the saints. Arminian readers see the chain applying to those foreknown as believers and read it alongside warnings that call believers to continue in faith.
