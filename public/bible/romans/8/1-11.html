Passage

Romans 8

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

Paul announces no condemnation for those in Christ Jesus, names the Spirit’s law of life that sets free from the law of sin and death, shows God condemning sin in the flesh by sending His own Son, describes walking according to the Spirit as life and peace, and promises that the Spirit of him who raised Jesus will give life to mortal bodies.

Romans 8:1

1There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.

Arminian

“There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.” Wesley’s text keeps the walking clause: who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit. No condemnation is for those in Christ—and for those so in Him that they walk after the Spirit. Either for things present or past, he says; now Paul comes to deliverance and liberty.

Classical Arminians hear a living people, not a closed ledger. Union with Christ is real, and it is walked in. The Spirit’s life is enabling presence; those who walk after the flesh are not named in this “now.” Wesley often keeps the clause because the gospel’s “no condemnation” is not a license to remain in the flesh. Remain in Christ; walk after the Spirit.

Reformed

No condemnation is the status of those in Christ Jesus. Calvin: though the godly are still beset by sin, they are yet exempt from the power of death and from every curse. The trembling have an invincible fortress: while they abide in Christ they are beyond every danger of condemnation.

Reformed reading: this is forensic peace by the Spirit’s effectual work, not a verdict that waits to see if the walk will hold. Those in Christ are those the Spirit has joined to Him. Walking after the Spirit (v. 4) describes that people; it does not reopen the court. God has already condemned sin in the flesh (v. 3). The “now” is present tense of a finished mercy.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take no condemnation as belonging to those in Christ who walk not after the flesh but after the Spirit (Wesley often keeps the walking clause)—a status that must be walked in by the Spirit’s enabling presence. Reformed readings typically take no condemnation as the forensic status of those in Christ by the Spirit’s effectual work; walking after the Spirit is the fruit and mark of that union, not a condition that could undo the verdict.

Romans 8:2

2For the law of the Spirit of life has set you free in Christ Jesus from the law of sin and death.

Arminian

The law of the Spirit of life has set you free in Christ Jesus from the law of sin and death. Wesley: the law of the Spirit is the gospel; it has freed from the law of sin and death. Freedom is Christ’s gift, and it is freedom into a new walk.

Arminian pastoral: the Spirit’s life is enabling presence that must be walked in. “Has set you free” is real deliverance, not a slogan over an unchanged life. Those who live according to the flesh still die (v. 13). The gospel frees; the freed still walk. Do not flatten this into a liberty that never needed the Spirit’s government.

Reformed

Calvin: by the law of the Spirit he designates the Spirit of God, who sprinkles our souls with the blood of Christ, not only to cleanse us from guilt but also to sanctify us. The Spirit of Christ abolishes the law of sin in us and delivers us from the peril of death. The grace of regeneration is never disjoined from the imputation of righteousness.

Reformed comfort: this freedom is the Spirit’s effectual work in those in Christ, not an offer left to our strength. Set free means actually freed—justified and begun in newness. The same Spirit who justifies also sanctifies; no one is left in the law of sin and death whom Christ has claimed.

Romans 8:3

3For God has done what the law, weakened by the flesh, could not do. By sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and for sin, he condemned sin in the flesh,

Arminian

God has done what the law, weakened by the flesh, could not do. Wesley: the law of Moses was incapable of conquering our evil nature; if it could, God need not have sent His own Son. God sending His Son in the likeness of that flesh, though pure from sin, condemned that sin which was in our flesh—gave sentence that sin should be destroyed, and the believer wholly delivered from it.

Classical Arminians hear both pardon and power: sin sentenced, the believer delivered. The sending is for us; the deliverance is to be walked in. The law could not conquer; the Spirit can. Those who remain under the flesh’s government are not the delivered people this verse describes.

Reformed

Calvin: the Lord has by His gratuitous mercy justified us in Christ, the very thing it was impossible for the law to do. Our sins were expiated by the death of Christ. God condemned sin in the very flesh of Christ; the condemnation of sin made us free and brought us righteousness, so that God counts us as just.

Reformed accent: God condemned sin—sin did not merely meet a better example. The verdict fell in Christ’s flesh, for sin, as a sin offering. Weakness was in our flesh, not in the law’s doctrine. We flee to Christ for righteousness, for in us there can be none. This is the ground of v. 1’s “no condemnation.”

Romans 8:4

4in order that the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not according to the flesh but according to the Spirit.

Arminian

The aim: that the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not according to the flesh but according to the Spirit. Wesley: the righteousness of the law is the holiness it required; fulfilled in us who are guided in all our thoughts, words, and actions, not by corrupt nature, but by the Spirit of God.

Arminian reading: “in us, who walk” is not a gloss. The Spirit’s life is enabling presence that must be walked in. From this place, Wesley says, Paul describes primarily the state of believers. The law’s requirement is not shelved; it is fulfilled in a people actually led by the Spirit. Those who walk after the flesh are not this “us.”

Reformed

Calvin: they who understand that the renewed, by the Spirit of Christ, fulfil the law, introduce a gloss wholly alien to Paul; the faithful never make such a proficiency that the justification of the law becomes in them full or complete. This then must be applied to forgiveness: when the obedience of Christ is accepted for us, the law is satisfied, so that we are counted just. The perfection the law demands was exhibited in our flesh, that its rigor should no longer condemn us.

Reformed reading: Christ communicates His righteousness to none but those He joins to Himself by the bond of His Spirit, so renewal is mentioned lest Christ be thought the minister of sin. Walking according to the Spirit marks those so joined. The fulfillment is Christ’s for us; the walk is the Spirit’s fruit in us—not our complete keeping of the law as the ground of no condemnation.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take the righteous requirement fulfilled “in us, who walk” as the holiness the law required, actually lived by those guided by the Spirit—enabling presence that must be walked in (Wesley). Reformed readings typically take it, with Calvin, as forgiveness: Christ’s obedience accepted for us so the law is satisfied; walking according to the Spirit is the bond of those to whom He communicates that righteousness, not our complete fulfillment of the law.

Romans 8:5

5For those who live according to the flesh set their minds on the things of the flesh, but those who live according to the Spirit set their minds on the things of the Spirit.

Arminian

Two minds, two lives. Wesley: they that are after the flesh remain under the guidance of corrupt nature and mind things visible and temporal—earth, pleasure, praise, riches. They who are after the Spirit, under His guidance, think of, relish, and love things invisible and eternal—the things the Spirit has revealed, works in us, moves us to, and promises to give us.

Classical Arminians hear a real fork in the road. Living according to the flesh is not a label glued on before birth; it is remaining under corrupt nature. The Spirit’s things must be minded. Those who live according to the flesh still die. The verse calls for a walk, not a glance at a finished certificate.

Reformed

Calvin introduces the difference to confirm that the grace of Christ belongs to none but those regenerated by the Spirit who strive after purity—and to relieve the faithful, lest, conscious of many infirmities, they despair. Carnal men are those who wholly devote themselves to the world; wherever the Spirit reigns, it is evidence of the saving grace of God.

Reformed accent: two states, not two equal options in an unrenewed heart. To be after the flesh is to be without the gift of regeneration. The spiritual mind is the Spirit’s fruit in those He indwells. Infirmity in the saints is not the same as living according to the flesh. The reigning power tells the state.

Romans 8:6

6For to set the mind on the flesh is death, but to set the mind on the Spirit is life and peace.

Arminian

To set the mind on the flesh is death; to set the mind on the Spirit is life and peace. Wesley: carnally minded is the sure mark of spiritual death and the way to death everlasting; spiritually minded is a sure mark of spiritual life and the way to life everlasting, attended with the peace of God—the foretaste of life everlasting—and peace with God, opposite to the enmity of v. 7.

Arminian pastoral: life and peace are the Spirit’s, and they attend those who mind His things. The mark is present tense. Those who live according to the flesh still die. Do not treat “life and peace” as a status that can sit atop a fleshly mind. The enabling presence must be walked in.

Reformed

Calvin: they cannot be partakers of the favor of Christ who abide in the flesh, for through the whole course of their life they hasten unto death. If anything in us tends to life, it is what the Spirit produces; no spark of life proceeds from our flesh. Peace, after the Hebrew manner, is every kind of happiness. The only cause of salvation is God’s good pleasure, whereby He makes us partakers of Christ.

Reformed comfort: life and peace are the Spirit’s work in those He has claimed, not wages we earn by a better mindset. The carnal mind is death now, leading to death. The spiritual mind is evidence of saving grace. God begins and completes salvation by renewing us; the cause remains His good pleasure.

Romans 8:7

7For the mind that is set on the flesh is hostile to God, for it does not submit to God’s law; indeed, it cannot.

Arminian

The mind set on the flesh is hostile to God: it does not submit to God’s law; indeed, it cannot. Wesley: enmity against God—His existence, power, and providence. The flesh under its own government cannot please Him (v. 8).

Classical Arminians do not pretend the unrenewed mind can keep the law. They hear inability of the flesh, and then the Spirit as enabling presence: what the flesh cannot do, God has done (v. 3), and the believer must walk after the Spirit. Hostility is the flesh’s; it is not a decree that a hearer can never be enabled to turn. Those who remain carnally minded still die.

Reformed

Calvin: nothing proceeds from the flesh but death, because it contends as an enemy against the will of God. “Nor can be”: behold the power of free-will—Paul affirms it is impossible for us to render our powers subject to the law. The heart is full of hardness and indomitable contumacy; it is never moved naturally to undertake the yoke of God. Far from a Christian heart be this heathen philosophy respecting the liberty of the will.

Reformed reading: “cannot” is moral inability, not a mild reluctance. The flesh will not and cannot submit until the Spirit effectually renews. Let every one acknowledge himself the servant of sin, that he may be set at liberty by the grace of Christ. This inability is why no condemnation and new life are the Spirit’s work, not our self-rescue.

Romans 8:8

8Those who are in the flesh cannot please God.

Arminian

Those who are in the flesh cannot please God. Wesley: they who are in the flesh—under the government of it. “In the flesh” is remaining under that government, not merely having a body.

Arminian note: inability here is honest. No one pleases God while the flesh rules. The Spirit’s life is the way out, and it must be walked in. Those who live according to the flesh still die. The verse is warning as well as diagnosis: do not claim Christ’s peace while remaining under the flesh’s government. Please God by walking after the Spirit who enables.

Reformed

Calvin infers that those who give themselves up to be guided by the lusts of the flesh are abominable before God; all who walk not after the Spirit are alienated from Christ, for they are without any spiritual life. To be in the flesh is to be without the gift of regeneration.

Reformed accent: “cannot please God” is the state of the unregenerate, not a temporary mood of the elect. Pleasing God begins when the Spirit dwells and rules (v. 9). The inability is not cured by trying harder in the flesh; it is cured by the Spirit’s effectual indwelling. Those in the flesh are not the people of v. 1.

Romans 8:9

9You, however, are not in the flesh but in the Spirit, if in fact the Spirit of God dwells in you. Anyone who does not have the Spirit of Christ does not belong to him.

Arminian

You are not in the flesh but in the Spirit, if in fact the Spirit of God dwells in you. Anyone who does not have the Spirit of Christ does not belong to Him. Wesley: in the Spirit—under His government. If any man have not the Spirit of Christ—dwelling and governing in him—he is none of His: not a member of Christ; not a Christian; not in a state of salvation. A plain, express declaration, which admits of no exception. He that hath ears to hear, let him hear.

Classical Arminians hear a real “if.” Indwelling that does not govern is not the Spirit Paul means. The Spirit’s life is enabling presence that must be walked in. Without that Spirit, none of His—not a maybe-Christian, not a Christian. Belonging is not a label that survives the Spirit’s absence. Those who live according to the flesh still die.

Reformed

Calvin applies the general truth to the church: they may gather that they are of the number from whom Christ has taken away the curse. At the same time, by explaining what the Spirit works in the elect, he encourages newness of life. It is the surest mark by which the children of God are distinguished from the children of the world, when by the Spirit they are renewed unto purity and holiness. Those in whom the Spirit reigns not belong not to Christ; gratuitous remission of sins can never be separated from the Spirit of regeneration.

Reformed reading: “if in fact” is the mark of the elect, not a maybe hanging over the justified. God rules His own by His Spirit; the Spirit dwelling has the superiority though flesh remains. Those without the Spirit never belonged to Christ. Indwelling marks the people of v. 1. It is not a possession one day on and one day off.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take “if in fact the Spirit of God dwells in you” as a living condition: without the Spirit dwelling and governing, one is none of His—not a Christian, not in a state of salvation (Wesley); the Spirit’s life must be walked in. Reformed readings typically take indwelling as the surest mark of the elect: God rules them by His Spirit; those without the Spirit never belonged; belonging is not a maybe, and remission is never separated from regeneration.

Romans 8:10

10But if Christ is in you, although the body is dead because of sin, the Spirit is life because of righteousness.

Arminian

If Christ is in you, the body is dead because of sin, but the Spirit is life because of righteousness. Wesley: where the Spirit of Christ is, there is Christ. The body indeed is dead—devoted to death because of sin heretofore committed. The Spirit is life—already truly alive because of righteousness now attained.

Arminian pastoral: Christ’s indwelling is the Spirit’s; righteousness now attained is the believer’s present life. The body still heads toward death; the Spirit already lives. That life is enabling presence, not a spark that cannot be neglected. Remain in Christ; the deadness of the body is not an excuse to walk after the flesh.

Reformed

Calvin: what he said of the Spirit he now says of Christ, that the mode of Christ’s dwelling in us might be intimated: by the Spirit He consecrates us as temples and dwells in us. The children of God are counted spiritual not on the ground of full perfection, but on account of the newness of life begun in them. The power of quickening is in the Spirit of Christ, which will be effectual in swallowing up our mortality. Even one spark of the Spirit is the seed of life.

Reformed comfort: body dead because of sin does not mean the verdict of v. 1 is in doubt. The Spirit is life because of righteousness—Christ’s, counted and begun. Infirmity and mortality remain; the seed will not fail. Wait until the relics of sin are abolished. The indwelling Christ is the elect’s present tense, not a maybe.

Romans 8:11

11If the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ Jesus from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you.

Arminian

If the Spirit of Him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, He who raised Christ Jesus will also give life to your mortal bodies through His Spirit who dwells in you. The promise is for those in whom that Spirit dwells—the same “if” as v. 9.

Classical Arminians hear enabling presence that must be walked in, not an unconditional raise-regardless. Wesley has just said that without the Spirit dwelling and governing, a man is none of His. Those who live according to the flesh still die. Life to mortal bodies is the hope of those who remain in the Spirit who raised Jesus. Indwelling is present and living; it is not a stamp that outlasts a return to the flesh.

Reformed

Calvin: since by the power of God’s Spirit Christ was raised, and since the Spirit possesses eternal power, He will also exert the same with regard to us. In the person of Christ was exhibited a specimen of the power which belongs to the whole body of the Church. God raised Christ by His Spirit and has communicated that Spirit to us; He thus makes sure the hope of resurrection.

Reformed reading: the Spirit who raised Jesus will give life to those in whom He dwells. Indwelling marks the elect, not a maybe. The same power that emptied the tomb will not abandon the bodies He already inhabits. Whether heard as the certain resurrection of the saints or as that power already mortifying the relics of the flesh, it is effectual. The people the Spirit indwells are the people He will raise.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take the promise as for those in whom the Spirit presently dwells and who walk after Him—life to mortal bodies is the hope of those who remain in that enabling presence; those who live according to the flesh still die. Reformed readings typically take it as certain: the Spirit who raised Jesus will give life to those in whom He dwells; indwelling marks the elect and the raising is as sure as Christ’s, not a maybe.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on no condemnation for those in Christ and the Spirit’s law of life—God condemning sin in the flesh, walking according to the Spirit, life and peace, and the Spirit who raised Jesus giving life to mortal bodies—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Romans 8:1–11.

Arminian Notable voices

“There is therefore now no condemnation—Either for things present or past. Now he comes to deliverance and liberty. The law of the Spirit—That is, the gospel. Hath freed me from the law of sin and death. God sending his own Son, in the likeness of that flesh, though pure from sin, condemned that sin which was in our flesh; gave sentence, that sin should be destroyed, and the believer wholly delivered from it. That the righteousness of the law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit—Who are guided in all our thoughts, words, and actions, not by corrupt nature, but by the Spirit of God. If any man have not the Spirit of Christ—Dwelling and governing in him. He is none of his—He is not a member of Christ; not a Christian; not in a state of salvation. A plain, express declaration, which admits of no exception.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Rom 8:1–11)

Reformed Notable voices

“Though they were still beset by sin, they were yet exempt from the power of death, and from every curse, provided they lived not in the flesh but in the Spirit. The trembling consciences of the godly have an invincible fortress, for they know that while they abide in Christ they are beyond every danger of condemnation. The Lord has by his gratuitous mercy justified us in Christ, the very thing which it was impossible for the law to do, by condemning sin in the very flesh of Christ. Christ communicates his righteousness to none but to those whom he joins to himself by the bond of his Spirit. It is the surest mark by which the children of God are distinguished from the children of the world, when by the Spirit of God they are renewed unto purity and holiness. Those in whom the Spirit reigns not, belong not to Christ. Since by the power of God’s Spirit Christ was raised, and since the Spirit possesses eternal power, he will also exert the same with regard to us.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Romans (on Rom 8:1–11)
