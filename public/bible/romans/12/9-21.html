Passage

Romans 12

Verses 9–21 · ESV · Voices

The living sacrifice now wears a face: genuine love, honor, zeal, hope in tribulation, and open hands toward the saints. Then the hard edge—bless persecutors, refuse revenge, feed the enemy, and overcome evil with good.

Romans 12:9

9Let love be genuine. Abhor what is evil; hold fast to what is good.

Arminian

Love without a mask. Abhor evil; hold fast to good. Wesley: having spoken of faith and its fruit, he comes now to love—both inwardly and outwardly, whatever ill-will or danger may follow. Genuine love is faith wearing clothes, not a sentiment we perform for the room.

Arminian pastoral: grace enables a real sincerity. The command is not a slogan; it is the Spirit’s call to a people who must still refuse play-acting and cling to what is good. Love that can be faked is exactly what Paul rules out.

Reformed

Calvin: all disguises are to be cast aside; love is no other than that which is free from dissimulation. Evil here is the malice that injures; good is the kindness that helps. The living sacrifice of 12:1 now has a manner.

Reformed note: genuine love is the bond of a people God has made. The indicative of mercy becomes the church’s ordinary life. We do not manufacture this love from native warmth; we receive it as the fruit of a heart already claimed.

Romans 12:10

10Love one another with brotherly affection. Outdo one another in showing honor.

Arminian

Brotherly affection; outdo one another in honor. Wesley: you will prefer one another if you habitually consider what is good in others and what is evil in yourselves. Rivalry is inverted: compete at giving dignity, not at collecting it.

Arminian church: honor is a practice grace makes possible. The family of faith is commanded to feel like a family and to act like one. Neglect of honor is not a small manners problem; it is a refusal of the love just named.

Reformed

Calvin: brotherly love with the affection that belongs to kin; nothing poisons concord like the thought that one is despised. Humility is love’s best fomenter—each honoring others rather than advancing himself.

Reformed accent: the family God created honors as He has honored. Preferring the other is the ethic of those admitted by mercy. Contempt is the solvent of the body; lowliness is its preservative.

Romans 12:11

11Do not be slothful in zeal, be fervent in spirit, serve the Lord.

Arminian

Not slothful, fervent in spirit, serving the Lord. Wesley: whatsoever ye do, do it with your might; in every business diligently and fervently serving the Lord—doing all to God, not to man.

Arminian reading: zeal is commanded because it can be kindled. Grace does not bless drift; believers must shake off sloth and serve. Fervency is not a temperament some were born with; it is a holy energy the Spirit enables and we must not quench.

Reformed

Calvin: a Christian life ought to be an active life; the flesh is always torpid, and only the fervency of the Spirit corrects sloth. Though fervency is God’s gift, the faithful are told to cherish the flame kindled by heaven, for the Spirit is often suppressed through our fault.

Reformed pastoral: diligence is Spirit-wrought and still enjoined. Serving the Lord locates every task in worship. We do not wait to feel fire; we tend the fire He has given.

Romans 12:12

12Rejoice in hope, be patient in tribulation, be constant in prayer.

Arminian

Rejoice in hope, patient in tribulation, constant in prayer. Wesley: hope of perfect holiness and everlasting happiness. Faith and love now join hope. The three belong together: joy looks forward, patience bears the present, prayer keeps both alive.

Arminian note: hope is living and to be exercised. Patience and prayer are the means by which believers endure; they are not spectators of a perseverance that happens without them. Constancy in prayer is commanded because it can be kept—or let go.

Reformed

Calvin: joy from the hope of future life, not from earth and earthly things; patience grows in adversities; both require instant, persevering prayer because the warfare is continual and new conflicts arise daily. No one calmly bears the cross who has not learned to seek happiness beyond this world.

Reformed comfort: hope is anchored in what God has promised and will finish. Prayer is the means He uses to keep hearts from fainting. Perseverance is His gift, practiced in the prayers He sustains.

Romans 12:13

13Contribute to the needs of the saints and seek to show hospitality.

Arminian

Contribute to the saints’ needs; seek hospitality. Wesley: relieve all Christians that are in want; pursue hospitality—not only embracing those that offer, but seeking opportunities to exercise it. Love spends, and it goes looking for the chance.

Arminian church: the communion of saints is a living traffic of help, not a theory. Grace opens the door and the purse. To seek hospitality is to refuse a closed house; the same mercy that received us now receives the stranger through us.

Reformed

Calvin: genuine love is proved when we relieve the needy for no recompense. Hospitality is love toward strangers, often the most neglected. We communicate to the saints’ necessities as though relieving ourselves; love extends to all, yet with peculiar feeling embraces the household of faith.

Reformed accent: the saints’ needs are the body’s own. God has bound us; generosity is the fruit of that bond. The more neglected a person is by men, the more attentive we ought to be to his wants.

Romans 12:14

14Bless those who persecute you; bless and do not curse them.

Arminian

Bless persecutors; bless and do not curse. Wesley: curse not—no, not in your heart. The living sacrifice now meets hostility with spoken good. Enemies remain enemies in role, yet become objects of active goodwill—the same hard edge Jesus named in the Sermon on the Mount.

Arminian emphasis: the command is sincere and costly. Grace enables a real blessing where the heart wants to curse; believers may refuse the inner malediction and pray for those who harm them. This is not culture-war rhetoric; it is the church’s ordinary holiness toward people who actually hurt us.

Reformed

Calvin: more difficult than dropping revenge—wish and pray that God would render all things prosperous to those who harass and cruelly treat us. Arduous, and wholly opposed to the nature of man; yet nothing too arduous for the power of God, which shall never be wanting if we seek it. God restrains not only the hands but the bitter feelings within.

Reformed accent: such blessing is the Spirit’s work in those who differ from the children of this world. The cross already loved us while we were enemies (Rom 5:10); the church images that love. No one can claim the name of Christian who has not in part attained this mind and does not daily resist the opposite.

Key contrast Both treat blessing persecutors as non-negotiable. Arminians typically stress grace-assisted, responsible refusal of the curse—a sincere command that can be obeyed or inwardly betrayed; Reformed readers typically stress that this mind is the Spirit’s power in the regenerate, the fruit of a people God has already claimed.

Romans 12:15

15Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep.

Arminian

Rejoice with the joyful; weep with the weeping. Sympathy is love sharing a nerve. Wesley noted that mere laughter is not the Christian opposite of weeping. Distance is easier; membership one of another is costlier and commanded.

Arminian pastoral: grace forms a people who enter another’s joy and grief. We are not spectators of the body’s life. The same Spirit who enables blessing of enemies also enables tenderness toward friends; both are the living sacrifice in motion.

Reformed

Calvin: the faithful consider another’s condition as their own. True love prefers to weep with a brother rather than watch grief from ease. Not to regard a brother’s happiness with joy is envy; not to grieve for his misfortunes is inhumanity.

Reformed note: sympathy is the body’s proper feeling. Union with Christ makes a brother’s lot our own. We transfer to ourselves the feeling of another, whether grief in adversity or joy in prosperity.

Romans 12:16

16Live in harmony with one another. Do not be haughty, but associate with the lowly. Never be wise in your own sight.

Arminian

Harmony; not haughty; associate with the lowly; never wise in your own sight. Wesley: mind not high things—desire not riches, honor, or the company of the great. Pride is the solvent of peace.

Arminian church: grace enables lowliness with the lowly—a chosen association, not accidental proximity. Harmony is practiced humility. Being wise in our own sight is the old high-mindedness of verse 3 wearing social clothes.

Reformed

Calvin: it is not a Christian’s part to aspire ambitiously to what excels others; the chief virtue of the faithful is lowliness of mind, which prefers to give honor rather than take it. Nothing swells the mind like a high notion of one’s own wisdom; the remedy is not to be over-wise in one’s own esteem.

Reformed accent: harmony is the fruit of a people who know they were admitted by mercy. Haughtiness contradicts the gospel that leveled us at the cross. Associate with the lowly because that is where the Lord has placed His name.

Romans 12:17

17Repay no one evil for evil, but give thought to do what is honorable in the sight of all.

Arminian

Repay no one evil for evil; take thought for what is honorable before all. Wesley: provide—think beforehand; contrive to give as little offence as may be to any. Non-retaliation is a practiced holiness, not a mood after the insult has cooled.

Arminian ethic: grace enables a real alternative to payback. Reputation is stewarded so the gospel is not stained. Thinking beforehand is love’s prudence: we plan not to become the injury we received.

Reformed

Calvin: even quiet unkindness can be evil for evil—withholding help from those who withheld it. Providing good things before men is not for applause but that God may be glorified and neighbors allured to His love. Purity of conscience before God and uprightness of character before people belong together.

Reformed pastoral: the church’s honor is the Lord’s honor. Uprightness before people serves the glory of the God who already justified. We do not repay because judgment is not ours to seize.

Romans 12:18

18If possible, so far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all.

Arminian

If possible, so far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all. Peace is pursued, not pretended. The clause “if possible” is honesty, not a loophole for grudges: our side of the peace is always our responsibility.

Arminian reading: believers are to spend themselves for a quiet life with neighbors, including those outside the household of faith. Grace enables courtesy without collapsing into flattery of vice. What depends on you is not small; it is the field of obedience.

Reformed

Calvin: two cautions—do not buy esteem by refusing the world’s hatred for Christ; do not let courtesy become compliance with men’s sins. Soldiers of Christ cannot have perpetual peace with a world whose prince is Satan. Yet we bear much, pardon offenses, and remit the full rigor of the law for the sake of peace.

Reformed note: peaceableness is real gift and real limit. We will not trade the gospel to keep peace, and we will not use the gospel as cover for a quarrelsome spirit. Piety and love tell us when peace must be broken; they also tell us how much we ought to bear first.

Romans 12:19

19Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God, for it is written, “Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord.”

Arminian

Beloved—Paul softens a rugged spirit. Never avenge yourselves; leave room for God’s wrath. Vengeance is the Lord’s. Wesley: revenge not yourselves, but leave that to God, to whom vengeance properly belongs.

Arminian pastoral: the command is personal and possible by grace. Refusing revenge is not passivity about evil; it is trust that God judges, and a refusal to seize His office. “Beloved” is not flattery; it is how a wounded people are called back from the cliff.

Reformed

Calvin: inordinate self-love begets the passion for revenge. Giving place to wrath is committing judgment to the Lord; those who anticipate Him leave no place for His help. Hearts, not only hands, must be free of the desire. We wait with quiet minds, praying that adversaries may by repentance become friends.

Reformed comfort: God is the church’s defender. Patience is not exposure to the wicked; it is room for the only Judge. He does not in vain take upon Himself the office of executing vengeance.

Romans 12:20

20To the contrary, “if your enemy is hungry, feed him; if he is thirsty, give him something to drink; for by so doing you will heap burning coals on his head.”

Arminian

If the enemy hungers, feed him; if thirsty, give drink. Wesley: with your own hand—if needful, even put bread into his mouth. Coals of fire: kindness that melts, as the artisan heaps coals to make the metal glow. Enemy-love is a meal, a cup, a hand—not a slogan.

Arminian hope: grace enables concrete good toward the person who harmed us. The aim is to overcome, even to win the enemy, by good. This is the same holy love that refuses a closed circle in the Sermon on the Mount—pastoral, costly, and near at hand.

Reformed

Calvin: not only abstain from injury; do good to those who wronged you. Under meat and drink, all acts of kindness. Coals: the enemy is either softened by benefits, or if so savage that nothing can tame him, burnt and tormented by the testimony of his own conscience when overwhelmed with our kindness.

Reformed accent: this is the gospel’s manner in those united to Christ. Kindness is not naivete; it is the Lord’s strategy for His people. We help the enemy according to the flesh, and much less may we oppose his salvation by imprecating vengeance.

Romans 12:21

21Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good.

Arminian

Do not be overcome by evil; overcome evil with good. Wesley: if you see no present fruit, yet persevere. All who avenge themselves are already overcome. Conquer enemies by kindness and patience. The victory is the long obedience of the living sacrifice, not a single dramatic gesture.

Arminian urgency: the command is sincere because it can be lived by grace. Evil overcomes us when we copy it; good overcomes when we keep presenting ourselves toward the enemy. Perseverance here is not automatic—it is the enabled, responsible refusal to let injury set the terms.

Reformed

Calvin: to retaliate is to confess we are overcome; to return good for evil shows the invincible firmness of our mind. A most glorious kind of victory, while the Lord gives success to their patience. He who attempts to overcome evil with evil may surpass his enemy in injury, but it is to his own ruin—he carries on war for the devil.

Reformed pastoral: this overcoming is the Spirit’s fruit in a people God will not abandon. Perseverance in good is how the called endure—and how evil is actually met. The victory is given, not seized; patience is the field in which the Lord succeeds.

Key contrast Both refuse retaliation as defeat. Arminians typically stress a sincere, grace-enabled perseverance in good that believers must not abandon; Reformed readers typically stress that this victory is the Lord’s work in the regenerate, patience He sustains to the end.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on genuine love and blessing those who persecute—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Romans 12:9–21.

Arminian Notable voices

“Having spoken of faith and its fruit, he comes now to love. Abhor that which is evil; cleave to that which is good—both inwardly and outwardly, whatever ill-will or danger may follow. Curse not—no, not in your heart. Feed him—with your own hand: if it be needful, even put bread into his mouth. And if you see no present fruit, yet persevere. Be not overcome with evil—as all are who avenge themselves. But overcome evil with good. Conquer your enemies by kindness and patience.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Rom 12:9, 14, 20–21)

Reformed Notable voices

“He requires something even more difficult—that we are not to imprecate evils on our enemies, but to wish and to pray God to render all things prosperous to them, how much soever they may harass and cruelly treat us. Arduous is this, I admit, and wholly opposed to the nature of man; but there is nothing too arduous to be overcome by the power of God, which shall never be wanting to us, provided we neglect not to seek for it. If we return good for evil, by that very deed we show the invincible firmness of our mind. This is truly a most glorious kind of victory.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Romans (on Rom 12:14, 21)
