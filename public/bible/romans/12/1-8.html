Passage

Romans 12

Verses 1–8 · ESV · Voices

The long argument of chapters 1–11 turns into worship. Paul appeals by the mercies of God already displayed: present your bodies as a living sacrifice, refuse the world’s mold, and take your place in one body whose gifts differ by grace.

Romans 12:1

1I appeal to you therefore, brothers, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and acceptable to God, which is your spiritual worship.

Arminian

“Therefore” rests on mercy already preached, yet the appeal is real: present your bodies. Wesley heard the whole economy of grace delivering from wrath and exciting to duty—mercy inferred as one’s own offering. The sacrifice is living, holy, acceptable: the whole self actually exhibited before God, not a dead animal and not a private idea of devotion.

Classical Arminians read this as enabled consecration that must still be presented. Prevenient and cooperating grace make the offering possible; the worshiper is not a spectator of a work God performs without him. Spiritual worship is the reasonable answer of those who, having received mercy, yield themselves. Holiness is response—and the response is sincerely required.

Reformed

Mercies (chs. 1–11) are not a footnote; they are the engine. Calvin: we are redeemed for this end—that we may consecrate ourselves. The living sacrifice is what mercy produces in a people God has made His own. Former life destroyed, new life raised: the offering is God’s work in those He has claimed. We are not our own; we have passed over to become His property.

Reformed pastoral weight: holiness is response, not a ladder into mercy. God works the presentation He commands. The church’s worship is the fruit of election, calling, and justification already set forth. “Therefore” is ethics from prior grace, not a new bargain.

Key contrast Both treat the living sacrifice as holy response to mercy. Arminians typically stress enabled consecration that must actually be presented—grace that can be yielded to or neglected. Reformed readers typically stress that the mercies of chapters 1–11 produce the offering God Himself works in His people.

Romans 12:2

2Do not be conformed to this world, but be transformed by the renewal of your mind, that by testing you may discern what is the will of God, what is good and acceptable and perfect.

Arminian

Two voices: do not be conformed; be transformed. Wesley: neither in judgment, spirit, nor behaviour; this world neglects God’s will and follows its own. Renewal of mind is grace at work in the believer who must still refuse the age’s mold. Discernment of the good, acceptable, and perfect will is the fruit of a self already presented—known by sure trial.

Arminian hope: transformation is real and responsible. The mind can be renewed; the will of God can be proved by those who cooperate with the Spirit rather than sliding back into the world’s pattern. The command is sincere: conformity is not fate, and renewal is not a spectator sport.

Reformed

Conformity to this world is the old man; transformation is newness of mind. Calvin: renovation is not of the flesh only but of the mind, the leading power—Paul pulls reason down from her throne. We prove God’s will by bidding adieu to our own counsels. The world invents “good”; God defines it in His commandments. Nothing pleases Him except what He has commanded.

Reformed accent: the mind is renewed because God regenerates. Discernment is gift before it is skill—the Spirit’s effectual work in those already made alive. The will of God is good, acceptable, and perfect because it is His, not because we have improved it.

Key contrast Both refuse worldly conformity and seek a renewed mind. Arminians typically describe transformation as grace-enabled yet resistible renewal the believer must pursue; Reformed readers typically describe it as the Spirit’s effectual renovation of minds God has already claimed.

Romans 12:3

3For by the grace given to me I say to everyone among you not to think of himself more highly than he ought to think, but to think with sober judgment, each according to the measure of faith that God has assigned.

Arminian

Grace given to Paul grounds a word to everyone: do not think more highly than you ought. Sober judgment matches the measure of faith God has assigned. Wesley: the measure of faith treated of in the opening chapters, from which all other gifts and graces flow. Pride is the first enemy of the body’s peace.

Arminian reading: the measure is God’s gift among those who believe. Assignment does not cancel responsible humility; it summons it. Each is to think and serve within what grace has actually given. High thoughts of self are a refusal of the grace that made room for every member.

Reformed

Paul speaks through the grace of apostleship, not self-made stature. God has distributed the measure of faith; sober thinking stays inside that allotment. Calvin: it is part of our reasonable sacrifice to surrender ourselves, meek and teachable, to be ruled by God; the faithful humbly keep themselves within the limits allotted to them.

Reformed note: assignment is the Lord’s. High thoughts of self trespass on God’s measuring. Humility is sanity about a grace we did not invent. The body will be safe when no one exceeds the portion God was pleased to give.

Key contrast Both credit God with measuring faith and gifts. Arminians typically hear a gracious assignment to believers who must still walk humbly in what they have received; Reformed readers typically hear God’s sovereign distribution that both limits and enables each member.

Romans 12:4

4For as in one body we have many members, and the members do not all have the same function,

Arminian

One body, many members, different functions. Variety is not a problem to solve; it is how a body works. No member is the whole, and no function is surplus.

Arminian church life: differing functions are gifts to be used, not ranks to defend. Grace that joined us to Christ also joins us to one another’s need. The body’s health depends on members actually serving in the function given, not coveting another’s.

Reformed

The body simile confirms vocation. Calvin: Christ has ordained a fellowship like the members of the human body, and He Himself is the bond of the connection. Functions differ because God distributed them.

Reformed accent: no member possesses all powers, nor may it seize another’s office. God has determined the order He would have observed among us. Comparison is a misreading of the Giver.

Romans 12:5

5so we, though many, are one body in Christ, and individually members one of another.

Arminian

Many, yet one body in Christ, members one of another. Wesley: all believers, closely connected together in Christ, and consequently ought to be helpful to each other. Belonging is mutual: we do not float as private Christians.

Arminian pastoral: oneness in Christ is real for all who are in Him by faith. Membership one of another is a duty of help, not a metaphor to admire. The connection can be honored or neglected; grace enables the help it requires.

Reformed

Union with Christ is the body’s life; membership one of another is its shape. God has made a people, not a pile of individuals. The many are one because they are in Christ.

Reformed comfort: helpfulness is the ethic of a union God established. We do not manufacture the body; we occupy the place we have been given in it, for the common good of what He has joined.

Romans 12:6

6Having gifts that differ according to the grace given to us, let us use them: if prophecy, in proportion to our faith;

Arminian

Gifts differ according to the grace given; use them. Wesley: gifts are various, grace is one. Prophecy here is chiefly the ordinary gift of expounding Scripture, according to the analogy of faith—the grand scheme of original sin, justification by faith, and present inward salvation.

Arminian encouragement: grace given is to be exercised. The Giver enables; the member must prophesy, serve, and speak in proportion to faith received, not bury the gift. Differing gifts are not a ranking of worth but a call to spend what has been placed in our hands.

Reformed

Calvin: no one is furnished with fullness of gifts, lest he despise his brethren. Difference of gifts proceeds not from the will of man but because it has pleased the Lord to distribute His grace in this manner. Prophecy is tested by the analogy of faith, the first principles of religion.

Reformed accent: use is obedience inside a sovereign distribution. The church is safe when each imparts what he received without trespassing on another’s function. To invert the order is to fight with God.

Romans 12:7

7if service, in our serving; the one who teaches, in his teaching;

Arminian

Service in serving; the teacher in teaching. Offices are for the work named, not for display. Wesley: ministering as deacons; teachers of catechumens, for whom particular instructors were appointed.

Arminian note: grace makes the task possible and required. Stay in the work given; do it fully. The body is helped when gifts are actually spent on the people they were given for, not held as titles.

Reformed

Let the minister fulfill the office by ministering faithfully, that he may answer to his name. Calvin: the teacher studies this one thing—that the church may be really instructed; he who excels in teaching knows the end is that the church may be built by the word of truth.

Reformed pastoral: function is stewardship. God assigned the post; faithfulness in it is the calling, not a search for a more impressive gift. The name of the office is a summons to the work.

Romans 12:8

8the one who exhorts, in his exhortation; the one who contributes, in generosity; the one who leads, with zeal; the one who does acts of mercy, with cheerfulness.

Arminian

Exhortation, generosity, leading with zeal, mercy with cheerfulness. The list ends in tone: open-handed, eager, glad. Wesley: the one who presides has the care of a flock; the one who shows mercy, in any instance, rejoices that he has such an opportunity.

Arminian church: these are graces to be practiced. Cheerful mercy is commanded because grace can form it. Leading with zeal is love of the flock, not love of rank. The exhortation is sincere—the Spirit enables a real gladness in the work of help.

Reformed

Calvin: givers in simplicity, without fraud or respect of persons; those who show mercy without peevishness, lest they mar the favor conferred; presidents watching day and night for the flock’s wellbeing. Cheerfulness is part of the gift’s proper use: the distressed are consoled by prompt, glad help and wounded by a grim face.

Reformed note: God distributes not only the office but the manner. Zeal and cheerfulness are the Spirit’s way of keeping the body’s care from becoming grim duty. The same Lord who assigned the gift assigns its tone.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on the living sacrifice and the one body whose gifts differ by grace—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Romans 12:1–8.

Arminian Notable voices

“I exhort you—St. Paul uses to suit his exhortations to the doctrines he has been delivering. So here the general use from the whole is contained in the first and second verses. By the tender mercies of God—the entire gospel, the whole economy of grace or mercy, delivering us from the wrath of God, and exciting us to all duty. To present—now actually to exhibit before God. Your bodies—that is, yourselves. A sacrifice—dead to sin and living. A Christian acts in all things by the highest reason, from the mercy of God inferring his own duty.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Rom 12:1)

Reformed Notable voices

“Paul lays down here the principle from which all the duties of holiness flow, even this—that we are redeemed by the Lord for this end—that we may consecrate to him ourselves and all our members. It is then the beginning of a right course in good works, when we understand that we are consecrated to the Lord; for it hence follows that we must cease to live to ourselves, in order that we may devote all the actions of our life to his service. By calling it living, he intimates that we are sacrificed to the Lord for this end—that our former life being destroyed in us, we may be raised up to a new life.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Romans (on Rom 12:1)
