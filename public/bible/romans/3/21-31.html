Passage

Romans 3

Verses 21–31 · WEB

After showing that Jews and Gentiles alike are under sin (1:18–3:20), Paul announces the good news: God’s righteousness has been revealed apart from the law, through faith in Jesus Christ, for all who believe. Sinners are justified freely by grace through the redemption in Christ, whom God set forth as an atoning sacrifice. Boasting is excluded, and God is shown to be both just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus. This passage is shared ground for Protestant theology, with differences in how the two traditions describe the atonement and faith.

Romans 3:21

21But now apart from the law, a righteousness of God has been revealed, being testified by the law and the prophets;

Arminian

“But now” marks the turning point of Romans. God’s righteousness has been revealed apart from the law, though the law and prophets bear witness to it. Arminian readers, with Wesley, see God’s way of justifying sinners through faith in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the righteousness of God revealed as the gift of a right standing before him, given apart from works of the law. The Old Testament itself pointed to it.

Romans 3:22

22even the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ to all and on all those who believe. For there is no distinction,

Arminian

This righteousness comes through faith in Jesus Christ to all who believe, without distinction. Arminian readers stress the universal scope: the same way of salvation is open to every person.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that righteousness is received through faith, not earned. The lack of distinction means Jews and Gentiles are saved the same way.

Romans 3:23

23for all have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God;

Arminian

All have sinned and fall short of God’s glory. Both traditions affirm universal sinfulness and the need of grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see here the doctrine of total depravity: no one is righteous before God apart from grace.

Romans 3:24

24being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus,

Arminian

Sinners are justified freely by God’s grace through the redemption in Christ Jesus. Arminian readers see justification as God’s gracious pardon, received by faith. Wesley defined justification chiefly as forgiveness of sins.

Reformed

Reformed readers see justification as God’s legal declaration that the sinner is righteous, based on the imputed righteousness of Christ. It is entirely free, a gift of grace.

Romans 3:25

25whom God sent to be an atoning sacrifice through faith in his blood, for a demonstration of his righteousness through the passing over of prior sins, in God’s forbearance;

Arminian

God set forth Christ as an atoning sacrifice through faith in his blood, to show his righteousness in passing over earlier sins. Arminian readers often emphasize that the sacrifice demonstrates God’s justice and is provided for all, to be received through faith. Some Arminians, following Grotius, describe the atonement as upholding God’s moral government; Wesley held to penal substitution.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the atoning sacrifice (propitiation) as Christ bearing God’s wrath in place of sinners. God’s justice is satisfied, so he can forgive without compromising his righteousness.

Romans 3:26

26to demonstrate his righteousness at this present time, that he might himself be just and the justifier of him who has faith in Jesus.

Arminian

God is both just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus. Arminian readers see God’s justice and mercy meeting at the cross, offered to all who believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the cross resolving the tension between justice and mercy. God remains just because Christ paid the penalty, and he justifies those who believe.

Romans 3:27

27Where then is the boasting? It is excluded. By what kind of law? Of works? No, but by a law of faith.

Arminian

Boasting is excluded by the law of faith, not by works. Both traditions agree that faith leaves no room for pride. Arminian readers stress that faith is not a work and therefore cannot be a ground of boasting, even though it is a real human response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see boasting excluded because salvation, including the faith that receives it, is entirely God’s gift.

Key contrast Both traditions agree that justification by faith excludes boasting. Arminian readers argue that faith, though a human response enabled by grace, is not a work and earns nothing. Reformed readers argue that boasting is excluded most fully when faith itself is understood as God’s gift.

Romans 3:28

28We maintain therefore that a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the law.

Arminian

A person is justified by faith apart from works of the law. Both traditions affirm this as the heart of the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Luther added the word “alone” in translation to capture the sense, and Protestants have confessed justification by faith alone.

Romans 3:29

29Or is God the God of Jews only? Isn’t he the God of Gentiles also? Yes, of Gentiles also,

Arminian

God is the God of Gentiles as well as Jews. Arminian readers see the universal scope of God’s saving purpose.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s purpose to save a people from every nation.

Romans 3:30

30since indeed there is one God who will justify the circumcised by faith and the uncircumcised through faith.

Arminian

Since God is one, he justifies Jews and Gentiles by the same faith. Both traditions agree.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the oneness of God as the ground for the one way of salvation.

Romans 3:31

31Do we then nullify the law through faith? May it never be! No, we establish the law.

Arminian

Faith does not overthrow the law; it establishes it. Wesley wrote a sermon on this verse, arguing that faith establishes the law by producing holy living.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faith establishing the law because Christ fulfilled its demands and because believers are renewed to keep it as a rule of life.
