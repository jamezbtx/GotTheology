Passage

Romans 3

Verses 1–8 · WEB

If Jews and Gentiles alike stand under judgment, what advantage does the Jew have? Paul answers that Israel was entrusted with the oracles of God, and that human unfaithfulness cannot cancel God’s faithfulness. He then rejects the twisted logic that human sin should be excused because it highlights God’s righteousness. Both traditions read this as a defense of God’s faithfulness and justice, with few points of disagreement.

Romans 3:1

1Then what advantage does the Jew have? Or what is the profit of circumcision?

Arminian

Paul asks what advantage the Jew has if all stand under judgment. Arminian readers see him guarding against the idea that God’s dealings with Israel were arbitrary. Israel’s privileges were real gifts, even if they did not guarantee salvation to every individual.

Reformed

Reformed readers see covenant privilege raised and then qualified. Belonging to the visible covenant people is a real blessing, but it is distinct from the inward grace that makes someone a true child of God.

Romans 3:2

2Much in every way! Because first of all, they were entrusted with the revelations of God.

Arminian

Much in every way; they were entrusted with the oracles of God. Arminian readers draw from this that God’s gifts of revelation create real responsibility. Those who receive more light are called to respond to it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Israel’s role as steward of revelation. God entrusted his word to a particular people in order to bring it to the nations, which Reformed theology reads as part of the unfolding covenant of grace.

Romans 3:3

3For what if some were without faith? Will their lack of faith nullify the faithfulness of God?

Arminian

Does their unbelief nullify God’s faithfulness? Arminian readers see God remaining faithful to his promises even when some individuals reject them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s faithfulness to his covenant purpose, which human unbelief cannot overturn. Paul develops the same point in Romans 9–11, where a remnant chosen by grace shows that God’s word has not failed.

Romans 3:4

4May it never be! Yes, let God be found true, but every man a liar. As it is written, “that you might be justified in your words, and might prevail when you come into judgment.”

Arminian

“Let God be found true, but every man a liar.” Arminian readers draw from this that God’s promises and warnings mean exactly what they say. His truthfulness guarantees that his offer of salvation to all is sincere.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God vindicated in his judgments. Quoting Psalm 51, Paul shows that even David’s confession of sin served to show that God is right when he judges.

Romans 3:5

5But if our unrighteousness commends the righteousness of God, what will we say? Is God unrighteous who inflicts wrath? I speak like men do.

Arminian

If our unrighteousness shows God’s righteousness, is God unjust to inflict wrath? Both traditions see Paul answering a false inference.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s right to judge even though he uses human sin to display his glory. Divine sovereignty over sin never makes God the author of sin or removes human guilt.

Romans 3:6

6May it never be! For then how will God judge the world?

Arminian

By no means, for then how could God judge the world? Arminian readers draw from this that God’s justice is the fixed point of Paul’s theology. Any doctrine that makes God seem unjust must be rejected, which is one reason Arminians resist unconditional reprobation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s justice as beyond question. Paul does not defend it by argument; he simply points out that the judge of all the earth must be just, or there could be no judgment at all.

Romans 3:7

7For if the truth of God through my lie abounded to his glory, why am I also still judged as a sinner?

Arminian

If my lie increases God’s glory, why am I still condemned? Arminian readers see Paul rejecting any idea that sin serves a necessary purpose. People remain fully responsible because their choices are their own.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that God’s sovereign use of sin does not excuse the sinner. God can bring good out of evil, as at the cross, while the evil remains truly evil and truly judged.

Romans 3:8

8Why not (as we are slanderously reported, and as some affirm that we say), “Let’s do evil, that good may come?” Those who say so are justly condemned.

Arminian

“Let’s do evil, that good may come”? Their condemnation is just. Arminian readers see Paul refusing to let grace become an excuse for sin. They connect this with Wesley’s concern that free grace must always produce holy living.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul was accused of teaching this, which shows how freely he preached grace. A gospel that never invites this misunderstanding, they argue, may not be preaching grace fully.
