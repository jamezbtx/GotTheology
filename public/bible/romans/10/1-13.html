Passage

Romans 10

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Paul turns from God’s choosing to Israel’s response. He prays for Israel’s salvation and grieves that their zeal for God is not based on knowledge: they sought their own righteousness instead of submitting to God’s. Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes, and the word of faith is near: whoever confesses Jesus as Lord and believes God raised him will be saved. Both traditions hear a universal offer here. Arminian readers see it as decisive for reading Romans 9; Reformed readers see it as the means through which God’s election is carried out.

Romans 10:1

1Brothers, my heart’s desire and my prayer to God is for Israel, that they may be saved.

Arminian

Paul’s heart’s desire and prayer is that Israel may be saved. Arminian readers note that he prays this right after Romans 9, which suggests he did not regard Israel’s unbelief as fixed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that belief in election did not stop Paul from praying for the lost. Prayer is one of the means God uses to save his people.

Romans 10:2

2For I testify about them that they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge.

Arminian

Israel has zeal for God, but not according to knowledge. Arminian readers see sincere religion that misses the way God has provided.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that zeal is not enough. Without the knowledge of God’s righteousness in Christ, religious effort leads away from him.

Romans 10:3

3For being ignorant of God’s righteousness, and seeking to establish their own righteousness, they didn’t subject themselves to the righteousness of God.

Arminian

Not knowing God’s righteousness, Israel tried to establish its own and did not submit to God’s. Arminian readers see Israel’s failure explained as a refusal to submit, a responsible choice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the human tendency to seek righteousness by works, which only grace overcomes.

Romans 10:4

4For Christ is the fulfillment of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes.

Arminian

Christ is the end, or fulfillment, of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes. Arminian readers stress “everyone who believes”: righteousness is offered on the same terms to all.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as the goal of the law, fulfilling its demands so that his righteousness is credited to believers.

Romans 10:5

5For Moses writes about the righteousness of the law, “The one who does them will live by them.”

Arminian

Moses describes the righteousness of the law: the one who does these things will live by them. Arminian readers see the contrast between earning life and receiving it by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the principle of the covenant of works, which no sinner can fulfill.

Romans 10:6

6But the righteousness which is of faith says this, “Don’t say in your heart, ‘Who will ascend into heaven?’ (that is, to bring Christ down);

Arminian

The righteousness of faith does not demand that someone ascend to heaven to bring Christ down. Both traditions see that faith receives what God has already done.

Reformed

Reformed readers see salvation accomplished by Christ, so the sinner has nothing to achieve.

Romans 10:7

7or, ‘Who will descend into the abyss?’ (that is, to bring Christ up from the dead.)”

Arminian

No one needs to descend into the abyss to bring Christ up from the dead. Christ has already been raised.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the finished work of Christ as the ground of faith.

Romans 10:8

8But what does it say? “The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart;” that is, the word of faith which we preach:

Arminian

The word is near, in your mouth and in your heart: the word of faith that Paul preaches. Arminian readers see salvation brought within reach of every hearer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gospel coming near through preaching, the ordinary means by which God calls his people.

Romans 10:9

9that if you will confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved.

Arminian

If you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him, you will be saved. Arminian readers see a clear and simple condition available to everyone who hears.

The verse shows that salvation comes through a personal response to the gospel, which grace makes possible.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the same promise and preach it to all. They add that the faith and confession described here come from a heart God has renewed. The promise is sincere, and those who believe will be saved.

Key contrast Both read verse 9 as a sincere promise to everyone who believes and confesses. Arminian readers see a condition that grace enables every hearer to meet. Reformed readers see faith and confession as the fruit of God’s renewing work in the heart.

Romans 10:10

10For with the heart one believes resulting in righteousness; and with the mouth confession is made resulting in salvation.

Arminian

With the heart one believes unto righteousness, and with the mouth one confesses unto salvation. Arminian readers see inner faith and outward confession joined.

Reformed

Reformed readers see justification received by heart faith and expressed in public confession.

Romans 10:11

11For the Scripture says, “Whoever believes in him will not be disappointed.”

Arminian

“Whoever believes in him will not be disappointed.” Arminian readers stress “whoever,” which leaves no one outside the offer.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the universal promise and see it fulfilled in all who believe.

Romans 10:12

12For there is no distinction between Jew and Greek; for the same Lord is Lord of all, and is rich to all who call on him.

Arminian

There is no distinction between Jew and Greek; the same Lord is rich to all who call on him. Arminian readers see God’s generosity toward all people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see one Lord gathering a people from every nation.

Romans 10:13

13For, “Whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved.”

Arminian

“Whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved.” Arminian readers see this as one of the plainest statements of the universal offer, coming right after Romans 9. They read it as confirming that God’s mercy is available to all who call.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm that whoever calls will be saved and that the gospel must be offered to all. They see verses 14–17 as explaining how people come to call: through preaching that produces faith, which God gives.

Key contrast Both affirm that whoever calls on the Lord will be saved. Arminian readers see this as a decisive balance to Romans 9. Reformed readers see it as the way God’s election is carried out, since those who call do so through the faith God gives.
