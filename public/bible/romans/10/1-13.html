Passage

Romans 10

Verses 1–13 · ESV · Voices

Paul’s heart’s desire and prayer to God for Israel is that they may be saved; they have zeal without knowledge, seeking to establish their own righteousness; Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes; the word is near in mouth and heart; confess Jesus as Lord, believe God raised him, and everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved.

Romans 10:1

1Brothers, my heart’s desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved.

Arminian

Wesley: my prayer to God is, that they may be saved—He would not have prayed for this, had they been absolutely reprobated. The “them” are Israel from chapter 9. Heart’s desire and prayer are not theatre: Paul asks God to save a people he still treats as savable.

Classical Arminians take the prayer as evidence against unconditional reprobation. You do not plead for those God has already, from eternity, shut out of mercy. The same apostle who wept in 9:1–3 now prays; the will of God, Wesley will say at v. 13, is that all should savingly call upon him. Prayer here is sincere means toward a real salvation.

Reformed

Paul’s heart’s desire and prayer is that they may be saved. The grief of 9:1–3 has become petition. Prayer for Israel does not retract the purpose of election already named; it is how God has appointed his servants to seek a people.

Reformed reading: Calvin will later treat calling on the Lord as the appointed means, not a proof that every Israelite is elect. Paul can pray for kinsmen, as Moses did, while still knowing that not all who are of Israel are Israel (9:6). Desire is real; so is a remnant. Prayer and particular election stand together.

Romans 10:2

2For I bear them witness that they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge.

Arminian

He bears them witness: the zeal is real. It is for God. What it lacks is knowledge. Israel is not charged with apathy but with heat aimed at the wrong righteousness (v. 3).

Classical Arminians hear culpable ignorance, not a hidden inability assigned from eternity. They could have known—9:32 has already said they did not pursue by faith. Zeal without knowledge is their miss, not a decree that made knowledge impossible. The next verse will name the ignorance: they would not submit.

Reformed

Calvin’s line through this chapter keeps Israel’s zeal in view: they labored, and they missed. Zeal for God without knowledge is not saving; it can be the energy of unbelief.

Reformed accent: the witness is generous and severe. They are not atheists; they are ignorant of the righteousness God gives in Christ (vv. 3–4). Knowledge here is not mere information. A remnant will obtain; the nation’s zeal does not. Chapter 9 already said they stumbled at the stone. This verse names the heat with which they ran the wrong way.

Romans 10:3

3For, being ignorant of the righteousness of God, and seeking to establish their own, they did not submit to God’s righteousness.

Arminian

The ignorance is specified: they did not know God’s righteousness, and they sought to establish their own. They did not submit. Wesley’s whole chapter treats that refusal as theirs—not a fate that made submitting impossible.

Arminian hinge: “they did not submit” is responsible unbelief, matching 9:32’s “because they did not pursue it by faith.” Seeking their own righteousness is a real alternative they chose. God’s righteousness was there to be submitted to. The fault is pride of works, not a reprobating decree that withheld the very righteousness they refused.

Reformed

They were ignorant of the righteousness of God and sought to establish their own; they did not submit to God’s righteousness. Calvin: we must not seek righteousness from the law once Christ has fulfilled it. Own-righteousness is the enemy of faith.

Reformed reading: the non-submission is real wickedness, and it sits under God’s purpose. Those who trust their works abolish the faith by which righteousness comes. Verse 4 will name Christ as the end of the law for everyone who believes—the appointed means, not a ladder Israel was never allowed to touch. Unbelief explains the miss; election explains who believes.

Romans 10:4

4For Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes.

Arminian

Wesley: Christ is the end of the law—the scope and aim of it. To every one that believeth—whether Jew or Gentile. Do not skip the “everyone.” The law pointed at Christ; righteousness is for whoever believes. The telos is not a closed list but the living Christ received by faith.

Classical Arminians take “everyone who believes” as the true width of the gospel. Jew and Gentile meet at the same believing. The law’s aim is Christ; its aim is not destroyed, and it is not restricted to an elect subset of hearers. Whoever believes is the people for whom this end was meant.

Reformed

Calvin: Christ is the end of the law for righteousness. The law is not destroyed by Christ but fulfilled, so that we must not seek righteousness from the law. Faith is the appointed means. “Everyone who believes” is a true gospel address: this is how God justifies.

Reformed accent: do not skip the everyone, and do not make it every person elect. The end of the law is Christ; righteousness is not from doing (v. 5) but from believing. Those who believe are the people God is saving. The whoever names the instrument, not a denial that faith itself is God’s gift to those He calls.

Key contrast Arminian readings, with Wesley, take Christ as the law’s scope and aim, and “everyone who believes” as the genuine width of righteousness—Jew or Gentile, whoever believes. Reformed readings, with Calvin, take the same end as fulfillment so that we must not seek righteousness from the law: “everyone who believes” is the true gospel address and appointed means, not proof that every hearer is elect.

Romans 10:5

5For Moses writes about the righteousness that is based on the law, that the person who does the commandments shall live by them.

Arminian

Moses writes a real sentence: the person who does the commandments shall live by them. That is law-righteousness—do and live. Paul sets it beside the righteousness based on faith (v. 6), not to mock Moses but to show Israel the path they chose and could not finish.

Arminian pastoral: the law’s “do and live” was never the gospel of v. 9. Israel sought to establish their own (v. 3) along this line. The contrast is two righteousnesses, both sincerely set forth. Faith-righteousness is near (v. 8); law-righteousness demands a doing they have not done. The miss is theirs.

Reformed

Moses on law-righteousness: do the commandments and live by them. Calvin’s point stands: we must not seek righteousness from the law, because Christ is its end. The doing-clause is true as law and fatal as a way of justification.

Reformed reading: Paul lets Moses speak so that works-righteousness is named from Scripture itself. Those who run this road, as Israel did (9:31–32), do not arrive. Life by doing is not the life God gives in the risen Christ (vv. 6–9). The law is fulfilled in Him; it is not a second gospel.

Romans 10:6

6But the righteousness based on faith says, “Do not say in your heart, ‘Who will ascend into heaven?’” (that is, to bring Christ down)

Arminian

Faith-righteousness has a voice: do not say in your heart, Who will ascend into heaven?—that is, to bring Christ down. The quest upward is forbidden because Christ has already come. The word will be near (v. 8), not stranded above the clouds.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere nearness. You need not fetch the Son; He has come. Unbelief is not lack of a ladder; it is refusal of a Christ already given. The aside “to bring Christ down” kills the excuse that salvation is too remote to take. It is as near as the incarnate Lord.

Reformed

The righteousness of faith forbids the heart’s question: who will ascend to bring Christ down? God has already sent the Son. Calvin’s means-language fits: we do not accomplish incarnation; we receive what God has done.

Reformed accent: faith does not climb. The parenthetical “that is, to bring Christ down” is Paul’s gloss on Deuteronomy: what Israel might treat as distance, God has closed in Christ. The righteousness He gives is His act before it is our confession (v. 9). Do not turn the near Christ into a human ascent.

Romans 10:7

7“or ‘Who will descend into the abyss?’” (that is, to bring Christ up from the dead).

Arminian

Nor say, Who will descend into the abyss?—that is, to bring Christ up from the dead. The resurrection is not a task left to the seeker. He is already raised. Faith does not fish the Lord out of death; it believes God has done it (v. 9).

Arminian note: both forbidden questions treat salvation as a human expedition. Paul shuts the expedition down. Christ has come down; Christ has come up. What remains is the near word (v. 8) that may be believed or refused. The abyss is not waiting for your descent; the risen Jesus is waiting for your confession.

Reformed

Who will descend into the abyss to bring Christ up from the dead? No one. The resurrection is God’s already-finished act. Faith will soon be defined as believing that God raised Him (v. 9).

Reformed reading: the two questions (vv. 6–7) strip merit from the believer. You neither incarnate nor raise the Son. Calvin: faith is the appointed means of a righteousness God has provided in the finished work of Christ. The abyss-question is unbelief dressed as piety. God has already brought Him up.

Romans 10:8

8But what does it say? “The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart” (that is, the word of faith that we proclaim);

Arminian

What does it say? The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart—that is, the word of faith we proclaim. Wesley’s later chain (vv. 14–17) will rest on this nearness: God has put a hearable word within reach. Mouth and heart are where v. 9 will locate confessing and believing.

Classical Arminians refuse to make “near” a metaphor for a decree that never arrives. The proclaimed word is sincerely present. You may confess it or keep it out of your mouth. Nearness is the opposite of the heaven-and-abyss quest: salvation is as close as the gospel Paul is preaching now.

Reformed

The word is near: in mouth and heart—the word of faith we proclaim. Calvin: faith is the appointed means; the gospel does not fall like rain from the clouds but is brought by those who preach (vv. 14–15). Nearness is God’s ordinary mode.

Reformed accent: “the word of faith that we proclaim” is the instrument. Where this word is, God is addressing sinners. Mouth and heart will act in v. 9 because the word has already been put there by preaching. Near does not mean every hearer is elect; it means God has not left righteousness in heaven or the abyss.

Romans 10:9

9because, if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved.

Arminian

Because: if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. Do not skip the “if you.” Wesley: to every one that believeth—whether Jew or Gentile. The will of God is that all should savingly call upon him. Confession and heart-faith are the living conditions of a promised salvation.

Classical Arminians take this as a genuine gospel address to the hearer. The “you” is not a code for the already elect. Jesus is Lord; God raised Him; believe and confess, and you will be saved. The offer is as wide as the mouth and heart the word has entered (v. 8). Refusal is possible; so is saving faith.

Reformed

If you confess Jesus as Lord and believe in your heart that God raised Him, you will be saved. Calvin: faith is the appointed means. Do not skip the whoever-address: this is truly how the gospel speaks. Mouth and heart are not optional decorations of a secret list.

Reformed reading: the “if you” is a real address, and it does not mean every person is elect. Confession and faith are the way God saves those He is calling. The same Lord will be Lord of all (v. 12) without flattening election into universal salvation. Believe the resurrection; confess the Lord—that is the means. Who so believes, God has already been at work to grant.

Key contrast Arminian readings, with Wesley, take the “if you confess—and believe” as a genuine whoever-address: God wills that all should savingly call, and the hearer who believes will be saved. Reformed readings, with Calvin, take the same confession-and-faith as the appointed means and a true gospel address, yet not as proof that every person is elect: this is how God saves those who call.

Romans 10:10

10For with the heart one believes and is justified, and with the mouth one confesses and is saved.

Arminian

Heart and mouth divide the labor without splitting the people: with the heart one believes and is justified; with the mouth one confesses and is saved. Inward trust and public confession belong together. Wesley’s “every one that believeth” still governs both clauses.

Arminian pastoral: justification and salvation here are not two gospels. Believing and confessing are the one response the near word seeks. Do not skip the human acts. A silent heart-faith that will not confess, or a mouth without a heart, is not what Paul describes. The way is open to whoever so believes and confesses.

Reformed

Heart believes unto justification; mouth confesses unto salvation. Calvin’s appointed means again: faith is not a work of the law (v. 5) but the receiving of the risen Lord. The pairing is one salvation in two aspects.

Reformed accent: these are the ordinary marks of those God saves. The verse does not hand justification to autonomous will; it names how the righteousness of faith appears—inwardly believed, outwardly confessed. The same people run both clauses. Chapter 10 will still need a preacher (vv. 14–15); the means remain God’s.

Romans 10:11

11For the Scripture says, “Everyone who believes in him will not be put to shame.”

Arminian

Scripture says: everyone who believes in him will not be put to shame. Do not skip the everyone. Wesley: to every one that believeth—whether Jew or Gentile. Isaiah’s whoever (already at 9:33) is repeated as the Bible’s own width. Shame does not fall on the believer, whoever that believer is.

Classical Arminians take “everyone who believes” as a promise as wide as the human race in its hearing of the gospel. The Scripture is not whispering a smaller list. Anyone—Jew or Greek (v. 12)—who trusts Christ will stand unashamed. That is why Paul can pray for Israel (v. 1): they too may believe.

Reformed

Everyone who believes in him will not be put to shame. Calvin treats this as true gospel address: faith is the appointed means, and the believer will not be shamed. Do not skip the everyone; do not make it every person elect.

Reformed reading: Scripture itself puts a whoever on the promise. That is how God speaks in the gospel. The same verse does not teach that every hearer is chosen. Those who believe—and only they—are the unashamed. Election explains who believes; the promise explains what believers receive. Isaiah is fulfilled in a people who actually trust Him.

Key contrast Arminian readings typically take “everyone who believes” as Scripture’s own unrestricted whoever: Jew or Gentile, the believer will not be shamed, and the promise is meant as widely as it is spoken. Reformed readings typically take the same everyone as a true gospel address and appointed means (Calvin), not as a claim that every person is elect: believers will not be shamed; faith is how God saves those He calls.

Romans 10:12

12For there is no distinction between Jew and Greek; for the same Lord is Lord of all, bestowing his riches on all who call on him.

Arminian

No distinction between Jew and Greek: the same Lord is Lord of all, bestowing His riches on all who call on him. Wesley: the same Lord of all is rich unto all that call upon him. Do not skip the alls. One Lord, one calling people, one wealth of mercy for whoever calls.

Classical Arminians hear “Lord of all” as sincere universality of saving intent, not a slogan over a closed roll. Riches are bestowed on all who call—and the will of God, Wesley says, is that all should savingly call. Jew and Greek stand on the same ground. The distinction the law might have suggested is gone at the point of calling on the Lord.

Reformed

Calvin: the same Lord is rich unto all that call upon him: he makes the way of salvation common to the whole world, to Jews and Gentiles. No distinction of peoples. Do not skip “Lord of all”; do not read it as every person elect.

Reformed accent: common to Jew and Greek is the way, not the claim that every individual is chosen. The same Lord bestows riches on all who call—the callers are the people faith has already named (vv. 9–11). “Lord of all” breaks ethnic restriction; it does not dissolve election into equal outcome for every soul. Riches go to callers; callers are those who believe.

Key contrast Arminian readings, with Wesley, take “the same Lord is Lord of all” as riches sincerely held out to all who call, Jew and Greek, because God wills that all should savingly call. Reformed readings, with Calvin, take the same Lord as making the way of salvation common to the whole world—Jews and Gentiles—yet “Lord of all” does not mean every person is elect: riches are bestowed on those who call, by the appointed means of faith.

Romans 10:13

13For “everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved.”

Arminian

Joel’s line, now Paul’s: everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved. Do not skip the everyone. Wesley: the will of God is, that all should savingly call upon him. He would not have prayed (v. 1) had they been absolutely reprobated. Calling is the appointed human act; salvation is the promised result; the “everyone” is as wide as the name of the Lord.

This is the hinge into 10:14–21. Classical Arminians will not let the whoever collapse into a disguised list. God wills that all should savingly call; therefore preachers will be sent (v. 15), and Israel’s unbelief will be charged as refusal of a real call, not as a show. Whoever calls is saved. That is the gospel Paul has just preached to Jew and Greek.

Reformed

Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved. Calvin: faith is the appointed means; the same Lord makes the way common to Jews and Gentiles. Do not skip the everyone: this is true gospel address. The callers will be saved.

Reformed close: “everyone who calls” names the instrument, not a census of the elect as every human being. The next verses will ask how they shall call without believing, hearing, a preacher, a sending. Calling is God’s ordinary path. The whoever does not mean every person is elect; it means there is no other name, and no narrower ethnic gate, by which the saved call. Hands will be held out (v. 21); a remnant will remain (11:5).

Key contrast Arminian readings, with Wesley, take “everyone who calls” as the will of God that all should savingly call: the whoever is genuine, prayer for Israel proves they are not absolutely reprobated, and salvation is promised to whoever calls. Reformed readings, with Calvin, take the same everyone as true gospel address and appointed means, common to Jew and Gentile, yet not as every person elect: those who call will be saved; calling is how God gathers a people, not a proof that all are chosen.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on Israel’s zeal, Christ as the end of the law, and the near word of faith—prayer that they may be saved, righteousness by believing, and everyone who calls on the Lord—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Romans 10:1–13.

Arminian Notable voices

“My prayer to God is, that they may be saved—He would not have prayed for this, had they been absolutely reprobated. Christ is the end of the law—The scope and aim of it. To every one that believeth—Whether Jew or Gentile. The same Lord of all is rich unto all that call upon him. The will of God is, that all should savingly call upon him.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Rom 10:1–13)

John Calvin · Commentary on Romans (on Rom 10:4–13)
