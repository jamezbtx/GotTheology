Passage

Romans 9

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Romans 9 opens with grief, not doctrine. Paul is in anguish because most of his fellow Israelites have not believed in their Messiah, though theirs are the covenants, the promises, and Christ himself according to the flesh. Has God’s word failed? Paul answers that not all who descend from Israel are Israel, and he points to Isaac over Ishmael and Jacob over Esau. Reformed readers see personal, unconditional election in these examples; Arminian readers see God’s freedom to choose the line of promise and to define his people by promise and faith rather than by descent.

Romans 9:1

1I tell the truth in Christ. I am not lying, my conscience testifying with me in the Holy Spirit

Arminian

Paul calls on Christ and the Holy Spirit as witnesses to his sincerity. Arminian readers note that the chapter begins with a missionary’s grief, which sets the tone for everything that follows.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same sincerity. Paul’s teaching on election, which follows, comes from a heart that longs for the salvation of the lost.

Romans 9:2

2that I have great sorrow and unceasing pain in my heart.

Arminian

His sorrow is great and unceasing. Arminian readers see a pastor who mourns unbelief rather than treating it as a settled fate.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that belief in God’s sovereign election does not dull compassion. Paul grieves even as he teaches God’s freedom in mercy.

Romans 9:3

3For I could wish that I myself were accursed from Christ for my brothers’ sake, my relatives according to the flesh

Arminian

Paul could wish himself cut off from Christ for the sake of his kinsmen. Arminian readers see an echo of Moses (Exodus 32:32) and a love that reflects God’s own desire for Israel’s salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same Moses-like love. Paul knows he cannot be separated from Christ (8:39), yet he expresses how deeply he wants Israel saved.

Romans 9:4

4who are Israelites; whose is the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the giving of the law, the service, and the promises;

Arminian

Israel’s privileges are listed: adoption, glory, covenants, law, worship, and promises. Arminian readers see real gifts that called Israel to faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see privileges that belonged to Israel as a nation, which did not guarantee the salvation of every Israelite.

Romans 9:5

5of whom are the fathers, and from whom is Christ as concerning the flesh, who is over all, God, blessed forever. Amen.

Arminian

The patriarchs and the Messiah himself come from Israel. Most interpreters in both traditions read the final clause as calling Christ “God over all, blessed forever.”

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a clear confession of Christ’s deity, and note that Israel’s greatest privilege is the Messiah.

Romans 9:6

6But it is not as though the word of God has come to nothing. For they are not all Israel that are of Israel.

Arminian

God’s word has not failed, because not all who are descended from Israel are Israel. Arminian readers see Paul distinguishing between physical descent and the true people of God, who are defined by faith, as Romans 4 has already argued.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul distinguishing an elect remnant within ethnic Israel. God’s promise was always to a chosen people within the nation, so Israel’s unbelief does not mean God’s word has failed.

Key contrast Both traditions read verse 6 as distinguishing true Israel from physical descent. Arminian readers define true Israel by faith, as in Romans 4. Reformed readers define it by God’s election, with faith as its fruit.

Romans 9:7

7Neither, because they are Abraham’s offspring, are they all children. But, “your offspring will be accounted as from Isaac.”

Arminian

Not all Abraham’s descendants are his children; the promise came through Isaac. Arminian readers see God choosing the line through which the promise would come.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s choice operating within Abraham’s family, a pattern of sovereign selection.

Romans 9:8

8That is, it is not the children of the flesh who are children of God, but the children of the promise are counted as heirs.

Arminian

The children of the promise, not the children of the flesh, are counted as heirs. Arminian readers connect this with Galatians 3–4, where the children of the promise are those who believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the children of the promise as those whom God’s promise creates, by his initiative rather than by natural descent.

Romans 9:9

9For this is a word of promise: “At the appointed time I will come, and Sarah will have a son.”

Arminian

The promise was God’s word that Sarah would have a son at the appointed time. Both traditions see God’s power fulfilling his word.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Isaac’s birth as entirely God’s doing, a picture of salvation by promise.

Romans 9:10

10Not only so, but Rebekah also conceived by one, by our father Isaac.

Arminian

Rebekah’s twins had the same father, so the distinction cannot be explained by different parentage. Arminian readers see Paul narrowing the case to show God’s freedom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul removing every human explanation: same mother, same father, same conception.

Romans 9:11

11For being not yet born, neither having done anything good or bad, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him who calls,

Arminian

Before the twins were born or had done anything, God announced his purpose, “not of works, but of him who calls.” Arminian readers note that “not of works” excludes merit, and that Paul never treats faith as a work. They argue that election can be free of works and still be in Christ through faith.

Many Arminians also observe that the choice concerned the line of promise and the roles of two peoples, not the eternal salvation of two individuals.

Reformed

Reformed readers press the timing. The choice was made before the twins were born, so it cannot rest on anything they would do. Calvin argued that if election depended on foreseen faith or works, Paul’s point about the unborn twins would lose its force. The phrase “of him who calls” places the cause of the distinction entirely in God.

On this reading, Jacob and Esau illustrate how God deals with every person: election rests on his purpose alone.

Key contrast Reformed readers see an unconditional choice of individuals made before they could do anything, with God’s purpose as the only cause. Arminian readers see a choice that excludes merit but not faith, and often apply it to the line of promise rather than to individual salvation.

Romans 9:12

12it was said to her, “The elder will serve the younger.”

Arminian

“The elder will serve the younger” comes from Genesis 25:23, which speaks of two nations. Arminian readers note that Esau personally never served Jacob, so the prophecy concerns the peoples descended from them.

Reformed

Reformed readers acknowledge the national dimension but argue that Paul uses the text to illustrate a principle that applies to individuals, as the following verses show.

Romans 9:13

13Even as it is written, “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated.”

Arminian

“Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated” comes from Malachi 1:2–3, written centuries later about Israel and Edom. Arminian readers see corporate election and read “hated” as a Hebrew idiom for “loved less” or “did not choose,” as in Luke 14:26.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that “hated” can mean “not chosen,” but they see Paul applying the text to God’s sovereign choice between persons. The objection Paul anticipates in verse 14 suggests he is speaking of something that seems unfair, which fits personal election better than national roles.

Key contrast Both traditions take “hated” as a strong idiom rather than personal malice. Arminian readers see Malachi’s nations and God’s choice of the line of promise. Reformed readers see Paul using the text to illustrate God’s unconditional choice of individuals.
