Passage

Romans 9

Verses 14–29 · WEB

Having described God’s choice of Jacob over Esau, Paul answers the objection that God is unjust. God says he will have mercy on whom he has mercy; he raised up Pharaoh to show his power; the potter has a right over the clay. Paul then speaks of vessels of wrath and vessels of mercy and of a people called from Jews and Gentiles alike. This is the most debated passage in the Arminian and Reformed discussion. Both sides read it as Scripture that silences human complaint; they differ on whether it describes unconditional individual election or God’s right to set the terms of mercy and to shape the history of Israel and the nations.

Romans 9:14

14What shall we say then? Is there unrighteousness with God? May it never be!

Arminian

Is God unjust in choosing Jacob over Esau? Paul’s answer is an emphatic no. Wesley read the objection as asking whether God may give the blessing to Jacob rather than Esau, or accept believers and only believers. God has the right to fix the terms on which he will show mercy.

Arminian readers hear God’s choice of the line of promise and of the terms of salvation, and his acceptance of those he foreknows will believe. Paul defends that right without making God the author of anyone’s unbelief.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the objection as evidence that Paul taught personal, unconditional election. If God chose people on the basis of foreseen faith, no one would accuse him of injustice. Calvin held that God may distinguish between persons without becoming unjust.

The verses that follow locate mercy and hardening in God’s will, and Reformed readers see them as explaining why the charge fails.

Key contrast Both agree that God is not unjust. Arminian readers see Paul defending God’s right to set faith as the condition of mercy. Reformed readers see Paul defending God’s right to choose individuals for mercy unconditionally, which is why the objection arises.

Romans 9:15

15For he said to Moses, “I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I have compassion.”

Arminian

God tells Moses, “I will have mercy on whom I have mercy” (Exodus 33:19). Wesley read this as mercy according to the terms God himself has fixed, namely faith. Arminian readers note that God is not obligated to show mercy on human terms, such as descent or works.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God claiming mercy as his own to give. The ground of compassion lies in God, not in the one who receives it. Faith appears in those who receive mercy, but it is not a condition God inspects before deciding.

Romans 9:16

16So then it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy.

Arminian

“It is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy.” Arminian readers agree that salvation cannot be earned or produced by human will or effort. They argue that the verse excludes merit, not a grace-enabled response, and point to 9:30–32, where Gentiles attain righteousness by faith.

Reformed

Calvin wrote that Paul has excluded all human worthiness: if the blessing depends on God’s mercy alone, it cannot be credited to human will or effort. Reformed readers see human willing and running as the fruit of mercy, not its cause.

Key contrast Both deny that salvation originates in human will or effort. Arminian readers see the verse excluding merit while leaving room for grace-enabled faith as God’s chosen condition. Reformed readers see it excluding human willing as a cause of mercy, so that faith itself is the fruit of mercy.

Romans 9:17

17For the Scripture says to Pharaoh, “For this very purpose I caused you to be raised up, that I might show in you my power, and that my name might be proclaimed in all the earth.”

Arminian

God raised up Pharaoh to show his power and make his name known. Arminian readers note that Pharaoh hardened his own heart repeatedly in Exodus before God is said to harden it, and see God using a rebellious ruler for his purposes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign purpose in Pharaoh’s life. God raised him up and used even his rebellion to display divine power.

Romans 9:18

18So then, he has mercy on whom he desires, and he hardens whom he desires.

Arminian

God has mercy on whom he wills and hardens whom he wills. Arminian readers read divine hardening as God confirming a person in a course already chosen, as in Romans 1, where God gives people over to what they desire. In Romans 11, Israel’s hardening is partial and can be reversed if they do not continue in unbelief.

Reformed

Reformed readers see mercy and hardening as both rooted in God’s will. Most Reformed theologians distinguish between election, in which God actively brings people to faith, and reprobation, in which he passes over others and leaves them in their sin. Hardening is a just judgment, not the creation of evil in an innocent heart.

Key contrast Arminian readers see hardening as God’s response to persistent rebellion, which can be reversed. Reformed readers see hardening as a judicial act rooted in God’s will, distinct from his active work in election.

Romans 9:19

19You will say then to me, “Why does he still find fault? For who withstands his will?”

Arminian

The objector asks why God still blames anyone if no one can resist his will. Arminian readers note that Paul does not answer by denying human responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an objection that only makes sense if Paul has taught that God’s will is decisive. Paul does not soften his teaching to answer it.

Romans 9:20

20But indeed, O man, who are you to reply against God? Will the thing formed ask him who formed it, “Why did you make me like this?”

Arminian

“Who are you to reply against God?” Arminian readers see a rebuke of human arrogance, not a denial that God’s ways are just.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the creature’s place before the Creator. Humans are not in a position to judge God.

Romans 9:21

21Or hasn’t the potter a right over the clay, from the same lump to make one part a vessel for honor, and another for dishonor?

Arminian

The potter has a right over the clay. Arminian readers note that the image comes from Jeremiah 18, where God says he will change what he intends for a nation if it repents. The potter’s freedom includes responding to the clay.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign right over fallen humanity, the same lump. God may show mercy to some and leave others to justice, and no one can accuse him, because mercy is never owed.

Key contrast Arminian readers read the potter in light of Jeremiah 18, where God responds to a nation’s repentance. Reformed readers see God’s sovereign right to show mercy to some from the same fallen lump and to leave others to justice.

Romans 9:22

22What if God, willing to show his wrath and to make his power known, endured with much patience vessels of wrath prepared for destruction,

Arminian

God endured with much patience the vessels of wrath prepared for destruction. Arminian readers see patience that gives time for repentance, as in Romans 2:4. Wesley read the vessels as fitted for destruction by their own willful and final impenitence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the vessels of wrath are described as prepared for destruction without naming who prepared them. Many see this as supporting the distinction between active election and passive reprobation. God’s patience displays his wrath and power.

Romans 9:23

23and that he might make known the riches of his glory on vessels of mercy, which he prepared beforehand for glory—

Arminian

God makes known the riches of his glory on vessels of mercy prepared beforehand for glory. Arminian readers identify the vessels of mercy as believers, prepared by God for the glory he intends for those who trust him.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God himself prepared the vessels of mercy beforehand. The contrast with verse 22 reflects the asymmetry between election, which is God’s active work, and reprobation.

Romans 9:24

24us, whom he also called, not from the Jews only, but also from the Gentiles?

Arminian

The vessels of mercy are “us,” called from Jews and Gentiles. Arminian readers see Paul’s argument arriving at its real point: God’s people now include believing Gentiles.

Reformed

Reformed readers see effectual calling of the elect from both Jews and Gentiles.

Romans 9:25

25As he says also in Hosea, “I will call them ‘my people,’ which were not my people; and her ‘beloved,’ who was not beloved.”

Arminian

Hosea’s words about restoring Israel are applied to the Gentiles. Arminian readers see God calling a people who were not his people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign grace creating a people out of those who were not a people.

Romans 9:26

26“It will be that in the place where it was said to them, ‘You are not my people,’ there they will be called ‘children of the living God.’”

Arminian

Those called “not my people” are called children of the living God. Both traditions see the inclusion of the Gentiles.

Reformed

Reformed readers see adoption into God’s family as an act of his grace.

Romans 9:27

27Isaiah cries concerning Israel, “If the number of the children of Israel are as the sand of the sea, it is the remnant who will be saved;

Arminian

Only a remnant of Israel will be saved. Arminian readers see that physical descent never guaranteed salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the remnant as chosen by grace, as Paul will say in 11:5.

Romans 9:28

28for he will finish the work and cut it short in righteousness, because the Lord will make a short work upon the earth.”

Arminian

The Lord will carry out his word on the earth. Both traditions see God’s faithfulness to his word in judgment and in salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the certainty of God’s purpose.

Romans 9:29

29As Isaiah has said before, “Unless the Lord of Armies had left us a seed, we would have become like Sodom, and would have been made like Gomorrah.”

Arminian

Unless the Lord had left a seed, Israel would have been like Sodom. Arminian readers see God’s mercy in preserving a faithful remnant.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that the existence of the remnant is entirely God’s doing.
