Passage

Romans 9

Verses 30–33 · WEB

After the hard sayings about election and the potter, Paul draws a surprising conclusion. Gentiles who were not seeking righteousness have obtained it by faith, while Israel, pursuing the law as a matter of works, missed it and stumbled over the stone God laid in Zion. These verses turn the argument from God’s choosing to human believing. Arminian readers see them as the key to the chapter; Reformed readers see them as describing how God’s purpose is carried out.

Romans 9:30

30What shall we say then? That the Gentiles, who didn’t follow after righteousness, attained to righteousness, even the righteousness which is of faith;

Arminian

Wesley called this the first conclusion of the whole argument. Gentiles, who had shown no concern for righteousness, have obtained it, and the righteousness they obtained is the righteousness of faith.

Arminian readers see this as the hinge of Romans 9. The potter and the mercy of verses 14–29 described God’s right to set the terms of salvation, and here Paul names those terms: righteousness by faith. Individuals still believe and receive, and God accepts those who do.

Reformed

Calvin observed that nothing seemed more unreasonable than Gentiles obtaining righteousness while Jews who labored at the law were shut out. Paul explains that the Gentiles’ righteousness came by faith and so depended on God’s mercy, not on their worth.

Calvin also insisted that faith is not the first cause. The Gentiles could believe only because God’s grace went before them, and faith is itself part of his favor. Reformed readers see faith as the instrument by which righteousness is received, with God’s purpose explaining who believes.

Key contrast Arminian readers take Gentile faith as the point the chapter has been building toward, showing that verses 14–29 do not cancel a real human response. Reformed readers agree that righteousness is received by faith, while holding that God’s grace precedes and produces that faith.

Romans 9:31

31but Israel, following after a law of righteousness, didn’t arrive at the law of righteousness.

Arminian

Wesley noted that Israel was pursuing a law that, rightly used, would have led them to faith and so to righteousness. They ran and did not arrive.

Arminian readers stress that the law was not a trap. The failure was in how Israel pursued it, and verse 32 will name that failure as Israel’s own. The potter’s right in the earlier verses did not make their pursuit pointless or their outcome fixed.

Reformed

Calvin said it was no wonder Israel gained nothing, since they were running off the path. Relying on the righteousness of the law, they did not understand how God justifies sinners.

Reformed readers do not mock Israel’s zeal. The pursuit was earnest, but it was aimed at the wrong goal. A remnant obtained righteousness, as Paul has already said (vv. 23–29), while the nation as a whole did not.

Romans 9:32

32Why? Because they didn’t seek it by faith, but as it were by works of the law. They stumbled over the stumbling stone,

Arminian

Paul asks why Israel failed. Wesley pressed the question: was it because God had eternally decreed they would not attain? He found nothing like that in the text. Paul gives the reason himself: they did not seek righteousness by faith, but as if by works, and they stumbled over Christ.

Arminian readers take this “because” as Paul’s own explanation of Israel’s failure. The cause is responsible unbelief, not a decree they could not resist. Christ is the stone, and the fault lies with those who refuse to trust him.

Reformed

Calvin agreed that those who try to gain salvation by their own works are rightly rejected, since faith and the merit of works cannot stand together. Israel stumbled over Christ, the only way to righteousness.

Reformed readers affirm the “because” and take Israel’s unbelief seriously as the real ground of their judgment. Calvin cautioned, however, against placing this cause above God’s secret purpose, which he regarded as the first cause. Human wickedness is the reason for God’s judgment, and God’s election explains why some believe.

Key contrast Both traditions read “because they didn’t seek it by faith” as a real explanation. Arminian readers take it as the final word on why Israel failed: responsible unbelief rather than a decree. Reformed readers take unbelief as the true ground of judgment while holding that God’s election stands behind the faith of those who believe.

Romans 9:33

33even as it is written, “Behold, I lay in Zion a stumbling stone and a rock of offense; and no one who believes in him will be disappointed.”

Arminian

Paul quotes Isaiah: God lays in Zion a stone that some will stumble over, and no one who believes in him will be disappointed. Wesley read the stone as Christ, the only sure foundation, who becomes an occasion of ruin to many through their persistent unbelief.

Arminian readers note that the promise is as wide as “no one who believes.” Ruin comes through unbelief, not through a fate that made believing impossible. The verse names believers and unbelievers in terms of their response to Christ.

Reformed

Calvin wrote that Christ is appointed for ruin to unbelievers and for life to the godly. The stumbling does not belong to Christ in himself but comes from human wickedness, since God made the way plain.

Reformed readers see Scripture fulfilled as Israel stumbles. The promise to everyone who believes is sincere, and faith is the means by which God’s people receive salvation, while God’s purpose explains why some believe and others stumble.

Key contrast Both read the stone as Christ and the promise to all who believe as sincere. Arminian readers locate the cause of ruin in resistible unbelief. Reformed readers agree that unbelief is culpable and see God’s purpose behind the faith of those who trust in Christ.
