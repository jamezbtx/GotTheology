Passage

Romans 2

Verses 1–11 · ESV · Voices

After exposing Gentile ungodliness and the handing-over of 1:18–32, Paul turns to the moralist who judges those sins while practicing them. God’s kindness, forbearance, and patience are meant to lead to repentance; a hard and impenitent heart stores wrath. He will render to each one according to his works, the Jew first and also the Greek, for God shows no partiality.

Romans 2:1

1Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges. For in passing judgment on another you condemn yourself, because you, the judge, practice the very same things.

Arminian

Therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges. The ’therefore’ looks back to the Gentile catalog of 1:18–32 and turns on the moralist who condemns it while walking in the same sins. In passing judgment on another you condemn yourself. Wesley’s later word (v. 4) names the next step: from hoping to escape wrath to the abuse of love.

Classical Arminians take this as pastoral, not tribal. The hypocrite’s verdict on the neighbor is a verdict on himself. Both camps refuse a cheap moralism that would shelter in comparison. The gospel will soon justify the ungodly; this chapter first shuts the mouth that would excuse itself by condemning others.

Reformed

Therefore you have no excuse. Calvin’s later word fits the scene: where the fear of God does not rule, confidence is a contempt and a mockery of goodness. The judge who practices the very same things has no shelter in his verdict on the Gentile. God’s judgment, not ours, is the bar that matters.

Reformed reading: this is not a license never to name sin; it is a warning that the one who sits in judgment while walking in the same sins is already self-condemned. God will not be used as a mascot for our comparisons. The ’O man’ is every moralist, Jew or Greek, who would hide behind a pointing finger.

Romans 2:2

2We know that the judgment of God rightly falls on those who practice such things.

Arminian

We know that the judgment of God rightly falls on those who practice such things. The ’such things’ are the sins just named. Wesley will soon say there is no respect of persons with God: he will reward every one according to his works. The standard is God’s, and it is right.

Classical Arminians take the rightness of this judgment with full seriousness. Kindness in v. 4 is not a denial of wrath; it is the patience of a God who still judges truly. No camp may soften ’rightly falls.’ The question is not whether God judges, but whether the one who judges others has any exemption.

Reformed

We know—this is shared knowledge, not a private theory—that the judgment of God rightly falls on those who practice such things. Calvin: the Lord will visit the wickedness of the reprobate with just vengeance and recompense them with what they have deserved. The sentence is righteous because God is righteous.

Reformed accent: ’rightly’ shuts every complaint against the bar. Judgment is not mood or tribal preference. Those who practice such things are under a just sentence, whether they have a Gentile resume or a Jewish one. God’s judgment is the one that stands.

Romans 2:3

3Do you suppose, O man—you who judge those who practice such things and yet do them yourself—that you will escape the judgment of God?

Arminian

Do you suppose, O man—you who judge those who practice such things and yet do them yourself—that you will escape the judgment of God? The supposed escape is the moralist’s last fiction. Wesley: from hoping to escape his wrath one may go farther still, to the abuse of his love. There is no escape by comparison.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere warning: the judge who does the same things will not slip the bar. Escape is not offered to the self-excusing. God’s judgment is real, and the kindness that follows (v. 4) is meant to lead to repentance, not to underwrite a theory that some sinners are too refined to face it.

Reformed

Do you suppose that you will escape the judgment of God? Calvin: where the fear of God does not rule, confidence on account of prosperity is a contempt and a mockery of his great goodness. The question expects no. The man who judges and does the same will not slip through.

Reformed pastoral: there is no caste of people too religious, too instructed, or too severe on others to come under this judgment. Escape is God’s to grant in the gospel that follows; it is not the wage of a comparing conscience. The bar will hold.

Romans 2:4

4Or do you presume on the riches of his kindness and forbearance and patience, not knowing that God’s kindness is meant to lead you to repentance?

Arminian

Or do you presume on the riches of his kindness and forbearance and patience, not knowing that God’s kindness is meant to lead you to repentance? Wesley: despisest thou—dost thou go farther still, from hoping to escape his wrath, to the abuse of his love. Leadeth thee: that is, is designed of God to lead or encourage thee to it. The riches are abundance; the design is repentance.

Classical Arminians hear a real design: kindness is meant to lead, and it can be abused. Presuming on the riches is resisting a love that actually aimed at turning the sinner. Kindness is not indifference and not a trap. It is God’s chosen means to lead; the hard heart that will not be led (v. 5) is responsible for that refusal, not for a kindness that never meant him.

Reformed

Or do you presume on the riches of his kindness? Calvin: dost thou despise the riches? Where the fear of God does not rule, confidence on account of prosperity is a contempt and a mockery of his great goodness. The Lord by his kindness shows that it is he to whom we ought to turn, if we desire to secure our wellbeing, and at the same time he strengthens our confidence in expecting mercy. If we use not God’s bounty for this end, we abuse it.

Reformed reading: goodness is truly kindness, and it is abused when not used to turn. The verse names the sinner’s ignorance and contempt, not a frustrated design that failed because God could not lead. Kindness is meant to lead; the heart that will not be led stores wrath. Bounty unused for repentance is bounty mocked.

Key contrast Both take the riches of kindness, forbearance, and patience as real, and both refuse to treat delay as permission. Arminians typically hear kindness designed of God to lead to repentance—a love that can be abused and resisted. Reformed readers typically hear the same kindness as bounty meant to turn us, abused when not used to that end: contempt of goodness, not a design that finally depends on our letting it succeed.

Romans 2:5

5But because of your hard and impenitent heart you are storing up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath when God’s righteous judgment will be revealed.

Arminian

But because of your hard and impenitent heart you are storing up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath when God’s righteous judgment will be revealed. The kindness of v. 4 was designed to lead to repentance; the hard heart refuses it. Wesley’s ’abuse of his love’ is not a small discourtesy. It stores wrath.

Classical Arminians take storing wrath as the sinner’s own doing. Impenitence is real; the day of wrath is real; the judgment revealed will be righteous. Kindness was meant to lead. Those who will not be led do not find that God’s patience cancelled the bar; they find they have been stacking judgment against themselves.

Reformed

Because of your hard and impenitent heart you are storing up wrath for yourself. Calvin: the Lord will visit the wickedness of the reprobate with just vengeance. The day of wrath is the revealing of a judgment already righteous, not the birth of a mood. Hardness is the heart’s; the store is wrath.

Reformed accent: impenitence is not a puzzle God failed to solve with more kindness. It is the heart that will not turn, storing what the day will reveal. Righteous judgment will be public. Those whom He has resolved to glorify He also sanctifies (v. 6); the impenitent face a vengeance they have deserved.

Romans 2:6

6He will render to each one according to his works:

Arminian

He will render to each one according to his works. Wesley: he will reward every one according to his works. This is not a Pelagian ladder into first acceptance. It is the public rendering that matches a life—whether that life answered kindness with repentance or stored wrath. Judgment according to works is real; merit as the meritorious cause of justification is not what this verse teaches.

Classical Arminians refuse ’saved by works’ as the ground of pardon as heartily as any Calvinist. Works here are the way a life shows itself at the bar. God’s rendering is according to works; the gospel that follows will still justify the ungodly by grace through faith. Do not flatten Paul into a wage-system, and do not empty this verse of a real according-to-works judgment.

Reformed

He will render to each one according to his works. Calvin: the Lord, by visiting the wickedness of the reprobate with just vengeance, will recompense them with what they have deserved; and as he sanctifies those whom he has previously resolved to glorify, he will also crown their good works, but not on account of any merit. Nor can this be proved from this verse: though it declares what reward good works are to have, it does not show what they are worth. It is an absurd inference to deduce merit from reward.

Reformed confession: works are the evidence of those God resolved to glorify, and the just desert of the reprobate. Reward names what good works will have; it does not price them as the cause of life. Do not invent a gospel of wages from a verse about rendering, and do not invent a Pelagian Paul. The crown is grace; the vengeance is just.

Key contrast Both take rendering according to works with full seriousness, and both refuse a Pelagian ’saved by works’ as the meritorious cause of life. Arminians typically hear works as the real public evidence of a life that answered or refused kindness—reward according to works, not pardon earned by them. Reformed readers typically hear the same rendering, with good works crowned in those God previously resolved to glorify, not on account of merit: it is absurd to deduce merit from reward.

Romans 2:7

7to those who by patience in well-doing seek for glory and honor and immortality, he will give eternal life;

Arminian

To those who by patience in well-doing seek for glory and honor and immortality, he will give eternal life. Wesley’s reward according to works is here the gift of life to a seeking, patient well-doing. This is the life of those who, by grace, persist—not a wage that purchases first justification, but the end God has appointed for well-doing.

Classical Arminians hear a real well-doing, enabled by grace, that God will honor with eternal life. Patience in well-doing is not a meritorious climb into pardon; it is the path of those who have not presumed on kindness but have been led toward repentance. Seek glory, honor, and immortality from God, not a boast before Him.

Reformed

To those who by patience in well-doing seek for glory and honor and immortality, he will give eternal life. Calvin: as he sanctifies those whom he has previously resolved to glorify, he will also crown their good works, but not on account of any merit. The seekers of this verse are the sanctified; the life is a crown, not a price they paid.

Reformed reading: eternal life is given. The well-doing is the fruit of those He resolved to glorify, not the cause that moved Him to resolve it. Patience in well-doing is real, and it will be crowned. Deduce merit from that crown and the inference is absurd. Glory, honor, and immortality remain gifts of the God who sanctifies.

Key contrast Both hear eternal life given to those who by patience in well-doing seek glory, honor, and immortality. Arminians typically hear graciously enabled well-doing as the life God rewards—not a meritorious purchase of first pardon. Reformed readers typically hear the good works of those He previously resolved to glorify, crowned, not on account of merit.

Romans 2:8

8but for those who are self-seeking and do not obey the truth, but obey unrighteousness, there will be wrath and fury.

Arminian

But for those who are self-seeking and do not obey the truth, but obey unrighteousness, there will be wrath and fury. The opposite of patient well-doing is not a small delay; it is self-seeking that will not obey the truth. Wesley’s abuse of love ends here, not in a milder court.

Classical Arminians take wrath and fury as honestly as they take kindness. Those who will not be led to repentance, who obey unrighteousness, meet a real judgment. Self-seeking is the refusal of the truth the kindness was meant to serve. No partiality will shelter it (v. 11).

Reformed

For those who are self-seeking and do not obey the truth, but obey unrighteousness, there will be wrath and fury. Calvin: the Lord, visiting the wickedness of the reprobate with just vengeance, will recompense them with what they have deserved. Wrath here is not excess; it is desert.

Reformed accent: self-seeking names the life that will not obey the truth. Fury is the public face of a judgment already just. The reprobate are recompensed; they are not mishandled. This verse will not be turned into a small warning. God renders; He does not negotiate the wages of unrighteousness.

Romans 2:9

9There will be tribulation and distress for every human being who does evil, the Jew first and also the Greek,

Arminian

There will be tribulation and distress for every human being who does evil, the Jew first and also the Greek. The order matches 1:16—Jew first, then Greek—now as judgment, not as a narrower mercy. Wesley: there is no respect of persons with God. Doing evil is the issue, not the badge.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere impartiality: every human being who does evil meets tribulation, without a Jewish exemption or a Gentile afterthought. ’First’ is historical honor and historical accountability, not a smaller or larger gospel. Both camps must say this together before they dispute how grace saves.

Reformed

Tribulation and distress for every human being who does evil, the Jew first and also the Greek. Calvin: there is no respect of persons with God; He regards purity of heart or inward integrity, and hath no respect for kindred, country, dignity, wealth. The Jew’s priority is not a shield.

Reformed reading: ’every human being’ levels the field so that no nation, rank, or rite can buy off the bar. Jew first means the people entrusted with the oracles are first at the bar as well as first in the offer. Doing evil draws distress. Kindred will not help.

Romans 2:10

10but glory and honor and peace for everyone who does good, the Jew first and also the Greek.

Arminian

But glory and honor and peace for everyone who does good, the Jew first and also the Greek. The positive twin of v. 9. Wesley: he will reward every one according to his works; this is well consistent with his distributing advantages and opportunities of improvement according to his own good pleasure. Doing good is honored; it is not a private Jewish wage.

Classical Arminians hear a real reward of well-doing, Jew and Greek, without partiality. Doing good is the fruit of a life led toward repentance, not a Pelagian climb that purchases first pardon. Glory, honor, and peace come from God to everyone who does good—the same wideness as the tribulation of v. 9, and the same refusal of a badge that would replace the life.

Reformed

Glory and honor and peace for everyone who does good, the Jew first and also the Greek. Calvin: He crowns the good works of those He has previously resolved to glorify, but not on account of any merit. The doer of good is the sanctified person; the glory is a crown, not a price.

Reformed confession: ’everyone who does good’ is as wide in kinds as v. 9—Jew and Greek—and as particular in persons as those He sanctifies. Do not deduce merit from this peace. The same God who shows no partiality of kindred shows no weakness in grace: He gives the crown to the works He first worked.

Romans 2:11

11For God shows no partiality.

Arminian

For God shows no partiality. Wesley: there is no respect of persons with God—he will reward every one according to his works. But this is well consistent with his distributing advantages and opportunities of improvement according to his own good pleasure. Impartial judgment does not mean identical gifts; it means no favorite caste at the bar.

Classical Arminians hear a pastoral comfort and a pastoral threat together. The Jew is not safer by blood; the Greek is not safer by ignorance. God may distribute opportunities as He pleases; He will not judge by faces. Kindness meant to lead to repentance (v. 4) was never a private kindness for a protected class.

Reformed

For God shows no partiality. Calvin: understand that what He regards is purity of heart or inward integrity; He hath no respect for those things highly valued by men—kindred, country, dignity, wealth, and similar things. The bar looks at the heart, not the resume.

Reformed pastoral: no partiality means God will not be bribed by ancestry, passport, rank, or purse. He regards inward integrity. The reprobate are recompensed as they deserve; the sanctified are crowned without merit. Jew and Greek stand at one bar, and the gospel that follows will be the power of God for both.
