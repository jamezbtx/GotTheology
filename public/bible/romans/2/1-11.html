Passage

Romans 2

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Paul turns to the person who condemns the sins of Romans 1 while doing the same things. God’s judgment is according to truth, and his kindness is meant to lead to repentance, not to be presumed upon. God will repay each person according to their works: eternal life to those who seek glory by patient well-doing, wrath to those who obey unrighteousness. There is no partiality with God. Both traditions agree that judgment is impartial. They differ on how verses 6–10 relate to justification by faith.

Romans 2:1

1Therefore you are without excuse, O man, whoever you are who judge. For in that which you judge another, you condemn yourself. For you who judge practice the same things.

Arminian

Those who judge others while doing the same things condemn themselves. Both traditions see the hypocrisy of self-righteous judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Paul preparing to show that the moral person is as guilty as the openly ungodly.

Romans 2:2

2We know that the judgment of God is according to truth against those who practice such things.

Arminian

God’s judgment rightly falls on those who practice such things. Both traditions affirm God’s just judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see judgment according to truth, not appearance.

Romans 2:3

3Do you think this, O man who judges those who practice such things, and do the same, that you will escape the judgment of God?

Arminian

Do you think you will escape God’s judgment? Both traditions see the folly of presuming on exemption.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that privilege does not exempt anyone from judgment.

Romans 2:4

4Or do you despise the riches of his goodness, forbearance, and patience, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?

Arminian

God’s kindness, forbearance, and patience are meant to lead you to repentance. Arminian readers see God’s patience as a sincere invitation to every sinner, one that can be refused.

They hold that God genuinely desires the repentance of all.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that God’s kindness calls people to repent and leaves the impenitent without excuse. They distinguish this common goodness from the special grace that actually brings the elect to repentance.

Key contrast Both read God’s kindness as a real call to repentance. Arminian readers see it as a sincere invitation to all that may be resisted. Reformed readers see it as common grace that leaves people without excuse, distinct from the effectual grace given to the elect.

Romans 2:5

5But according to your hardness and unrepentant heart you are treasuring up for yourself wrath in the day of wrath, revelation, and of the righteous judgment of God,

Arminian

Hard and impenitent hearts store up wrath for the day of judgment. Both traditions see the danger of refusing God’s patience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wrath accumulating against unrepentant sin.

Romans 2:6

6who “will pay back to everyone according to their works:”

Arminian

God will repay each person according to their works. Arminian readers see works as the evidence of genuine, living faith at the judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see works as the evidence, not the ground, of justification.

Key contrast Both affirm judgment according to works and justification by faith. Some read verses 6–10 as a hypothetical standard that no one meets, while others read them as describing the fruit of genuine faith. This debate cuts across both traditions.

Romans 2:7

7to those who by perseverance in well-doing seek for glory, honor, and incorruptibility, eternal life;

Arminian

Eternal life to those who seek glory and honor by patient well-doing. Arminian readers see perseverance in well-doing as necessary for final salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see such perseverance as the fruit of grace in the elect.

Romans 2:8

8but to those who are self-seeking and don’t obey the truth, but obey unrighteousness, will be wrath, indignation,

Arminian

Wrath to those who are self-seeking and disobey the truth. Both traditions see judgment on persistent unrighteousness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the just penalty of sin.

Romans 2:9

9oppression, and anguish on every soul of man who does evil, to the Jew first, and also to the Greek.

Arminian

Tribulation for everyone who does evil, the Jew first and also the Greek. Both traditions see equal accountability.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that covenant privilege brings greater responsibility.

Romans 2:10

10But glory, honor, and peace go to every man who does good, to the Jew first, and also to the Greek.

Arminian

Glory, honor, and peace for everyone who does good. Both traditions agree.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the reward of good works done in grace.

Romans 2:11

11For there is no partiality with God.

Arminian

God shows no partiality. Arminian readers see this as consistent with God’s universal love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see impartiality in judgment, which they distinguish from God’s freedom in election.
