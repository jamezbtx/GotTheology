Passage

1 Peter 1

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Peter writes to believers scattered across five Roman provinces in Asia Minor, calling them chosen ones living as foreigners. He grounds their identity in the Father’s foreknowledge, the Spirit’s sanctifying work, and Christ’s blood, then breaks into praise for new birth to a living hope. Suffering is real but brief; the inheritance is kept in heaven and the believers are guarded through faith. The prophets searched for this salvation and angels long to look into it. Both Arminian and Reformed readers find deep comfort here; they differ chiefly on how foreknowledge relates to election and how God’s guarding relates to persevering faith.

1 Peter 1:1

1Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ, to the chosen ones who are living as foreigners in the Dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia,

Arminian

Peter writes with apostolic authority to scattered believers, calling them chosen ones and foreigners. Arminian readers stress that the election language describes the church as God’s people in Christ, a corporate identity that individuals share by faith. Their status as exiles reminds them that this world is not home.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Peter opens with election, addressing believers as God’s chosen before anything else. Their exile is not an accident of history but part of God’s purpose for a people set apart. Election here is a source of comfort for a pressured and marginal church.

1 Peter 1:2

2according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, in sanctification of the Spirit, that you may obey Jesus Christ and be sprinkled with his blood: Grace to you and peace be multiplied.

Arminian

Election is according to the foreknowledge of God the Father. Classical Arminians commonly read foreknowledge as God’s foresight of those who would believe in Christ, so that election follows God’s knowledge of faith. The aim of election is clear on any reading: sanctification by the Spirit, obedience to Christ, and cleansing by his blood.

Reformed

Reformed readers often take foreknowledge here in the relational sense found elsewhere in Scripture (e.g., Amos 3:2; Rom 8:29), meaning God’s prior, loving set regard rather than bare foresight. On this view election rests on God’s purpose, not on foreseen faith. The Trinitarian pattern shows the whole of salvation flowing from God’s initiative.

Key contrast Both affirm election according to the Father’s foreknowledge. Arminians typically read foreknowledge as foresight of faith in Christ; Reformed readers typically read it as God’s prior loving choice that grounds faith.

1 Peter 1:3

3Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who according to his great mercy caused us to be born again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead,

Arminian

Peter praises God, who in great mercy caused believers to be born again to a living hope through Christ’s resurrection. Arminian readers affirm that new birth is wholly God’s merciful work, received by those who respond to the gospel in faith (see 1:23). The hope is living because it rests on a living, risen Lord.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that God is the subject of the verb: he caused us to be born again. New birth is a sovereign act of mercy, not a human achievement, and it gives rise to faith and hope. The resurrection of Christ is the ground and power of this new life.

1 Peter 1:4

4to an incorruptible and undefiled inheritance that doesn’t fade away, reserved in Heaven for you,

Arminian

The inheritance is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven. Arminian readers find great assurance in the security of the inheritance itself; nothing on earth can corrupt or steal it. They hold that it is reserved for those who continue to trust Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the inheritance is reserved for you, a pledge that God keeps what he has promised his people. The three negatives underline its permanence. The kept inheritance pairs naturally with the guarded heirs of the next verse.

1 Peter 1:5

5who by the power of God are guarded through faith for a salvation ready to be revealed in the last time.

Arminian

Believers are guarded by God’s power through faith. Arminian readers emphasize that the instrument of God’s guarding is faith, so believers are kept as they go on trusting. God’s power is real and sufficient, and the means is a living faith that believers are called to maintain.

Reformed

Reformed readers read this as a strong text for the perseverance of the saints. God’s power guards believers, and faith itself is sustained by that power, so the salvation ready to be revealed will not be lost. Faith is the means God uses to keep, not a separate condition he waits on.

Key contrast Both affirm that God’s power guards believers through faith. Arminians typically stress faith as the ongoing condition through which God guards; Reformed readers typically stress God’s power as what preserves faith itself, securing final salvation.

1 Peter 1:6

6In this you greatly rejoice, though now for a little while, if need be, you have been grieved in various trials,

Arminian

Believers rejoice in this hope though grieved for a little while by various trials. Arminian readers note the pastoral realism: joy and grief coexist, and trials are temporary. The phrase if need be suggests that suffering has a purpose under God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in if need be a reminder that trials come under God’s wise providence. Nothing is random in the life of the believer. Present grief is real but brief compared with the coming salvation.

1 Peter 1:7

7that the proof of your faith, which is more precious than gold that perishes, even though it is tested by fire, may be found to result in praise, glory, and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ—

Arminian

Trials test faith, as fire tests gold, so that genuine faith may result in praise, glory, and honor at Christ’s revelation. Arminian readers stress that tested faith is proven and strengthened through endurance, and that the call to persevere is real.

Reformed

Reformed readers see testing as the refining of a faith God has given and will preserve. The fire does not destroy true faith but displays its genuineness. The outcome is praise and glory at Christ’s return.

1 Peter 1:8

8whom, not having known, you love. In him, though now you don’t see him, yet believing, you rejoice greatly with joy that is unspeakable and full of glory,

Arminian

Though they have not seen Christ, they love him and rejoice with inexpressible joy. Arminian readers highlight the relational heart of faith, a living love for an unseen Lord. Joy is the fruit of trusting him in the present.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that love for an unseen Christ is evidence of the Spirit’s work in the heart. Joy full of glory anticipates the coming glory now. Faith, love, and joy are woven together as gifts of grace.

1 Peter 1:9

9receiving the result of your faith, the salvation of your souls.

Arminian

They are receiving the outcome of faith, the salvation of their souls. Arminian readers see salvation here with a present and future dimension, enjoyed now and completed at the end by those who keep believing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the present tense receiving as the experience of a salvation already secured. What is begun will be completed. Both traditions agree salvation has present and future aspects.

1 Peter 1:10

10Concerning this salvation, the prophets sought and searched diligently. They prophesied of the grace that would come to you,

Arminian

The prophets searched carefully concerning this salvation and the grace meant for the church. Arminian readers note the continuity of God’s saving plan from the prophets to the gospel. The grace the prophets foresaw is now offered to all who hear.

Reformed

Reformed readers see one covenant purpose unfolding across redemptive history. The prophets served a plan larger than their own day. The grace they foretold has now come to Peter’s readers.

1 Peter 1:11

11searching for who or what kind of time the Spirit of Christ which was in them pointed to when he predicted the sufferings of Christ and the glories that would follow them.

Arminian

The Spirit of Christ in the prophets foretold Christ’s sufferings and the glories to follow. Arminian readers note that the phrase Spirit of Christ points to Christ’s preexistence and the Spirit’s role in prophecy, a point Reformed readers share. The pattern of suffering then glory frames the whole letter.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the Old Testament is Christ-centered by divine design; the same Spirit who inspired the prophets now proclaims Christ through the apostles. The suffering-glory pattern assures suffering believers that glory follows.

1 Peter 1:12

12To them it was revealed that they served not themselves, but you, in these things, which now have been announced to you through those who preached the Good News to you by the Holy Spirit sent out from heaven; which things angels desire to look into.

Arminian

The prophets learned they served not themselves but later believers, and the same message is now preached by the Holy Spirit. Arminian readers see the privilege of hearing the gospel in its fullness, a privilege angels long to look into.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the Spirit-empowered preaching through which God now reveals what was long hidden. The angels’ desire highlights the wonder of redemption. Both traditions share deep reverence for the gospel announced here.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
