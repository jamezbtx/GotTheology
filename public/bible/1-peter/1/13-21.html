Passage

1 Peter 1

Verses 13–21 · WEB

Because of this living hope, Peter turns to exhortation: prepare the mind, be sober, and set hope fully on coming grace. The holy God who called them summons them to holiness, and the Father who judges impartially calls for reverent fear during their exile. The ground of it all is redemption by the precious blood of Christ, a Lamb foreknown before creation and revealed for their sake. Both traditions read this as gospel-shaped holiness; they share the conviction that grace produces obedience, while differing on how the call relates to the response.

1 Peter 1:13

13Therefore prepare your minds for action. Be sober, and set your hope fully on the grace that will be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ—

Arminian

Therefore, believers are to prepare their minds, be sober, and set their hope fully on coming grace. Arminian readers stress the active responsibility this places on believers: hope is something they must fix and maintain.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the exhortation rests on the gospel indicative of verses 3 to 12. Hope is set on grace still to come, so the Christian life looks forward to what God will do. Grace both grounds and motivates the call.

1 Peter 1:14

14as children of obedience, not conforming yourselves according to your former lusts as in your ignorance,

Arminian

As obedient children, they must not be shaped by former desires. Arminian readers emphasize the real moral choice before believers: to refuse conformity to old patterns.

Reformed

Reformed readers see children of obedience as describing a new identity created by the new birth. The command flows from who they are. Former ignorance has been replaced by knowledge of God.

1 Peter 1:15

15but just as he who called you is holy, you yourselves also be holy in all of your behavior,

Arminian

The God who called them is holy, so they must be holy in all conduct. Arminian readers often take called here as the gospel summons answered in faith, which now obligates holy living.

Reformed

Reformed readers often read called as God’s effective call that brought them to faith, a call that produces holiness. Both agree that holiness is the necessary shape of a life claimed by God.

1 Peter 1:16

16because it is written, “You shall be holy, for I am holy.”

Arminian

Peter cites Leviticus: You shall be holy, for I am holy. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that God’s character is the standard and motive for holiness. The Old Testament call to Israel now applies to the church.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the continuity between Israel and the church as God’s holy people. Holiness is not optional ornament but the family likeness of God’s children.

1 Peter 1:17

17If you call on him as Father, who without respect of persons judges according to each man’s work, pass the time of your living as foreigners here in reverent fear,

Arminian

If they call on God as Father, the impartial Judge of each person’s work, they should live in reverent fear during their exile. Arminian readers stress real accountability for believers and the seriousness of persevering in holiness.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that judgment according to works is real for believers, while holding that works here evidence a genuine faith rather than earn standing. Fear here is reverent awe, not servile dread, for those who know God as Father.

1 Peter 1:18

18knowing that you were redeemed, not with corruptible things like silver or gold, from the useless way of life handed down from your fathers,

Arminian

They were redeemed not with perishable silver or gold from an empty inherited way of life. Arminian and Reformed readers alike see redemption as a costly liberation from futility. The phrase handed down from your fathers suggests many readers came from pagan backgrounds.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the definite accomplishment of redemption: Christ actually freed his people from a futile way of life. Both traditions share the contrast between perishable wealth and the priceless blood that follows.

1 Peter 1:19

19but with precious blood, as of a lamb without blemish or spot, the blood of Christ,

Arminian

Redemption came by the precious blood of Christ, like a spotless lamb. Arminian readers stress Christ’s atoning sacrifice as sufficient and offered for all who will believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the sacrificial and substitutionary character of the Lamb’s death, which secures redemption for God’s people. Both see Passover and sacrificial imagery fulfilled in Christ.

1 Peter 1:20

20who was foreknown indeed before the foundation of the world, but was revealed in this last age for your sake,

Arminian

Christ was foreknown before the foundation of the world and revealed in these last times. Arminian readers see God’s eternal plan to save through Christ, with the incarnation as its historical unveiling.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the eternal decree centered on Christ, the Lamb appointed before creation. God’s saving purpose is neither reactive nor improvised. Both agree that Christ’s coming marks the last age.

1 Peter 1:21

21who through him are believers in God, who raised him from the dead and gave him glory, so that your faith and hope might be in God.

Arminian

Through Christ they believe in God, who raised and glorified him, so that their faith and hope are in God. Arminian readers stress that faith is the human response to God’s work in Christ, directed fully to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that faith comes through Christ, as his gift and work. The resurrection and glorification of Christ secure the object of faith. Faith and hope rest on God, not on the believer.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
