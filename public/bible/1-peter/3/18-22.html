Passage

1 Peter 3

Verses 18–22 · WEB

Christ’s suffering, resurrection, and exaltation ground Peter’s encouragement to believers facing hostility. The movement from Noah’s rescue to baptism places their deliverance within God’s victory through judgment. The spirits in prison and baptismal language have several interpretations that do not divide neatly along Arminian and Reformed lines.

1 Peter 3:18

18Because Christ also suffered for sins once, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring you to God, being put to death in the flesh, but made alive in the Spirit,

Arminian

Christ’s once-for-all suffering brings the unrighteous to God through the righteous one. Arminian readers affirm substitution and emphasise that its benefits are received through faith. His death and life ground the hope of suffering believers; the contrast between flesh and Spirit is interpreted in more than one way.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm Christ’s sufficient, unrepeatable sacrifice and effective reconciliation of his people to God. The verse centres on Christ’s achievement rather than explicitly defining the extent of the atonement. Readings differ over whether Spirit refers especially to the Holy Spirit or Christ’s risen mode of life.

1 Peter 3:19

19in whom he also went and preached to the spirits in prison,

Arminian

The spirits are disputed: Christ may have preached through Noah to people now imprisoned, proclaimed victory to fallen angelic spirits, or descended to the dead to announce his work. Some descent readings include an offer of salvation, others do not. These options cross Arminian and Reformed lines; the passage should not establish a confident scheme of postmortem conversion.

Reformed

Reformed interpretations also differ: preaching through Noah, victory proclaimed to fallen spirits, and descent to the departed are distinct possibilities, not one agreed position. The angelic reading connects Noah’s setting with the Genesis 6 sons of God tradition reflected in 1 Enoch. Peter’s clear pastoral direction is toward Christ’s vindication, though the proclamation’s audience and timing remain debated.

1 Peter 3:20

20who before were disobedient when God waited patiently in the days of Noah while the ship was being built. In it, few, that is, eight souls, were saved through water.

Arminian

God’s patience in Noah’s day and the rescue of eight people encourage a vulnerable minority. Arminian readers stress that patience gives space for repentance without removing judgment. Noah’s setting supports the competing interpretations of verse 19 but does not by itself settle whether the spirits are human or angelic.

Reformed

Reformed readers see rescue through judgment as the link between Noah’s household and the baptismal comparison that follows. God preserved a few while the world remained disobedient. Both traditions recognise that the water imagery holds judgment and deliverance together rather than attributing saving power to water by itself.

1 Peter 3:21

21This is a symbol of baptism, which now saves you—not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God—through the resurrection of Jesus Christ,

Arminian

Baptism saves through Christ’s resurrection, not bodily washing. Arminian readings include baptism as a sign of faith and as a sacramental means of grace received in faith; neither makes bare ritual sufficient. The good conscience expression is variously understood as an appeal, pledge, or answer toward God. These readings overlap with Reformed interpretations rather than forming a simple denominational divide.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly connect the saving language to the reality signified and sealed in baptism, received through faith by the Spirit. Some emphasise sacramental instrumentality, while others foreground the sign’s relation to union with Christ; the rite does not save automatically. Appeal, pledge, and answer readings of conscience cross traditions. Peter locates saving efficacy in Christ’s resurrection, not physical cleansing.

Key contrast Both ground salvation in Christ’s resurrection and reject bodily washing as sufficient. Arminian and Reformed readers differ internally over baptism as sign, pledge, appeal, and sacramental means; those differences do not align neatly with their broader disagreement about grace.

1 Peter 3:22

22who is at the right hand of God, having gone into heaven, angels and authorities and powers being made subject to him.

Arminian

The risen Christ rules over every spiritual power. Arminian readers see assurance that suffering believers need not fear hostile forces as ultimate authorities. His victory, rather than speculation about the spirits, supplies the paragraph’s firm conclusion.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasise Christ’s exaltation and the subjection of every rival power. The suffering righteous one now reigns, assuring his people that evil will not have the last word. Whatever view one takes of verses 19–21, Christ’s lordship remains central.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
