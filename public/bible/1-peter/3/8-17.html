Passage

1 Peter 3

Verses 8–17 · WEB

Peter widens his counsel from households to the whole community, drawing on a psalm to commend peace, truthful speech, and prayer. He then prepares believers for unjust suffering and asks them to explain their hope with humility. Shared trust in God should produce gentle witness rather than retaliation.

1 Peter 3:8

8Finally, all of you be like-minded, compassionate, loving as brothers, tenderhearted, courteous,

Arminian

Peter now addresses the whole community, making compassion and shared affection everyone’s calling. Arminian readers see unity as something believers actively cultivate through grace. Like-mindedness is not uniform personality but a common commitment to Christ expressed in tenderhearted treatment of others.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the common life of God’s people taking shape in mutual sympathy and love. Grace creates a family whose members must act accordingly. Both traditions recognise that unity requires practical kindness, not merely agreement on doctrine.

1 Peter 3:9

9not rendering evil for evil or insult for insult; but instead blessing, knowing that you were called to this, that you may inherit a blessing.

Arminian

Believers answer hostility with blessing rather than retaliation. Arminian readers emphasise the calling to respond as Christ did and to continue on the path toward the promised inheritance. This obedience depends on grace and does not purchase God’s blessing.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that those called to inherit blessing are also called to bless their enemies. God’s promise forms their conduct rather than licensing revenge. Both traditions see non-retaliation as trust in God, not indifference to justice or protection.

1 Peter 3:10

10For, “He who would love life and see good days, let him keep his tongue from evil and his lips from speaking deceit.

Arminian

Peter uses the psalm to connect the desire for life with truthful speech. Arminian readers see a concrete response to grace: refusing slander and deceit even under pressure. The instruction is not a promise that faithful people will avoid every hardship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see guarded speech as part of a life shaped by God’s covenant care. The psalm’s wisdom describes faithful living without cancelling Peter’s warnings about suffering. Truthfulness is especially important when believers are tempted to answer insult with insult.

1 Peter 3:11

11Let him turn away from evil and do good. Let him seek peace and pursue it.

Arminian

Peace requires both turning from evil and actively seeking good. Arminian readers stress responsible, grace-enabled action rather than merely avoiding conflict. Pursuing peace does not require concealing injustice or abandoning those who need protection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see repentance and peacemaking as fruits of God’s renewing work. Peace is pursued deliberately, not assumed to arise from passivity. Both traditions can affirm that reconciliation must be truthful and cannot be built on tolerating harm.

1 Peter 3:12

12For the eyes of the Lord are on the righteous, and his ears open to their prayer; but the face of the Lord is against those who do evil.”

Arminian

God attends to the righteous and opposes wrongdoing. Arminian readers find encouragement to pray and persevere in faithful conduct, not a formula for earning his attention. His opposition to evil also assures suffering believers that their mistreatment matters.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress God’s attentive care for his people and his holy opposition to evil. Righteous conduct is the fruit of grace rather than a claim upon God apart from Christ. The verse supports prayerful endurance when immediate circumstances seem to contradict divine care.

1 Peter 3:13

13Now who will harm you if you become imitators of that which is good?

Arminian

Doing good ordinarily removes grounds for hostility, yet verse 14 immediately allows for unjust suffering. Arminian readers hear encouragement to remain zealous for good without expecting guaranteed safety. Faithfulness is worthwhile even when neighbours respond badly.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read this question with the qualification that follows. God’s care does not mean that faithful people cannot be injured. The question strengthens moral courage rather than promising immunity from persecution.

1 Peter 3:14

14But even if you should suffer for righteousness’ sake, you are blessed. “Don’t fear what they fear, neither be troubled.”

Arminian

Suffering for righteousness does not mean God has withdrawn his blessing. Arminian readers stress trust that resists intimidation while acknowledging real pain. The command against fear is encouragement for vulnerable people, not blame for the distress they experience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see blessing grounded in God’s favour rather than the approval of persecutors. The echo of Isaiah directs fear away from threatening people and toward confidence in God. Both traditions should offer reassurance rather than shame to frightened believers.

1 Peter 3:15

15But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts. Always be ready to give an answer to everyone who asks you a reason concerning the hope that is in you, with humility and fear,

Arminian

Reverence for God grounds a ready explanation of Christian hope. Arminian readers stress sincere witness that invites a response without manipulation. Humility and reverence govern both the content and manner of the answer; this is not permission to humiliate an opponent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise ground witness in devotion to the Lord rather than confidence in argumentative skill. Believers explain their hope while depending on God to work through their words. Gentleness belongs to faithful apologetics, especially when the questioner is hostile.

1 Peter 3:16

16having a good conscience. Thus, while you are spoken against as evildoers, they may be disappointed who curse your good way of life in Christ.

Arminian

A good conscience joins truthful witness to consistent conduct. Arminian readers stress that grace enables believers to answer slander without becoming slanderers. The possible exposure of false accusations is a hope, not a guarantee of immediate vindication.

Reformed

Reformed readers see integrity as the fitting accompaniment to the defence of hope. A life in Christ can expose accusations as groundless, even if opponents do not admit it. Both traditions distinguish a clear conscience from self-righteous insistence that every criticism must be persecution.

1 Peter 3:17

17For it is better, if it is God’s will, that you suffer for doing what is right than for doing evil.

Arminian

Peter distinguishes suffering for good from consequences of wrongdoing. Arminian readers understand God as permitting trials within his care, without morally approving the evil of those who inflict them. The call is to faithfulness, not to manufacture suffering or refuse lawful protection.

Reformed

Reformed readers place even unjust suffering within God’s wise providence while maintaining the responsibility of wrongdoers. The contrast concerns why believers suffer, not whether suffering itself is good. Seeking help and resisting injustice can accompany trust in God’s will.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
