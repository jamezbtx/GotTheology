Passage

1 Peter 3

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Continuing the household instructions, Peter addresses wives and husbands among the suffering communities of Asia Minor. He calls for faithful witness, inward beauty, and honour for wives as fellow heirs of grace, locating household conduct within accountability to God. Applications of marital roles differ within both Arminian and Reformed traditions, while the passage must not be used to excuse abuse.

1 Peter 3:1

1In the same way, wives, be in subjection to your own husbands, so that, even if any don’t obey the Word, they may be won by the behavior of their wives without a word,

Arminian

Peter addresses wives whose husbands may reject the gospel, continuing his counsel about faithful conduct under pressure. Arminian readers stress witness through grace-enabled behaviour, not a guarantee that a husband will convert. Applications of submission differ within both traditions; this instruction does not authorise coercion or require remaining in danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see patient conduct as a means God may use to draw an unbelieving husband to faith. The household setting shapes the instruction without making the husband a spiritual saviour. Reformed applications differ over marital roles, but submission cannot excuse abuse or silence a request for protection.

1 Peter 3:2

2seeing your pure behavior in fear.

Arminian

Pure conduct and reverence give substance to the witness just described. Arminian readers understand this as lived faith made possible by grace, not moral effort that earns another person’s salvation. Fear here should be read alongside Peter’s later encouragement not to be terrified.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that reverent, faithful conduct commends the gospel without controlling its reception. The emphasis is on the character of the believer rather than outward religious argument. Reverence for God must not be confused with treating intimidation by a spouse as a virtue.

1 Peter 3:3

3Let your beauty come not from the outward adorning of braiding your hair, and of wearing gold ornaments or of putting on fine clothing,

Arminian

Peter contrasts outward display with the inward beauty described next. Arminian readers stress the priority of transformed character over social status or attractiveness. Interpretations of particular adornments vary, but the passage does not make a woman’s worth depend on her appearance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise read this contrast in light of the heart’s adornment in verse 4. Some apply the listed restrictions more literally, while others see a warning against ostentatious display. Both approaches should preserve Peter’s concern for godliness rather than police women’s value by their clothing.

1 Peter 3:4

4but from the hidden person of the heart, in the incorruptible adornment of a gentle and quiet spirit, which is very precious in God’s sight.

Arminian

The gentle spirit is precious to God even when society overlooks it. Arminian readers see grace shaping an inward character that believers are called to cultivate. Gentleness is not voicelessness, and a quiet spirit need not mean refusing to speak truth or seek help.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasise the Spirit’s inward work producing beauty that does not fade. God’s valuation overturns external measures of importance. Like Arminian readers, they can distinguish humble strength from passivity in the face of wrongdoing.

1 Peter 3:5

5For this is how in the past the holy women who hoped in God also adorned themselves, being in subjection to their own husbands.

Arminian

The women Peter commends placed their hope in God. Arminian readers stress that their conduct flowed from trust rather than a husband’s entitlement to unquestioning obedience. Their example invites faithful dependence on God within the pressures of household life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see hope in God as the source of the conduct Peter praises. The example concerns holiness rooted in divine faithfulness, not the superiority of men. Readers differ over how the household pattern applies today while sharing the call to trust God.

1 Peter 3:6

6So Sarah obeyed Abraham, calling him lord, whose children you now are if you do well and are not put in fear by any terror.

Arminian

Sarah’s example joins doing good with freedom from terror. Arminian readers see a call to courageous faith expressed in action, not a command to imitate every decision in Abraham’s household. Belonging to this family of faith is described through its lived resemblance.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand Sarah’s children as those who display the faith and good conduct Peter commends. The conditional language calls for a visible expression of trust rather than earning spiritual ancestry. Not being terrified also guards against reading submission as fearful surrender to intimidation.

1 Peter 3:7

7You husbands, in the same way, live with your wives according to knowledge, giving honor to the woman as to the weaker vessel, as also being joint heirs of the grace of life, that your prayers may not be hindered.

Arminian

Husbands must honour their wives as fellow heirs of life’s grace. Arminian readers stress equal standing before God and responsible, considerate conduct; the weaker vessel language need not imply moral or spiritual inferiority. Whatever its physical or social reference, the command opposes domination and makes mistreatment a matter of accountability to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that wives share fully in salvation and must receive understanding and honour. Interpretations of the weaker vessel vary, including physical vulnerability and social disadvantage, but spiritual inferiority is not the point. Hindered prayer warns that religious practice cannot be separated from the way a husband treats his wife.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
