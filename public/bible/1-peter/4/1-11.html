Passage

1 Peter 4

Verses 1–11 · WEB

Peter applies Christ’s suffering to believers who face social hostility after abandoning former practices. Judgment and life with God frame the call to holy living, while the nearness of the end directs the community toward prayer, love, hospitality, and service. Every gift is to serve others and glorify God through Christ.

1 Peter 4:1

1Therefore, since Christ suffered for us in the flesh, arm yourselves also with the same mind; for he who has suffered in the flesh has ceased from sin,

Arminian

Christ’s suffering calls believers to a settled resolve against sin. Arminian readers understand ceasing from sin as a break with its rule, not the claim that pain automatically removes all wrongdoing. They may read the sufferer as the believer following Christ or connect the statement closely with Christ’s own victory.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a decisive break with sin’s dominion rather than sinless perfection produced by hardship. The identification of the sufferer is debated, but Christ’s example governs the command. Grace strengthens believers to choose faithfulness even when obedience is costly.

1 Peter 4:2

2that you no longer should live the rest of your time in the flesh for the lusts of men, but for the will of God.

Arminian

The purpose of this resolve is a life directed by God’s will rather than disordered desire. Arminian readers stress continuing, grace-enabled obedience in the time that remains. Both traditions see conversion changing the direction of ordinary embodied life, not dismissing the body as evil.

Reformed

Reformed readers see renewed desires expressed in a life increasingly conformed to God’s will. Sanctification is the fruit of belonging to Christ, yet it requires active resistance to sin. Like Arminian readers, they understand this as a new allegiance rather than instant moral completion.

1 Peter 4:3

3For we have spent enough of our past time doing the desire of the Gentiles, and having walked in lewdness, lusts, drunken binges, orgies, carousings, and abominable idolatries.

Arminian

Peter declares the former pattern of excess and idolatry sufficient: believers need not return to it. Arminian readers stress the real break repentance demands and the help of grace in sustaining it. The list names destructive practices, not an ethnic judgment on every non-Jewish person.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the old life contrasted with the holiness of those God has called. Past participation does not define believers’ future or make grace unnecessary. Both traditions read the warning as a call to leave idolatrous patterns rather than despise outsiders.

1 Peter 4:4

4They think it is strange that you don’t run with them into the same excess of riot, speaking evil of you.

Arminian

Changed conduct can provoke bewilderment and slander from former companions. Arminian readers stress the cost of a faithful response to grace without treating social rejection as proof that every Christian choice is wise. Peter prepares believers to resist pressure without contempt for their neighbours.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see estrangement as a possible consequence of God’s renewing work. The community need not return to destructive practices to recover approval. Its witness should remain patient and truthful even when others misrepresent its motives.

1 Peter 4:5

5They will give account to him who is ready to judge the living and the dead.

Arminian

Those who malign believers remain accountable to the judge of all. Arminian readers find a reason to leave vengeance to God and continue inviting repentance. Neither death nor social power removes moral responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress God’s universal judicial authority over both the living and the dead. This assures the persecuted that wrong will not be overlooked. Both traditions distinguish confidence in final justice from permission to delight in an opponent’s ruin.

1 Peter 4:6

6For to this end the Good News was preached even to the dead, that they might be judged indeed as men in the flesh, but live as to God in the spirit.

Arminian

The dead may be believers who heard the gospel while alive and have since died; physical death has not defeated their life with God. Other readings take them as spiritually dead when evangelised, or connect the verse with proclamation to the departed. Arminian interpreters differ, and the verse does not clearly establish a further opportunity after death.

Reformed

Reformed readers can understand this as reassurance about Christians now dead who received the gospel during life. Readings involving spiritual death or proclamation in the realm of the dead also arise in discussion, without forming a simple Arminian–Reformed divide. Whether judged refers to human condemnation or mortal judgment is debated; the central reassurance is life before God.

1 Peter 4:7

7But the end of all things is near. Therefore be of sound mind, self-controlled, and sober in prayer.

Arminian

The nearness of the end calls for clear thinking and prayer, not speculation or panic. Arminian readers stress watchful participation in the life grace makes possible. End-time hope turns believers toward sober dependence on God in the present.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see expectation of Christ’s consummated reign producing disciplined prayer. Peter does not provide a calendar for calculating dates. Confidence in God’s purpose should deepen practical attentiveness rather than encourage withdrawal from ordinary responsibilities.

1 Peter 4:8

8And above all things be earnest in your love among yourselves, for love covers a multitude of sins.

Arminian

Earnest love refuses to magnify every offence and seeks forgiveness. Arminian readers stress the grace-enabled practice that keeps a pressured community together. Covering sins is not atoning for them, concealing abuse, or refusing the truth needed for repentance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see forgiving love as a fruit of God’s prior mercy. Love bears with ordinary failings rather than feeding resentment and division. Both traditions should distinguish this generosity from hiding serious wrongdoing or preventing accountability.

1 Peter 4:9

9Be hospitable to one another without grumbling.

Arminian

Hospitality turns love into shared space and practical care. Arminian readers stress willing service rather than reluctant compliance, with grace sustaining generosity under pressure. The instruction includes the attitude of the host as well as the outward act.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see hospitality as stewardship of what God has given. A vulnerable community needs more than words of welcome. Freedom from grumbling expresses gratitude to God, without requiring people to ignore reasonable limits or safety.

1 Peter 4:10

10As each has received a gift, employ it in serving one another, as good managers of the grace of God in its various forms.

Arminian

Each gift is received, not self-created, and is entrusted for others’ good. Arminian readers emphasise responsible use of God’s varied grace rather than competition over status. The community flourishes as believers actively serve with what they have been given.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the divine source and diversity of gifts. Stewardship rules out both pride in one’s abilities and refusal to use them. Both traditions understand ministry as the whole community’s service, not merely the activity of a privileged few.

1 Peter 4:11

11If anyone speaks, let it be as it were the very words of God. If anyone serves, let it be as of the strength which God supplies, that in all things God may be glorified through Jesus Christ, to whom belong the glory and the dominion forever and ever. Amen.

Arminian

Speaking and serving depend on God’s provision and aim at his glory through Christ. Arminian readers stress faithful stewardship empowered by grace. Speaking as God’s words requires serious care with the message, not treating every personal opinion as revelation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God supplying both the message to be honoured and the strength for service. The resulting glory belongs to God rather than the minister. Both traditions find here dependence that energises action instead of replacing it with passivity.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
