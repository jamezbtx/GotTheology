Passage

1 Peter 4

Verses 12–19 · WEB

Peter returns to the trials of communities suffering for Christ, distinguishing that suffering from the consequences of wrongdoing. Present testing is set against the revelation of Christ’s glory and the seriousness of divine judgment. The closing appeal joins trust in the faithful Creator with persistent good conduct.

1 Peter 4:12

12Beloved, don’t be astonished at the fiery trial which has come upon you to test you, as though a strange thing happened to you.

Arminian

The fiery trial should not be mistaken for evidence that believers no longer belong to God. Arminian readers see testing as an occasion for faith to be exercised and strengthened through grace. Peter normalises costly discipleship without calling suffering painless or every injury necessary.

Reformed

Reformed readers see trials within God’s refining care rather than outside his providence. Testing exposes and strengthens faith, but the text does not identify every sufferer’s precise lesson. Both traditions should comfort those in pain rather than accuse them of failing to understand God’s plan.

1 Peter 4:13

13But because you are partakers of Christ’s sufferings, rejoice, that at the revelation of his glory you also may rejoice with exceeding joy.

Arminian

Sharing Christ’s sufferings anticipates joy at his revealed glory. Arminian readers stress enduring allegiance to Christ, sustained by hope rather than delight in pain itself. The believer’s suffering neither repeats his atonement nor adds to its saving worth.

Reformed

Reformed readers ground this hope in union with the suffering and exalted Christ. Present affliction cannot cancel the glory he will reveal. Both traditions distinguish participation in his path from contributing to his unique redeeming sacrifice.

1 Peter 4:14

14If you are insulted for the name of Christ, you are blessed, because the Spirit of glory and of God rests on you. On their part he is blasphemed, but on your part he is glorified.

Arminian

Insult for Christ’s name can coexist with the Spirit’s gracious presence. Arminian readers hear assurance that faithful witness is not abandoned when it is rejected. The blessing concerns belonging to Christ, not the idea that every insult a Christian receives proves righteousness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Spirit’s resting on believers as divine honour amid public shame. God’s verdict outweighs the verdict of hostile observers. The verse calls for confidence in his presence rather than the pursuit of conflict for its own sake.

1 Peter 4:15

15But let none of you suffer as a murderer, or a thief, or an evil doer, or a meddler in other men’s matters.

Arminian

Peter refuses to confuse punishment for wrongdoing with suffering for Christ. Arminian readers stress accountable choices, including restraint from intrusive behaviour toward others. Grace is not an excuse for criminal conduct or self-important interference.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise insist that a Christian identity does not sanctify harmful behaviour. The inclusion of meddling brings the warning close to everyday conduct, not only obvious crimes. Believers must test the cause of their suffering rather than automatically claim persecution.

1 Peter 4:16

16But if one of you suffers for being a Christian, let him not be ashamed; but let him glorify God in this matter.

Arminian

Suffering specifically for belonging to Christ need not bring shame. Arminian readers emphasise faithful confession and praise under pressure, supported by God’s grace. This does not forbid grief, fear, or asking others for help.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Christian name as a reason for honouring God when society uses it as a reproach. Assurance rests in belonging to Christ rather than public respectability. Both traditions encourage courage without demanding emotional invulnerability.

1 Peter 4:17

17For the time has come for judgment to begin with the household of God. If it begins first with us, what will happen to those who don’t obey the Good News of God?

Arminian

Judgment begins with God’s own household, so membership is not a reason for complacency. Arminian readers hear a serious call to faithful endurance and repentance, while distinguishing testing from automatic condemnation. The fate of those rejecting the gospel gives the warning its solemn force.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly understand the household’s judgment as purifying discipline, distinct from the final condemnation of unbelief. Such warnings are among the means God uses to preserve his people. Both traditions recognise that Peter comforts the church without exempting it from searching divine evaluation.

1 Peter 4:18

18“If it is hard for the righteous to be saved, what will happen to the ungodly and the sinner?”

Arminian

The righteous are saved through severe trial, not by an easy route or by their own merit. Arminian readers connect the warning with the need to continue in faith through enabling grace; many understand final apostasy as a genuine danger. The proverb underscores the seriousness of judgment rather than suggesting God is barely able to save.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand the difficulty as the trials through which God surely brings his people, not uncertainty about Christ’s sufficient work. Perseverance is secured by grace and includes real warnings and endurance. The contrast with the ungodly sharpens the call to holiness, not a calculation of how much merit is enough.

Key contrast Both see salvation through costly trial rather than human merit. Classical Arminian readers commonly allow the possibility of abandoning saving faith, with some internal diversity; Reformed readers understand persevering faith as secured by God’s preserving grace. This verse itself emphasises difficulty and judgment rather than fully resolving that debate.

1 Peter 4:19

19Therefore let them also who suffer according to the will of God in doing good entrust their souls to him, as to a faithful Creator.

Arminian

Sufferers entrust themselves to a faithful Creator while continuing to do good. Arminian readers commonly stress God’s permission of suffering and his redemptive care without making him the moral author of evil. Trust remains active, and seeking safety or justice need not contradict submission to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly stress that suffering falls within God’s sovereign providence, while wrongdoers remain responsible and God is not the author of sin. The faithful Creator can therefore be trusted amid events believers cannot explain. Both traditions join confidence in God to doing good rather than fatalism or refusal of help.

Key contrast Both affirm God’s faithful rule and reject making him the moral author of evil. Arminian readings commonly frame unjust suffering through divine permission and care; Reformed readings commonly stress sovereign ordination and providence. Neither requires passive acceptance of abuse.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
