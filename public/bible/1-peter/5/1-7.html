Passage

1 Peter 5

Verses 1–7 · WEB

Peter closes his letter to scattered, suffering believers by turning to church life. He addresses elders as a fellow elder and witness of Christ’s sufferings, charging them to shepherd God’s flock willingly, honestly, and by example, under the chief Shepherd who will reward them. He then calls younger believers, and finally everyone, to clothe themselves with humility, to humble themselves under God’s mighty hand, and to cast every worry on the God who cares for them. Arminian and Reformed readers share nearly all of this pastoral ground; their differences are mostly of emphasis.

1 Peter 5:1

1Therefore I exhort the elders among you, as a fellow elder and a witness of the sufferings of Christ, and who will also share in the glory that will be revealed:

Arminian

“Therefore” ties this charge to the suffering just described in chapter 4: a church under pressure needs faithful leaders. Peter, an apostle, speaks to elders as a fellow elder, appealing rather than commanding from above. Arminian readers note that he holds suffering and future glory together, and that sharing in that glory is set before those who remain faithful in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Peter grounding pastoral authority in Christ’s suffering and coming glory rather than in personal status. His humility models what he is about to ask of elders. That he “will also share in the glory” is spoken with settled confidence, a certainty Reformed theology grounds in God’s keeping power, which Peter already stressed in 1:5.

1 Peter 5:2

2shepherd the flock of God which is among you, exercising the oversight, not under compulsion, but voluntarily; not for dishonest gain, but willingly;

Arminian

The flock belongs to God, not to the elders; they are under-shepherds entrusted with it. Peter names two dangers, serving grudgingly and serving for money, and calls for willing, eager oversight. Arminian readers hear a call to cultivate the right motives, which grace enables but which leaders must actually choose and keep choosing.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that “the flock of God” means the church is God’s possession, bought by Christ, so shepherding is stewardship of what belongs to him. Willing service flows from hearts renewed by grace. Both traditions draw the same practical point: pastoral work done for gain or under duress betrays the flock’s true owner.

1 Peter 5:3

3not as lording it over those entrusted to you, but making yourselves examples to the flock.

Arminian

Leadership in the church is not domination. Elders lead chiefly by example, living what they teach. Arminian readers see here a pattern of influence through persuasion and character rather than coercion, consistent with how they understand God’s own dealings with people.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise reject heavy-handed rule, often connecting this verse to Jesus’ teaching that leaders among his people must serve rather than lord it over others. The flock is “entrusted” to elders, a reminder that they will give an account. Shared ground here is wide: both traditions prize servant leadership and holy example.

1 Peter 5:4

4When the chief Shepherd is revealed, you will receive the crown of glory that doesn’t fade away.

Arminian

Christ is the chief Shepherd; elders are his helpers. Faithful service looks ahead to his appearing and to a crown that does not fade, unlike the withering wreaths of ancient victors. Arminian readers often read the promise as a genuine reward set before those who persevere in faithful ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the crown is real reward, while emphasizing that it is a gift of grace and not earned merit; God crowns what he himself worked in his servants. The fixed promise, “you will receive,” grounds pastoral endurance in Christ’s certain return. Both traditions use this verse to steady weary leaders.

1 Peter 5:5

5Likewise, you younger ones, be subject to the elder. Yes, all of you clothe yourselves with humility and subject yourselves to one another; for “God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble.”

Arminian

Peter turns from elders to younger members and then to the whole church. Humility is to be worn like a garment, and mutual submission marks every relationship. Quoting Proverbs 3:34, he warns that God opposes the proud. Arminian readers often note that grace is given to the humble, so humility is the posture in which grace is received rather than resisted.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the same proverb as a statement of how God deals with people: he resists pride and gives grace to the lowly. They typically add that humility itself is a fruit of grace, since God first humbles the proud before he lifts them up. Both traditions agree that pride and the gospel cannot coexist.

Key contrast Both read the proverb as a real pattern of God’s dealing. Arminians often treat humility as the receptive posture in which grace is welcomed rather than resisted; Reformed readers typically treat humility as itself the work of grace in a person.

1 Peter 5:6

6Humble yourselves therefore under the mighty hand of God, that he may exalt you in due time,

Arminian

“Humble yourselves” is a command. Believers are to bow willingly under God’s mighty hand, the same hand that delivered Israel from Egypt and now presses on them through trials. Arminian readers stress that the response is truly ours to make, and that God promises to lift up the humble at the right time.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that God’s mighty hand governs the trials his people face, so humbling oneself means trusting his providence rather than resisting it. “In due time” leaves the timing to God’s wisdom. Both traditions hold the command and the promise together: we humble ourselves, and God does the exalting.

1 Peter 5:7

7casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you.

Arminian

Humility shows itself in prayerful dependence: throwing every anxiety onto God. The reason is personal and tender: he cares for you. Arminian readers often stress that this care is sincerely extended, inviting every believer to come with their burdens.

Reformed

Reformed readers find the same comfort, linking it to God’s fatherly providence: the One who governs all things cares for each of his children. Pride carries its own worries; humility hands them to God. On this verse the two traditions stand on almost entirely shared ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
