Passage

1 Peter 5

Verses 8–14 · WEB

Peter ends with a warning, a promise, and greetings. Believers must be sober and watchful, because the devil prowls like a roaring lion, and they must resist him firm in the faith, knowing Christians everywhere suffer the same way. Then comes a benediction: after a little suffering, the God of all grace will himself restore, establish, strengthen, and settle them. The letter closes with greetings through Silvanus, from the church “in Babylon” and from Mark, and a blessing of peace. Arminian and Reformed readers both treat this as a call to vigilance and a promise of God’s keeping; they weigh those two notes somewhat differently.

1 Peter 5:8

8Be sober and self-controlled. Be watchful. Your adversary, the devil, walks around like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour.

Arminian

Peter names a real enemy. The devil is pictured as a hungry lion looking for prey, so believers must stay clear-headed and alert. Arminian readers take the warning seriously as addressed to believers: spiritual danger is real, and vigilance is part of continuing in the faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers also take the threat seriously, noting that the lion roars and prowls but remains under God’s sovereign limits, as in Job. Watchfulness is one of the means God uses to keep his people. The two traditions share the call to sobriety; they differ mainly in how they relate warnings like this to the question of whether true believers can finally fall away.

Key contrast Arminians commonly read warnings like this as signaling a real possibility that a believer could be overcome and fall away. Reformed theology commonly reads them as God’s appointed means of preserving the elect, who will heed them, rather than as a sign that the elect could finally perish.

1 Peter 5:9

9Withstand him steadfast in your faith, knowing that your brothers who are in the world are undergoing the same sufferings.

Arminian

Believers resist the devil by standing firm in faith, not by their own strength. Knowing that Christians worldwide face the same sufferings guards against the lie that they are alone or uniquely forsaken. Arminian readers stress the believer’s active resistance, enabled by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that faith itself is the means of resistance, and faith is God’s gift that he sustains. The worldwide family of suffering believers is a reminder that trials are the ordinary path of the church, not proof of God’s absence. Both traditions find great pastoral comfort in this shared experience.

1 Peter 5:10

10But may the God of all grace, who called you to his eternal glory by Christ Jesus, after you have suffered a little while, perfect, establish, strengthen, and settle you.

Arminian

After the command to resist comes the promise: God himself will restore and strengthen. He is “the God of all grace,” and suffering lasts only “a little while” compared with eternal glory. Arminian readers rejoice in God’s strengthening grace and understand his call as a gracious summons that believers receive and continue in by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers often treat this verse as a strong comfort for the perseverance of the saints: the God who called them to eternal glory will himself perfect, establish, strengthen, and settle them. They typically read the call here as God’s effectual call, which brings about what it summons.

Key contrast Both see God as the source of strength that sustains believers. Arminians typically understand the call as a sincere, grace-enabled summons that can be resisted; Reformed theology typically understands it as the effectual call that infallibly brings the called to glory.

1 Peter 5:11

11To him be the glory and the power forever and ever. Amen.

Arminian

Peter’s promise turns into praise. Glory and power belong to God forever. Arminian readers note that the doxology fits a letter that has kept God’s faithfulness at the center from beginning to end.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the doxology as the natural end of all theology: since salvation is God’s work from calling to glory, all glory belongs to him. The two traditions join in the same “Amen.”

1 Peter 5:12

12Through Silvanus, our faithful brother, as I consider him, I have written to you briefly, exhorting and testifying that this is the true grace of God in which you stand.

Arminian

Silvanus, likely the Silas who traveled with Paul, is commended as a faithful brother. Scholars debate whether he carried the letter, helped write it, or both. Peter sums up his purpose: to encourage and testify that the grace they have received is the true grace of God, and to urge them to stand firm in it.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that believers “stand” in grace: their footing is God’s favor in Christ, not their own performance. Like Arminians, they read the closing appeal as a call to remain where grace has placed them. The open question of Silvanus’s exact role is a historical matter, not a theological dividing line.

1 Peter 5:13

13She who is in Babylon, chosen together with you, greets you. So does Mark, my son.

Arminian

“She who is in Babylon” is most often read as the church in Rome, using Babylon as a code name for the capital of a powerful, hostile world, though some take it literally. The church is “chosen together with you,” sharing the same election as the scattered believers addressed in 1:1. Arminian readers typically understand that election corporately and in Christ, as the elect people received by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers read “chosen together with you” in line with 1:1–2, where believers are elect according to God’s foreknowledge, and typically understand this as God’s gracious, personal choice of his people. Mark, whom Peter calls “my son,” is probably John Mark, a spiritual son in the faith. Both traditions agree that this greeting binds distant churches into one chosen family.

Key contrast Arminians commonly understand this election as corporate and in Christ, with individuals belonging to it through faith, grounded in God’s foreknowledge of that faith. Reformed theology commonly understands it as God’s unconditional, personal choice of those he will save.

1 Peter 5:14

14Greet one another with a kiss of love. Peace be to all of you who are in Christ Jesus. Amen.

Arminian

The letter ends with family warmth: a kiss of love, the customary greeting of the early church, expressing real affection among believers. Peace, so needed by a suffering church, is pronounced on all who are in Christ. Arminian readers note that this peace is found in union with Christ, received by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see peace as the gift of being in Christ, the believer’s secure standing through his work. For a persecuted people the final word is not fear but peace. On this closing benediction both traditions stand together.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
