Passage

1 Peter 2

Verses 1–10 · WEB

Born again through the enduring word, believers must put away malice and deceit and crave the pure spiritual milk that makes them grow. Peter then shifts images: coming to Christ, the living stone rejected by men but chosen by God, they become living stones in a spiritual house and a holy priesthood. Three Old Testament stone texts show Christ as honor for believers and stumbling for the disobedient. Peter closes by giving the church Israel’s titles: a chosen race, royal priesthood, holy nation, and people for God’s possession. Both traditions treasure this passage; they read verse 8’s appointment and the election language of verse 9 differently.

1 Peter 2:1

1Putting away therefore all wickedness, all deceit, hypocrisies, envies, and all evil speaking,

Arminian

Believers must put away all malice, deceit, hypocrisy, envy, and slander. Arminian readers stress the real effort required to lay aside sins that destroy the brotherly love of 1:22.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the therefore linking this command to the new birth just described. Those born of the imperishable word must strip off what belongs to the old life. The vices listed are especially sins of community.

1 Peter 2:2

2as newborn babies, long for the pure spiritual milk, that with it you may grow,

Arminian

Like newborn babies, they should crave pure spiritual milk in order to grow. Arminian readers emphasize that growth is expected and that believers must actively desire nourishment.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the milk as the word of God, continuing the theme of 1:23 to 25. Growth comes through the ordinary means God provides, and longing for the word is itself a sign of new life.

1 Peter 2:3

3if indeed you have tasted that the Lord is gracious.

Arminian

If indeed they have tasted that the Lord is good, drawing on Psalm 34. Arminian readers see the if as an appeal to their real experience of grace, which should stir further longing.

Reformed

Reformed readers read the if as assumed fact: since they have tasted, they should crave more. Experienced grace fuels desire for the word. Both agree the Lord here is Christ.

1 Peter 2:4

4Come to him, a living stone, rejected indeed by men, but chosen by God, precious.

Arminian

Coming to Christ, the living stone rejected by men but chosen and precious to God. Arminian readers stress the ongoing action of coming, the continual turning of faith toward Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Christ is the chosen one par excellence. Human rejection does not undo God’s choice. Believers share his chosen status by union with him.

1 Peter 2:5

5You also as living stones are built up as a spiritual house, to be a holy priesthood, to offer up spiritual sacrifices, acceptable to God through Jesus Christ.

Arminian

Believers are living stones built into a spiritual house and a holy priesthood offering spiritual sacrifices through Christ. Arminian and Reformed readers both see the priesthood of all believers here. Every member has access and service before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the church is God’s temple, built by God, with sacrifices acceptable only through Jesus Christ. Spiritual sacrifices include praise, good works, and lives offered to God.

1 Peter 2:6

6Because it is contained in Scripture, “Behold, I lay in Zion a chief cornerstone, chosen and precious. He who believes in him will not be disappointed.”

Arminian

Peter cites Isaiah 28: God lays in Zion a chosen and precious cornerstone, and whoever believes will not be put to shame. Arminian readers stress the universal offer: whoever believes.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God lays the stone; Christ’s place is God’s own act. The promise to the believer is sure. Both agree faith in Christ is the way of honor.

1 Peter 2:7

7For you who believe therefore is the honor, but for those who are disobedient, “The stone which the builders rejected has become the chief cornerstone,”

Arminian

For believers he is honor, but for the disobedient he is the rejected stone become the cornerstone (Psalm 118). Arminian readers note that the dividing line is faith versus disobedience, a real human response.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God vindicates the rejected stone regardless of human rejection. The same Christ brings honor or judgment depending on one’s relation to him.

1 Peter 2:8

8and, “a stumbling stone and a rock of offense.” For they stumble at the word, being disobedient, to which also they were appointed.

Arminian

He is a stone of stumbling, and they stumble because they disobey the word, to which they were appointed. Many Arminian readers take the appointment to refer to the stumbling as the destined consequence of disobedience: God appointed that those who reject the word will stumble, without appointing the unbelief itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly see here a reference to God’s sovereign ordination over the destiny of the disobedient, while holding that their disobedience is their own culpable act. Reformed interpreters vary on whether appointment covers the unbelief or the stumbling that follows it.

Key contrast Both agree unbelievers stumble because they disobey the word. Arminians typically read the appointment as God ordaining stumbling as the consequence for disobedience; Reformed readers typically see a broader sovereign ordination over their destiny, without excusing their guilt.

1 Peter 2:9

9But you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God’s own possession, that you may proclaim the excellence of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light.

Arminian

The church is a chosen race, royal priesthood, holy nation, and God’s own people, called to proclaim his excellencies. Arminian readers emphasize corporate election: the church in Christ inherits Israel’s titles, and believers share them by faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the corporate dimension while holding that it consists of individuals God has chosen and effectually called out of darkness. Both agree that election is unto mission: to proclaim the God who called them.

Key contrast Both see Israel’s titles applied to the church. Arminians typically stress corporate election in Christ shared by faith; Reformed readers typically see corporate identity made up of individually chosen and effectually called people.

1 Peter 2:10

10In the past, you were not a people, but now are God’s people, who had not obtained mercy, but now have obtained mercy.

Arminian

Once not a people, now God’s people; once without mercy, now receiving mercy (echoing Hosea). Arminian readers see the gracious inclusion of Gentiles who respond to the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sovereign mercy creating a people where there was none. Both delight in Hosea’s promise fulfilled in the church.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
