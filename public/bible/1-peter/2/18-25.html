Passage

1 Peter 2

Verses 18–25 · WEB

Peter addresses household servants, many of whom had no legal protection, urging respectful submission even to harsh masters. He neither endorses slavery nor calls for revolt; instead he grounds patient endurance of unjust suffering in Christ’s example. Drawing on Isaiah 53, he presents Christ as sinless, silent under abuse, trusting the righteous Judge, and bearing our sins in his body on the tree. The wandering sheep have returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of their souls. Both traditions see substitutionary atonement here; they differ mainly on the intended scope of the atonement, which this passage does not settle.

1 Peter 2:18

18Servants, be in subjection to your masters with all respect, not only to the good and gentle, but also to the wicked.

Arminian

Servants should submit to masters with respect, even harsh ones. Arminian readers, like Reformed, note that Peter addresses household servants directly as moral agents, a dignifying move in the ancient world. The text regulates conduct within a social reality without approving slavery.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that Peter neither commends slavery nor calls for revolt here; he speaks to believers in an existing situation. Respect is shown for God’s sake, and the deeper concern is Christlike endurance.

1 Peter 2:19

19For it is commendable if someone endures pain, suffering unjustly, because of conscience toward God.

Arminian

It is commendable when someone endures unjust suffering because of conscience toward God. Arminian readers stress that patient endurance is a real virtue God approves.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the Greek word translated commendable here is charis, usually rendered grace. Patient endurance under unjust suffering is God’s favor at work in suffering believers.

1 Peter 2:20

20For what glory is it if, when you sin, you patiently endure beating? But if when you do well, you patiently endure suffering, this is commendable with God.

Arminian

There is no credit in enduring punishment for wrongdoing; enduring suffering for doing good is commendable before God. Arminian and Reformed readers agree on the moral distinction between deserved and undeserved suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God’s approval rests on suffering for righteousness. This prepares for Christ as the supreme innocent sufferer.

1 Peter 2:21

21For you were called to this, because Christ also suffered for us, leaving you an example, that you should follow his steps,

Arminian

Believers were called to this, since Christ suffered for them, leaving an example to follow. Arminian readers emphasize Christ’s example as a real pattern for discipleship, alongside his suffering for us.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Christ suffered for us first as substitute, and only then as example. The calling is God’s effectual call that includes following Christ in suffering.

Key contrast Both hold Christ’s suffering as both substitution and example. Arminians typically give strong weight to the call to follow his steps; Reformed readers typically root that call in the prior substitutionary work and God’s effectual calling.

1 Peter 2:22

22who didn’t sin, “neither was deceit found in his mouth.”

Arminian

Christ committed no sin and no deceit was found in his mouth (Isaiah 53:9). Arminian and Reformed readers agree on Christ’s sinlessness as essential to his saving work and example.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Isaiah’s Servant fulfilled precisely in Christ. Only a sinless one could bear the sins of others.

1 Peter 2:23

23When he was cursed, he didn’t curse back. When he suffered, he didn’t threaten, but committed himself to him who judges righteously.

Arminian

When reviled, he did not revile; when suffering, he did not threaten, but entrusted himself to the righteous Judge. Arminian readers see a model for believers facing abuse: trust God rather than retaliate.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s trust in the Father as the pattern of faith under suffering. Leaving vengeance to God is not passivity but confidence in God’s justice.

1 Peter 2:24

24He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree, that we, having died to sins, might live to righteousness. You were healed by his wounds.

Arminian

Christ bore our sins in his body on the tree so that we might die to sin and live to righteousness; by his wounds we are healed. Classical Arminians affirm that Christ bore sins as a substitute and stress the ethical goal: dying to sin and living righteously.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a clear statement of penal substitution: Christ bore our sins. They also stress that his death effectively secures the new life of those for whom he died. The healing is primarily spiritual, from sin.

Key contrast Both affirm that Christ bore our sins. Arminians typically see the atonement as provided for all and applied to believers; Reformed readers typically see it as definite in intent, effectively securing the new life of God’s people. The verse itself does not settle that scope question.

1 Peter 2:25

25For you were going astray like sheep; but now you have returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls.

Arminian

They were straying like sheep but have now returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of their souls. Arminian readers stress the real turning of the sheep, a returning that involves their response.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the Shepherd who seeks and brings back his sheep. The Greek verb for returned is passive in form, which some read as pointing to the Shepherd’s action, though it can also carry a middle sense. Both traditions rejoice in Christ as the caring Overseer of souls.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
