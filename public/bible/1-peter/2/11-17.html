Passage

1 Peter 2

Verses 11–17 · WEB

Peter now urges these foreigners and pilgrims to abstain from desires that war against the soul and to live so well among unbelievers that slanderers may glorify God. For the Lord’s sake they are to submit to human authorities, silence ignorance by doing good, and use their freedom as God’s servants. The passage ends with a fourfold summary: honor everyone, love the brotherhood, fear God, honor the king. Arminian and Reformed readers largely share this reading; their differences are minor here.

1 Peter 2:11

11Beloved, I beg you as foreigners and pilgrims to abstain from fleshly lusts which war against the soul,

Arminian

As foreigners and pilgrims, they must abstain from fleshly desires that war against the soul. Arminian readers stress the ongoing battle and the believer’s real responsibility to resist.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree the war is real and see the Spirit-empowered fight as part of sanctification. Pilgrim identity frames holiness as fitting for those whose home is elsewhere.

1 Peter 2:12

12having good behavior among the nations, so in that of which they speak against you as evildoers, they may see your good works and glorify God in the day of visitation.

Arminian

Their good conduct among the nations may lead slanderers to glorify God on the day of visitation. Arminian readers see hopeful witness: observers may come to faith by seeing good works.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that good works serve witness. Interpreters debate whether the day of visitation means conversion or final judgment; both readings are held across traditions.

1 Peter 2:13

13Therefore subject yourselves to every ordinance of man for the Lord’s sake: whether to the king, as supreme,

Arminian

For the Lord’s sake, submit to every human institution, whether the emperor as supreme. Arminian and Reformed readers share the view that civil authority is ordained by God and that submission is a form of serving the Lord.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect this with Romans 13 and the doctrine of the civil magistrate. Submission is for the Lord’s sake, so it is limited by obedience to God (compare Acts 5:29).

1 Peter 2:14

14or to governors, as sent by him for vengeance on evildoers and for praise to those who do well.

Arminian

Governors are sent to punish evildoers and praise those who do good. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this describes the purpose of government, not a guarantee that every ruler fulfills it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see government as an instrument of God’s common grace restraining evil. The purpose clause defines what rulers are for.

1 Peter 2:15

15For this is the will of God, that by well-doing you should put to silence the ignorance of foolish men.

Arminian

It is God’s will that by doing good they silence the ignorance of foolish people. Arminian readers stress the believer’s active doing good as the answer to slander.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s revealed will here as a moral command, not a hidden decree. Good conduct is the apologetic of a holy life.

1 Peter 2:16

16Live as free people, yet not using your freedom for a cloak of wickedness, but as bondservants of God.

Arminian

Live as free people, not using freedom as cover for evil, but as servants of God. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that Christian freedom is freedom for service, not license.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the paradox: true freedom is bondservice to God. Liberty from sin and law’s condemnation leads to glad obedience.

1 Peter 2:17

17Honor all men. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the king.

Arminian

Honor everyone, love the brotherhood, fear God, honor the king. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this summary sets priorities: universal honor, special love for believers, fear reserved for God alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the king receives honor, like everyone else, but only God receives fear. The ordering guards against idolizing the state.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
