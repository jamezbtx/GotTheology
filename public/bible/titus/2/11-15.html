Passage

Titus 2

Verses 11–15 · ESV · Voices

The household charges rest on a gospel: the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people, training us to renounce ungodliness and to live godly lives while we wait for the appearing of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ, who gave himself for us to redeem us and to purify for himself a people for his own possession, zealous for good works. Titus must declare these things with authority.

Titus 2:11

11For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people,

Arminian

For: the household counsel is grounded in an appearing. The grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people. Wesley: the saving grace of God—so it is in its nature, tendency, and design—has appeared to all men, high and low. “All” is not a cipher for a secret few.

Classical Arminians hear a sincere universal saving appearance. Grace has come in Christ for every person; the ranks just named, including bondservants, are not a footnote to a narrower offer. The gospel that adorns a slave’s fidelity (vv. 9–10) is the same gospel held out to the master. Flattening “all people” into “all the elect” would empty the “for” that Paul just used to lift the low.

Reformed

Calvin: bringing salvation to all men is expressly testified on account of the slaves of whom he had spoken. Yet he does not mean individual men, but rather describes individual classes, or various ranks of life. “All” here is all kinds: high and low, slave and free, not every person taken one by one.

Reformed readers typically hear a gospel that has appeared without fencing off ranks, while actual salvation remains God’s effectual work among the elect. The verse fuels a wide proclamation—including to those in bondage—without requiring that grace savingly appear for every individual. Do not flatten “all”; do not, on this reading, turn it into a frustrated wish that every person be saved.

Key contrast Both refuse to skip “bringing salvation for all people,” and both hear good news that includes the lowly just addressed. Arminians typically take “all people” as every person: saving grace sincerely appeared for all, high and low. Reformed readers typically take “all” as all kinds and ranks (Calvin: not individual men but classes, including slaves), with effectual salvation particular.

Titus 2:12

12training us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age,

Arminian

That grace trains us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions, and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age. Wesley: instructing us—all who do not reject it. The training is moral and present; it is not a ticket postponed to heaven.

Arminian soteriology: the same grace that appeared for all now instructs those who do not refuse it. Training is resistible; ungodliness can be clung to. Holiness is not optional decoration on a finished ticket. Those who receive the appearing are taught to say no, and they must actually say it.

Reformed

Grace not only appears; it trains. Renouncing ungodliness and living self-controlled, upright, and godly lives is the curriculum of the salvation just named. The present age is the classroom.

Reformed accent: this training is the Spirit’s work in those actually saved, not a bare offer left to chance. God effectually teaches His people to renounce what He has redeemed them from. The command is real; the power is His. Worldly passions are not the leftover liberty of the justified.

Titus 2:13

13waiting for our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ,

Arminian

Waiting for our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ. The first appearing (v. 11) trains; the second appearing is hope. Wesley’s saving grace already come does not cancel longing for the glory still to come.

Arminian pastoral: hope is for those who do not reject the grace that appeared. The title is high: our great God and Savior. Both camps can confess the deity and saving office of Jesus here without a fight. The wait is active holiness (v. 12), not idle speculation.

Reformed

Blessed hope is the appearing of Jesus Christ, named our great God and Savior. The church lives between two appearings: grace already come, glory not yet.

Reformed comfort: those He has purified as His people (v. 14) wait for a hope God will not fail. The Christ who appeared in grace will appear in glory. Confession of His deity and saving name is common ground; the wait belongs to the people He actually redeems.

Titus 2:14

14who gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works.

Arminian

Who gave himself for us to redeem us from all lawlessness and to purify for himself a people for his own possession who are zealous for good works. Wesley: He gave himself for us to die in our stead; that he might redeem us—miserable bondslaves—from the power and the very being, as from the guilt, of all our sins. Do not ignore “a people for his own possession”: the people are those who do not reject the grace that appeared for all (vv. 11–12).

Classical Arminians keep both clauses. The gift of Himself is sincerely for us as humankind under the appearing of v. 11; the purified people are those who respond and are actually redeemed from lawlessness. Zeal for good works is the mark of that people, not a second savior. Possession is real; it is the possession of a willing people grace has trained, not a secret list that empties “all people.”

Reformed

Calvin: who gave himself for us, that he might redeem us from the bondage of sin, and purchase us to himself as his heritage. A peculiar people, zealous of good works. “Us” and “a people for his own possession” name a particular people effectually gathered, not a potential crowd.

Reformed soteriology: do not flatten “all” in v. 11, and do not ignore this verse. The self-giving actually redeems and actually purifies a people for His possession. Zeal for good works is fruit of that purchase. The appearing was as wide as every rank; the possession is the elect He bought and cleanses. Heritage language is Calvin’s way of saying the people are His, not merely invited.

Key contrast Both refuse to skip either “all people” (v. 11) or “a people for his own possession.” Arminians typically hear a sincere gift of Himself for all, with the possessed people those who respond and are purified. Reformed readers typically hear a particular people effectually redeemed and purchased as His heritage (Calvin), gathered from all kinds while “his own possession” remains particular.

Titus 2:15

15Declare these things; exhort and rebuke with all authority. Let no one disregard you.

Arminian

Declare these things; exhort and rebuke with all authority. Let no one disregard you. The gospel of appearing grace, and the household life that matches it, are not suggestions. Titus is young (v. 7); he is still not to be ignored.

Arminian charge: declaration is a means of grace for hearers who can still receive or refuse. Authority here is the apostolic gospel, not a personality. Exhortation and rebuke belong together because the same people need both comfort and a holy sting.

Reformed

These things—the appearing, the training, the possessed people—must be declared with authority. Exhort and rebuke: the same Word gathers and corrects. Let no one disregard you.

Reformed ministry: God uses a charged mouth as a means. The authority is Christ’s in the gospel, not Titus’s age or force of will. A disregarded minister often means a disregarded Savior; the church is not free to treat this doctrine as optional color on an already settled life.

Historic words on grace appearing for all people, a people for Christ’s own possession, and the training that belongs to salvation—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Titus 2:11–15.

Arminian Notable voices

“The saving grace of God—So it is in its nature, tendency, and design. Hath appeared to all men—High and low. Instructing us—All who do not reject it. Who gave himself for us—To die in our stead. That he might redeem us—Miserable bondslaves, as well from the power and the very being, as from the guilt, of all our sins.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Titus 2:11–14)

Reformed Notable voices

“Bringing salvation to all men: that it is common to all is expressly testified by him on account of the slaves of whom he had spoken. Yet he does not mean individual men, but rather describes individual classes, or various ranks of life. Who gave himself for us, that he might redeem us from the bondage of sin, and purchase us to himself as his heritage. A peculiar people, zealous of good works.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Titus (on Titus 2:11, 14)
