Passage

Titus 1

Verses 5–9 · ESV · Voices

Paul left Titus in Crete to put what remained into order and appoint elders in every town: men above reproach, the husband of one wife, with believing children not open to debauchery or insubordination. An overseer, as God’s steward, must not be arrogant, quick-tempered, a drunkard, violent, or greedy, but hospitable, a lover of good, self-controlled, upright, holy, and disciplined, holding firm the trustworthy word so as to instruct in sound doctrine and rebuke those who contradict it.

Titus 1:5

5This is why I left you in Crete, so that you might put what remained into order, and appoint elders in every town as I directed you—

Arminian

Titus is left in Crete to put remaining things into order and to appoint elders in every town as Paul directed. Wesley: ordain elders—appoint the most faithful, zealous men to watch over the rest; these were the elders, or bishops, that Paul approved of—men that had living faith, a pure conscience, a blameless life.

Arminian order: the church is not left without watchers. Living faith and a blameless life are the first credentials. Appointment is pastoral work, not a power-grab; Crete needs ordered towns, not a vacuum.

Reformed

Calvin: churches cannot safely remain without the ministry of pastors. He does not give permission to Titus that he alone may do everything, but only bids him preside, as moderator, at the elections. This passage shows there is no distinction between a presbyter and a bishop.

Reformed order: every town needs pastors. Titus moderates the appointing; he does not swallow the church. Elder and overseer name the same office. What remained is unfinished church order, not optional décor.

Titus 1:6

6if anyone is above reproach, the husband of one wife, and his children are believers and not open to the charge of debauchery or insubordination.

Arminian

Above reproach, the husband of one wife, with believing children not open to debauchery or insubordination. Wesley on this verse: the Holy Ghost, by repeating this so often, designed to leave the Romanists without excuse. On the same phrase at 1 Timothy 3:2 he added: How needless was that question, whether a man having two wives at once could be a bishop? St. Paul did not so much as suppose such a case. He that hath been married more than once (though he were a widower) was not to be admitted.

Classical Arminians (with the broader catholic stream) have treated this as a character list for male overseers in the ordinary case, not a culture-war slogan. Shared: one-woman faithfulness, not polygamy. Wesley read more strictly, even against successive marriages after widowhood. The household must not be a scandal of debauchery or insubordination.

Reformed

Calvin: polygamy was so common among the Jews that Paul enjoined them to elect those who had lived chastely in marriage—satisfied with a single wife—and forbade those who had manifested lust by marrying many wives. He who, having become unmarried by the death of his wife, marries another, ought nevertheless to be accounted the husband of one wife: the apostle does not say him who has been, but him who is, the husband of one wife.

Reformed historic reading likewise hears a one-woman faithfulness and a public life that can bear the church’s weight. Calvin allowed the unmarried and a second marriage after death, while still condemning polygamy. Believing children not open to debauchery or insubordination belong to the same household test.

Key contrast Shared: the overseer must be a one-woman man in loyal marriage, not a polygamist or a wanderer, and must be able to teach. Wesley read the phrase more strictly (against even successive marriages). Calvin allowed the unmarried and the widower who marries again, while still condemning polygamy. Neither side should be pressed into a modern blog fight the verse is not staging.

Titus 1:7

7For an overseer, as God’s steward, must be above reproach. He must not be arrogant or quick-tempered or a drunkard or violent or greedy for gain,

Arminian

An overseer, as God’s steward, must be above reproach. Wesley: as the steward of God—to whom he intrusts immortal souls. Not selfwilled—literally, pleasing himself. The negatives: not arrogant, not quick-tempered, not a drunkard, not violent, not greedy for gain.

Arminian church life: these are visible, trainable refusals. Grace that saves also trains a man not to rule immortal souls as a brawler, a drinker, or a hireling. Stewardship is trust, not self-pleasing.

Reformed

Overseer (episkopos) is God’s steward and must be above reproach. Calvin already heard no distinction between presbyter and bishop in this passage. The negatives protect the sheep from arrogance, temper, drink, violence, and greed.

Reformed seriousness: a man can be orthodox and still disqualified here. The church’s household is not safe under a self-pleasing steward. God requires a life that can bear the trust.

Titus 1:8

8but hospitable, a lover of good, self-controlled, upright, holy, and disciplined.

Arminian

The positive list: hospitable, a lover of good, self-controlled, upright, holy, and disciplined. Wesley’s approved elders were men of living faith, a pure conscience, a blameless life.

Arminian holiness: these are not optional color. The same grace that calls into faith trains a public, hospitable, disciplined life. Crete will not be ordered by gifted men who will not be good.

Reformed

Hospitality, love of good, self-control, uprightness, holiness, and discipline sit as required as the refusals of v. 7.

Reformed historic reading: the office is not a platform for the gifted but ungoverned. A steward of God must look like this list. Character is not a prelude to doctrine; it is part of the stewardship.

Titus 1:9

9He must hold firm to the trustworthy word as taught, so that he may be able to give instruction in sound doctrine and also to rebuke those who contradict it.

Arminian

He must hold firm the trustworthy word as taught, able to instruct in sound doctrine and to rebuke those who contradict it. Wesley: holding fast the faithful word as he hath been taught, that he may be able by sound doctrine both to exhort and to convince the gainsayers.

Arminian teaching: the elder is not self-taught in a new gospel. He holds what he was taught, then exhorts and convinces. Sound doctrine is for the flock and against the contradictors; both tasks require a man who has first held fast.

Reformed

Calvin: holding fast the faithful word. The pastor ought to have two voices: one, for gathering the sheep; and another, for warding off and driving away wolves.

Reformed charge: instruction and rebuke are one office. The trustworthy word is given; the overseer does not invent it. Sound doctrine feeds, and it also silences contradiction. A man who cannot do both is not yet the steward of v. 7.

Historic words on appointing elders in every town, a life above reproach, and holding firm the trustworthy word—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Titus 1:5–9.

Arminian Notable voices

“Ordain elders—Appoint the most faithful, zealous men to watch over the rest. These were the elders, or bishops, that Paul approved of;—men that had living faith, a pure conscience, a blameless life. The husband of one wife—Surely the Holy Ghost, by repeating this so often, designed to leave the Romanists without excuse. As the steward of God—To whom he intrusts immortal souls. Not selfwilled—Literally, pleasing himself. Holding fast the faithful word as he hath been taught, that he may be able by sound doctrine both to exhort and to convince the gainsayers.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Titus 1:5–9)

Reformed Notable voices

“And appoint presbyters in each city. Churches cannot safely remain without the ministry of pastors. He does not give permission to Titus that he alone may do everything, but only bids him preside, as moderator, at the elections. The husband of one wife. Polygamy was so common among the Jews. The meaning is the same as if Paul had enjoined them to elect those who had lived chastely in marriage—had been satisfied with having a single wife, and had forbidden those who had manifested the power of lust by marrying many wives. At the same time, he who, having become an unmarried man by the death of his wife, marries another, ought, nevertheless, to be accounted the husband of one wife; for the apostle does not say that they shall choose him who has been, but him who is, the husband of one wife. This passage plainly shows that there is no distinction between a presbyter and a bishop. Holding fast the faithful word. The pastor ought to have two voices: one, for gathering the sheep; and another, for warding off and driving away wolves.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Titus (on Titus 1:5–9)
