Passage

Titus 1

Verses 10–16 · ESV · Voices

The reason for such elders: many are insubordinate, empty talkers and deceivers, especially of the circumcision party, upsetting whole families by teaching for shameful gain what they ought not. A Cretan prophet said Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons; the testimony is true, so rebuke them sharply that they may be sound in the faith, not devoted to Jewish myths and human commands. To the pure all things are pure, but to the defiled and unbelieving nothing is; they profess to know God but deny him by their works.

Titus 1:10

10For there are many who are insubordinate, empty talkers and deceivers, especially those of the circumcision party.

Arminian

For: this is why the overseer must be able to rebuke. Many are insubordinate, empty talkers and deceivers, especially those of the circumcision party. Wesley: they of the circumcision—the Jewish converts.

Arminian pastoral: insubordination and empty talk are not small interior faults; they deceive. The circumcision party names a real pressure toward myths and commands that turn from the truth. Sound doctrine in v. 9 has a present enemy.

Reformed

The for ties v. 9’s rebuke to a crowded field of insubordinate talkers, especially of the circumcision.

Reformed gravity: empty talk is not harmless. Deceivers, many of them, unsettle the church from a Judaizing angle. God provides elders with two voices because wolves are already in the towns.

Titus 1:11

11They must be silenced, since they are upsetting whole families by teaching for shameful gain what they ought not to teach.

Arminian

They must be silenced: Wesley heard “stopped”—to put a bit into the mouth of an unruly horse. Whole families are being upset by teaching for shameful gain what ought not to be taught.

Arminian seriousness: silencing is for the sake of households, not a taste for control. Shameful gain names a hireling motive. Grace that saves families also refuses teachers who wreck them for pay.

Reformed

Calvin: whose mouth must be stopped. When they have been smitten down by the sword of God’s word, by shutting the mouth Paul simply means to refute their vain talking.

Reformed silencing: the bit is the word, not a fist. Refutation stops the mouth. Shameful gain and upset families show why the second pastoral voice of v. 9 exists: wolves must be driven off so households can stand.

Titus 1:12

12One of the Cretans, a prophet of their own, said, “Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons.”

Arminian

A Cretan prophet of their own: Wesley notes that all poets were anciently called prophets. The line is ugly: always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons.

Arminian reading: Paul cites a native witness, not a racial curse from outside. The point is pastoral realism about a reputation Crete already knew, so that rebuke in v. 13 is not surprising. Grace still aims at sound faith, not at writing a people off.

Reformed

One of their own said it. Paul does not flatter Crete; he uses a proverb the island already owned.

Reformed realism: the gospel does not deny local vice in order to be polite. Liars, beasts, gluttons name a culture the church must not baptize. The citation serves the sharp rebuke that follows, not a theory of blood.

Titus 1:13

13This testimony is true. Therefore rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith,

Arminian

This testimony is true. Therefore rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith. The aim of the sharp word is soundness, not humiliation.

Arminian hope: rebuke is a means toward faith made sound. They may be—soundness is the goal held out to those now insubordinate. Sharpness serves restoration; it is not a license to despise Cretans as beyond the gospel.

Reformed

The testimony is true; therefore rebuke. Soundness in the faith is the end of the sharpness.

Reformed pastoral: God uses a cutting word to make sound what is diseased. The therefore is logical, not cruel. Elders who will not rebuke will not have a sound church; elders who rebuke without aiming at faith have missed Paul’s purpose.

Titus 1:14

14not devoting themselves to Jewish myths and the commands of people who turn away from the truth.

Arminian

Sound faith does not devote itself to Jewish myths and the commands of people who turn away from the truth. The circumcision party’s extra commands are not holiness; they are a turning.

Arminian warning: myths and human commands can capture a church that still talks about God. Turning from the truth is a real departure. The gospel that invites all also unmasks commands that replace it.

Reformed

Jewish myths and human commands: a turning away from the truth, not a stricter version of it.

Reformed doctrine: the truth is given; people who add commands that God has not given are already turning. The elder’s rebuke protects the flock from a second law. Sound faith holds the trustworthy word of v. 9, not the myths of the circumcision party.

Titus 1:15

15To the pure, all things are pure, but to the defiled and unbelieving, nothing is pure; but both their minds and their consciences are defiled.

Arminian

To the pure, all things are pure. Wesley: those whose hearts are purified by faith—this we allow. All things are pure: all kinds of meat; the Mosaic distinction between clean and unclean meats being now taken away. But to the defiled and unbelieving nothing is pure: the apostle joins defiled and unbelieving to intimate that nothing can be clean without a true faith; understanding and conscience, those leading powers of the soul, are polluted.

Arminian reading: this is not a license for a careless life. Purity is a heart purified by faith; food laws are gone, but unbelief defiles everything it touches. The verse is about defiled minds versus the gospel, not about calling sin clean.

Reformed

Calvin: he upholds Christian liberty, asserting that to believers nothing is unclean; at the same time he censures false apostles who set no value on inward purity, which alone God esteems. The subject is not the health of the body but peace of conscience: the distinction of foods in force under the Law has been abolished. Because there is no purity in the sight of God but that of faith, all unbelievers are unclean.

Reformed reading: liberty of food is real, and it is not a license. Inward purity is what God esteems. An unbelieving mind and conscience pollute all; the defiled are not free, they are unclean in what they handle.

Key contrast The live fork is not whether this verse licenses sin—both camps refuse that. It is how purity and defilement relate to faith and to the old food laws. Wesley hears all kinds of meat now clean, and nothing clean without a true faith that purifies the heart; defiled and unbelieving go together because understanding and conscience are polluted. Calvin hears Christian liberty of food, inward purity esteemed by God, and an unbelieving mind and conscience that leave all unbelievers unclean.

Titus 1:16

16They profess to know God, but they deny him by their works. They are detestable, disobedient, unfit for any good work.

Arminian

They profess to know God, but they deny him by their works. Wesley’s joining of defiled and unbelieving already prepared this: a polluted conscience will not produce a life that matches the profession. Detestable, disobedient, unfit for any good work.

Arminian holiness: profession without works is denial. Knowing God is not a slogan; disobedience shows the profession false or abandoned. Good work requires a purified heart; the unfit are not excused by their talk.

Reformed

Calvin: they profess that they know God; but in works they deny him. Inward impurity of v. 15 now shows in public denial.

Reformed gravity: confession without obedience is a lie about God. Detestable, disobedient, unfit: God names the fruit. The circumcision talkers of v. 10 are not merely mistaken about meats; they deny the God they claim, and they are unfit for the good work the steward of v. 7 must do.

Historic quotes

Voices from both camps

Historic words on silencing empty talkers, a sharp rebuke toward sound faith, and purity of mind and conscience—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Titus 1:10–16.

Arminian Notable voices

“They of the circumcision—The Jewish converts. Stopped—The word properly means, to put a bit into the mouth of an unruly horse. A prophet—So all poets were anciently called. To the pure—Those whose hearts are purified by faith this we allow. All things are pure—All kinds of meat; the Mosaic distinction between clean and unclean meats being now taken away. But to the defiled and unbelieving nothing is pure—The apostle joins defiled and unbelieving, to intimate that nothing can be clean without a true faith: for both the understanding and conscience, those leading powers of the soul, are polluted.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Titus 1:10–16)

Reformed Notable voices

“Whose mouth must be stopped. When they have been smitten down by the sword of God’s word, by shutting the mouth Paul simply means to refute their vain talking. To the pure all things indeed are pure. He upholds Christian liberty, by asserting that to believers nothing is unclean; but at the same time he indirectly censures the false apostles who set no value on inward purity, which alone is esteemed by God. But as the subject now in hand is not the health of the body, but peace of conscience, he means nothing else than that the distinction of the kinds of food, which was in force under the Law, has now been abolished. Because there is no purity in the sight of God but that of faith, it follows that all unbelievers are unclean. They profess that they know God; but in works they deny him.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Titus (on Titus 1:10–16)
