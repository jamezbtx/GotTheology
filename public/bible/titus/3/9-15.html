Passage

Titus 3

Verses 9–15 · ESV · Voices

After the trustworthy saying Paul fences the church: avoid foolish controversies, genealogies, dissensions, and quarrels about the law; after a first and second warning, have nothing more to do with a person who stirs up division. Travel plans, help for Zenas and Apollos, a charge that our people learn to devote themselves to good works, and a closing greeting and grace.

Titus 3:9

9But avoid foolish controversies, genealogies, dissensions, and quarrels about the law, for they are unprofitable and worthless.

Arminian

But avoid foolish controversies, genealogies, dissensions, and quarrels about the law, for they are unprofitable and worthless. The “but” sets this against the profitable good works of v. 8. Not every religious argument is obedience.

Arminian pastoral: controversy can be a way of resisting the mercy just preached. The Spirit enables a holy refusal of worthless talk; the church can still dive in and call it zeal. Profit is the test Paul just used: good works help people; these fights do not.

Reformed

Calvin will soon forbid wasting much time in debate with heretics. Here the catalog is foolish questions, genealogies, dissensions, legal quarrels—unprofitable and worthless. The trustworthy saying (v. 8) is not served by this diet.

Reformed reading: God feeds His people on mercy and good works, not on speculative fights. Avoidance is obedience. The law-quarrels of Crete are not a higher spirituality; they are a substitute for the regeneration just described.

Titus 3:10

10As for a person who stirs up division, after warning him once and then twice, have nothing more to do with him,

Arminian

As for a person who stirs up division, after warning him once and then twice, have nothing more to do with him. Wesley: an heretic, after a first and second admonition, reject—avoid, leave to himself. Here it means a man who obstinately persists in contending about foolish questions and thereby occasions strife and parties in the church.

Arminian pastoral: two warnings are real means of grace; the person can still turn. After that, distance is also obedience. The aim is the church’s peace and the man’s conscience, not a taste for cutting people off. Persistence in party-making is treated as a settled refusal, not a personality quirk.

Reformed

Calvin: he forbids him to waste much time in debating with heretics. Under this name he includes all ambitious, unruly, contentious persons who disturb the peace of the Church. After the first and second admonition: then, in effect, be done.

Reformed church order: patient warning, then separation. God guards His flock by a limited debate, not by endless argument that treats strife as a gift. The factious person is not made the church’s tutor. Two admonitions honor both truth and patience; refusal after that is to be recognized, not endlessly managed.

Titus 3:11

11knowing that such a person is warped and sinful; he is self-condemned.

Arminian

Knowing that such a person is warped and sinful; he is self-condemned. The diagnosis is moral, not merely intellectual. He has judged himself by refusing the warnings.

Arminian seriousness: a professing person can become warped by practiced division. Self-condemned names a responsibility he will not shift onto the church. The warnings of v. 10 were his door; remaining in the party is his verdict on himself. This is pastoral grief, not a sneer.

Reformed

Warped, sinful, self-condemned: Paul will not treat the factious as a misunderstood ally. The second warning having failed, the man’s own stance condemns him.

Reformed reading: persistent division after admonition is evidence of a heart not ruled by the trustworthy saying. Self-condemned means the church is not inventing the charge; he displays it. God keeps His people from being tutored by a man who has already passed sentence on himself.

Titus 3:12

12When I send Artemas or Tychicus to you, do your best to come to me at Nicopolis, for I have decided to spend the winter there.

Arminian

When I send Artemas or Tychicus to you, do your best to come to me at Nicopolis, for I have decided to spend the winter there. The letter lands in real travel, real winter, real colleagues. Ministry is not only a doctrine chart.

Arminian pastoral: Titus’s presence with Paul is a good work of companionship. Enabling grace includes showing up. The names remind Crete that the apostolic circle is larger than one young pastor on an island.

Reformed

Artemas or Tychicus will cover Crete so Titus can come to Nicopolis. Paul has decided to winter there. The mission has logistics because the Lord uses means: replacements, travel, a season.

Reformed comfort: God orders His servants’ steps, including wintering. The same mercy that saved (vv. 4–7) now steers an apostle’s calendar. Titus is not indispensable to Crete in a way that forbids him to come; the church is larger than one man.

Titus 3:13

13Do your best to speed Zenas the lawyer and Apollos on their way; see that they lack nothing.

Arminian

Do your best to speed Zenas the lawyer and Apollos on their way; see that they lack nothing. Hospitality is part of the good works just commanded. A lawyer and a preacher both need the church’s purse and haste.

Arminian accent: “lack nothing” is practical love. Grace that appeared for people trains people to fund the next mile. Neglect here would make the doctrine look cheap while controversies (v. 9) stay well fed.

Reformed

Zenas and Apollos are to be sped on, lacking nothing. The church’s generosity is how God moves His workers. Apollos’s name ties Crete to the wider apostolic mission.

Reformed pastoral: provision for traveling servants is fruit of the regeneration already named. God does not scorn the purse. A justified people see that the laborers lack nothing, because the Savior who poured out the Spirit richly also cares for the journey.

Titus 3:14

14And let our people learn to devote themselves to good works, so as to help cases of urgent need, and not be unfruitful.

Arminian

And let our people learn to devote themselves to good works, so as to help cases of urgent need, and not be unfruitful. Wesley: let ours—all our brethren at Crete—learn, both by thy admonition and example. Learning implies they might otherwise be unfruitful.

Arminian pastoral: our people are the household of faith, taught to meet urgent need. Good works are learned; they can be neglected. Fruitfulness is the opposite of the worthless controversies of v. 9. Mercy (vv. 4–7) is the root; urgent need is a reason the fruit must actually appear.

Reformed

Calvin: let ours also learn to excel in good works, that they may not be unfruitful. The same note as 3:8, now aimed at urgent need. “Our people” are the church in distinction from the factious.

Reformed accent: God teaches a justified people to be fruitful. Learning is a means; unfruitfulness is not a harmless option for the regenerate. Urgent need is where the trustworthy saying becomes bread, escort, and care. Works remain fruit, not foundation.

Titus 3:15

15All who are with me send greetings to you. Greet those who love us in the faith. Grace be with you all.

Arminian

All who are with me send greetings to you. Greet those who love us in the faith. Grace be with you all. The letter ends in affection and benediction. “You all” is as wide as the Cretan church Paul has been correcting and comforting.

Arminian pastoral: love in the faith is the family mark; grace with you all is the last word, not the controversies. The same grace that appeared for all people (2:11) and saved us by mercy (3:5) is now wished upon the whole church. Receive it; do not refuse it.

Reformed

Greetings from Paul’s company, and a greeting to those who love us in the faith. Then: Grace be with you all. The apostle’s last word is the grace he has been preaching.

Reformed comfort: those who love in the faith are the people Christ purified for Himself (2:14). Grace with you all is a real benediction God means to apply to His church. The trustworthy saying ends as a blessing, not as a debate. The Lord who saved us according to mercy now greets His people through Paul’s mouth.

Voices from both camps

Historic words on avoiding worthless controversy, a factious person after warning, and a people devoted to good works—so each camp speaks in its own voice beside Titus 3:9–15.

Arminian Notable voices

“An heretic (after a first and second admonition) reject—Avoid, leave to himself. Here it evidently means a man that obstinately persists in contending about foolish questions, and thereby occasions strife and parties in the church. And let ours—All our brethren at Crete. Learn—Both by thy admonition and example.”
John Wesley · Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament (on Titus 3:10, 14)

Reformed Notable voices

“He forbids him to waste much time in debating with heretics. Under this name he includes all ambitious, unruly, contentious persons who disturb the peace of the Church. After the first and second admonition. Let ours also learn to excel in good works, that they may not be unfruitful.”
John Calvin · Commentary on Titus (on Titus 3:10, 14)
