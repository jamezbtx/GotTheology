Passage

Mark 12

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Jesus tells a parable drawn from Isaiah 5 about a vineyard owner whose tenants abuse his servants and finally kill his beloved son. The owner will destroy them and give the vineyard to others, and Jesus seals the point with Psalm 118: the rejected stone becomes the cornerstone. The leaders recognize the parable is aimed at them. Both traditions read it as Jesus’ claim to be the unique Son and a warning of judgment on Israel’s faithless leaders.

Mark 12:1

1He began to speak to them in parables. “A man planted a vineyard, put a hedge around it, dug a pit for the wine press, built a tower, rented it out to a farmer, and went into another country.

Arminian

A man plants a vineyard, builds a hedge, winepress, and tower, rents it to farmers, and leaves. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this echoes Isaiah 5, where the vineyard is Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the owner’s care and provision, which make the tenants’ response inexcusable.

Mark 12:2

2When it was time, he sent a servant to the farmer to get from the farmer his share of the fruit of the vineyard.

Arminian

At harvest he sends a servant to collect his share. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the servants represent the prophets sent to Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers note God’s rightful claim on the fruit of his people’s lives.

Mark 12:3

3They took him, beat him, and sent him away empty.

Arminian

They beat the servant and send him away empty. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this recalls Israel’s treatment of the prophets.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the persistent rebellion that God patiently endured.

Mark 12:4

4Again, he sent another servant to them; and they threw stones at him, wounded him in the head, and sent him away shamefully treated.

Arminian

Another servant is stoned, wounded in the head, and shamefully treated. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the abuse escalates.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the growing guilt of the tenants.

Mark 12:5

5Again he sent another, and they killed him, and many others, beating some, and killing some.

Arminian

Another is killed, and many others beaten or killed. Arminian readers note God’s repeated patience and appeals.

Reformed

Reformed readers note both God’s patience and the depth of human rebellion. Both see the history of rejected prophets (compare 2 Chronicles 36:15 to 16).

Mark 12:6

6Therefore still having one, his beloved son, he sent him last to them, saying, ‘They will respect my son.’

Arminian

Last of all he sends his beloved son, saying they will respect my son. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the beloved son echoes the voice at Jesus’ baptism and transfiguration; Jesus claims a unique sonship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the owner’s words belong to the parable’s story, not a statement about God’s knowledge. The Son’s sending is the climax of God’s dealings.

Mark 12:7

7But those farmers said among themselves, ‘This is the heir. Come, let’s kill him, and the inheritance will be ours.’

Arminian

The tenants plot to kill the heir and take the inheritance. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this foretells the leaders’ plot against Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the folly of rebels who think killing the heir secures the vineyard.

Mark 12:8

8They took him, killed him, and cast him out of the vineyard.

Arminian

They kill him and throw him out of the vineyard. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this anticipates Jesus’ death.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the rejection that God will turn into the foundation of salvation.

Mark 12:9

9What therefore will the lord of the vineyard do? He will come and destroy the farmers, and will give the vineyard to others.

Arminian

The owner will come, destroy the tenants, and give the vineyard to others. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this is judgment on the faithless leaders and a transfer of stewardship; many interpreters connect it with the events of AD 70.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly see the others as the apostles and the new covenant people of Jews and Gentiles. Interpreters in both traditions differ on how far the parable addresses Israel as a whole.

Mark 12:10

10Haven’t you even read this Scripture: ‘The stone which the builders rejected was made the head of the corner.

Arminian

Jesus cites Psalm 118: the rejected stone became the cornerstone. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the same psalm sung at the entry now speaks of rejection and vindication.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the resurrection as the Father’s vindication of the rejected Son.

Mark 12:11

11This was from the Lord. It is marvelous in our eyes’?”

Arminian

This was from the Lord and is marvelous in our eyes. Arminian and Reformed readers agree God is the author of the reversal.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that God’s plan stands behind the rejection and exaltation of Christ (compare Acts 2:23).

Mark 12:12

12They tried to seize him, but they feared the multitude; for they perceived that he spoke the parable against them. They left him and went away.

Arminian

They want to arrest him but fear the crowd, knowing the parable was against them, and leave. Arminian readers note that understanding without repentance hardens opposition.

Reformed

Reformed readers note their recognition of guilt without submission. Both see the leaders choosing their course.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
