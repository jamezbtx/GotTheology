Passage

Mark 12

Verses 28–44 · WEB

A scribe asks which commandment is greatest. Jesus joins the Shema to love of neighbor, and the scribe agrees that such love outweighs sacrifices; Jesus says he is not far from God’s kingdom. Jesus then asks how the Christ can be David’s son when David calls him Lord in Psalm 110, warns against scribes who seek honor and exploit widows, and praises a poor widow who gives all she has. Both traditions share the reading; they differ somewhat on what being not far from the kingdom implies about human ability and grace.

Mark 12:28

28One of the scribes came and heard them questioning together, and knowing that he had answered them well, asked him, “Which commandment is the greatest of all?”

Arminian

A scribe, impressed by his answer, asks which commandment is first. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this scribe appears to ask sincerely.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Mark portrays him more positively than the earlier questioners.

Mark 12:29

29Jesus answered, “The greatest is: ‘Hear, Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one.

Arminian

Jesus quotes the Shema: Hear, Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the greatest command begins with who God is.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that obedience rests on God’s unique being and covenant relation.

Mark 12:30

30You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind, and with all your strength.’ This is the first commandment.

Arminian

Love God with all heart, soul, mind, and strength. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this demands the whole person; Arminian readers stress that grace enables believers to grow in such love.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that no one fulfills it perfectly, which drives sinners to Christ, and that the renewed heart then grows in love by grace. Both see it as the shape of the new life.

Mark 12:31

31The second is like this: ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself.’ There is no other commandment greater than these.”

Arminian

The second: love your neighbor as yourself (Leviticus 19:18). Arminian and Reformed readers agree love of God and neighbor belong together.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the two commands sum up the law (compare Romans 13:9 to 10).

Mark 12:32

32The scribe said to him, “Truly, teacher, you have said well that he is one, and there is none other but he;

Arminian

The scribe affirms that God is one and there is no other. Arminian and Reformed readers agree he echoes Deuteronomy 4:35.

Reformed

Reformed readers note his agreement with Jesus’ teaching.

Mark 12:33

33and to love him with all the heart, with all the understanding, all the soul, and with all the strength, and to love his neighbor as himself, is more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices.”

Arminian

Loving God and neighbor is more than burnt offerings and sacrifices. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this reflects the prophets (1 Samuel 15:22; Hosea 6:6).

Reformed

Reformed readers note the remark’s force spoken in the temple courts.

Mark 12:34

34When Jesus saw that he answered wisely, he said to him, “You are not far from God’s Kingdom.” No one dared ask him any question after that.

Arminian

Seeing his wise answer, Jesus says he is not far from God’s kingdom; no one dares question him further. Arminian readers see a man responding to grace already at work, who must still go on to trust Jesus himself; prevenient grace enables such movement toward the kingdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the man’s understanding as real but not yet saving; entering the kingdom requires the new birth God gives. Both note that being near is not the same as being in.

Key contrast Both say nearness is not entry. Arminians typically describe a person enabled by prevenient grace who must still respond in faith; Reformed readers typically stress that crossing into the kingdom requires regenerating grace.

Mark 12:35

35Jesus responded, as he taught in the temple, “How is it that the scribes say that the Christ is the son of David?

Arminian

Teaching in the temple, Jesus asks how the scribes can say the Christ is David’s son. Arminian and Reformed readers agree he does not deny Davidic descent but probes its meaning.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus pointing beyond a merely human messiah.

Mark 12:36

36For David himself said in the Holy Spirit, ‘The Lord said to my Lord, “Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies the footstool of your feet.”’

Arminian

David, speaking by the Holy Spirit, said the Lord said to my Lord, sit at my right hand (Psalm 110:1). Arminian and Reformed readers agree Jesus affirms the psalm’s inspiration and Davidic authorship.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Psalm 110:1 is cited often in the New Testament for Christ’s exaltation (for example Acts 2:34 to 35; Hebrews 1:13).

Mark 12:37

37Therefore David himself calls him Lord, so how can he be his son?” The common people heard him gladly.

Arminian

David calls him Lord, so how is he his son? The crowd hears him gladly. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the answer is that the Messiah is both David’s descendant and his Lord.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a hint of Christ’s divine identity alongside his human lineage.

Mark 12:38

38In his teaching he said to them, “Beware of the scribes, who like to walk in long robes, and to get greetings in the marketplaces,

Arminian

Beware the scribes who like long robes and greetings in the marketplaces. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the warning targets love of status, not every scribe.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the contrast with the sincere scribe of verses 28 to 34.

Mark 12:39

39and to get the best seats in the synagogues and the best places at feasts,

Arminian

They want the best seats in synagogues and places of honor at feasts. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this exposes religious ambition.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a warning for church leaders in every age.

Mark 12:40

40those who devour widows’ houses, and for a pretense make long prayers. These will receive greater condemnation.”

Arminian

They devour widows’ houses and make long prayers for show; they will receive greater condemnation. Arminian and Reformed readers agree that hypocrisy joined with exploitation brings heavier judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note degrees of judgment taught here (compare Luke 12:47 to 48).

Mark 12:41

41Jesus sat down opposite the treasury and saw how the multitude cast money into the treasury. Many who were rich cast in much.

Arminian

Jesus watches people giving at the treasury; many rich give much. Arminian and Reformed readers agree Jesus observes the heart behind giving.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that God sees what people give and why.

Mark 12:42

42A poor widow came and she cast in two small brass coins, which equal a quadrans coin.

Arminian

A poor widow puts in two small coins worth a quadrans. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the coins were the smallest in use.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Mark’s explanation for Gentile readers by using a Roman coin.

Mark 12:43

43He called his disciples to himself and said to them, “Most certainly I tell you, this poor widow gave more than all those who are giving into the treasury,

Arminian

Jesus tells the disciples she gave more than all the others. Arminian and Reformed readers agree God measures giving by sacrifice, not amount.

Reformed

Reformed readers note his solemn introduction: most certainly I tell you.

Mark 12:44

44for they all gave out of their abundance, but she, out of her poverty, gave all that she had to live on.”

Arminian

They gave from abundance; she gave from poverty all she had to live on. Arminian readers emphasize her wholehearted devotion as a model of trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see her total trust; some interpreters read it as a lament over a system that devours widows (v. 40). Both readings are held across traditions.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
