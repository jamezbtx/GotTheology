Passage

Mark 9

Verses 14–29 · WEB

Jesus returns from the mountain to find the other disciples unable to deliver an afflicted child and caught in a public dispute. The father’s desperate appeal brings faith and unbelief into the open, while Jesus’ command demonstrates the authority the disciples lack in themselves. The private explanation turns their attention to prayerful dependence. Both traditions locate hope in Christ’s mercy and power, while differing in their wider accounts of how grace brings about the believing response.

Mark 9:14

14Coming to the disciples, he saw a great multitude around them, and scribes questioning them.

Arminian

The descent from the mountain brings Jesus into a scene of argument around the other disciples. Arminian readers notice the contrast between revealed glory and ordinary ministry’s confusion. The passage will show that disciples must continue depending on Christ when confrontation exposes the limits of their confidence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the inability of the remaining disciples setting the stage for Christ’s sufficient authority. Their association with him does not make power an independent possession. Mark moves from the Father’s testimony about the Son to a practical demonstration of why distressed people need that Son.

Mark 9:15

15Immediately all the multitude, when they saw him, were greatly amazed, and running to him, greeted him.

Arminian

The crowd’s amazement turns quickly into eager movement toward Jesus. Arminian readers see fresh hope entering a situation marked by argument and failure. Mark does not explain the cause of their astonishment, so claims that Jesus still visibly shone would go beyond what the narrative says.

Reformed

Reformed readers focus on the way Jesus’ arrival reorients the whole gathering. The answer to the problem will not emerge from the dispute alone but from his intervention. The crowd’s excitement is real, yet Mark will distinguish outward eagerness from the dependent faith Jesus seeks.

Mark 9:16

16He asked the scribes, “What are you asking them?”

Arminian

Jesus questions the scribes about their dispute with the disciples, bringing the conflict into the open. Arminian readers see attentive leadership that does not abandon struggling followers to public embarrassment. His question also makes room for the concrete need that argument has failed to resolve.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Jesus’ authority to take charge when his representatives cannot answer the challenge before them. The coming deliverance will vindicate his power rather than the disciples’ competence. His question redirects attention from religious contention toward the suffering person who needs his help.

Mark 9:17

17One of the multitude answered, “Teacher, I brought to you my son, who has a mute spirit;

Arminian

The father explains that he brought his afflicted son to Jesus, describing a spirit that prevents speech. Arminian readers attend to his active search for mercy on another’s behalf. The story encourages bringing suffering to Christ without turning the child’s condition into an accusation against his family.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a helpless child and a distressed father whose need cannot be met by human strength. Mark identifies spiritual oppression in this particular case. That description should be respected without assuming every inability to speak, or every similar illness, has the same cause.

Mark 9:18

18and wherever it seizes him, it throws him down; and he foams at the mouth, grinds his teeth, and becomes rigid. I asked your disciples to cast it out, and they weren’t able.”

Arminian

The father describes frightening symptoms and the disciples’ inability to deliver his son. Arminian readers notice that earlier ministry success did not remove their need for present trust and prayer. Their failure is not proof that Jesus is powerless, nor grounds for blaming the suffering child.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that delegated authority never becomes power the disciples possess apart from God. Their inability exposes dependence that previous success might have obscured. Mark prepares for Jesus’ explanation about prayer, directing ministry away from confidence in office, reputation, or remembered achievements.

Mark 9:19

19He answered him, “Unbelieving generation, how long shall I be with you? How long shall I bear with you? Bring him to me.”

Arminian

Jesus grieves over an unbelieving generation but immediately orders the boy brought to him. Arminian readers hold the rebuke and the invitation together: unbelief is serious, yet mercy remains available. His patience calls people away from distrust rather than requiring flawless faith before they may approach.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear both judgment on human unbelief and the compassion that persists despite it. Jesus does not withdraw when the disciples and crowd prove inadequate. His command to bring the boy makes his own presence, rather than anyone’s spiritual performance, the decisive hope in the scene.

Mark 9:20

20They brought him to him, and when he saw him, immediately the spirit convulsed him and he fell on the ground, wallowing and foaming at the mouth.

Arminian

The boy’s violent convulsion follows his being brought before Jesus. Arminian readers see that approaching Christ does not mean the struggle instantly looks easier. The worsening appearance must be read in light of the deliverance that follows, not treated as evidence that seeking help was mistaken.

Reformed

Reformed readers see hostile spiritual power reacting in the presence of the one who will command its departure. The violence is frightening, but it does not establish a contest of equal powers. Christ’s authority will prove sufficient even when the visible circumstances seem to deteriorate.

Mark 9:21

21He asked his father, “How long has it been since this has been happening to him?” He said, “From childhood.

Arminian

Jesus asks about the duration of the suffering, and the father answers that it began in childhood. Arminian readers notice compassionate attention to a family’s long experience. The question gives the father room to speak, showing that personal care is not secondary to a powerful public miracle.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the long history of affliction underscoring the family’s helplessness and the depth of Christ’s mercy. Jesus does not approach the child as an anonymous problem. His attention to the father’s account accompanies authority that years of suffering have not exhausted or defeated.

Mark 9:22

22Often it has cast him both into the fire and into the water to destroy him. But if you can do anything, have compassion on us and help us.”

Arminian

The father asks for compassion while expressing uncertainty about Jesus’ ability. Arminian readers see an imperfect but genuine movement toward trust, not a polished religious performance. His plea includes himself in his son’s misery, reminding caregivers that Christ’s mercy addresses the burden shared by a whole family.

Reformed

Reformed readers notice that the father’s hope rests on Jesus’ compassion even while his confidence in Jesus’ power falters. The narrative will locate sufficient strength in Christ rather than in the petitioner. Weak prayer can therefore be honest about need without pretending that doubt is harmless.

Mark 9:23

23Jesus said to him, “If you can believe, all things are possible to him who believes.”

Arminian

Jesus redirects the father’s uncertain appeal toward believing reliance on God’s power. Arminian readers emphasize the real response of trust that Jesus invites, a response dependent on grace rather than human merit. The promise concerns God’s ability, not a guarantee that strong conviction secures every desired outcome.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that faith does not create power but rests on the God for whom deliverance is possible. Jesus challenges unbelief without presenting faith as a technique that controls him. The father’s next words show that even believing dependence needs help from the one it trusts.

Mark 9:24

24Immediately the father of the child cried out with tears, “I believe. Help my unbelief!”

Arminian

The father both confesses belief and asks Jesus to overcome the unbelief that remains. Arminian readers see a grace-enabled response that is real without being self-sufficient or complete. His willing trust and plea for further help fit an understanding of faith as responsive dependence rather than an achievement.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the appeal for help as an acknowledgment that even faith must be sustained by divine mercy. The father does not wait to make himself spiritually adequate before calling on Christ. This fits the Reformed emphasis on faith as God’s gift, while the verse itself is a prayer rather than a full account of regeneration.

Key contrast Both traditions regard this weak faith as dependent on grace: Arminian readers emphasize the father’s grace-enabled response and request for strengthening, while Reformed readers emphasize faith itself as God’s gift and sustaining work. The prayer does not independently settle the order of regeneration and faith.

Mark 9:25

25When Jesus saw that a multitude came running together, he rebuked the unclean spirit, saying to him, “You mute and deaf spirit, I command you, come out of him, and never enter him again!”

Arminian

Jesus commands the spirit to leave and forbids its return, acting as the crowd gathers. Arminian readers see a decisive rescue that answers the father’s appeal for compassion. The emphasis falls on Christ’s authority over the oppressor, not on a dramatic display or a method others can mechanically reproduce.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the effective authority of Jesus’ spoken command over an enemy that the disciples could not remove. His word secures the boy’s freedom rather than merely proposing it. This deliverance displays Christ’s power without by itself supplying an exact model for every aspect of conversion.

Mark 9:26

26After crying out and convulsing him greatly, it came out of him. The boy became like one dead, so much that most of them said, “He is dead.”

Arminian

The spirit departs violently, leaving the boy so still that observers think he has died. Arminian readers notice how easily appearances can obscure what Jesus is accomplishing. The story has not reached its conclusion, and the crowd’s judgment should not become the reader’s final assessment of Christ’s mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an apparent movement from death to life preparing for Jesus’ restoring action in the next verse. Mark reports that the boy looked dead, not that the crowd correctly diagnosed his condition. The rescue depends on Christ’s power rather than the spectators’ interpretation of events.

Mark 9:27

27But Jesus took him by the hand and raised him up; and he arose.

Arminian

Jesus takes the boy’s hand and raises him, completing the rescue with personal tenderness. Arminian readers see mercy restoring an afflicted person, not simply defeating an enemy in public. The action answers the father’s plea and directs attention back to the child whose suffering prompted the encounter.

Reformed

Reformed readers notice the movement from apparent death to standing life under Christ’s hand. The language resonates with the surrounding predictions of resurrection, though Mark does not explicitly say this boy had died. Jesus’ authority and gentle care belong together in the same saving action.

Mark 9:28

28When he had come into the house, his disciples asked him privately, “Why couldn’t we cast it out?”

Arminian

In private, the disciples ask Jesus to explain their failure. Arminian readers see an opportunity for teachable dependence rather than concealment or blame. Faithful ministry includes bringing inadequacy to Christ and receiving correction, not defending a reputation for spiritual power at the expense of those who suffer.

Reformed

Reformed readers recognize that Jesus continues teaching representatives whose weakness has been publicly exposed. Their commission has not made them autonomous, and failure becomes an occasion to learn that dependence afresh. His coming answer directs them toward God rather than toward a more impressive technique.

Mark 9:29

29He said to them, “This kind can come out by nothing but by prayer and fasting.”

Arminian

Jesus identifies prayer and fasting as the necessary posture of dependence rather than confidence in a repeatable technique. Arminian readers stress practices through which disciples seek God’s help, never ways to purchase power. Many critical editions omit the words about fasting; the retained WEB wording is preserved here.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that prayer confesses the source of deliverance to be God rather than the minister. Fasting, in the WEB wording, belongs to that dependence and has no automatic efficacy. The textual variation over fasting does not remove the shared call to rely on God in prayer.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
