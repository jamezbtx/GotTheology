Passage

Mark 9

Verses 30–50 · WEB

Jesus again predicts his death and resurrection, but the disciples answer the journey with rivalry about greatness. He teaches them to serve, welcome the lowly, and avoid obstructing others who act in his name. Severe warnings about causing believers to stumble and tolerating personal sin lead to a final command to live at peace. Both traditions hear a searching call to humble holiness rather than status, while the retained WEB repetitions in verses 44 and 46 require transparent acknowledgment of their textual variation.

Mark 9:30

30They went out from there and passed through Galilee. He didn’t want anyone to know it,

Arminian

Jesus passes through Galilee quietly in order to teach his disciples about what lies ahead. Arminian readers see intentional preparation for costly obedience rather than an effort to maintain public popularity. Those who minister to crowds also need time to hear Christ’s word and have their expectations corrected.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Jesus’ purposeful direction of his ministry as the cross approaches. He is not carried along by public demand but gives priority to forming his witnesses. Withdrawal here serves revelation and instruction, showing that visible activity is not the sole measure of faithful ministry.

Mark 9:31

31for he was teaching his disciples, and said to them, “The Son of Man is being handed over to the hands of men, and they will kill him; and when he is killed, on the third day he will rise again.”

Arminian

Jesus announces betrayal, death, and resurrection as the approaching shape of his mission. Arminian readers emphasize his knowing, willing movement toward suffering for others. Human violence remains morally responsible, while the promised resurrection assures disciples that their Lord’s self-giving will not end in the triumph of death.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the handing over of the Son of Man within God’s saving purpose, without excusing the human agents who will kill him. The certainty of resurrection accompanies the certainty of suffering. God’s plan is fulfilled through the crucified and risen Christ, not despite his cross.

Mark 9:32

32But they didn’t understand the saying, and were afraid to ask him.

Arminian

The disciples fail to understand and fear asking Jesus for clarification. Arminian readers see how anxiety can interrupt the openness needed for growth in faith. Mark’s account encourages honest questions rather than concealment, because belonging among Jesus’ followers does not mean already understanding the cost of his mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers recognize persistent dullness even in those who have received extraordinary instruction. Their weakness highlights their need for Christ’s continuing patience and revelation. The contrast with his clear announcement prevents treating their confusion as uncertainty in the divine purpose or in Jesus’ knowledge of what is coming.

Mark 9:33

33He came to Capernaum, and when he was in the house he asked them, “What were you arguing among yourselves on the way?”

Arminian

Jesus brings the disciples’ private argument into discussion once they are in the house at Capernaum. Arminian readers see a searching invitation to acknowledge motives they would rather leave unexamined. Learning discipleship requires honesty about ordinary ambitions, not only attention to dramatic revelations or successful ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ addressing the hidden rivalry that contradicts his teaching about suffering. His pastoral question exposes the heart without immediately discarding the learners. The house becomes a setting for correction, showing that the community needs his instruction precisely where it is most inclined to self-importance.

Mark 9:34

34But they were silent, for they had disputed with one another on the way about who was the greatest.

Arminian

The disciples’ silence reveals embarrassment over their argument about greatness. Arminian readers notice the clash between Jesus’ coming self-giving and their concern for rank. The problem is not merely ignorance of future events but a present desire that must be redirected toward humble love.

Reformed

Reformed readers see self-exalting pride persisting close to Jesus and even immediately after teaching about his death. Religious privilege has not removed the need for inward renewal. Mark’s honest portrait warns churches against assuming that spiritual office or close association with ministry guarantees freedom from rivalry.

Mark 9:35

35He sat down and called the twelve; and he said to them, “If any man wants to be first, he shall be last of all, and servant of all.”

Arminian

Jesus does not answer ambition with a better method for attaining prestige; he redefines being first as serving everyone. Arminian readers emphasize the real call to choose humble action under grace. Such service expresses discipleship rather than purchasing a superior place in God’s favor.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand the reversal as the pattern of life formed by allegiance to the servant King. Christ teaches the twelve together because their community must not reproduce the competition they have just displayed. Grace humbles rather than authorizes believers to claim superiority over those they serve.

Mark 9:36

36He took a little child and set him in the middle of them. Taking him in his arms, he said to them,

Arminian

Jesus places a child in the center and holds the child in his arms. Arminian readers see a concrete lesson in welcoming someone who cannot advance their ambitions. The tender action gives the coming teaching its shape: humility must be practiced toward actual people, not merely praised as an ideal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the child overturning the disciples’ calculations of importance. Jesus draws attention away from competing leaders toward someone dependent on others’ care. His embrace embodies the welcome he commands, grounding the lesson in his own character rather than in a romantic picture of childhood innocence.

Mark 9:37

37“Whoever receives one such little child in my name receives me; and whoever receives me, doesn’t receive me, but him who sent me.”

Arminian

Welcoming a child in Jesus’ name becomes a way of welcoming Jesus and the Father who sent him. Arminian readers emphasize that allegiance to Christ takes practical form in hospitality toward the overlooked. The teaching rules out a spirituality that professes devotion while neglecting dependent neighbors.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s identification with those received for his sake and the Father’s authority behind his mission. Humble service therefore reaches beyond social courtesy into obedience to God. The child’s lack of prestige exposes the error of measuring kingdom faithfulness by influence or worldly recognition.

Mark 9:38

38John said to him, “Teacher, we saw someone who doesn’t follow us casting out demons in your name; and we forbade him, because he doesn’t follow us.”

Arminian

John reports stopping a man who acted in Jesus’ name because he did not follow their own group. Arminian readers see the danger of confusing loyalty to Christ with loyalty to a particular circle. The disciples must learn to recognize genuine service without making their own approval its source.

Reformed

Reformed readers notice the irony that disciples who recently failed at deliverance now seek to restrict another worker. Their criterion is association with themselves rather than opposition to Christ. The episode challenges possessiveness in ministry while leaving room for the discernment that Jesus’ next words will supply.

Mark 9:39

39But Jesus said, “Don’t forbid him, for there is no one who will do a mighty work in my name and be able quickly to speak evil of me.

Arminian

Jesus forbids their attempt to silence the worker and points to his action in Jesus’ name as reason for restraint. Arminian readers see a call to charitable judgment about apparent allies. It is not necessary to invent a hostile motive simply because someone serves outside a familiar group.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Christ, not the disciples’ circle, governs the recognition of service done in his name. His immediate judgment favors welcome rather than rivalry. This local instruction does not establish that every impressive act proves saving faith; Matthew 7:22–23 warns against that broader inference.

Mark 9:40

40For whoever is not against us is on our side.

Arminian

Jesus directs the disciples to regard the worker as an ally rather than an enemy. Arminian readers understand the saying in its immediate setting of service in Jesus’ name, not as universal approval of religious indifference. It challenges exclusion based merely on whether others belong to their group.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish opposition to Christ from absence of membership in the disciples’ immediate company. The saying curbs factional suspicion without removing the need to discern truth and error. Christ’s cause is larger than their circle, and they must not obstruct those genuinely advancing it.

Mark 9:41

41For whoever will give you a cup of water to drink in my name because you are Christ’s, most certainly I tell you, he will in no way lose his reward.

Arminian

Jesus promises that even giving water to someone because that person belongs to Christ will not be overlooked. Arminian readers see encouragement for small, deliberate acts of love. The reward is grounded in God’s generosity, not in the idea that ordinary kindness earns salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the importance of belonging to Christ as the reason for the kindness described. God graciously acknowledges service that receives his people for their Lord’s sake. The promise dignifies hidden hospitality without turning good works into a competing foundation alongside Christ’s saving work.

Mark 9:42

42“Whoever will cause one of these little ones who believe in me to stumble, it would be better for him if he were thrown into the sea with a millstone hung around his neck.

Arminian

Jesus turns from welcoming the lowly to the terrible responsibility of causing a believing little one to stumble. Arminian readers hear a serious summons to guard others from destructive influence. The warning belongs to discipleship and should prompt repentance, protection of the vulnerable, and accountable use of authority.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s fierce concern for those who trust him, including believers whom others may regard as insignificant. The drowning image measures the gravity of leading them into sin. Assurance in God’s care never licenses careless leadership; his warnings are to shape how the church treats its weakest members.

Mark 9:43

43If your hand causes you to stumble, cut it off. It is better for you to enter into life maimed, rather than having your two hands to go into Gehenna, into the unquenchable fire,

Arminian

The command to cut off a stumbling hand uses shocking imagery to demand decisive resistance to sin. Arminian readers stress responsive repentance rather than casual confidence that harmful habits do not matter. Jesus calls for abandoning sin and its occasions, not literal self-injury, because bodily loss cannot cleanse the heart.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect the warning with the serious work of putting sin to death in dependence on grace. The contrast between life and Gehenna makes repentance urgent, not optional. Christ’s severe language is a summons to moral change, never permission to harm one’s body as a saving remedy.

Mark 9:44

44‘where their worm doesn’t die, and the fire is not quenched.’

Arminian

The WEB retains this refrain from Isaiah 66:24, while its footnote reports that NU omits the verse. Arminian readers should acknowledge the textual question without silently deleting the assigned verse. The repeated warning still directs hearers toward repentance, and the same words occur again in verse 48.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish the manuscript question about this repeated line from the passage’s clear warning of judgment. Many critical editions omit verse 44, but verse 48 preserves the refrain. Keeping the WEB wording here respects the chosen translation without pretending that all witnesses contain the repetition.

Mark 9:45

45If your foot causes you to stumble, cut it off. It is better for you to enter into life lame, rather than having your two feet to be cast into Gehenna, into the fire that will never be quenched—

Arminian

Jesus repeats the contrast with the foot, making costly repentance more important than preserving every present advantage. Arminian readers see an appeal to remove patterns that carry a person toward sin. The bodily imagery intensifies the warning; it does not prescribe mutilation as the means of receiving life.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear a sustained summons to abandon sin rather than accommodate it under religious language. Entering life matters more than retaining the apparent completeness of one’s present circumstances. The warning calls believers to active obedience, while the power for genuine change remains dependent on God’s grace.

Mark 9:46

46‘where their worm doesn’t die, and the fire is not quenched.’

Arminian

This repeated line is included in the WEB, whose footnote says that NU omits verse 46. Arminian readers can acknowledge the variation while receiving the force of the warning preserved in verse 48. Faithful reading distinguishes transparency about textual evidence from attempts to evade Jesus’ serious teaching.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the uncertainty concerns this location’s repetition, not whether Jesus warns about final judgment in the passage. The WEB text is retained without renumbering the assigned verses. The same image in verse 48 prevents the textual question from removing the paragraph’s solemn conclusion.

Mark 9:47

47If your eye causes you to stumble, throw it out. It is better for you to enter into God’s Kingdom with one eye, rather than having two eyes to be cast into the Gehenna of fire,

Arminian

The eye completes the sequence of hand, foot, and sight, pressing the demand to relinquish whatever becomes an occasion for sin. Arminian readers see a call for concrete choices sustained by grace. Entering God’s Kingdom is worth more than keeping a cherished desire that leads away from faithful obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers notice that entering the Kingdom parallels entering life in the preceding warnings. The imagery addresses the whole person rather than locating evil in one body part. Christ calls for searching repentance and disciplined refusal of temptation, not bodily damage or confidence in outward religious association.

Mark 9:48

48‘where their worm doesn’t die, and the fire is not quenched.’

Arminian

The image from Isaiah 66:24 gives the warning an enduring, inescapable seriousness. Arminian readers understand the appeal as a reason to turn from sin while hearing Christ’s call. The prophetic picture should not be reduced to a threat with no consequences, nor expanded into speculative descriptions beyond the text.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the unquenched fire and undying worm portray judgment that human resistance cannot bring to an end. God’s holiness gives weight to the repeated calls to repentance. The imagery is severe, but its purpose here is moral urgency rather than a detailed map of the world to come.

Mark 9:49

49For everyone will be salted with fire, and every sacrifice will be seasoned with salt.

Arminian

The saying joins fire with salt and, in the WEB wording, salt with sacrifice. Arminian readers may connect it with the costly purification of discipleship and the salted offerings of Leviticus 2:13. Its precise application is difficult, so it should not become a confident formula for every experience of suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers can hear sacrificial consecration and refining trial in this compressed image, while recognizing uncertainty about the scope of everyone and fire. The surrounding warnings also keep judgment in view. The verse calls for seriousness before God, but does not explain a mechanical process by which pain saves anyone.

Mark 9:50

50Salt is good, but if the salt has lost its saltiness, with what will you season it? Have salt in yourselves, and be at peace with one another.”

Arminian

Jesus ends the teaching by calling for salt within the disciples and peace among them. Arminian readers see enduring faithfulness expressed in relationships, directly correcting the earlier contest for greatness. Distinctive discipleship cannot be separated from the deliberate practice of humility and reconciliation within Christ’s community.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect the warning about tasteless salt with the danger of a community losing the character appropriate to its Lord. Jesus directs the disciples away from rivalry toward holiness and mutual peace. Grace should make their shared life visibly different, not merely furnish language for claiming superior standing.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
