Passage

Mark 9

Verses 1–13 · WEB

After calling his followers to take up the cross, Jesus gives three disciples a glimpse of his glory on a mountain. Moses and Elijah appear, and the Father directs the disciples to listen to his beloved Son. On the descent, Jesus places the revelation within the approaching resurrection and the scriptural pattern of suffering. Arminian and Reformed readers share this focus on Christ’s identity and the need to hear his word rather than impose a triumphal story upon it.

Mark 9:1

1He said to them, “Most certainly I tell you, there are some standing here who will in no way taste death until they see God’s Kingdom come with power.”

Arminian

Jesus promises that some of his hearers will see the Kingdom manifested in power before they die. Arminian readers can connect the following transfiguration with this promise as an anticipatory glimpse of glory. The resurrection and the gospel’s advance also belong to the wider horizon; the verse does not settle a distinctive debate between these traditions.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the certainty of Christ’s promise after his demanding call to take up the cross. The mountain scene immediately supplies a revelation of royal glory, while interpretations also relate the saying to later saving events. Its placement encourages disciples to trust the King whose present path includes suffering.

Mark 9:2

2After six days Jesus took with him Peter, James, and John, and brought them up onto a high mountain privately by themselves, and he was changed into another form in front of them.

Arminian

Jesus takes three disciples away from the larger group and reveals a transformed appearance before them. Arminian readers see gracious encouragement for followers unsettled by the prediction of his death. Their privileged experience calls for renewed trust and obedience rather than confidence that discipleship will avoid suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that Jesus initiates this revelation and selects its witnesses; they do not discover his glory by their own insight. The scene confirms the identity of the one who has announced the cross. This selection concerns witnesses here, not an explicit account of election to salvation.

Mark 9:3

3His clothing became glistening, exceedingly white, like snow, such as no launderer on earth can whiten them.

Arminian

The extraordinary whiteness places Jesus’ appearance beyond ordinary human achievement. Arminian readers see a visible disclosure of heavenly glory that strengthens the disciples’ reason to trust him. Mark’s homely comparison with laundering makes the wonder vivid without explaining the physical mechanism of the transformation.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish this heavenly radiance from the ordinary honor people confer on religious teachers. Jesus’ glory is revealed rather than manufactured by his followers. The detail prepares for the Father’s declaration, anchoring the disciples’ confidence in who Christ is rather than in their own expectations.

Mark 9:4

4Elijah and Moses appeared to them, and they were talking with Jesus.

Arminian

Moses and Elijah appear in conversation with Jesus, connecting this revelation with Israel’s scriptural story. Arminian readers may see the Law and the Prophets represented in these two figures. Their presence points toward continuity in God’s work, not a rivalry between Jesus and Israel’s earlier witnesses.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the older revelation bearing witness to its fulfillment in Christ. Moses and Elijah both belong to accounts of encountering God on a mountain, making their presence especially fitting here. Yet Mark does not report their conversation’s content, so the interpretation should not depend on invented details.

Mark 9:5

5Peter answered Jesus, “Rabbi, it is good for us to be here. Let’s make three tents: one for you, one for Moses, and one for Elijah.”

Arminian

Peter’s wish to build three tents expresses delight but also a failure to grasp what this moment requires. Arminian readers notice how a sincere disciple can respond to revelation with a mistaken proposal. Jesus needs to be heard on his own terms, especially concerning the suffering Peter has resisted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the danger of trying to arrange divine glory according to human preference. Three tents may suggest that Peter has not yet recognized Jesus’ unique place alongside the earlier witnesses. Mark’s next sentence explains his confusion, so elaborate reconstructions of his intentions remain uncertain.

Mark 9:6

6For he didn’t know what to say, for they were very afraid.

Arminian

Mark explains Peter’s speech through fear rather than mature understanding. Arminian readers find a realistic portrait of disciples who need continuing instruction even after following Jesus closely. An overwhelming spiritual experience does not itself produce wisdom, and frightened words should not be mistaken for settled insight.

Reformed

Reformed readers recognize human weakness exposed before a glory the disciples cannot control. Peter’s confusion does not prevent God from making his Son known. The Father’s coming word supplies the interpretation the witnesses cannot provide for themselves, showing their dependence on revelation rather than religious instinct.

Mark 9:7

7A cloud came, overshadowing them, and a voice came out of the cloud, “This is my beloved Son. Listen to him.”

Arminian

The Father identifies Jesus as his beloved Son and commands the disciples to listen. Arminian readers stress the responsibility to receive that testimony and obey the Son’s teaching about the cross. Divine revelation calls for a lived response, not merely admiration of the remarkable scene.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the Son’s authority rests on the Father’s testimony, not the disciples’ assessment. The cloud and voice give the event its decisive interpretation. Listening to Jesus therefore includes accepting his announced suffering, rather than setting human expectations above the word God has confirmed.

Mark 9:8

8Suddenly looking around, they saw no one with them any more, except Jesus only.

Arminian

When the other figures are no longer visible, Jesus remains with the disciples. Arminian readers see the practical focus of the scene: they must continue with him rather than remain absorbed in Moses, Elijah, or the experience itself. The revelation directs them back to the person they are called to follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s unique centrality as the older witnesses withdraw from view. This does not make Israel’s Scriptures worthless; their testimony leads to him. The disciples’ lasting foundation is the Son who remains with them, not their ability to preserve an extraordinary moment of glory.

Mark 9:9

9As they were coming down from the mountain, he commanded them that they should tell no one what things they had seen, until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead.

Arminian

Jesus sets a limit on public testimony until after his resurrection. Arminian readers see that glory must be understood together with the cross rather than used to encourage triumphal expectations. Faithful witnesses must respect his timing as well as speak accurately about what they have seen.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s authority over the disclosure of his identity and mission. The resurrection will make possible a fuller understanding of the mountain revelation. Until then, telling the story without the suffering it confirms could misrepresent the kind of Messiah God has sent.

Mark 9:10

10They kept this saying to themselves, questioning what the “rising from the dead” meant.

Arminian

The disciples obey the restriction while puzzling over Jesus’ words about rising from the dead. Arminian readers distinguish an obedient response from complete understanding: following Christ can precede clarity about everything he teaches. Their uncertainty invites patient learning rather than pretense that faithful people never have questions.

Reformed

Reformed readers observe how limited the disciples’ insight remains despite the Father’s testimony and the visible glory. Their understanding will need to be reshaped by Jesus’ death and resurrection. Mark presents this limitation honestly, directing confidence toward Christ’s reliable word rather than the disciples’ interpretive strength.

Mark 9:11

11They asked him, saying, “Why do the scribes say that Elijah must come first?”

Arminian

The disciples bring a scriptural question to Jesus after seeing Elijah. Arminian readers connect their expectation with Malachi 4:5, where Elijah’s coming precedes the day of the Lord. They are trying to understand the promised sequence, and Jesus receives their question rather than treating inquiry itself as disloyalty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Israel’s prophetic hope providing the framework for the disciples’ question. Their difficulty is not that Scripture is unreliable, but that their expectations have not included the Messiah’s suffering. Jesus will interpret the promised restoration alongside the scriptural pattern of rejection.

Mark 9:12

12He said to them, “Elijah indeed comes first, and restores all things. How is it written about the Son of Man, that he should suffer many things and be despised?

Arminian

Jesus affirms Elijah’s restoring role while asking why Scripture also speaks of the Son of Man’s suffering and rejection. Arminian readers see a correction of selective expectations. Trusting God’s promises requires receiving the costly parts of his saving purpose as well as the welcome prospect of restoration.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the cross belongs to the scriptural shape of the Messiah’s work, not an emergency departure from it. Restoration and suffering must be held together. Jesus challenges the disciples to read God’s promises as a whole rather than isolate the elements that fit their hopes.

Mark 9:13

13But I tell you that Elijah has come, and they have also done to him whatever they wanted to, even as it is written about him.”

Arminian

Jesus speaks of Elijah as already having come and suffered mistreatment, pointing to John the Baptist in his Elijah-like ministry. Arminian readers emphasize the responsibility of those who rejected God’s messenger. His rejection warns that a gracious call can meet hostility, and it foreshadows opposition to Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers connect John’s fate with the suffering pattern Jesus has just drawn from Scripture. Human hostility does not overturn God’s saving purpose, yet its agents remain responsible for their actions. Mark does not identify a single prediction detailing John’s treatment, so precision beyond that pattern would be unwarranted.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
