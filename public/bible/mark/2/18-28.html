Passage

Mark 2

Verses 18–28 · WEB

Questions about fasting and Sabbath conduct reveal the significance of Jesus’ presence and authority. The bridegroom and the images of cloth and wineskins explain why the new moment calls for a fitting response. Jesus then defends the disciples through David’s example, the Sabbath’s humane purpose, and the Son of Man’s lordship. These themes are largely shared ground; differences over particular devotional practices do not map neatly onto an Arminian and Reformed divide.

Mark 2:18

18John’s disciples and the Pharisees were fasting, and they came and asked him, “Why do John’s disciples and the disciples of the Pharisees fast, but your disciples don’t fast?”

Arminian

The question compares the fasting of other disciples with Jesus’ disciples’ practice. Arminian readers see a sincere issue of religious conduct that Jesus answers through his own presence. The passage does not begin by condemning fasting; it asks what response is fitting to the particular moment of his ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see inherited patterns of devotion brought into question by the arrival of Christ. The issue is not whether self-denial can ever be appropriate. Jesus will interpret fasting in relation to the bridegroom, redirecting attention from comparison with other groups to the significance of his presence.

Mark 2:19

19Jesus said to them, “Can the groomsmen fast while the bridegroom is with them? As long as they have the bridegroom with them, they can’t fast.

Arminian

Jesus compares his presence to a bridegroom’s company at a wedding. Arminian readers see joy as the fitting response to the arrival of God’s saving work. His answer locates devotion in a living relationship with him rather than a rule that treats every time and circumstance identically.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the bridegroom image placing Jesus at the center of the disciples’ shared life. Fasting associated with mourning would not fit the present celebration. He is not abolishing disciplined devotion but explaining why the form it takes must answer to who he is and what God is doing.

Mark 2:20

20But the days will come when the bridegroom will be taken away from them, and then they will fast in that day.

Arminian

Jesus anticipates a time when the bridegroom will be taken away and fasting will follow. Arminian readers see joy and costly loss both belonging within discipleship. Future fasting is not a denial of grace but an appropriate expression of longing in circumstances different from the present companionship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an early shadow of the separation that will mark Jesus’ path. The saying prevents the wedding image from becoming a promise of uninterrupted earthly celebration. Fasting remains appropriate in its time, grounded in relation to Christ rather than an attempt to earn acceptance through deprivation.

Mark 2:21

21No one sews a piece of unshrunk cloth on an old garment, or else the patch shrinks and the new tears away from the old, and a worse hole is made.

Arminian

The unshrunk patch would damage the old garment instead of repairing it. Arminian readers see the need to receive what Jesus brings on its own terms, not simply add it to unchanged expectations. The illustration warns against an unfitting combination without requiring contempt for everything that preceded his ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the image explaining why Christ’s arrival cannot be treated as a minor adjustment to existing practice. The new has a character that reshapes its setting. The point concerns compatibility and fulfillment in this context, not a blanket claim that earlier divine revelation was worthless.

Mark 2:22

22No one puts new wine into old wineskins; or else the new wine will burst the skins, and the wine pours out, and the skins will be destroyed; but they put new wine into fresh wineskins.”

Arminian

New wine needs fresh skins capable of receiving it without ruin. Arminian readers see readiness for the new situation created by Jesus’ ministry. The saying challenges rigid expectations about devotion, while the narrative context remains his explanation of fasting rather than a rejection of every inherited spiritual discipline.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s presence requiring forms appropriate to the reality he brings. Preserving the wine and skins, not destruction for its own sake, drives the image. Readers within both traditions differ on broader applications; these differences do not form a simple Arminian and Reformed dividing line.

Mark 2:23

23He was going on the Sabbath day through the grain fields; and his disciples began, as they went, to pluck the ears of grain.

Arminian

The disciples pluck grain while passing through the fields on the Sabbath. Arminian readers see an ordinary human action becoming the setting for a dispute about faithful observance. The narrative will interpret their conduct through need and Jesus’ authority rather than treating the mere occurrence of criticism as proof of wrongdoing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see daily bodily provision placed within the question of Sabbath obedience. The act is not presented as commercial harvesting, and Jesus’ response will challenge the accusation. The scene draws attention to the purpose of God’s gift rather than inviting casual dismissal of all moral obligation.

Mark 2:24

24The Pharisees said to him, “Behold, why do they do that which is not lawful on the Sabbath day?”

Arminian

The Pharisees challenge the action as unlawful on the Sabbath. Arminian readers see the need to examine an accusation through the purpose of God’s instruction rather than accept it merely because it is religiously framed. Jesus will answer from Scripture and then explain the Sabbath’s relation to human good.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a dispute over how the Sabbath command is applied. Concern for obedience is not itself rejected, but the charge must answer to Scripture and the authority of Christ. The story critiques this judgment without requiring a caricature of every Pharisee as insincere or indifferent to God.

Mark 2:25

25He said to them, “Did you never read what David did when he had need and was hungry—he, and those who were with him?

Arminian

Jesus recalls David’s hunger and the need of those with him. Arminian readers see Scripture used to recover attention to concrete human need within faithful judgment. His question challenges selective reading rather than setting compassion against God’s word as though the two must always conflict.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus answering the accusation by appealing to a scriptural example rather than discarding Scripture. David’s need and companions matter to the comparison. The appeal prepares a conclusion about the Sabbath’s purpose under the Son of Man’s authority, not a general permission to ignore every command.

Mark 2:26

26How he entered into God’s house at the time of Abiathar the high priest, and ate the show bread, which is not lawful to eat except for the priests, and gave also to those who were with him?”

Arminian

David and his companions received bread ordinarily reserved for priests. Arminian readers see a case in which necessity matters for interpreting holy provision. The example serves Jesus’ defense of his disciples; it does not imply that religious gifts exist independently of God’s concern for the people who need them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sacred provision considered alongside hunger rather than reduced to a boundary detached from mercy. Jesus’ appeal requires the critics to account for a recognized scriptural example. Its immediate role is to challenge their conclusion about his disciples and prepare the assertion of his own lordship.

Mark 2:27

27He said to them, “The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath.

Arminian

Jesus identifies the Sabbath as a gift made for human good. Arminian readers see divine commands serving life and mercy rather than making people expendable to a rule. The saying does not make convenience the measure of every obligation; it restores the purpose of this particular gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Sabbath’s humane purpose as integral to God’s institution, not a correction of a supposedly unkind Creator. Its good must guide faithful observance. Both traditions contain differing Sabbath practices, so this verse should not be turned into a uniform party contrast about Christian calendars.

Mark 2:28

28Therefore the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath.”

Arminian

Jesus concludes that the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath. Arminian readers see his authority as the decisive basis for understanding the disciples’ conduct. The issue reaches beyond permission to pluck grain: the one announcing God’s kingdom claims authority over the institution under dispute.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s lordship governing the interpretation and use of the Sabbath. His claim is greater than an appeal to personal freedom from inconvenient rules. Both traditions recognize the shared Christological center, even where Christians differ over how Sabbath observance applies after his resurrection.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
