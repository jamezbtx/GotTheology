Passage

Mark 2

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Back in Capernaum, Jesus teaches a crowded house when four people bring a paralyzed man through the roof. His announcement of forgiveness provokes an inward charge of blasphemy, which he answers with a public healing demonstrating the Son of Man’s authority. Both traditions recognize the shared center in Christ’s forgiving power and the reality of faith, without making the friends’ action a mechanism for transferring salvation from one person to another.

Mark 2:1

1When he entered again into Capernaum after some days, it was heard that he was at home.

Arminian

Jesus returns to Capernaum, and news of his presence spreads. Arminian readers see the ministry continuing within a familiar community after the wider Galilean mission. The report brings people toward him, setting the stage for an encounter in which physical need and forgiveness will meet.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an ordinary return becoming the setting for a further revelation of Christ’s authority. The news that he is at home draws attention to his presence rather than a carefully staged spectacle. The following scene will center on what he says and does for a particular sufferer.

Mark 2:2

2Immediately many were gathered together, so that there was no more room, not even around the door; and he spoke the word to them.

Arminian

The house is so crowded that even the doorway is blocked, yet Jesus speaks the word. Arminian readers see instruction remaining central amid intense demand for access. A large audience offers an opportunity to hear, but numbers alone do not establish that every hearer receives the message in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see proclamation at the heart of Jesus’ ministry even in a setting associated with healing. The crowd creates both an audience and an obstacle for those arriving next. The word is not a preliminary distraction from the real work; it frames the act of forgiveness and restoration.

Mark 2:3

3Four people came, carrying a paralytic to him.

Arminian

Four people carry a paralyzed man to Jesus. Arminian readers see practical concern making access possible for someone unable to come by his own strength. Their action prepares for the recognition of their faith, while the narrative gives no reason to identify disability itself with personal blame.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the care of others bringing a helpless sufferer into the encounter with Christ. Their service is concrete rather than merely verbal sympathy. The account highlights dependence and shared effort without making the bearers, rather than Jesus, the source of the man’s healing or forgiveness.

Mark 2:4

4When they could not come near to him for the crowd, they removed the roof where he was. When they had broken it up, they let down the mat that the paralytic was lying on.

Arminian

Blocked by the crowd, the bearers open the roof and lower the man’s mat. Arminian readers see persistent trust taking costly, imaginative action. Such persistence does not earn a miracle or establish a general right to disregard others’ property; it serves this narrative’s portrayal of determined access to Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faith expressed visibly through the effort to place the man before Christ. The obstacle does not become the final word in their approach. Their action makes dependence concrete, yet the saving authority revealed in the scene belongs to Jesus rather than to the intensity of human effort.

Mark 2:5

5Jesus, seeing their faith, said to the paralytic, “Son, your sins are forgiven you.”

Arminian

Jesus recognizes their faith and announces forgiveness to the man. Arminian readers see trust expressed through a shared approach to Christ, whose mercy reaches deeper than the visible need. The plural does not establish that another person’s faith can mechanically replace the man’s own relation to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faith as receiving rather than purchasing Christ’s gracious action. The group’s visible trust is acknowledged, but Jesus himself has authority to forgive. The verse does not explain the precise inward state of every participant or turn the friends’ action into transferable saving merit.

Mark 2:6

6But there were some of the scribes sitting there and reasoning in their hearts,

Arminian

Some scribes consider Jesus’ words inwardly. Arminian readers see the controversy beginning at the level of judgment before open accusation. Religious knowledge can become the setting either for recognizing God’s work or for resisting it; the following verses disclose the particular concern occupying these hearers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the narrative turning from the man’s need to the challenge raised by Jesus’ claim. The scribes’ silent reasoning will not remain hidden from him. Their presence permits the miracle to address not only physical restoration but the question of who has authority to forgive.

Mark 2:7

7“Why does this man speak blasphemies like that? Who can forgive sins but God alone?”

Arminian

The scribes recognize that forgiveness belongs ultimately to God but accuse Jesus of blasphemy. Arminian readers see the right question meeting an inadequate judgment of his identity. The narrative does not deny God’s unique prerogative; it reveals why Jesus’ exercise of that authority must be taken seriously.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the controversy sharpen the claim implicit in Jesus’ words. If he were merely presumptuous, the objection would carry force; the following demonstration reveals otherwise. The scene’s shared theological center is Christ’s authority to forgive, not a special disagreement between the two traditions.

Mark 2:8

8Immediately Jesus, perceiving in his spirit that they so reasoned within themselves, said to them, “Why do you reason these things in your hearts?

Arminian

Jesus perceives the scribes’ inward reasoning and confronts it directly. Arminian readers see his authority reaching beneath outward appearance to the judgments shaping response. He does not require them to hide their objection; he brings it into the open so that the meaning of his action can be addressed.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ disclosing what has remained unspoken and taking charge of the controversy. His question is not a request for information about the objection. It prepares an observable act that will demonstrate the authority they have challenged in the unseen matter of forgiveness.

Mark 2:9

9Which is easier, to tell the paralytic, ‘Your sins are forgiven;’ or to say, ‘Arise, and take up your bed, and walk’?

Arminian

Jesus compares a declaration of forgiveness with a command to rise and walk. Arminian readers see the coming healing offered as visible evidence for a claim that cannot be inspected in the same way. He is not suggesting that either forgiveness or restoration comes from easy words without real authority.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a contrast between an invisible verdict and a command whose outcome can be publicly tested. The question prepares the link between healing and forgiveness. Jesus will demonstrate that his words are not empty claims, while preserving the greater significance of his authority over sin.

Mark 2:10

10But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins”—he said to the paralytic—

Arminian

Jesus identifies the healing’s purpose: to show the Son of Man’s authority to forgive on earth. Arminian readers see bodily restoration confirming a saving claim rather than replacing it. The title centers attention on Jesus’ identity and mission, not on a technique that his observers could independently reproduce.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the miracle explicitly interpreted by Christ himself. Its meaning is not left to speculation: it attests the Son of Man’s authority in the matter of sin. Both traditions recognize that the scene discloses who Jesus is rather than merely celebrating remarkable human faith.

Mark 2:11

11“I tell you, arise, take up your mat, and go to your house.”

Arminian

Jesus commands the man to rise, carry his mat, and return home. Arminian readers see the command accompanied by the power needed to respond. The healing encounter illustrates dependence on Christ, but it should not alone be made a complete account of how saving faith arises in every person.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s effective word restoring a capacity the man did not possess beforehand. His obedience will be visible to all. The scene establishes Jesus’ authority in this particular sign; its primary purpose remains the claim to forgive rather than an exhaustive analogy for conversion.

Mark 2:12

12He arose, and immediately took up the mat and went out in front of them all, so that they were all amazed and glorified God, saying, “We never saw anything like this!”

Arminian

The man rises and carries his mat before the witnesses, who glorify God. Arminian readers see effective restoration confirming Jesus’ words and calling forth praise. The public response is appropriate wonder, yet the narrative does not identify every astonished observer as a fully committed disciple.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the visible result verifying the authority contested by the scribes. The man who was carried in now carries his own mat out. Praise is directed to God, while the sign invites the witnesses to recognize the significance of the Son of Man’s forgiving word.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
