Passage

Mark 2

Verses 13–17 · WEB

Jesus teaches beside the sea, calls Levi from the tax office, and shares a meal with tax collectors and sinners. Criticism of his company leads to the image of a physician whose mission addresses those in need. Arminian and Reformed readers share the conviction that grace reaches sinners and calls for repentance, while rejecting any account of holiness that makes respectable status the qualification for approaching Christ.

Mark 2:13

13He went out again by the seaside. All the multitude came to him, and he taught them.

Arminian

Jesus again teaches the crowd beside the sea. Arminian readers see the continuing openness of his public ministry and the importance of hearing his word. The change of setting does not change the center of the work: he calls people into the reality of God’s kingdom through teaching and encounter.

Reformed

Reformed readers see instruction continuing beyond the house where forgiveness and healing were displayed. The crowd’s presence creates the setting for another personal summons. Jesus’ ministry joins public proclamation with particular acts of calling, rather than making either crowds or private experience sufficient in isolation.

Mark 2:14

14As he passed by, he saw Levi the son of Alphaeus sitting at the tax office. He said to him, “Follow me.” And he arose and followed him.

Arminian

Jesus calls Levi from the tax office, and Levi rises to follow. Arminian readers see grace initiating a new direction and a genuine response that leaves established work behind. The call is not earned by respectable standing; Christ approaches a person whose occupation will soon provoke criticism of his company.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the initiative and authority of Christ bearing fruit in Levi’s decisive action. Discipleship begins with Jesus’ summons rather than social approval. The account shows him gathering an unlikely follower without supplying every detail of Levi’s earlier knowledge or inward experience.

Mark 2:15

15He was reclining at the table in his house, and many tax collectors and sinners sat down with Jesus and his disciples, for there were many, and they followed him.

Arminian

Many tax collectors and sinners share a table with Jesus and his disciples. Arminian readers see welcome opening the way for people to draw near rather than requiring prior social acceptance. His company does not declare sin harmless; the next words explain his presence as the work of a physician.

Reformed

Reformed readers see grace reaching people whom respectable observers regard with suspicion. Table fellowship displays the mission to sinners, not the reward of those already judged worthy. The account’s welcome and the call to repentance belong together rather than competing accounts of what Jesus has come to do.

Mark 2:16

16The scribes and the Pharisees, when they saw that he was eating with the sinners and tax collectors, said to his disciples, “Why is it that he eats and drinks with tax collectors and sinners?”

Arminian

The scribes and Pharisees question the disciples about Jesus’ companions at the meal. Arminian readers see the danger of allowing concern for religious standing to block mercy. The criticism comes from these observers in this encounter and should not be generalized into a judgment against all Jewish people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the controversy exposing a misunderstanding of the purpose of Jesus’ fellowship with sinners. His holiness is not maintained by refusing all contact with those who need restoration. The question prepares his explanation of a saving mission that challenges confidence in one’s own respectable position.

Mark 2:17

17When Jesus heard it, he said to them, “Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick. I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.”

Arminian

Jesus likens his mission to a physician’s care and calls sinners to repentance. Arminian readers see grace seeking people in real need and summoning a changed response, not merely affirming them as they are. The contrast challenges self-sufficiency rather than establishing a class who need no mercy before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ coming as the remedy for sin rather than a patron of human achievement. Those who count themselves healthy fail to recognize their need. Both traditions affirm that his welcome does not dispense with repentance, and this statement alone need not become a dispute over the extent of effectual calling.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
