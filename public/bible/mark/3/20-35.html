Passage

Mark 3

Verses 20–35 · WEB

Mark places the scribes' accusation between the attempt by Jesus' own people to restrain him and the arrival of his mother and brothers. Jesus explains his exorcisms as victory over Satan, warns against maligning the Holy Spirit, and identifies those doing God's will as his family. Both traditions affirm his victory and the seriousness of hardened rejection; they differ over whether a truly regenerate person can finally repudiate saving grace.

Mark 3:20

20The multitude came together again, so that they could not so much as eat bread.

Arminian

The gathering crowd leaves Jesus and his companions unable even to eat. Both traditions see the costly pressure of his ministry and the setting for the concern and opposition that follow, rather than evidence that bodily needs are unimportant.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize Jesus' real human circumstances amid his authority. The crowd's demands interrupt ordinary life and help explain why those close to him attempt to intervene.

Mark 3:21

21When his friends heard it, they went out to seize him; for they said, “He is insane.”

Arminian

Those close to Jesus attempt to seize him, judging him insane. The WEB's friends can refer to his own people; the later arrival of his family frames this episode. Arminian readers see how familiarity and concern can still misunderstand God's work.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish natural closeness to Jesus from spiritual understanding. The family scene that follows helps frame this intervention, but their claim about his sanity is the narrative's report of their judgment, not its endorsement.

Mark 3:22

22The scribes who came down from Jerusalem said, “He has Beelzebul,” and, “By the prince of the demons he casts out the demons.”

Arminian

The Jerusalem scribes attribute Jesus' deliverance of sufferers to demonic power. Both traditions distinguish this hostile reversal of good and evil from innocent uncertainty; the accusation prepares for his warning about blaspheming the Spirit.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see deliberate hostility in explaining Christ's authority as allegiance to the prince of demons. Their official standing does not validate the charge, which Jesus answers by exposing its incoherence.

Mark 3:23

23He summoned them and said to them in parables, “How can Satan cast out Satan?

Arminian

Jesus answers his accusers directly with parabolic reasoning. Arminian readers see an appeal that exposes their responsible misuse of judgment: casting out Satan's agents cannot simply be explained as serving Satan's cause.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that Jesus' reply exposes the accusation's contradiction. He does not concede its premise; his question directs attention to the actual conflict between his liberating work and Satan's rule.

Mark 3:24

24If a kingdom is divided against itself, that kingdom cannot stand.

Arminian

A kingdom torn by internal warfare cannot remain secure. Both traditions read the image within Jesus' answer to the demonic charge: his victories over demons undermine Satan's kingdom rather than advance it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the divided kingdom as a simple analogy exposing the accusation. The issue is the source and meaning of Jesus' exorcisms, not a promise that every outwardly united kingdom is righteous.

Mark 3:25

25If a house is divided against itself, that house cannot stand.

Arminian

The household image brings the same argument into ordinary experience. Arminian readers see the absurdity of treating Jesus' liberating work as Satan's successful policy; a household fighting itself cannot remain intact.

Reformed

Reformed readers share the reading of this image as a parallel to the divided kingdom. Internal conflict destroys a house, so the scribes' explanation fails to account coherently for Jesus' authority over demons.

Mark 3:26

26If Satan has risen up against himself, and is divided, he can’t stand, but has an end.

Arminian

If the scribes' account were true, Satan would be bringing his own power to an end. Both traditions see Jesus pressing their claim to its consequence, not acknowledging that his mission actually operates by Satan's power.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take this as the conclusion of Jesus' rebuttal. The downfall of Satan's dominion is real in Jesus' ministry, but it comes through a stronger opponent, as the next image explains.

Mark 3:27

27But no one can enter into the house of the strong man to plunder unless he first binds the strong man; then he will plunder his house.

Arminian

Jesus is the stronger deliverer who overcomes Satan, the strong man, and frees those under his power. Both traditions affirm that liberation depends first on Christ's victory, not human strength; the image does not itself specify every person's response to grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise identify Christ's superior authority as the explanation of his exorcisms. Satan is being overcome, not assisting Jesus. Binding here explains these acts of deliverance without implying that all satanic activity has already ceased.

Mark 3:28

28“Most certainly I tell you, all sins of the descendants of man will be forgiven, including their blasphemies with which they may blaspheme;

Arminian

The breadth of promised forgiveness includes even blasphemies; Jesus is not portraying God as reluctant to pardon. Arminian readers understand this mercy as received through repentant faith, not as an announcement that every person is forgiven regardless of response.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the vast reach of divine pardon, including grave offenses against God. The promise encourages repentant sinners and is not universal salvation; it introduces the solemn exception stated next.

Mark 3:29

29but whoever may blaspheme against the Holy Spirit never has forgiveness, but is subject to eternal condemnation.”

Arminian

In context, blasphemy against the Spirit is a hardened, malicious repudiation of God's manifest work, not an unwanted thought or an ordinary lapse. Arminian readers who affirm possible final apostasy understand that saving grace does not make a believer incapable of such decisive rejection; not every fall is this sin, and the verse does not identify the scribes as former believers.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand this sin as knowing, malicious rejection of the Spirit's manifest testimony, not a passing doubt or an ordinary lapse. They hold that God preserves the truly regenerate from this final repudiation, though people may receive substantial light and outward privileges without saving faith. The verse does not identify the scribes as former believers.

Key contrast Both identify the sin as hardened, malicious rejection of the Spirit's manifest testimony, not an ordinary lapse. Arminian readers who affirm possible final apostasy allow that a true believer can decisively repudiate grace; Reformed readers hold that God preserves every truly regenerate believer from this final rejection. Neither reading requires that the scribes were once regenerate.

Mark 3:30

30—because they said, “He has an unclean spirit.”

Arminian

Mark explicitly links the warning to the accusation that Jesus has an unclean spirit. Both traditions therefore locate the danger in maligning the Spirit's work as demonic, not in every mistaken word or anxious fear of having sinned beyond mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise let Mark's explanation govern the warning. The scribes are calling the holy source of Christ's deliverance unclean; this is a grave reversal of spiritual judgment, not simply a failure to understand everything about Jesus.

Mark 3:31

31His mother and his brothers came, and standing outside, they sent to him, calling him.

Arminian

The return to Jesus' mother and brothers completes the family frame around the scribes' charge. Both traditions see a question of allegiance emerging: biological relationship does not by itself determine membership in the community gathered around Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise notice the contrast between relatives outside and the gathering around Jesus. The scene establishes the priority of spiritual kinship without requiring contempt for natural family relationships.

Mark 3:32

32A multitude was sitting around him, and they told him, “Behold, your mother, your brothers, and your sisters are outside looking for you.”

Arminian

The message interrupts the circle seated around Jesus with the claims of his relatives outside. Arminian readers see the setting for his wider invitation to belong through doing God's will, rather than through privileged family access.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the narrative contrast between physical kinship and the community listening to Jesus. The relatives' presence raises the question he will answer: what truly constitutes his family?

Mark 3:33

33He answered them, “Who are my mother and my brothers?”

Arminian

Jesus' question redefines family around allegiance to God rather than denying that he has relatives. Both traditions recognize that even the closest natural ties must be ordered under the claims of his mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the question as an invitation to consider spiritual kinship. Jesus does not abolish family obligations; he denies that blood relationship alone establishes privileged standing in his kingdom.

Mark 3:34

34Looking around at those who sat around him, he said, “Behold, my mother and my brothers!

Arminian

Jesus identifies those gathered around him as his family, preparing for the explanation in verse 35. Arminian readers see the welcome of a new community whose closeness to him rests on responsive discipleship rather than ancestry.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ forming a household around himself. His welcome gives disciples a relationship deeper than shared descent, with the next verse describing the obedient character of that belonging.

Mark 3:35

35For whoever does the will of God is my brother, my sister, and mother.”

Arminian

Doing God's will identifies Jesus' true family, and the invitation extends beyond inherited status. Arminian readers understand this obedience as the grace-enabled expression of living faith, not a price paid to earn kinship with Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that obedience marks those who belong to Christ. Doing God's will is the fruit of renewing grace rather than the meritorious basis of adoption; both traditions reject ancestry or mere profession as sufficient.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
