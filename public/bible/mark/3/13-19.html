Passage

Mark 3

Verses 13–19 · WEB

After the gathering crowds and demonic confessions, Jesus calls a chosen company to the mountain and appoints the Twelve to be with him and to share his mission. Their number recalls Israel, while their preaching and authority depend on him. Judas's betrayal introduces a sobering note. Both traditions distinguish appointment to apostolic office from a simple guarantee of final salvation.

Mark 3:13

13He went up into the mountain and called to himself those whom he wanted, and they went to him.

Arminian

Jesus takes the initiative in choosing those who will serve in the apostolic company, and they come to him. Arminian readers affirm this gracious choice for office; the verse concerns his appointment of disciples rather than a full account of election to salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize Christ's freedom and initiative in choosing his messengers. The immediate subject is apostolic service, so this calling need not be treated as identical with the effectual saving call.

Mark 3:14

14He appointed twelve, that they might be with him, and that he might send them out to preach

Arminian

The Twelve are appointed first to be with Jesus and then to preach. Both traditions see fellowship with him as foundational for mission; the number twelve also recalls Israel's twelve tribes.

Reformed

Reformed readers share the connection between companionship and commission. Jesus forms his messengers through life with him, and the Twelve signal the gathering of God's people around the Messiah.

Mark 3:15

15and to have authority to heal sicknesses and to cast out demons:

Arminian

Their authority over sickness and demons is given by Jesus, not generated by their own power. Arminian readers see these works as serving his merciful mission and accompanying the proclamation entrusted to them.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise regard the authority as delegated and dependent on Christ. The messengers' healing and exorcism extend the signs of his kingdom; the power remains his rather than becoming their independent possession.

Mark 3:16

16Simon (to whom he gave the name Peter);

Arminian

Simon receives the name Peter as part of Jesus' formation of the apostolic company. Both traditions recognize his prominent place without treating a new name or appointment as a claim that he will never stumble.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ's authority in naming Simon. Peter's place among the Twelve is a gift of calling, not proof of personal superiority or a basis for self-confidence apart from Jesus.

Mark 3:17

17James the son of Zebedee; and John, the brother of James, (whom he called Boanerges, which means, Sons of Thunder);

Arminian

James and John are identified as brothers and given a shared name by Jesus. Both traditions can affirm his personal knowledge of those he calls without deriving a complete account of their character from the nickname alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the personal character of Jesus' calling and naming. The brothers enter a company formed by his authority; the title does not make their gifts or temperament the ground of their commission.

Mark 3:18

18Andrew; Philip; Bartholomew; Matthew; Thomas; James, the son of Alphaeus; Thaddaeus; Simon the Zealot;

Arminian

The list names individual disciples within the one apostolic company. Arminian readers see a shared commission that does not erase personal identity; Simon's designation alone does not establish the details of his political activity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize unity under Christ among distinct people. The list anchors the mission in named witnesses rather than anonymous enthusiasm, while giving little basis for reconstructing each man's background here.

Mark 3:19

19and Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him. Then he came into a house.

Arminian

The mention of Judas's betrayal casts a shadow over the appointment. Arminian readers distinguish genuine privilege and responsibility from guaranteed final faithfulness; this verse alone does not settle Judas's prior spiritual condition. The closing move into a house introduces the next scene.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish selection for apostolic office from election to salvation. Judas's presence does not mean Christ's saving purpose fails, and his betrayal remains culpable. The final sentence moves the narrative from the mountain to a house.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
