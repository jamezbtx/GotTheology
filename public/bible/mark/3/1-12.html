Passage

Mark 3

Verses 1–12 · WEB

Following the Sabbath controversies of chapter 2, Jesus restores a withered hand while his opponents plan his death. Pharisees and Herodians unite against him, but crowds from a wide region seek healing. Unclean spirits recognize his sonship and are silenced. Both traditions affirm his merciful authority and distinguish public excitement or demonic recognition from faithful discipleship.

Mark 3:1

1He entered again into the synagogue, and there was a man there whose hand was withered.

Arminian

Jesus returns to the synagogue, where a man's disability becomes the occasion for another Sabbath dispute. Both traditions see his ministry as bringing mercy into Israel's worship, not treating the sufferer as a mere test case.

Reformed

The setting continues the Sabbath controversy of chapter 2. Reformed readers share the recognition that Jesus' authority serves the afflicted and exposes religious judgments that miss God's merciful purpose.

Mark 3:2

2They watched him, whether he would heal him on the Sabbath day, that they might accuse him.

Arminian

The watchers seek grounds for accusation rather than the man's restoration. Arminian readers emphasize the responsibility of those who encounter Christ's goodness yet choose hostility; their scrutiny is not receptive faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see culpable hostility in the observers. Religious attention can coexist with a heart opposed to God, so outward familiarity with holy things does not itself produce faith.

Mark 3:3

3He said to the man whose hand was withered, “Stand up.”

Arminian

Jesus brings the man into public view instead of avoiding the conflict. His command gives the sufferer a place before the assembly and makes the question of mercy impossible to ignore.

Reformed

Jesus openly confronts an interpretation of Sabbath observance that obstructs compassion. Both traditions recognize his deliberate authority and concern for the man, even under hostile scrutiny.

Mark 3:4

4He said to them, “Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good or to do harm? To save a life or to kill?” But they were silent.

Arminian

Jesus frames Sabbath obedience around doing good and preserving life. Arminian readers see a summons to active love: refusing available mercy cannot be defended simply as religious caution.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that works of mercy accord with God's law. The opponents' silence exposes their inability to justify withholding good, while the mention of killing anticipates their plot.

Mark 3:5

5When he had looked around at them with anger, being grieved at the hardening of their hearts, he said to the man, “Stretch out your hand.” He stretched it out, and his hand was restored as healthy as the other.

Arminian

Jesus' anger and grief express holy opposition to hardened hearts, not indifference to those resisting him. The man's obedient action accompanies a restoration Jesus alone supplies; both traditions deny that he earns the healing.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the harmony of Christ's righteous anger and compassionate grief. His effective command restores the hand, displaying divine power through the man's response without making the healing a reward for merit.

Mark 3:6

6The Pharisees went out, and immediately conspired with the Herodians against him, how they might destroy him.

Arminian

The Pharisees join the Herodians, supporters of the Herodian ruling house, to plan Jesus' destruction. Both traditions see the bitter irony: those scrutinizing Sabbath healing now conspire to kill its healer.

Reformed

Religious opponents and allies of Herodian power unite against Jesus. Reformed readers recognize responsible human wickedness in the plot; divine providence does not excuse those who answer life-giving mercy with murderous intent.

Mark 3:7

7Jesus withdrew to the sea with his disciples; and a great multitude followed him from Galilee, from Judea,

Arminian

Jesus withdraws with his disciples, yet crowds continue to follow. Arminian readers see both prudent movement away from danger and the widening reach of his ministry; following a crowd is not yet the same as committed discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish Jesus' growing public following from saving faith. His withdrawal does not signal defeat: the opposition has not ended his ministry to people from across the region.

Mark 3:8

8from Jerusalem, from Idumaea, beyond the Jordan, and those from around Tyre and Sidon. A great multitude, hearing what great things he did, came to him.

Arminian

The list extends beyond Galilee and Judea to neighboring regions, including the area around Tyre and Sidon. Both traditions recognize the broad attraction of Jesus' deeds without assuming that everyone seeking help understands his mission.

Reformed

The crowd's geographical breadth shows that reports of Jesus have traveled widely. Reformed readers share the distinction between hearing of mighty works and receiving Christ in faith; widespread interest alone does not establish discipleship.

Mark 3:9

9He spoke to his disciples that a little boat should stay near him because of the crowd, so that they wouldn’t press on him.

Arminian

Jesus arranges a boat to avoid being crushed by the crowd. Both traditions see ordinary practical care within his extraordinary ministry, with the disciples serving a concrete need rather than merely observing miracles.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize the legitimacy of prudent means. Jesus' authority does not require ignoring bodily danger, and the disciples' simple preparation supports his work among the people.

Mark 3:10

10For he had healed many, so that as many as had diseases pressed on him that they might touch him.

Arminian

The healings explain the crowd's urgent desire for contact. Arminian readers affirm Jesus' generous compassion while distinguishing the search for physical relief from the fuller response of faith and obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see real mercy toward bodily suffering. The pressure of those seeking healing describes their need and expectation, not a technique by which human touch controls divine power.

Mark 3:11

11The unclean spirits, whenever they saw him, fell down before him and cried, “You are the Son of God!”

Arminian

The spirits recognize Jesus as God's Son and fall before him. Both traditions distinguish compelled acknowledgment of his identity from loving trust; knowledge of who Jesus is can coexist with opposition to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ's authority over the unclean spirits. Their confession is true but does not make them faithful witnesses or worshipers reconciled to God; recognition alone is not saving faith.

Mark 3:12

12He sternly warned them that they should not make him known.

Arminian

Jesus refuses to let unclean spirits direct the announcement of his identity. Both traditions see his authority over revelation as well as demons: his sonship must be understood through his own mission, not their cries.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Jesus governing how he is made known. Silencing the spirits shows that even a true title does not authorize an unclean source to shape public understanding of his person.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
