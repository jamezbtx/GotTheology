Passage

Mark 15

Verses 21–32 · WEB

Mark’s account of the crucifixion is spare and restrained. A passerby, Simon of Cyrene, is forced to carry the cross. At Golgotha Jesus refuses drugged wine, the soldiers divide his clothes, and the charge “THE KING OF THE JEWS” is posted above him between two robbers. Passersby, chief priests, and even the men crucified with him mock him: “He saved others. He can’t save himself.” Both traditions see the deep irony: by not saving himself, Jesus saves others. Verse 28 is missing from many early manuscripts and is omitted from some modern editions; the WEB includes it with a note.

Mark 15:21

21They compelled one passing by, coming from the country, Simon of Cyrene, the father of Alexander and Rufus, to go with them that he might bear his cross.

Arminian

Simon of Cyrene, from North Africa, is forced to carry the cross. Mark names his sons Alexander and Rufus, probably because Mark’s readers knew them (compare Romans 16:13, though the identification is uncertain). Arminian readers see a man drawn unexpectedly into Jesus’ story.

Reformed

Reformed readers often see God’s providence even in this forced labor. Both traditions find here a picture of discipleship: taking up the cross behind Jesus (Mark 8:34).

Mark 15:22

22They brought him to the place called Golgotha, which is, being interpreted, “The place of a skull.”

Arminian

Golgotha means “the place of a skull.” Arminian readers note Mark’s care to translate for his non-Aramaic readers.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. The exact location is a historical question, not a theological one.

Mark 15:23

23They offered him wine mixed with myrrh to drink, but he didn’t take it.

Arminian

Jesus refuses wine mixed with myrrh, probably offered to dull the pain. Arminian readers see Jesus choosing to face his suffering fully aware.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a willing acceptance of the cup the Father had given him (Mark 14:36). Both traditions read this as a sign of his deliberate obedience.

Mark 15:24

24Crucifying him, they parted his garments among them, casting lots on them, what each should take.

Arminian

The soldiers crucify him and gamble for his clothes, echoing Psalm 22:18. Arminian readers note that the psalm of the righteous sufferer shapes the scene.

Reformed

Reformed readers see fulfilled Scripture and God’s purpose at work. Both traditions read Psalm 22 as a key to Mark’s account, which reaches its peak in verse 34.

Mark 15:25

25It was the third hour when they crucified him.

Arminian

Mark places the crucifixion at the third hour, about 9 a.m. John 19:14 places a stage of the trial around the sixth hour, a known difficulty that interpreters handle in several ways. Arminian readers note the question honestly without forcing a single solution.

Reformed

Reformed readers also acknowledge the difficulty and offer various harmonizations, such as different ways of reckoning time. Neither tradition treats this question as a dividing line.

Mark 15:26

26The superscription of his accusation was written over him: “THE KING OF THE JEWS.”

Arminian

The written charge reads “THE KING OF THE JEWS.” Arminian readers note that Rome’s mockery announces the truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s irony: the cross proclaims Christ’s kingship. Both traditions hear the title as true in a way its authors never intended.

Mark 15:27

27With him they crucified two robbers, one on his right hand, and one on his left.

Arminian

Jesus is crucified between two robbers, perhaps rebels like Barabbas. Arminian readers see him placed among sinners in death.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see him counted with the guilty, an image of substitution often linked to Isaiah 53:12. Both traditions note Mark’s restraint in describing it.

Mark 15:28

28The Scripture was fulfilled which says, “He was counted with transgressors.”

Arminian

This verse quotes Isaiah 53:12, “He was counted with transgressors.” It is missing from the earliest manuscripts and is omitted from many modern critical editions; the WEB includes it and notes the omission. Arminian readers note that the idea is fully biblical (Luke 22:37), whatever its place in Mark.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point: the textual question does not affect doctrine, since Luke 22:37 records Jesus applying Isaiah 53:12 to himself. Both traditions treat it as an honest textual question.

Mark 15:29

29Those who passed by blasphemed him, wagging their heads and saying, “Ha! You who destroy the temple and build it in three days,

Arminian

Passersby mock him, wagging their heads (Psalm 22:7), and twist his words about the temple. Arminian readers note how misunderstanding his claims leads to scorn.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the irony: the true temple is about to be destroyed and raised in three days. Both traditions see the mockers saying more than they understand.

Mark 15:30

30save yourself, and come down from the cross!”

Arminian

“Save yourself, and come down from the cross!” Arminian readers see the final temptation to take a path without suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same temptation, echoing Peter’s rebuke in Mark 8:32–33. Both traditions agree that by staying on the cross, Jesus chose obedience.

Mark 15:31

31Likewise, also the chief priests mocking among themselves with the scribes said, “He saved others. He can’t save himself.

Arminian

“He saved others. He can’t save himself.” The leaders speak deeper truth than they know. Arminian readers stress that Jesus could have saved himself but freely chose not to, out of love for others.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that his “cannot” is a matter of obedience to the Father’s will, not lack of power: to save others, he would not save himself. Both traditions see the gospel’s heart in this taunt.

Mark 15:32

32Let the Christ, the King of Israel, now come down from the cross, that we may see and believe him.” Those who were crucified with him also insulted him.

Arminian

The leaders demand a sign: come down, and “we will believe.” Even those crucified with him insult him. Arminian readers note that demanding signs on one’s own terms is not true faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point, noting that faith comes not from such signs but from God’s work in the heart. Both traditions see the scene as the depth of Jesus’ rejection.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
