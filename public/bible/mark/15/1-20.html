Passage

Mark 15

Verses 1–20 · WEB

At dawn the Jewish council binds Jesus and hands him over to Pilate, the Roman governor. Jesus says little; Pilate is amazed, sees that envy lies behind the charges, and offers to release “the King of the Jews.” The crowd, stirred up by the chief priests, asks for Barabbas instead, and Pilate, wanting to satisfy them, has Jesus flogged and handed over to be crucified. Soldiers then mock him as a king. Mark’s irony runs throughout: those who mock Jesus as king speak more truly than they know. Arminian and Reformed readers share this reading; their emphases differ mainly on how divine purpose and human guilt relate.

Mark 15:1

1Immediately in the morning the chief priests, with the elders, scribes, and the whole council, held a consultation, bound Jesus, carried him away, and delivered him up to Pilate.

Arminian

Mark stresses speed and official action: the whole council meets at dawn, binds Jesus, and hands him to Pilate. Arminian readers note that the leaders act freely and are fully responsible for rejecting the Messiah.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree the leaders are fully guilty, and they also read “delivered him up” against Jesus’ repeated predictions that the Son of Man would be handed over (Mark 9:31; 10:33), so that human wickedness serves God’s purpose (compare Acts 2:23). Both traditions hold that God’s plan and human responsibility stand together here.

Key contrast Both affirm God’s purpose and human guilt. Arminians commonly frame God’s plan as wisely incorporating foreseen free acts; Reformed theology commonly holds that God ordained the event while the agents remain fully culpable, without being coerced.

Mark 15:2

2Pilate asked him, “Are you the King of the Jews?” He answered, “So you say.”

Arminian

Pilate asks the political question, and Jesus gives a guarded answer, “So you say,” neither denying the title nor accepting Pilate’s meaning of it. Arminian readers note that Jesus is a king, though not the rebel king Pilate fears.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Jesus affirming a kingship that Pilate cannot grasp. Both traditions note that “King of the Jews” becomes the title that runs through the whole chapter.

Mark 15:3

3The chief priests accused him of many things.

Arminian

The chief priests pile up accusations. Arminian readers see the leaders choosing to press their case despite the weakness of the evidence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the innocent Servant surrounded by false charges. This verse is shared ground for both traditions.

Mark 15:4

4Pilate again asked him, “Have you no answer? See how many things they testify against you!”

Arminian

Pilate presses Jesus to defend himself against the many charges. Arminian readers note that Pilate seems unsure what to do with this prisoner.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same uncertainty. Both traditions see the scene contrasting the anxious governor with the calm prisoner.

Mark 15:5

5But Jesus made no further answer, so that Pilate marveled.

Arminian

Jesus’ silence amazes Pilate. Many readers on both sides hear an echo of Isaiah 53:7, the Servant who did not open his mouth. Arminian readers see Jesus freely choosing the path of suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Servant’s willing silence, which fulfills Scripture. Both traditions agree that Jesus’ death was not forced on him against his will.

Mark 15:6

6Now at the feast he used to release to them one prisoner, whomever they asked of him.

Arminian

Mark describes a custom of releasing a prisoner at Passover. Apart from the Gospels, there is little direct evidence for this custom, and historians debate its details. Arminian readers simply note that the custom sets up the choice that follows.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same point. The historical question is not a dividing line between the traditions.

Mark 15:7

7There was one called Barabbas, bound with his fellow insurgents, men who in the insurrection had committed murder.

Arminian

Barabbas was jailed with rebels who had committed murder in an uprising. Arminian readers note the contrast: a real insurrectionist alongside the falsely accused “king.”

Reformed

Reformed readers often see a picture of substitution as the guilty man goes free while the innocent one dies, though Mark does not spell out this meaning. Both traditions use the scene carefully as an illustration rather than as a doctrine in itself.

Mark 15:8

8The multitude, crying aloud, began to ask him to do as he always did for them.

Arminian

The crowd asks Pilate to keep the custom. Arminian readers note how quickly the crowd’s mood can be shaped.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared narrative ground.

Mark 15:9

9Pilate answered them, saying, “Do you want me to release to you the King of the Jews?”

Arminian

Pilate offers to release “the King of the Jews,” possibly to provoke the leaders. Arminian readers see Pilate trying to avoid responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Pilate dodging. Both traditions note the irony that the title is mockery on Pilate’s lips but true of Jesus.

Mark 15:10

10For he perceived that for envy the chief priests had delivered him up.

Arminian

Pilate sees that envy drives the chief priests. Arminian readers stress that the leaders’ choice comes from a heart that has refused the truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see envy as an expression of the sinful heart that resists God’s Messiah. Both traditions treat envy as a serious, deadly sin.

Mark 15:11

11But the chief priests stirred up the multitude, that he should release Barabbas to them instead.

Arminian

The chief priests stir up the crowd to ask for Barabbas. Arminian readers note the shared guilt of leaders who manipulate people and of a crowd that goes along.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that both share the guilt. Both traditions warn against reading this as blame on the Jewish people as a whole; Mark names particular leaders and a particular crowd.

Mark 15:12

12Pilate again asked them, “What then should I do to him whom you call the King of the Jews?”

Arminian

Pilate asks what to do with “him whom you call the King of the Jews.” Arminian readers see Pilate putting the decision back on the crowd.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same evasion. This verse is shared ground.

Mark 15:13

13They cried out again, “Crucify him!”

Arminian

The crowd shouts, “Crucify him!” Arminian readers see a sobering picture of the human capacity to reject God’s Messiah.

Reformed

Reformed readers see in the cry a picture of human sin that the cross itself answers. Both traditions read it as a mirror of human rebellion, not of one nation alone.

Mark 15:14

14Pilate said to them, “Why, what evil has he done?” But they cried out exceedingly, “Crucify him!”

Arminian

Pilate asks what crime Jesus has committed, but the crowd only shouts louder. Arminian readers note that Jesus’ innocence is plain even to his judge.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that Jesus’ innocence matters theologically: the one who dies is without sin. Both traditions see Pilate’s question as a witness to Christ’s innocence.

Mark 15:15

15Pilate, wishing to please the multitude, released Barabbas to them, and handed over Jesus, when he had flogged him, to be crucified.

Arminian

Wanting to satisfy the crowd, Pilate releases Barabbas, has Jesus flogged, and hands him over to be crucified. Arminian readers see Pilate choosing popularity over justice and holding real responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree Pilate is guilty, while seeing the handing over as part of the plan Jesus foretold. Both traditions hold that Pilate’s choice was truly his own.

Mark 15:16

16The soldiers led him away within the court, which is the Praetorium; and they called together the whole cohort.

Arminian

Soldiers take Jesus into the Praetorium, the governor’s headquarters, and gather the whole cohort. Arminian readers note the growing humiliation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared narrative ground.

Mark 15:17

17They clothed him with purple; and weaving a crown of thorns, they put it on him.

Arminian

They dress him in purple, a royal color, and press on him a crown of thorns. Arminian readers see cruel mockery of Jesus’ claim to be king.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same mockery, and also the irony that the true King wears a crown of suffering. Both traditions find in it a powerful picture of humility.

Mark 15:18

18They began to salute him, “Hail, King of the Jews!”

Arminian

The soldiers mock him with a royal greeting. Arminian readers note that their words, meant as a joke, are true.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same irony. Both traditions see Mark inviting readers to recognize the King whom the soldiers scorn.

Mark 15:19

19They struck his head with a reed and spat on him, and bowing their knees, did homage to him.

Arminian

They strike his head, spit on him, and kneel in mock worship. Arminian readers see Jesus enduring violence without retaliation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same, connecting it to Isaiah 50:6. Both traditions find in Jesus a model of patient suffering (1 Peter 2:23).

Mark 15:20

20When they had mocked him, they took the purple cloak off him, and put his own garments on him. They led him out to crucify him.

Arminian

After the mockery, they put his own clothes back on him and lead him out to be crucified. Arminian readers note that the path to the cross is now set.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. This verse is shared narrative ground.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
