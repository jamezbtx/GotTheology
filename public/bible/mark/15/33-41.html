Passage

Mark 15

Verses 33–41 · WEB

From noon until three, darkness covers the land. Then Jesus cries out the opening words of Psalm 22, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”, is misunderstood as calling Elijah, and dies with a loud cry. At that moment the temple veil is torn from top to bottom, and a Roman centurion confesses, “Truly this man was the Son of God!” Women who had followed him from Galilee watch from a distance. Both traditions treat this as the center of Mark’s Gospel. They share a high view of the cross and differ over certain details of how the atonement works.

Mark 15:33

33When the sixth hour had come, there was darkness over the whole land until the ninth hour.

Arminian

Darkness covers the land from the sixth to the ninth hour, noon to 3 p.m. Arminian readers often see a sign of judgment, echoing Amos 8:9.

Reformed

Reformed readers also read the darkness as a sign of judgment, often connecting it to Christ bearing God’s judgment against sin. Both traditions see cosmic meaning in this event.

Mark 15:34

34At the ninth hour Jesus cried with a loud voice, saying, “Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?” which is, being interpreted, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”

Arminian

Jesus cries the opening words of Psalm 22. Arminian readers affirm that Jesus truly experienced abandonment for sinners. Many Arminians, including Wesley, held to penal substitution, while some in the broader Arminian tradition have favored a governmental emphasis, so internal diversity exists. All agree that the psalm ends in vindication.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly read the cry as Christ bearing God’s judgment against sin in the place of sinners (penal substitution), while insisting that the Trinity was not divided. Both traditions see real anguish here, not acting.

Key contrast Both see real abandonment for sin. Reformed theology consistently frames it as penal substitution. Arminian theology includes both penal-substitution and governmental emphases, so not all Arminians frame the cry the same way.

Mark 15:35

35Some of those who stood by, when they heard it, said, “Behold, he is calling Elijah.”

Arminian

Some bystanders mishear “Eloi” as a call for Elijah. Arminian readers note the tragic misunderstanding that surrounds Jesus to the end.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared narrative ground.

Mark 15:36

36One ran, and filling a sponge full of vinegar, put it on a reed and gave it to him to drink, saying, “Let him be. Let’s see whether Elijah comes to take him down.”

Arminian

Someone offers sour wine, perhaps echoing Psalm 69:21, while mocking. Arminian readers see a mix of kindness and scorn in the crowd.

Reformed

Reformed readers see further fulfillment of the psalms of the suffering righteous one. Both traditions read Scripture as shaping the details of the cross.

Mark 15:37

37Jesus cried out with a loud voice, and gave up the spirit.

Arminian

Jesus cries out loudly and breathes his last. Arminian readers note that he dies with strength, not in slow fading.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a willing, deliberate death (John 10:18). Both traditions affirm that Jesus truly died.

Mark 15:38

38The veil of the temple was torn in two from the top to the bottom.

Arminian

The temple veil is torn from top to bottom, an act of God, not of man. Interpreters debate whether this is the inner veil before the holy of holies or the outer curtain. Arminian readers stress that access to God is now opened for all through Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the same opening of access (Hebrews 10:19–20), and some also see a sign of judgment on the temple. Both traditions find great hope here.

Mark 15:39

39When the centurion, who stood by opposite him, saw that he cried out like this and breathed his last, he said, “Truly this man was the Son of God!”

Arminian

A Roman centurion, seeing how Jesus died, confesses him as the Son of God. Whether he meant this in a full Christian sense is debated, but Mark presents it as the climax of his Gospel (Mark 1:1). Arminian readers stress that a Gentile outsider responds to the revelation of the cross.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God opening the eyes of an unlikely witness. Both traditions note that the Gospel’s first full human confession of Jesus as Son of God comes at the cross.

Mark 15:40

40There were also women watching from afar, among whom were both Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of James the less and of Joses, and Salome;

Arminian

Women watch from a distance: Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James and Joses, and Salome. Arminian readers note their faithfulness when most disciples had fled.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise honor their loyalty. Both traditions note that these women will become witnesses of the burial and the empty tomb.

Mark 15:41

41who, when he was in Galilee, followed him and served him; and many other women who came up with him to Jerusalem.

Arminian

These women had followed and served Jesus in Galilee, and many others had come to Jerusalem with him. Arminian readers stress the important role of women among Jesus’ disciples.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree. Both traditions see them as faithful disciples whose testimony Mark carefully records.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
