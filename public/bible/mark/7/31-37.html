Passage

Mark 7

Verses 31–37 · WEB

After traveling through the Decapolis region, Jesus personally restores a man’s hearing and speech. Both traditions recognize his compassionate authority, while Mark’s account distinguishes the crowd’s amazement from obedience to Jesus’ command.

Mark 7:31

31Again he departed from the borders of Tyre and Sidon, and came to the sea of Galilee through the middle of the region of Decapolis.

Arminian

Jesus returns toward the sea of Galilee through the Decapolis region. Arminian readers see the continuing movement beyond the setting of the earlier purity dispute, with another act of restoration soon to follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to Mark’s geographical transition without inferring the identity of every person present. The narrative prepares another display of Jesus’ compassionate authority.

Mark 7:32

32They brought to him one who was deaf and had an impediment in his speech. They begged him to lay his hand on him.

Arminian

Others bring a man who cannot hear and has difficulty speaking, asking Jesus to touch him. Arminian readers see the practical love of those who help a vulnerable neighbor approach Christ.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize intercession and human care as part of the encounter. The request looks to Jesus’ power rather than to the man’s ability to explain his own need.

Mark 7:33

33He took him aside from the multitude privately and put his fingers into his ears; and he spat and touched his tongue.

Arminian

Jesus takes the man aside and uses bodily gestures directed to his ears and tongue. Arminian readers see personal, concrete care rather than treatment of the man as a public spectacle.

Reformed

Reformed readers also emphasize Christ’s attentive condescension to bodily need. These particular actions describe his freedom to heal; they do not establish a technique that others can control or reproduce at will.

Mark 7:34

34Looking up to heaven, he sighed, and said to him, “Ephphatha!” that is, “Be opened!”

Arminian

Jesus looks toward heaven, sighs, and commands the man to be opened. Arminian readers see compassion joined to dependence on the Father; Mark supplies the meaning of Ephphatha within the verse.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the authority of Jesus’ command while sharing the emphasis on his compassionate sigh. The healing is personal and purposeful, not a detached exhibition of power.

Mark 7:35

35Immediately his ears were opened, and the impediment of his tongue was released, and he spoke clearly.

Arminian

The man immediately hears and speaks clearly. Arminian readers see a gracious restoration of communication and participation in ordinary life, not a reward for a specified achievement.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize that Christ’s word effectively accomplishes the bodily healing. The event displays his authority without requiring every detail to become an allegory of conversion.

Mark 7:36

36He commanded them that they should tell no one, but the more he commanded them, so much the more widely they proclaimed it.

Arminian

The spreading report directly conflicts with Jesus’ command to remain silent. Arminian readers note that enthusiasm is not the same as obedience, even when the news being shared is true.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish amazement from submission to Christ’s direction. Mark records the widening fame without presenting disobedience as a model for faithful witness.

Mark 7:37

37They were astonished beyond measure, saying, “He has done all things well. He makes even the deaf hear and the mute speak!”

Arminian

The crowd marvels at Jesus’ restoration of hearing and speech. Arminian readers see praise arising from tangible mercy, while the wider story will show that amazement alone does not equal mature faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers share the joy in Christ’s good work and the caution about confusing wonder with understanding. The opened ears also sharpen the coming question of whether the disciples themselves truly hear.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
