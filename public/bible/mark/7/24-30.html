Passage

Mark 7

Verses 24–30 · WEB

In the region of Tyre and Sidon, a Gentile mother pleads for her daughter’s deliverance. The difficult exchange about children and dogs affirms Israel’s priority while ending in mercy for an outsider, showing that this priority does not mean permanent Gentile exclusion.

Mark 7:24

24From there he arose and went away into the borders of Tyre and Sidon. He entered into a house and didn’t want anyone to know it, but he couldn’t escape notice.

Arminian

Jesus moves into the region of Tyre and Sidon, yet cannot remain unnoticed. Arminian readers see the narrative opening toward a Gentile household rather than a ministry confined to familiar boundaries.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that Jesus’ withdrawal does not prevent mercy from reaching an outsider. The verse describes his movement and privacy without explaining every purpose behind them.

Mark 7:25

25For a woman whose little daughter had an unclean spirit, having heard of him, came and fell down at his feet.

Arminian

The mother hears of Jesus and falls at his feet on behalf of her afflicted daughter. Arminian readers see dependent petition that seeks mercy rather than claims a right to it.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the humility of approaching Jesus in helplessness. The child’s suffering draws out a mother’s intercession, and the narrative directs attention to Christ’s authority to deliver.

Mark 7:26

26Now the woman was a Greek, a Syrophoenician by race. She begged him that he would cast the demon out of her daughter.

Arminian

Mark identifies the woman as Greek and Syrophoenician, making her Gentile identity explicit. Arminian readers see mercy crossing an ethnic boundary as she pleads for her daughter.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize Gentile inclusion without erasing the particular place of Israel in Jesus’ mission. Her identity is important to the exchange, but it does not make her need unworthy of compassion.

Mark 7:27

27But Jesus said to her, “Let the children be filled first, for it is not appropriate to take the children’s bread and throw it to the dogs.”

Arminian

The image of children and dogs is difficult, and the priority given to the children should not be softened away. Arminian readers notice that first leaves room for mercy beyond Israel rather than announcing a permanent exclusion.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the ordered priority of Israel in the mission while noting that the encounter ends in Gentile deliverance. Both traditions should read the hard image within that outcome, not use it to justify contempt for outsiders.

Mark 7:28

28But she answered him, “Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs.”

Arminian

The woman accepts the image yet appeals to the abundance available even beneath the table. Arminian readers see persistent trust that finds hope in Jesus’ mercy rather than demands precedence over Israel.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see humble confidence in Christ’s sufficiency. Her reply does not claim merit; it rests on the possibility that his provision can reach her daughter too.

Mark 7:29

29He said to her, “For this saying, go your way. The demon has gone out of your daughter.”

Arminian

Jesus explicitly responds to her saying and announces the demon’s departure. Arminian readers see a genuine response to persistent trust, with deliverance remaining entirely his gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that her plea matters in the encounter without becoming the power that expels the demon. Christ’s authoritative word accomplishes what she could not do for her child.

Mark 7:30

30She went away to her house, and found the child having been laid on the bed, with the demon gone out.

Arminian

The mother finds her child delivered, confirming Jesus’ announcement before she returned home. Arminian readers see trust vindicated by mercy that reaches beyond the place where the request was made.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s effective authority over the demon despite physical distance. Both traditions receive this quiet ending as the restoration of a Gentile household, not an abstract argument about ethnicity.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
