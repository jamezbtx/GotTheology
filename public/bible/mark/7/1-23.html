Passage

Mark 7

Verses 1–23 · WEB

A dispute over unwashed hands leads Jesus to expose traditions that evade God’s command, especially the use of Corban to withhold care from parents. He then teaches the crowd and disciples that moral defilement comes from the heart rather than food, a diagnosis of inward sin that both Arminian and Reformed readers share.

Mark 7:1

1Then the Pharisees and some of the scribes gathered together to him, having come from Jerusalem.

Arminian

The arrival of religious leaders from Jerusalem brings questions of authority into focus. Arminian readers see the need to test religious scrutiny against Jesus’ teaching rather than assume that official standing guarantees discernment.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish religious office from faithfulness to God’s word. The coming dispute exposes the danger of examining others while leaving one’s own heart unexamined.

Mark 7:2

2Now when they saw some of his disciples eating bread with defiled, that is unwashed, hands, they found fault.

Arminian

The complaint concerns ritual defilement, not a lesson in hygiene. Arminian readers note that the disciples’ conduct becomes an occasion for Jesus to distinguish inherited custom from obedience to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that the issue is ceremonial standing rather than bodily cleanliness. Religious criticism can misjudge people when human rules become the measure of faithfulness.

Mark 7:3

3(For the Pharisees and all the Jews don’t eat unless they wash their hands and forearms, holding to the tradition of the elders.

Arminian

Mark explains the elders’ washing tradition for his readers. Arminian readers distinguish the value of inherited practices from any claim that they necessarily carry God’s authority.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same distinction between useful custom and binding divine command. Mark is setting out the practice behind this dispute, not authorizing contempt for Jewish people.

Mark 7:4

4They don’t eat when they come from the marketplace unless they bathe themselves, and there are many other things which they have received to hold to: washings of cups, pitchers, bronze vessels, and couches.)

Arminian

The detailed list shows how ritual washing shaped ordinary meals and household life. Arminian readers see the setting for Jesus’ challenge to misplaced confidence in outward observances.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the reach of these practices into daily life. The WEB includes couches in the list and provides no textual footnote here; the central question remains what truly defiles a person.

Mark 7:5

5The Pharisees and the scribes asked him, “Why don’t your disciples walk according to the tradition of the elders, but eat their bread with unwashed hands?”

Arminian

The leaders make the elders’ tradition the standard by which they question Jesus’ disciples. Arminian readers see a warning against treating conformity to human expectations as the test of devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that inherited authority must answer to God’s command. Jesus’ response will address the standard itself, not merely offer an exception for his disciples.

Mark 7:6

6He answered them, “Well did Isaiah prophesy of you hypocrites, as it is written, ‘This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me.

Arminian

Jesus applies Isaiah’s rebuke to worship that sounds faithful while the heart remains distant. Arminian readers see a summons to sincere love and responsive obedience, not merely correct religious speech.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that outward profession cannot substitute for a heart turned toward God. Both traditions recognize that this warning also searches the church rather than merely condemning outsiders.

Mark 7:7

7They worship me in vain, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men.’

Arminian

Human commands become destructive when taught as divine doctrine. Arminian readers see that earnest religious activity can still be empty if it replaces the obedience God actually seeks.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress God’s authority to define faithful worship. Both traditions reject the assumption that elaborate observance can compensate for a heart and life opposed to his word.

Mark 7:8

8“For you set aside the commandment of God, and hold tightly to the tradition of men—the washing of pitchers and cups, and you do many other such things.”

Arminian

Jesus identifies the exchange at the heart of the dispute: God’s command is set aside while human tradition is protected. Arminian readers see responsibility for this reversal, not an innocent misunderstanding.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see religious zeal becoming disobedience when it displaces Scripture. The target is tradition used against God’s command, not every inherited practice simply because it is inherited.

Mark 7:9

9He said to them, “Full well do you reject the commandment of God, that you may keep your tradition.

Arminian

Jesus’ sharp irony exposes how carefully people can preserve a custom while rejecting God’s authority. Arminian readers hear a call to relinquish cherished practices when they obstruct obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same need for reform under God’s word. Longevity and communal approval do not make a practice faithful when it contradicts what God has commanded.

Mark 7:10

10For Moses said, ‘Honor your father and your mother;’ and, ‘He who speaks evil of father or mother, let him be put to death.’

Arminian

Jesus appeals to Moses’ commands about honoring parents and the seriousness of reviling them. Arminian readers note that genuine devotion must take concrete family obligations seriously.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see continuity between reverence for God and care for parents. Jesus invokes the law to expose evasion, not to give the church a new mandate to enforce this penalty.

Mark 7:11

11But you say, ‘If a man tells his father or his mother, “Whatever profit you might have received from me is Corban,” ’ ” that is to say, given to God,

Arminian

Corban identifies something devoted to God, as the WEB’s explanatory footnote states. Arminian readers see the abuse: a religious designation becomes a pretext for withholding help that parents should receive.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise reject using a sacred commitment as cover for lovelessness. The criticism concerns a practice that makes devotion to God compete with the care God commands.

Mark 7:12

12“then you no longer allow him to do anything for his father or his mother,

Arminian

The problem extends beyond an individual excuse: the leaders no longer permit help for parents. Arminian readers see how a religious system can reinforce choices that evade love and responsibility.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that institutional approval cannot sanctify disobedience. Religious rules must never be used to prevent the neighbor-love required by God’s word.

Mark 7:13

13making void the word of God by your tradition which you have handed down. You do many things like this.”

Arminian

Jesus names the result as nullifying God’s word through handed-down tradition. Arminian readers hear a warning to examine whether their own habits protect self-interest rather than serve God.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the priority of God’s word over ecclesial custom. Both traditions should receive the rebuke self-critically rather than assume that only another community can make this mistake.

Mark 7:14

14He called all the multitude to himself and said to them, “Hear me, all of you, and understand.

Arminian

Jesus addresses the crowd, making understanding his teaching a public concern. Arminian readers emphasize the summons to attend and respond rather than leave discernment to religious specialists.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the authority and generosity of Jesus’ instruction. The crowd is called to listen carefully as he redirects attention from ceremonial disputes to the heart.

Mark 7:15

15There is nothing from outside of the man that going into him can defile him; but the things which proceed out of the man are those that defile the man.

Arminian

Jesus locates moral defilement in what comes out of a person rather than in food entering the body. Arminian readers see that external safeguards cannot replace inward cleansing by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that ritual separation cannot cure sin. The explanation in the following verses concerns the heart’s evil, not a denial that bodily conduct or obedience matters.

Mark 7:16

16If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear!”

Arminian

The official WEB includes this verse unbracketed in the main text and notes that NU omits it. Arminian readers receive its call to attentive hearing while recognizing the textual difference rather than making a disputed verse bear a distinctive doctrine.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise acknowledge the WEB’s note that NU omits this verse. The appeal to hear fits Jesus’ surrounding instruction, but the textual issue should remain visible and no doctrinal contrast needs to be forced here.

Mark 7:17

17When he had entered into a house away from the multitude, his disciples asked him about the parable.

Arminian

The disciples ask for explanation away from the crowd. Arminian readers see teachability in bringing confusion to Jesus, even though proximity to him has not yet produced full understanding.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise find encouragement in Jesus’ continued instruction of slow learners. Private explanation prepares them to grasp the difference between food entering the body and sin arising within.

Mark 7:18

18He said to them, “Are you also without understanding? Don’t you perceive that whatever goes into the man from outside can’t defile him,

Arminian

Jesus’ question exposes the disciples’ failure to understand, not just the crowd’s. Arminian readers see that followers remain responsible to attend to teaching and allow it to correct entrenched assumptions.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize their dependence on Christ for spiritual understanding. Both traditions recognize that discipleship includes correction, not an immediate end to confusion.

Mark 7:19

19because it doesn’t go into his heart, but into his stomach, then into the latrine, making all foods clean?”

Arminian

Jesus distinguishes digestion from the heart’s moral condition. The WEB keeps making all foods clean within his question; its footnote reports that NU instead closes the question after latrine and presents the cleansing of foods as a concluding declaration.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise preserve the WEB’s wording while acknowledging its alternative-punctuation footnote. Both traditions see a decisive challenge to locating moral purity in diet, though the two presentations frame the statement about foods differently.

Mark 7:20

20He said, “That which proceeds out of the man, that defiles the man.

Arminian

Jesus returns to his central claim: what proceeds from a person defiles that person. Arminian readers see the need for inward renewal, not simply a new list of outward restrictions.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that sinful acts reveal a deeper source. Both traditions understand the coming catalogue as exposing the heart, not dismissing the seriousness of concrete wrongdoing.

Mark 7:21

21For from within, out of the hearts of men, proceed evil thoughts, adulteries, sexual sins, murders, thefts,

Arminian

Jesus traces destructive thoughts and deeds to the human heart. Classical Arminian readers affirm deep corruption and inability apart from grace; they understand prevenient grace as enabling a response to God, though that remedy is not spelled out in this verse.

Reformed

Reformed readers also affirm the heart’s pervasive corruption and inability to turn to God unaided. They connect its remedy with regenerating grace that effectively renews the sinner; this verse diagnoses sin without itself setting out the full order of salvation.

Key contrast Both traditions affirm pervasive heart corruption and inability apart from grace; the difference is not depravity versus its denial. Arminian readers locate a grace-enabled response in prevenient grace, while Reformed readers emphasize effective regeneration; neither full account of the remedy is stated in this diagnostic verse.

Mark 7:22

22covetings, wickedness, deceit, lustful desires, an evil eye, blasphemy, pride, and foolishness.

Arminian

The list reaches beyond violent acts to greed, deceit, envy, pride, and folly. Arminian readers see that moral defilement includes inward desires and attitudes that grace must expose and transform.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note sin’s reach into motives as well as actions. Total depravity does not mean everyone commits every listed sin, but that no part of human life is untouched by corruption.

Mark 7:23

23All these evil things come from within and defile the man.”

Arminian

Jesus concludes that these evils arise within and defile the person. Arminian readers see why mere ritual cannot heal the heart and why repentance requires God’s renewing help.

Reformed

Reformed readers share that diagnosis and the need for inward cleansing. Both traditions reject a view of sin that blames food or external contact while excusing the person’s own desires and deeds.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
