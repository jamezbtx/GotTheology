Passage

Mark 6

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Jesus returns to His hometown and teaches in the synagogue, but familiarity breeds offense: they know Him as the carpenter, Mary’s son, with brothers and sisters among them. He does few mighty works there and marvels at their unbelief. He then sends the Twelve two by two with authority over unclean spirits, to travel light, depend on hospitality, and preach repentance. Both traditions see Jesus’ authority and the seriousness of rejecting Him; they differ in how they explain the link between unbelief and His mighty works.

Mark 6:1

1He went out from there. He came into his own country, and his disciples followed him.

Arminian

Jesus goes to His hometown with His disciples. Arminian readers see Him offering His ministry to those who knew Him best.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the hometown visit as a test of how familiarity responds to grace.

Mark 6:2

2When the Sabbath had come, he began to teach in the synagogue, and many hearing him were astonished, saying, “Where did this man get these things?” and, “What is the wisdom that is given to this man, that such mighty works come about by his hands?

Arminian

They are astonished at His wisdom and mighty works. Arminian readers see real amazement that falls short of faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see astonishment without trust. Recognizing power is not the same as receiving the person.

Mark 6:3

3Isn’t this the carpenter, the son of Mary and brother of James, Joses, Judah, and Simon? Aren’t his sisters here with us?” So they were offended at him.

Arminian

“Isn’t this the carpenter?” They take offense. Arminian readers see familiarity blinding people to who Jesus is. Some manuscripts read son of the carpenter instead.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same stumbling. Christians have long differed on how to understand the brothers and sisters named here, but the text’s point is the offense of Jesus’ ordinary origins.

Mark 6:4

4Jesus said to them, “A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country, and among his own relatives, and in his own house.”

Arminian

A prophet is not without honor except in his own country. Arminian readers see Jesus naming a common pattern of rejection by those closest.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus placing Himself in the line of rejected prophets.

Mark 6:5

5He could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and healed them.

Arminian

He could do no mighty work there except heal a few. Arminian readers see unbelief limiting what Jesus chose to do. God’s power is not overcome, but He does not force His works on those who reject Him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see could not as a moral or purposeful restraint, not a lack of power. Jesus does not display mighty works where unbelief would only scorn them, yet He still heals some.

Key contrast Both deny that unbelief overpowers Jesus. Arminian readers more often stress that God freely limits His works in response to human unbelief. Reformed readers more often stress Jesus’ purposeful restraint in judgment on unbelief, within God’s sovereign plan.

Mark 6:6

6He marveled because of their unbelief. He went around the villages teaching.

Arminian

He marvels at their unbelief and goes on teaching. Arminian readers see unbelief as a real human response that even surprises Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ true humanity in His marveling. Rejection does not stop His mission.

Mark 6:7

7He called to himself the twelve, and began to send them out two by two; and he gave them authority over the unclean spirits.

Arminian

He sends the Twelve two by two with authority over unclean spirits. Arminian readers see Jesus sharing His mission with His followers. Sending them in pairs provides mutual support and credible witness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see apostolic authority delegated by Christ. The disciples act in His name and power. The Twelve recall Israel’s twelve tribes, a sign of God renewing His people.

Mark 6:8

8He commanded them that they should take nothing for their journey, except a staff only: no bread, no wallet, no money in their purse,

Arminian

Take nothing but a staff. Arminian readers see dependence on God’s provision through hospitality. Traveling light teaches trust and keeps the focus on the message.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same dependence. The mission is urgent and simple. The instructions fit an urgent, short-term mission in Galilee.

Mark 6:9

9but to wear sandals, and not put on two tunics.

Arminian

Wear sandals, not two tunics. Arminian readers see simplicity matching the message. Simplicity guards against making ministry a means of gain.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Mark permits staff and sandals, differing in detail from Matthew and Luke; harmonizations vary. The differences among the Gospels are minor and do not change the point of dependence.

Mark 6:10

10He said to them, “Wherever you enter into a house, stay there until you depart from there.

Arminian

Stay in one house until you leave. Arminian readers see contentment and stability in mission. Moving around for better lodging would distract from the work.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wisdom in not seeking better lodging. Accepting hospitality builds relationship with those who receive the message.

Mark 6:11

11Whoever will not receive you nor hear you, as you depart from there, shake off the dust that is under your feet for a testimony against them. Assuredly, I tell you, it will be more tolerable for Sodom and Gomorrah in the day of judgment than for that city!”

Arminian

Shake off the dust as a testimony against those who reject you. Arminian readers see accountability for rejecting the message. The comparison with Sodom and Gomorrah is absent from many manuscripts and critical editions here, though it appears in Matthew 10:15.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a solemn sign of judgment on those who refuse the gospel. The textual note on Sodom does not change the warning.

Mark 6:12

12They went out and preached that people should repent.

Arminian

They preached repentance. Arminian readers see a call that all can and should answer. The message matches John’s and Jesus’ own call to turn back to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see repentance proclaimed as God’s command and gift. The disciples announce what Jesus announced in 1:15.

Mark 6:13

13They cast out many demons, and anointed many with oil who were sick and healed them.

Arminian

They cast out demons and anointed the sick with oil. Arminian readers see the kingdom’s power at work through ordinary followers. The healing ministry shows God’s care for whole persons.

Reformed

Reformed readers see signs confirming the message. Anointing with oil also appears in James 5:14. The disciples’ power is derived; it comes from Jesus’ authority given in verse 7.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
