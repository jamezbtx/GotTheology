Passage

Mark 6

Verses 45–56 · WEB

Jesus sends the disciples ahead by boat and prays alone. In the night He comes walking on the sea, intending to pass by, and calms their fear with “It is I.” The wind ceases, but they are astounded because they had not understood the loaves and their hearts were hardened. Back on land, crowds bring the sick, and all who touch His fringe are healed. Both traditions see Jesus’ divine identity revealed; they read the disciples’ hardened hearts with different emphases.

Mark 6:45

45Immediately he made his disciples get into the boat and go ahead to the other side, to Bethsaida, while he himself sent the multitude away.

Arminian

He makes the disciples go ahead while He dismisses the crowd. Arminian readers see Jesus directing His followers. Mark gives no reason for the urgency; John 6:15 reports that the crowd wanted to make Him king.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ purposeful leading. Jesus sends the disciples into the storm on purpose.

Mark 6:46

46After he had taken leave of them, he went up the mountain to pray.

Arminian

He goes up the mountain to pray. Arminian readers see Jesus’ dependence on the Father. Prayer follows a day of great ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see prayer at the heart of His mission. He seeks communion with the Father after the crowd’s acclaim.

Mark 6:47

47When evening had come, the boat was in the middle of the sea, and he was alone on the land.

Arminian

The boat is mid-sea; He is alone on land. Arminian readers see the disciples apart from Jesus. They are not out of His sight even when He is absent.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the setting for revelation. Mark stresses the distance to heighten the coming revelation.

Mark 6:48

48Seeing them distressed in rowing, for the wind was contrary to them, about the fourth watch of the night he came to them, walking on the sea; and he would have passed by them,

Arminian

He comes walking on the sea, meaning to pass by. Arminian readers see Jesus’ power over nature and His care for disciples who were straining at the oars.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an echo of God passing by Moses and Elijah, revealing divine glory.

Mark 6:49

49but they, when they saw him walking on the sea, supposed that it was a ghost, and cried out;

Arminian

They think He is a ghost and cry out. Arminian readers see fear and misunderstanding. Their terror shows how unexpected His presence is.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the disciples’ failure to recognize Him. Fear follows when they meet the unexpected glory.

Mark 6:50

50for they all saw him and were troubled. But he immediately spoke with them and said to them, “Cheer up! It is I! Don’t be afraid.”

Arminian

“Cheer up! It is I! Don’t be afraid.” Arminian readers see Jesus meeting fear with His own presence. His words reassure before the storm is even stilled.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that It is I can echo the divine name I AM. Along with walking on the sea, the words point to Jesus’ divine identity.

Mark 6:51

51He got into the boat with them; and the wind ceased, and they were very amazed among themselves, and marveled;

Arminian

He gets into the boat; the wind stops; they are amazed. Arminian readers see peace when Jesus is present. His presence brings calm.

Reformed

Reformed readers see amazement without understanding. Their astonishment reveals a lack of understanding, explained in verse 52.

Mark 6:52

52for they hadn’t understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened.

Arminian

They did not understand the loaves; their hearts were hardened. Arminian readers see hardness as a condition the disciples are responsible for and can overcome through Jesus’ patient teaching.

Reformed

Reformed readers see hardness as spiritual dullness only God can remove, and Jesus continues to teach them.

Key contrast Both see the disciples’ hardened hearts as a real failure that Jesus patiently addresses. Arminian readers more often emphasize human responsibility for dullness. Reformed readers more often emphasize the need for God to open hearts.

Mark 6:53

53When they had crossed over, they came to land at Gennesaret and moored to the shore.

Arminian

They land at Gennesaret. Arminian readers see the mission continuing. They come ashore at a different place than planned, but the work goes on.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ministry resuming. Gennesaret was a fertile plain on the northwest shore.

Mark 6:54

54When they had come out of the boat, immediately the people recognized him,

Arminian

People recognize Him. Arminian readers see eager response. Recognition quickly turns into a flood of requests.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus recognized at once wherever He goes. His reputation draws people, though recognizing His power is not yet understanding who He is.

Mark 6:55

55and ran around that whole region, and began to bring those who were sick on their mats to where they heard he was.

Arminian

They run around the region and bring the sick on mats wherever He is. Arminian readers see faith and love in action as people carry others to Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the community responding to need. Their effort mirrors the faith of the friends in 2:3–4.

Mark 6:56

56Wherever he entered—into villages, or into cities, or into the country—they laid the sick in the marketplaces and begged him that they might just touch the fringe of his garment; and as many as touched him were made well.

Arminian

All who touch His fringe are healed. Arminian readers see Jesus’ power available to all. The fringe recalls the woman of 5:27 and the tassels commanded in Numbers 15:38.

Reformed

Reformed readers see mercy flowing freely. Healing is a sign of the kingdom’s arrival in Jesus.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
