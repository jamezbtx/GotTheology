Passage

Mark 6

Verses 14–29 · WEB

As Jesus’ fame spreads, Herod Antipas fears that John the Baptizer has risen from the dead. Mark then tells in flashback how Herod imprisoned John for condemning his marriage to Herodias, and how a rash oath at a birthday banquet led to John’s beheading. The account foreshadows Jesus’ own death and shows the cost of faithful witness. Arminian and Reformed readers share the main lessons about courage, conscience, and the cost of truth.

Mark 6:14

14King Herod heard this, for his name had become known, and he said, “John the Baptizer has risen from the dead, and therefore these powers are at work in him.”

Arminian

Herod hears of Jesus and thinks John has risen. Arminian readers see a guilty conscience at work. Jesus’ growing reputation forces even rulers to respond.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Herod’s fear as conscience accusing him of John’s death. Mark uses Herod’s fear to introduce the story of John’s death.

Mark 6:15

15But others said, “He is Elijah.” Others said, “He is a prophet, or like one of the prophets.”

Arminian

Others say He is Elijah or a prophet. Arminian readers see people grasping toward Jesus’ identity but falling short. The opinions honor Jesus but fall short of the truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see popular opinions that anticipate Peter’s confession in 8:28–29. Each guess captures something true about Jesus while missing who He is.

Mark 6:16

16But Herod, when he heard this, said, “This is John, whom I beheaded. He has risen from the dead.”

Arminian

Herod insists it is John, whom he beheaded. Arminian readers see sin haunting the one who committed it. Herod cannot escape what he has done.

Reformed

Reformed readers see guilt that refuses to rest. His certainty reveals that conscience was still speaking.

Mark 6:17

17For Herod himself had sent out and arrested John and bound him in prison for the sake of Herodias, his brother Philip’s wife, for he had married her.

Arminian

Herod had imprisoned John because of Herodias, his brother Philip’s wife. Arminian readers see political power used against a prophet. Historians discuss the identity of Herodias’s first husband.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same abuse of power. The historical question about the name does not alter the moral point.

Mark 6:18

18For John had said to Herod, “It is not lawful for you to have your brother’s wife.”

Arminian

John told Herod it was unlawful to have his brother’s wife. Arminian readers see courageous truth-telling to power. John does not soften the message for the powerful.

Reformed

Reformed readers see John applying God’s law to a ruler, compare Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21. Faithful witness speaks to rulers as well as common people.

Mark 6:19

19Herodias set herself against him and desired to kill him, but she couldn’t,

Arminian

Herodias wanted to kill him but could not. Arminian readers see resentment seeking revenge. Herodias’s grudge shows how sin hardens when it is confronted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providence restraining evil for a time. God sets limits even on the plans of the powerful until His purposes are served.

Mark 6:20

20for Herod feared John, knowing that he was a righteous and holy man, and kept him safe. When he heard him, he did many things, and he heard him gladly.

Arminian

Herod feared John, protected him, and heard him gladly though perplexed. Arminian readers see a conscience moved by truth yet unwilling to change.

Reformed

Reformed readers see attraction to truth without repentance. Herod is drawn but not converted.

Key contrast Both see Herod as conscience-stricken but unrepentant. Arminian readers often see him resisting grace that was genuinely drawing him. Reformed readers often see the attraction as common grace or conviction that falls short of saving change.

Mark 6:21

21Then a convenient day came when Herod on his birthday made a supper for his nobles, the high officers, and the chief men of Galilee.

Arminian

An opportune day comes at Herod’s birthday banquet. Arminian readers note the setting of indulgence and display. Mark presents a gathering of the powerful that turns into a scene of injustice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the convenient day as Herodias’s opportunity. The banquet contrasts sharply with the feeding of the five thousand that follows.

Mark 6:22

22When the daughter of Herodias herself came in and danced, she pleased Herod and those sitting with him. The king said to the young lady, “Ask me whatever you want, and I will give it to you.”

Arminian

Herodias’s daughter dances and pleases Herod, who offers anything. Arminian readers see rash pride and lust shaping decisions. Some manuscripts read his daughter Herodias.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a ruler entrapped by his own words. The textual variant does not change the narrative.

Mark 6:23

23He swore to her, “Whatever you ask of me, I will give you, up to half of my kingdom.”

Arminian

He swears to give up to half his kingdom. Arminian readers see reckless oaths. Herod’s grand promise displays pride before his guests.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the echo of Esther 5:3, with a tragic reversal. The offer of half a kingdom is extravagant boasting, not real generosity.

Mark 6:24

24She went out and said to her mother, “What shall I ask?” She said, “The head of John the Baptizer.”

Arminian

Her mother tells her to ask for John’s head. Arminian readers see hatred seizing its chance. The daughter is drawn into her mother’s hatred.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sin’s plans moving toward their goal. Sin spreads through family and influence.

Mark 6:25

25She came in immediately with haste to the king and requested, “I want you to give me right now the head of John the Baptizer on a platter.”

Arminian

She asks for the head immediately on a platter. Arminian readers see the cruelty of the request. Her haste shows how quickly evil acts when given opportunity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the urgency of evil. The request on a platter turns the feast into a scene of death.

Mark 6:26

26The king was exceedingly sorry, but for the sake of his oaths and of his dinner guests, he didn’t wish to refuse her.

Arminian

The king is sorry but keeps his oath for his guests. Arminian readers see fear of people overriding conscience. Herod values reputation over righteousness, a warning to all.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pride and fear of shame leading to sin. A wrong oath is better broken than kept, yet Herod keeps it to save face.

Mark 6:27

27Immediately the king sent out a soldier of his guard and commanded to bring John’s head; and he went and beheaded him in the prison,

Arminian

John is beheaded in prison. Arminian readers see the cost of faithfulness. John dies for speaking truth, a pattern many faithful witnesses have shared.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the forerunner suffering before the Messiah. His death foreshadows the rejection of the one he came to prepare for.

Mark 6:28

28and brought his head on a platter, and gave it to the young lady; and the young lady gave it to her mother.

Arminian

The head is brought on a platter. Arminian readers see the grim result of indulgence and resentment. Mark tells the scene plainly, without softening its horror.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the world’s hostility to God’s messengers. The account exposes the corruption of power without restraint.

Mark 6:29

29When his disciples heard this, they came and took up his corpse and laid it in a tomb.

Arminian

John’s disciples bury him. Arminian readers see faithful love toward a martyred teacher. Faithfulness to John continues even after his death.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a quiet foreshadowing of Jesus’ burial. Mark’s note sets the stage for the greater burial and resurrection to come.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
