Passage

Mark 1

Verses 29–45 · WEB

Jesus’ ministry moves from a household healing to a crowded evening of mercy, then to solitary prayer and a wider preaching mission. His compassionate cleansing of a man with leprosy includes a clear command about what should follow, but the man’s publicity restricts Jesus’ public movement. Both traditions see power, tenderness, prayer, proclamation, and obedience held together rather than treating popularity or physical healing as the whole meaning of the kingdom.

Mark 1:29

29Immediately, when they had come out of the synagogue, they came into the house of Simon and Andrew, with James and John.

Arminian

The scene moves from public synagogue ministry to the home of Simon and Andrew. Arminian readers see Christ’s care entering ordinary domestic life. The named disciples accompany him, connecting their new calling with relationships and needs that remain present rather than disappearing when they begin to follow.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same Lord exercising care in household and synagogue alike. Mark keeps the disciples’ fellowship concrete through places and names. The movement prepares a healing that is quiet and personal, showing that Christ’s authority is not limited to public confrontations.

Mark 1:30

30Now Simon’s wife’s mother lay sick with a fever, and immediately they told him about her.

Arminian

The household tells Jesus about Simon’s mother-in-law and her fever. Arminian readers see concern bringing a particular need to Christ rather than assuming that illness proves spiritual failure. The account does not describe a special formula or sufficient level of faith required before the family may seek help.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a simple appeal arising from the household’s need. Illness reaches the families of disciples, and closeness to Jesus does not mean immunity from distress. Their report leads to his personal intervention without making the woman’s recovery depend on a narrated achievement of her own.

Mark 1:31

31He came and took her by the hand and raised her up. The fever left her immediately, and she served them.

Arminian

Jesus takes her hand, raises her, and the fever leaves; she then serves them. Arminian readers see restored strength answering merciful action with practical service. The recovery is a gift, not payment for hospitality. The WEB notes that NU omits the word immediately, while retaining it in the displayed text.

Reformed

Reformed readers see effective healing displayed in the woman’s ability to resume active service. Christ’s personal touch joins power with care. Her service follows recovery rather than earning it, giving a simple picture of received mercy bearing fruit without turning every detail into an allegory of conversion.

Mark 1:32

32At evening, when the sun had set, they brought to him all who were sick and those who were possessed by demons.

Arminian

As evening comes, people bring the sick and those afflicted by demons to Jesus. Arminian readers see the reach of human need and the readiness to seek his help. Mark distinguishes illness and demonic affliction here; the account does not equate every disease with a possessing spirit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the impact of the earlier healings drawing many sufferers to Christ. Their varied needs meet one source of mercy and authority. The description keeps bodily disease and demonic oppression distinguishable, preventing a simplistic claim that all physical sickness has the same immediate cause.

Mark 1:33

33All the city was gathered together at the door.

Arminian

The gathering at the door conveys intense public interest. Arminian readers see a community drawn by the possibility of help, while recognizing that crowds contain varied expectations. The pressure of so many needs will form part of the setting for Jesus’ prayer and decision to preach elsewhere.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the whole city pictured as converging on the house. The growing demand is real, but it does not become the final authority over Jesus’ mission. Mark’s sweeping description emphasizes the event’s public impact rather than counting the inward faith of each person present.

Mark 1:34

34He healed many who were sick with various diseases and cast out many demons. He didn’t allow the demons to speak, because they knew him.

Arminian

Jesus heals varied diseases and expels demons while refusing their speech. Arminian readers see mercy joined to control over how his identity is made known. Recognition by demons is not the testimony he seeks, and bodily healing should not be detached from his proclamation of God’s kingdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers see authority over illness and evil exercised without dependence on demonic endorsement. Jesus governs both the acts of deliverance and the accompanying speech. The scene displays his power broadly but does not supply a promise that every later believer will always receive immediate physical healing.

Mark 1:35

35Early in the morning, while it was still dark, he rose up and went out, and departed into a deserted place, and prayed there.

Arminian

Jesus withdraws before dawn to pray after a day of demanding ministry. Arminian readers see the incarnate Son’s genuine communion with the Father, not a display of self-sufficient activity. Prayer belongs within faithful service and helps correct any assumption that attending to people’s needs makes dependence on God unnecessary.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the true humanity of Jesus expressed in prayerful fellowship with the Father. His divine identity does not make this communion unreal or optional to his earthly mission. The withdrawal places the coming decisions about ministry within that relationship rather than the demands of popularity.

Mark 1:36

36Simon and those who were with him searched for him.

Arminian

Simon and his companions search for Jesus after his withdrawal. Arminian readers see disciples still learning how their teacher’s purpose relates to urgent public expectations. The narrative reports their search without making access to him something they can organize entirely according to the crowd’s wishes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the disciples’ practical concern set alongside Jesus’ prayer and mission. They know where the demand is concentrated but do not yet direct the work. The search prepares for an instruction that will broaden their understanding beyond remaining where the first success has occurred.

Mark 1:37

37They found him and told him, “Everyone is looking for you.”

Arminian

The disciples report that everyone is looking for Jesus. Arminian readers see the attraction of meeting immediate needs, while the next verse shows that popularity cannot alone determine faithful service. The report expresses the urgency they perceive; it is not a command that binds Jesus to remain.

Reformed

Reformed readers see public demand contrasted with the purpose governing Christ’s ministry. Many wanting his presence does not necessarily mean they understand his message. The scene invites attention to Jesus’ own explanation of his work rather than assuming that the largest crowd sets the right agenda.

Mark 1:38

38He said to them, “Let’s go elsewhere into the next towns, that I may preach there also, because I came out for this reason.”

Arminian

Jesus chooses to go to other towns so that he may preach there also. Arminian readers see a mission wider than satisfying the expectations of one place. Compassionate healing remains real, but the Good News must be announced and heard; his purpose cannot be reduced to maintaining local popularity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priority of proclamation directing Christ’s movement. He is not driven simply by where his reputation has become strongest. The decision does not oppose mercy to preaching; it keeps the signs of the kingdom within the wider mission of making God’s reign known.

Mark 1:39

39He went into their synagogues throughout all Galilee, preaching and casting out demons.

Arminian

Jesus travels throughout Galilee, preaching and casting out demons. Arminian readers see his words and liberating acts extending beyond the first community. The broader movement fulfills his stated purpose and gives more hearers occasion to encounter the message rather than requiring everyone to reach a single center.

Reformed

Reformed readers see proclamation and authority over evil remaining joined as the mission expands. The synagogue setting recurs, tying the wider ministry to its beginning at Capernaum. Christ’s work is governed by his calling, not contained by the expectations generated in one successful location.

Mark 1:40

40A leper came to him, begging him, kneeling down to him, and saying to him, “If you want to, you can make me clean.”

Arminian

The man with leprosy trusts Jesus’ ability while appealing to his willingness. Arminian readers see humble dependence rather than a demand that mercy can be compelled by sufficient confidence. His approach places a personal need before Christ without treating healing as a debt owed to the petitioner.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a direct appeal to Christ’s power and compassionate will. The man does not offer merit or bargain for help; he asks to be made clean. His posture shows dependence, while the narrative’s focus turns toward Jesus’ response rather than a technique for guaranteeing miraculous answers.

Mark 1:41

41Being moved with compassion, he stretched out his hand, and touched him, and said to him, “I want to. Be made clean.”

Arminian

Jesus responds with compassion, touch, and a willing word of cleansing. Arminian readers see mercy reaching a person marked by disease and exclusion. His willingness in this encounter reveals genuine care; it should not become a claim that every unanswered request for healing reflects deficient faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s power expressed through personal compassion rather than distant indifference. The touch does not overcome reluctance in him; his own willing word brings cleansing. The verse reveals the character of his mercy without requiring that every later illness be removed in the same way.

Mark 1:42

42When he had said this, immediately the leprosy departed from him and he was made clean.

Arminian

The leprosy departs and the man is made clean immediately. Arminian readers see Christ’s spoken willingness becoming effective restoration. The healing is not presented as the man’s achievement, nor does the narrative identify his disease as the result of a particular personal sin.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the efficacy of Christ’s word in the complete cleansing that follows. The focus is not on a prolonged struggle for sufficient spiritual power. Bodily restoration is a real act of mercy and a sign of authority, not merely a metaphor that makes the man’s suffering unimportant.

Mark 1:43

43He strictly warned him and immediately sent him out,

Arminian

Jesus sends the healed man away with a strict warning. Arminian readers see that receiving mercy does not remove the responsibility to obey the giver. The warning introduces specific directions rather than inviting the man to decide that any enthusiastic response will serve Christ’s purpose equally well.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the healer also exercising authority over what follows the miracle. Gratitude must take its shape from Christ’s instruction, not simply spontaneous publicity. The sternness does not negate the compassion already shown; both belong to his purposeful care and ministry.

Mark 1:44

44and said to him, “See that you say nothing to anybody, but go show yourself to the priest and offer for your cleansing the things which Moses commanded, for a testimony to them.”

Arminian

Jesus directs the man to the priest and the offering commanded by Moses, while forbidding general publicity. Arminian readers see obedience as the proper response to received cleansing, not its price. The instruction gives the restored man a concrete next step rather than leaving his gratitude without direction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ respecting the prescribed witness to cleansing rather than treating the healing as permission to disregard God’s commands. The offering follows the cure. Jesus’ concern for a testimony to them keeps the event connected to public acknowledgment, even as he restricts indiscriminate proclamation.

Mark 1:45

45But he went out, and began to proclaim it much, and to spread about the matter, so that Jesus could no more openly enter into a city, but was outside in desert places. People came to him from everywhere.

Arminian

The man publicizes the event despite Jesus’ warning, and crowds restrict Jesus’ movement. Arminian readers see that enthusiasm can disregard instruction and have real consequences. His disobedience does not undo the healing, but grateful excitement should not be assumed to justify ignoring Christ’s explicit direction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see mercy received followed by an action that complicates the ministry’s public course. The report spreads widely, yet increased attention is not an automatic measure of obedience. Christ remains the focus of those who come, while the narrative plainly records the cost of disregarding his command.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
