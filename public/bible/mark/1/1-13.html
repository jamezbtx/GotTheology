Passage

Mark 1

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Mark opens with the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God. Prophetic preparation takes shape in John’s preaching, baptism, and announcement of the stronger one who gives the Holy Spirit. Jesus is baptized, affirmed by the Father, and driven by the Spirit into wilderness testing. Arminian and Reformed readers share the central confession of his identity and obedient mission, recognizing that divine initiative precedes the response the gospel will demand.

Mark 1:1

1The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God.

Arminian

Mark begins with good news about Jesus as Messiah and Son of God. Arminian readers see salvation centered on his person before any human response is described. The opening announces God’s initiative and invites readers to understand the following events as the unfolding of that saving news.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the narrative framed by the identity of Jesus rather than human religious achievement. He is the promised Christ and the Son of God. This opening confession prepares the reader to recognize the authority revealed in his baptism, teaching, healings, and forgiveness.

Mark 1:2

2As it is written in the prophets, “Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way before you:

Arminian

The promised messenger prepares the way for the coming Lord. Arminian readers see God taking the initiative through his word and through John’s ministry, calling people to readiness. The WEB’s cross-reference identifies Malachi 3:1; the emphasis here is preparation for Jesus, not the messenger’s independent importance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the arrival of Jesus set within God’s prior promise. John’s role is preparatory and subordinate: he does not create the saving event but announces its approach. The prophetic introduction connects the Good News with God’s faithfulness rather than presenting it as an unforeseen religious innovation.

Mark 1:3

3the voice of one crying in the wilderness, ‘Make ready the way of the Lord! Make his paths straight!’ ”

Arminian

The wilderness voice calls people to prepare the Lord’s way. Arminian readers see a summons to repentance answering divine initiative. Read with John’s ministry in the following verses, making the path ready concerns a changed response to God rather than a human achievement that brings salvation into existence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the prophetic promise taking recognizable form in John’s preaching. The WEB’s cross-reference identifies Isaiah 40:3. Preparation directs attention toward the coming Lord, and the command belongs to the means God uses to awaken hearers rather than supplying a rival source of saving power.

Mark 1:4

4John came baptizing in the wilderness and preaching the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins.

Arminian

John’s baptism accompanies repentance and the promise of forgiveness. Arminian readers see an outward action joined to a real turning from sin, not a substitute for it. This preparatory ministry calls people to receive God’s mercy and points beyond John to the stronger one who will give the Spirit.

Reformed

Reformed readers see John summoning Israel to repent in readiness for Christ. Baptism cannot be isolated from the repentance his preaching requires. The verse joins cleansing, forgiveness, and preparation for the coming Lord without making the water an independent source of saving grace.

Mark 1:5

5All the country of Judea and all those of Jerusalem went out to him. They were baptized by him in the Jordan river, confessing their sins.

Arminian

The widespread response includes confession of sins and baptism in the Jordan. Arminian readers see repentance expressed openly rather than hidden behind religious standing. The sweeping description conveys the reach of John’s ministry; it need not assert that every individual in Judea responded with the same inward sincerity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God gathering hearers through John’s call and confronting their need for cleansing. Public confession acknowledges that inherited privilege does not remove guilt. The large response should not be confused with a claim that everyone who appeared at the river necessarily possessed lasting saving faith.

Mark 1:6

6John was clothed with camel’s hair and a leather belt around his waist. He ate locusts and wild honey.

Arminian

John’s clothing and food locate his ministry in a spare wilderness setting. Arminian readers see a messenger whose life does not seek social prestige or luxury. The description serves the narrative of preparation; it does not make his particular diet or clothing a universal requirement for faithful discipleship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the messenger’s simple manner reinforcing his subordinate role in the Good News. Attention is directed away from worldly display and toward the message he carries. The details describe John’s calling rather than establishing that physical austerity itself earns divine favor.

Mark 1:7

7He preached, saying, “After me comes he who is mightier than I, the strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and loosen.

Arminian

John contrasts his own unworthiness with the greatness of the one who follows. Arminian readers see humility recognizing that ministry depends on someone greater than the minister. His task is to direct hearers to Jesus, not gather permanent allegiance to his own authority or religious achievement.

Reformed

Reformed readers see John refusing to occupy the place belonging to Christ. The comparison with loosening sandals underlines the immense difference between messenger and Lord. Spiritual service is faithful when it points beyond the servant to the one whose authority and saving gifts surpass his own.

Mark 1:8

8I baptized you in water, but he will baptize you in the Holy Spirit.”

Arminian

John distinguishes his water baptism from Jesus’ gift of the Holy Spirit. Arminian readers see the inward renewal and empowerment only Christ can supply. The contrast makes dependence on divine grace explicit; the verse alone does not settle every later disagreement about the timing or experience of Spirit baptism.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ as the giver of the Spirit, accomplishing what an outward rite cannot produce by itself. John’s ministry prepares; Jesus supplies the promised gift. Both traditions recognize this decisive superiority without needing to treat every difference about baptism as an Arminian and Reformed divide.

Mark 1:9

9In those days, Jesus came from Nazareth of Galilee, and was baptized by John in the Jordan.

Arminian

Jesus comes to John and enters the waters of baptism. Arminian readers affirm that his participation does not mean he needs forgiveness for personal sin. He identifies with the people to whom he comes and enters his public ministry in obedient solidarity, rather than appearing as a sinner seeking cleansing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the sinless Son voluntarily taking his place among those he has come to serve. His baptism inaugurates the public mission affirmed in the following verses. The event concerns his obedient calling, not a change from personal guilt to forgiveness or a first acquisition of divine sonship.

Mark 1:10

10Immediately coming up from the water, he saw the heavens parting and the Spirit descending on him like a dove.

Arminian

The opened heavens and descending Spirit mark Jesus’ baptism as divine commissioning. Arminian readers see the Son’s ministry carried out in the Spirit’s presence and power. The image is a dove-like descent; it does not imply that the Spirit becomes an animal or that Jesus was previously without divine identity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Spirit’s descent publicly marking the Messiah’s calling and anointing for ministry. Father, Son, and Spirit are distinguished across this scene. The gift does not create Jesus’ deity but reveals the divine approval and empowerment attending his incarnate mission.

Mark 1:11

11A voice came out of the sky, “You are my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.”

Arminian

The heavenly voice identifies Jesus as the beloved Son in whom the Father delights. Arminian readers see the Father’s approval preceding the ministry’s public achievements. Sonship is not portrayed as a reward for impressing the crowds; this declaration anchors the identity through which the rest of the story is read.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Father’s testimony confirming the Son’s unique identity and accepted mission. Together with the Spirit’s descent, the voice reveals the personal distinctions within the one divine work. The baptism does not suggest that Jesus only now becomes God’s Son.

Mark 1:12

12Immediately the Spirit drove him out into the wilderness.

Arminian

The Spirit leads Jesus forcefully into the wilderness immediately after the baptism. Arminian readers see divine guidance that does not avoid conflict or testing. The Spirit’s direction and Satan’s temptation have different moral purposes; being led into a trial does not make God the source of evil enticement.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the trial within the obedient mission of the Spirit-anointed Son. Divine approval does not exempt Jesus from confrontation with evil. Mark distinguishes the Spirit’s sending from the tempter’s activity, allowing confidence in God’s purpose without confusing it with Satan’s hostile intention.

Mark 1:13

13He was there in the wilderness forty days, tempted by Satan. He was with the wild animals; and the angels were serving him.

Arminian

Jesus faces Satan’s temptation amid wilderness danger and angelic service. Arminian readers see the genuine cost of his obedient mission and God’s sustaining care. Mark gives no detailed dialogue here, so the emphasis remains on the tested Son rather than imagined accounts of each temptation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Son entering real conflict with the adversary without being abandoned by the Father. The angels’ service accompanies, rather than cancels, the hardship. Both traditions affirm his sinless faithfulness and true humanity without deriving a separate dispute from the mention of wild animals.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
