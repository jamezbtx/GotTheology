Passage

Mark 1

Verses 14–28 · WEB

After John’s arrest, Jesus announces God’s kingdom and calls hearers to repent and believe. He summons fishermen to follow and then displays authority in teaching and deliverance at Capernaum. Both traditions affirm the necessity of repentance, faith, and discipleship, while explaining the grace that brings about saving response differently. The exorcism directs attention to Christ’s authority rather than making every narrative detail a dispute about conversion.

Mark 1:14

14Now after John was taken into custody, Jesus came into Galilee, preaching the Good News of God’s Kingdom,

Arminian

John’s arrest precedes Jesus’ proclamation in Galilee. Arminian readers see the Good News advancing despite opposition to its preparatory messenger. The kingdom is God’s initiative, not the result of favorable political circumstances, and its announcement will call hearers into a changed relationship with the coming King.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the movement from John to Jesus unfolding within God’s saving purpose. The imprisonment of the messenger does not halt the message. Jesus announces God’s reign in a setting of real hostility, preparing readers for a kingdom whose advance is not measured by worldly security.

Mark 1:15

15and saying, “The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News.”

Arminian

Jesus announces fulfilled time and the nearness of God’s kingdom, calling for repentance and faith. Arminian readers understand these as genuine responses made possible by prior grace, not unaided human achievements. They hold that the grace enabling the hearer’s response can be resisted rather than making acceptance inevitable.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the proclamation summoning everyone who hears to turn and trust the Good News. Repentance and faith are necessary responses, not meritorious works. They understand saving response as the fruit of God’s effectual grace, while the command itself states the hearer’s obligation rather than explaining the entire order of salvation.

Key contrast Both affirm that repentance and faith are necessary and depend on grace rather than unaided ability. Arminian readers understand saving response through enabling grace that can be resisted. Reformed readers understand it as the fruit of effectual grace. The command itself does not settle every question about how that response arises.

Mark 1:16

16Passing along by the sea of Galilee, he saw Simon and Andrew, the brother of Simon, casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen.

Arminian

Jesus encounters Simon and Andrew during their ordinary work as fishermen. Arminian readers see the initiative of Christ reaching people within everyday life. Nothing here makes wealth, scholarly standing, or prior religious distinction the qualification for discipleship; the next verses place the emphasis on his invitation and their response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus taking the initiative in forming the circle of disciples. The brothers are engaged in ordinary labor when he approaches. Their occupation will become an image for a new vocation, showing that service begins with his summons rather than self-appointment to spiritual leadership.

Mark 1:17

17Jesus said to them, “Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men.”

Arminian

Jesus calls the brothers to follow and promises to make them gather others. Arminian readers see willing discipleship supported by Christ’s transforming purpose. Their future usefulness does not come from existing ability alone; they must learn from the one whose invitation creates the possibility of a new vocation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ joining command and promise: he summons disciples and undertakes their formation. The fishing image redirects ordinary experience toward gospel service. The narrative foregrounds his authority and transforming agency without making the manner of these brothers’ call a complete account of every conversion.

Mark 1:18

18Immediately they left their nets, and followed him.

Arminian

The brothers leave their nets and follow at once. Arminian readers see a concrete response with real vocational cost, not merely agreement with an idea. Their prompt obedience does not earn the invitation; it answers the initiative of Jesus and begins a life of learning from him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the authoritative summons of Jesus bearing visible fruit in decisive action. Grace does not make the brothers passive: they leave and follow. Their immediate response displays discipleship’s priority, while later growth and correction will show that this beginning is not yet mature understanding.

Mark 1:19

19Going on a little further from there, he saw James the son of Zebedee, and John his brother, who were also in the boat mending the nets.

Arminian

James and John are also found in the middle of their trade, repairing nets. Arminian readers see the widening of Jesus’ summons through ordinary relationships and responsibilities. The narrative does not portray discipleship as reserved for those who have already escaped daily labor or achieved special religious status.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus deliberately gathering additional disciples rather than waiting for a self-selected spiritual elite. Their family and work setting makes the coming change concrete. Mark names real relationships and occupations, grounding the beginnings of the mission in everyday life rather than abstract religious ideals.

Mark 1:20

20Immediately he called them, and they left their father, Zebedee, in the boat with the hired servants, and went after him.

Arminian

James and John leave their father and work to follow Jesus. Arminian readers see the costly reordering of loyalties that a response to Christ can require. The presence of hired servants is part of the account; it does not establish a universal command to abandon all family obligations.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the priority of Christ’s summons over an established vocation and family arrangement. Their action is personal and decisive, not merely inward approval. The passage describes these disciples’ particular calling rather than making identical economic choices the test of every believer’s faithfulness.

Mark 1:21

21They went into Capernaum, and immediately on the Sabbath day he entered into the synagogue and taught.

Arminian

Jesus teaches in the synagogue at Capernaum on the Sabbath. Arminian readers see his kingdom announcement taking the form of instruction within the community’s worship. The setting introduces the public encounter with evil that follows and shows that his ministry includes teaching, not only extraordinary acts of power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the authority of Jesus exercised through the word before the account of deliverance. The synagogue and Sabbath provide the setting for his public ministry. Mark does not treat instruction as incidental; the response to his teaching helps frame the recognition of his unique authority.

Mark 1:22

22They were astonished at his teaching, for he taught them as having authority, and not as the scribes.

Arminian

The hearers are astonished by the authority of Jesus’ teaching. Arminian readers see an invitation to attend to the messenger as well as the message. Astonishment alone is not yet discipleship, and the contrast with the scribes should not become a blanket claim that all Jewish teaching lacked truth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see authority intrinsic to Jesus’ teaching rather than borrowed prestige or clever presentation. The audience notices a real distinction from familiar instruction. Their amazement points toward his identity, while the narrative leaves room to distinguish being impressed from truly following the one who speaks.

Mark 1:23

23Immediately there was in their synagogue a man with an unclean spirit, and he cried out,

Arminian

An unclean spirit’s presence interrupts the synagogue gathering. Arminian readers see the arrival of Jesus exposing bondage even within a religious setting. The account concerns this man’s particular affliction; it does not justify identifying every mental illness or disruption in worship as demonic activity.

Reformed

Reformed readers see spiritual opposition confronted by Christ’s presence and authority. Being in a synagogue does not by itself free a person from bondage. The narrative introduces a specific deliverance rather than supplying a basis for speculative diagnoses of every form of distress.

Mark 1:24

24saying, “Ha! What do we have to do with you, Jesus, you Nazarene? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are: the Holy One of God!”

Arminian

The spirit recognizes Jesus as God’s Holy One and fears his authority. Arminian readers see that correct identification does not amount to loving trust or discipleship. The hostile recognition confirms the conflict: Jesus has come with power to challenge evil rather than negotiate peaceful coexistence with it.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the adversary acknowledging an identity it cannot resist with equal power. Knowledge about Jesus can coexist with opposition to him, so verbal accuracy alone is not saving faith. The question about destruction reveals fear of judgment before the Holy One’s authority.

Mark 1:25

25Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be quiet, and come out of him!”

Arminian

Jesus commands silence and orders the spirit to leave. Arminian readers see direct, liberating authority exercised for the afflicted man’s good. The deliverance depends on Christ’s word, not a contest of ritual skill or the man first proving himself worthy of help.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command sufficient to confront the unclean spirit. Jesus does not require demonic testimony to establish his identity and does not bargain with evil. The focus falls on his sovereign authority in this encounter, not on a prescribed technique for human exorcists.

Mark 1:26

26The unclean spirit, convulsing him and crying with a loud voice, came out of him.

Arminian

The spirit leaves with a final convulsion and cry. Arminian readers see real conflict ending in liberation through Christ’s authority. The violence of the departure does not imply that evil is his equal; the narrative’s outcome is obedience to his command and release for the man.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the unclean spirit compelled to leave despite its disturbing resistance. Christ’s word determines the outcome. The description makes the deliverance visible to those present without offering a universal pattern by which every genuine healing or spiritual rescue must be recognized.

Mark 1:27

27They were all amazed, so that they questioned among themselves, saying, “What is this? What can this new doctrine be? For with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!”

Arminian

The people connect Jesus’ teaching with his authority over unclean spirits. Arminian readers see word and action together pressing the question of his identity. Their amazement is understandable, but the narrative calls for more than fascination with power: the kingdom announcement summons repentance and faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the command over evil confirming the authority already heard in Jesus’ teaching. The crowd’s questions register something extraordinary without yet expressing full understanding. Christ is the center of the scene, not the excitement or reputation generated by the miracle.

Mark 1:28

28The report of him went out immediately into all the region of Galilee and its surrounding area.

Arminian

The report about Jesus spreads quickly across Galilee and beyond. Arminian readers see the public consequences of his teaching and deliverance. A growing reputation creates opportunities to hear, but hearing about him must still be distinguished from responding to his call in faith and obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the impact of Christ’s authority reaching beyond the original witnesses. Public notice is not identical with saving reception, and crowds do not by themselves define the success of his mission. The expanding report prepares for the pressure and movement described in the following scenes.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
