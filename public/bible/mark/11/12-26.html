Passage

Mark 11

Verses 12–26 · WEB

Mark sandwiches the temple cleansing between the cursing of a leafy but fruitless fig tree and its withering. Read together, the scenes form an enacted judgment on a temple system full of activity but empty of the fruit God sought, a house meant for prayer for all nations. Jesus then teaches the disciples about faith in God, believing prayer, and forgiving others. Both traditions share this reading; they differ mildly on how to describe the link between forgiving others and being forgiven.

Mark 11:12

12The next day, when they had come out from Bethany, he was hungry.

Arminian

Leaving Bethany the next day, Jesus is hungry. Arminian and Reformed readers agree Mark shows Jesus’ real humanity.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Christ’s true humanity stands alongside his divine authority in the same scene.

Mark 11:13

13Seeing a fig tree afar off having leaves, he came to see if perhaps he might find anything on it. When he came to it, he found nothing but leaves, for it was not the season for figs.

Arminian

He finds a leafy fig tree with nothing but leaves, for it was not the season for figs. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the act is symbolic; the season note signals that Jesus is enacting a parable, not venting frustration.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the leaves promised fruit that was absent, a picture of religious show without substance. Interpreters debate the exact horticultural detail.

Mark 11:14

14Jesus told it, “May no one ever eat fruit from you again!” and his disciples heard it.

Arminian

Jesus says no one will eat its fruit again, and the disciples hear. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this is a prophetic sign of judgment, echoing fig imagery in the prophets (compare Jeremiah 8:13).

Reformed

Reformed readers note the disciples hearing it prepares for the lesson in verses 20 to 25.

Mark 11:15

15They came to Jerusalem, and Jesus entered into the temple and began to throw out those who sold and those who bought in the temple, and overthrew the money changers’ tables and the seats of those who sold the doves.

Arminian

In the temple Jesus drives out sellers and buyers and overturns the tables of money changers and dove sellers. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this is a prophetic act of judgment on the temple’s abuse.

Reformed

Reformed readers note his zeal for the Father’s house and the authority he claims over it.

Mark 11:16

16He would not allow anyone to carry a container through the temple.

Arminian

He does not allow anyone to carry goods through the temple. Arminian and Reformed readers agree he halts the temple courts’ use as a thoroughfare and marketplace.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus asserting control over the sacred space.

Mark 11:17

17He taught, saying to them, “Isn’t it written, ‘My house will be called a house of prayer for all the nations’? But you have made it a den of robbers!”

Arminian

He teaches from Isaiah 56:7 and Jeremiah 7:11: a house of prayer for all nations made a den of robbers. Arminian readers stress the inclusion of the nations that the temple trade obstructed.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the Jeremiah context of judgment on a temple trusted falsely. Both see Jesus fulfilling God’s purpose for the nations.

Mark 11:18

18The chief priests and the scribes heard it, and sought how they might destroy him. For they feared him, because all the multitude was astonished at his teaching.

Arminian

The chief priests and scribes seek to destroy him but fear him because the crowd is amazed at his teaching. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the cleansing sharpens the conflict leading to the cross.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human hostility serving the path God has set for his Son.

Mark 11:19

19When evening came, he went out of the city.

Arminian

At evening Jesus leaves the city. Arminian and Reformed readers agree Mark keeps a careful day-by-day sequence.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Jesus’ steady control over his movements until his hour comes.

Mark 11:20

20As they passed by in the morning, they saw the fig tree withered away from the roots.

Arminian

Next morning they see the fig tree withered from the roots. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the total withering confirms the sign of judgment.

Reformed

Reformed readers note from the roots signals complete, not partial, judgment.

Mark 11:21

21Peter, remembering, said to him, “Rabbi, look! The fig tree which you cursed has withered away.”

Arminian

Peter remembers and points out the cursed fig tree. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the disciples are struck by the power of Jesus’ word.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the moment as an opening for teaching on faith.

Mark 11:22

22Jesus answered them, “Have faith in God.

Arminian

Jesus answers, have faith in God. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the object of faith is God himself, not faith as a force.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the lesson turns from judgment on the temple to trust in God who answers prayer.

Mark 11:23

23For most certainly I tell you, whoever may tell this mountain, ‘Be taken up and cast into the sea,’ and doesn’t doubt in his heart, but believes that what he says is happening, he shall have whatever he says.

Arminian

Whoever tells this mountain to be cast into the sea without doubting will have it. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this is vivid hyperbole for what God can do in response to faith; some suggest an allusion to the temple mount.

Reformed

Reformed readers caution against reading it as a guarantee for any wish. Faith rests on God’s will and promises (compare 1 John 5:14).

Mark 11:24

24Therefore I tell you, all things whatever you pray and ask for, believe that you have received them, and you shall have them.

Arminian

Whatever you pray and ask, believe you have received it and it will be yours. Arminian and Reformed readers agree this calls for confident prayer, shaped by God’s will and the rest of Scripture.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the promise is for prayer in line with God’s purposes, not a technique for controlling outcomes.

Mark 11:25

25Whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone; so that your Father, who is in heaven, may also forgive you your transgressions.

Arminian

When praying, forgive anyone, so that the Father may forgive your trespasses. Arminian readers stress that ongoing forgiveness from God is tied to the believer’s forgiving spirit, a real condition to be heeded.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree forgiving others is necessary, and read it as the fruit and evidence of having been forgiven, not a merit that earns forgiveness.

Key contrast Both insist forgiveness of others is required of disciples. Arminians typically treat it as a real ongoing condition of remaining in God’s forgiveness; Reformed readers typically treat it as the necessary evidence of a forgiven heart.

Mark 11:26

26But if you do not forgive, neither will your Father in heaven forgive your transgressions.”

Arminian

If you do not forgive, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses. Arminian and Reformed readers agree the warning is serious; some manuscripts (the NU text) omit this verse, but its teaching matches Matthew 6:15.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that an unforgiving heart contradicts the gospel and calls a profession into question.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
