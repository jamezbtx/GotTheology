Passage

Mark 10

Verses 17–31 · WEB

An earnest question about eternal life follows Jesus’ teaching that the Kingdom must be received like a child. Jesus loves the wealthy questioner, yet his summons exposes an attachment the man will not relinquish. The disciples’ astonishment leads to the declaration that salvation, impossible for human beings, is possible with God. Both traditions reject self-salvation and a prosperity reading of the promised rewards, while differing over resistible grace and the renewal by which sinners come to faith.

Mark 10:17

17As he was going out into the way, one ran to him, knelt before him, and asked him, “Good Teacher, what shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?”

Arminian

The man runs and kneels before Jesus, asking how he may inherit eternal life. Arminian readers see earnest interest without assuming that earnestness already amounts to saving faith. Coming immediately after the children’s welcome, his question about doing prepares a lesson about dependence on grace rather than personal accomplishment.

Reformed

Reformed readers notice the tension between inheriting life and asking what deed can secure it. The man’s reverence does not yet expose his deepest allegiance. Jesus will use the commandments and a personal summons to reveal a need that respectable conduct and material resources cannot resolve.

Mark 10:18

18Jesus said to him, “Why do you call me good? No one is good except one—God.

Arminian

Jesus challenges the man’s use of good by directing attention to God as the standard of goodness. Arminian readers see a searching correction of easy religious language and self-assessment. The question need not deny Jesus’ goodness; it presses the man to consider the seriousness of the God he approaches.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that genuine goodness is measured by God, not by comparative human respectability. The reply destabilizes any assumption that the man can simply add one admirable deed to an already sufficient life. It also invites reflection on the identity of the teacher he addresses.

Mark 10:19

19You know the commandments: ‘Do not murder,’ ‘Do not commit adultery,’ ‘Do not steal,’ ‘Do not give false testimony,’ ‘Do not defraud,’ ‘Honor your father and mother.’ ”

Arminian

Jesus names commands governing treatment of neighbors, including a prohibition of defrauding. Arminian readers see ethical obedience taken seriously rather than dismissed as irrelevant to faith. The list prepares to test the man’s relationship to possessions and the poor, showing that love requires more than an impressive self-description.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law functioning as a searching revelation of God’s requirements. The man must confront more than the outward appearance of respectability. The command against defrauding makes economic conduct particularly relevant to the coming challenge, without proving that Mark has accused him of a specific hidden crime.

Mark 10:20

20He said to him, “Teacher, I have observed all these things from my youth.”

Arminian

The man says he has observed these commands from his youth. Arminian readers need not assume deliberate dishonesty; a conscientious life can coexist with incomplete self-knowledge. Jesus’ next words reveal an attachment that his own account has not addressed, calling him beyond confidence in a respectable history.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish outward conformity from the wholehearted love God’s law requires. The man’s reply shows how easily moral accounting can miss a ruling attachment. Jesus neither endorses self-justification nor merely adds an arbitrary task; he will expose what keeps this particular man from following him.

Mark 10:21

21Jesus looking at him loved him, and said to him, “One thing you lack. Go, sell whatever you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me, taking up the cross.”

Arminian

Jesus loves the man and sincerely calls him to relinquish his wealth, give to the poor, and follow. Arminian readers see grace addressing someone who can resist its summons, as the next verse shows. The demand confronts his controlling attachment rather than teaching that a charitable payment purchases eternal life.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the reality of Jesus’ love and the sincerity of his command while distinguishing this outward call from effectual renewal. The challenge reveals the man’s allegiance to wealth and his inability to free himself. His subsequent refusal does not make Christ’s compassion false or establish his ultimate destiny.

Key contrast Both affirm Christ’s genuine love and the man’s responsible refusal in verse 22: Arminian readers see a sincere saving call resisted, while Reformed readers distinguish the outward summons from effectual grace and see the refusal revealing an unrenewed allegiance. Neither reading requires a claim about the man’s unreported later life.

Mark 10:22

22But his face fell at that saying, and he went away sorrowful, for he was one who had great possessions.

Arminian

The man leaves sorrowfully because he has many possessions, revealing the practical weight of Jesus’ demand. Arminian readers see a responsible refusal of a genuine invitation, illustrating resistance to grace. His grief does not equal obedient trust, and Mark does not tell readers whether he ever later returned.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the departure exposing bondage to a cherished treasure beneath the man’s respectable obedience. His decision is willing and morally responsible, not imposed against a desire to follow Christ. The narrative diagnoses the heart’s need for divine renewal without explicitly identifying the man’s final spiritual condition.

Mark 10:23

23Jesus looked around and said to his disciples, “How difficult it is for those who have riches to enter into God’s Kingdom!”

Arminian

Jesus turns the man’s departure into a warning about wealth and entrance into the Kingdom. Arminian readers see a real danger to trusting obedience when possessions become indispensable. The warning is not that poverty earns salvation, but that riches can compete with the allegiance Christ rightly asks of everyone.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize how wealth can reinforce the illusion of self-sufficiency and conceal spiritual need. The rich man’s moral seriousness did not free him from that attachment. Jesus diagnoses an obstacle grace must overcome, rather than offering a technique by which affluent people can secure entrance on their own.

Mark 10:24

24The disciples were amazed at his words. But Jesus answered again, “Children, how hard it is for those who trust in riches to enter into God’s Kingdom!

Arminian

Jesus repeats the warning while addressing the disciples tenderly as children. Arminian readers note that the WEB specifies those trusting in riches, words many critical editions omit. Either reading confronts misplaced confidence; the wider passage also insists that no person can achieve salvation without God’s gracious action.

Reformed

Reformed readers recognize that the shorter critical reading broadens the immediate wording, while the WEB explicitly names reliance on riches. Neither form turns salvation into a human accomplishment once wealth is removed. Jesus is leading the disciples toward a confession of helplessness and the sufficiency of God’s power.

Mark 10:25

25It is easier for a camel to go through a needle’s eye than for a rich man to enter into God’s Kingdom.”

Arminian

The camel and needle create an intentionally impossible picture, intensifying the warning about wealth. Arminian readers see Jesus exposing the futility of self-reliance rather than prescribing a difficult but achievable technique. The image prepares for grace as the answer, not a greater effort to squeeze possessions through a manageable opening.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the impossibility rather than reducing the image to inconvenience. Its force lies in confronting the idea that resources can secure access to God’s Kingdom. The next two verses interpret the saying through human inability and divine possibility, so softening the image would weaken its stated point.

Mark 10:26

26They were exceedingly astonished, saying to him, “Then who can be saved?”

Arminian

The disciples’ astonishment leads them to ask who can be saved at all. Arminian readers see an important movement beyond judging the rich man to recognizing shared need. Their question prepares for an answer grounded in God’s grace, not in discovering a social group capable of rescuing itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers hear the disciples reaching the implication of Jesus’ impossible image: salvation exceeds human capacity. Their question is answered neither by exceptional virtue nor by poverty as a substitute qualification. The narrative turns attention from the resources of those being saved to the power of the saving God.

Mark 10:27

27Jesus, looking at them, said, “With men it is impossible, but not with God, for all things are possible with God.”

Arminian

Jesus locates the possibility of salvation in God rather than human strength. Classical Arminian readers affirm that fallen people cannot save themselves or believe apart from enabling grace. They relate this promise to grace offered to all through Christ, received through faith rather than making salvation automatic or self-produced.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human impossibility answered by God’s effective saving action, including the renewal that brings a sinner to faith. God does more than supplement an independent human capacity. The verse fits that account of effectual grace, though it does not by itself spell out every step in the order of salvation.

Key contrast Both deny that people can save themselves: Arminian readers emphasize God’s enabling grace offered to all and received through faith, while Reformed readers emphasize effectual renewal that brings the elect to faith. The saying establishes divine necessity without explicitly detailing that wider debate.

Mark 10:28

28Peter began to tell him, “Behold, we have left all and have followed you.”

Arminian

Peter contrasts the disciples’ costly following with the rich man’s departure. Arminian readers recognize the significance of a real response to Jesus without making sacrifice a purchase of eternal life. His words lead into reassurance, but the coming reversal saying also guards against turning obedience into self-congratulation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that grace has drawn these imperfect disciples into a path the wealthy man refused at this point. Their sacrifices are real, yet cannot establish a claim that obligates God to save them. Jesus answers with generous promises rather than a calculation of what they have earned.

Mark 10:29

29Jesus said, “Most certainly I tell you, there is no one who has left house, or brothers, or sisters, or father, or mother, or wife, or children, or land, for my sake, and for the sake of the Good News,

Arminian

Jesus acknowledges losses endured for his sake and for the Good News, naming family, home, and land. Arminian readers see the cost of faithful allegiance taken seriously. The promise concerns sacrifices arising from following Christ, not permission to neglect ordinary duties or abandon dependents for personal spiritual advancement.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the Christ-centered reason for the sacrifices named. It is not deprivation by itself that receives this promise, but loss borne in allegiance to Jesus and his gospel. God’s generous provision assures disciples that costly obedience does not place them outside his fatherly care.

Mark 10:30

30but he will receive one hundred times more now in this time: houses, brothers, sisters, mothers, children, and land, with persecutions; and in the age to come eternal life.

Arminian

The promised abundance includes a new network of homes and relationships, yet expressly includes persecutions. Arminian readers see encouragement through the shared life of believers and hope of eternal life. Jesus does not promise a trouble-free exchange or a guaranteed multiplication of private wealth in return for sacrifice.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the generosity of God’s provision through his people alongside the continued reality of suffering. Present blessing and future eternal life are distinguished, not collapsed into material prosperity now. The promise rests on Christ’s faithfulness and gives courage for discipleship without presenting sacrifice as merit.

Mark 10:31

31But many who are first will be last, and the last first.”

Arminian

Jesus warns that present rank can be reversed, addressing disciples who have just described their sacrifices. Arminian readers see a call to persevere humbly rather than presume on an early response. Having followed when another walked away must not become a new reason for pride or contempt.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God overturning human measures of importance, including religious accomplishments that might seem to establish priority. Grace excludes boasting even among those who truly follow. The saying prepares for the next dispute over prominent seats, showing how persistent the desire for rank remains.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
