Passage

Mark 10

Verses 1–16 · WEB

On the journey toward Jerusalem, Jesus is tested with a question about divorce and answers by returning to God’s intention in creation. He distinguishes the Mosaic accommodation to hard hearts from the enduring seriousness of the marriage bond. The following welcome of children exposes the disciples’ mistaken judgments about who belongs near him. Arminian and Reformed readers share the call to marital faithfulness and humble reception of the Kingdom, while pastoral questions about divorce and doctrinal questions about baptism require more than this passage alone.

Mark 10:1

1He arose from there and came into the borders of Judea and beyond the Jordan. Multitudes came together to him again. As he usually did, he was again teaching them.

Arminian

Jesus continues teaching crowds as he moves into Judea and beyond the Jordan. Arminian readers see a ministry of gracious instruction continuing on the journey toward suffering. The setting connects the following moral questions with the larger call to follow Jesus, rather than presenting them as detached rules.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the purposeful progress toward Jerusalem while Jesus continues to reveal God’s will. Public teaching and private instruction together prepare the way for the cross. His authority over discipleship extends to ordinary relationships, not only to miracles or statements about the coming Kingdom.

Mark 10:2

2Pharisees came to him testing him, and asked him, “Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife?”

Arminian

The Pharisees introduce divorce as a test rather than simply asking for pastoral guidance. Arminian readers notice how a real question about human relationships can be used to trap a teacher. Jesus’ response will move beyond managing controversy to the responsibilities people owe one another before God.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish the challengers’ testing motive from the authority of Scripture to which Jesus appeals. Their framing asks what a man may lawfully do to his wife. Jesus redirects attention toward the Creator’s purpose, refusing to let a debate over permission define the whole meaning of marriage.

Mark 10:3

3He answered, “What did Moses command you?”

Arminian

Jesus answers by asking what Moses commanded, directing the discussion back to Scripture. Arminian readers see an invitation to examine the challengers’ own understanding rather than accept their premise uncritically. Responsible moral judgment begins with hearing God’s word, not searching for an authorization of a preferred course.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize Jesus’ recognition of the Mosaic text as authoritative while preparing to interpret its place within the whole scriptural witness. Merely citing a provision will not settle its purpose. The coming distinction between concession and creation guards against turning regulation of sin into endorsement of it.

Mark 10:4

4They said, “Moses allowed a certificate of divorce to be written, and to divorce her.”

Arminian

The answer appeals to Moses’ permission for a written certificate of divorce, recalling Deuteronomy 24:1–4. Arminian readers note that describing what was allowed does not yet establish God’s positive intention for marriage. A regulated practice can still reflect the damage human sin has brought into relationships.

Reformed

Reformed readers attend to the difference between a legal concession and the creation pattern Jesus is about to expound. The certificate belongs to a law addressing life among sinful people. It cannot be treated as though God commands husbands to dismiss wives whenever they wish to end a marriage.

Mark 10:5

5But Jesus said to them, “For your hardness of heart, he wrote you this commandment.

Arminian

Jesus identifies hardness of heart as the reason for the Mosaic provision. Arminian readers see the law confronting a sinful reality rather than celebrating divorce as an ideal. The explanation calls hearers to examine their willingness to obey God, not merely defend conduct because a legal procedure exists.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that civil regulation within the Mosaic order could restrain the effects of sin without expressing the fullness of God’s moral intention. Jesus exposes the heart problem behind the legal question. External permission cannot substitute for the inward faithfulness required by the Creator.

Mark 10:6

6But from the beginning of the creation, God made them male and female.

Arminian

Jesus appeals to the beginning of creation and the language of Genesis 1:27. Arminian readers see marriage interpreted through God’s purposeful making of humanity rather than convenience alone. The appeal grounds mutual responsibilities in the Creator’s intention, calling hearers beyond the minimum a disputed regulation might allow.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the authority of God’s created order beneath the later Mosaic concession. Jesus does not pit Scripture against Scripture but reads the provision in light of the foundational creation account. Marriage is therefore accountable to divine purpose rather than merely to changing human arrangements.

Mark 10:7

7For this cause a man will leave his father and mother, and will join to his wife,

Arminian

The leaving of father and mother describes a new primary bond with one’s spouse, using Genesis 2:24. Arminian readers stress the deliberate loyalty this relationship requires. Honoring parents remains important, but marriage establishes obligations that cannot be reduced to existing family interests or personal convenience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the creation text establishing an ordered, enduring union rather than a disposable private agreement. The new household carries responsibilities before God as well as affection between spouses. Jesus’ appeal challenges attempts to treat the bond as something one partner may lightly set aside.

Mark 10:8

8and the two will become one flesh, so that they are no longer two, but one flesh.

Arminian

Becoming one flesh describes a union that cannot be measured merely as two individuals’ competing preferences. Arminian readers emphasize the responsibility to honor that shared life through faithful love. The language gives moral weight to marriage without suggesting that either spouse loses personal dignity or accountability.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand the one-flesh bond as central to the covenantal seriousness of marriage. Jesus draws his conclusion from what the partners have become through the union, not simply from a rule about paperwork. Their relationship is to be treated as a real bond under God’s authority.

Mark 10:9

9What therefore God has joined together, let no man separate.”

Arminian

Jesus forbids people to separate what God has joined, stressing marriage’s intended permanence. Arminian readers hear a call to faithful responsibility rather than treating divorce as an easy entitlement. Questions about exceptions require careful attention to other passages; this command must not be used to excuse violence or deny a person’s immediate safety.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize God’s agency in the marriage bond and the limits it places on human authority to dissolve it. The warning requires serious pastoral care, not casual legalism. Interpreters differ over the application of texts such as Matthew 19:9 and 1 Corinthians 7:15, so those questions should not be hidden or oversimplified.

Mark 10:10

10In the house, his disciples asked him again about the same matter.

Arminian

The disciples return to the subject in the house, where Jesus gives further instruction. Arminian readers see room to ask difficult questions about demanding moral teaching. Private inquiry should lead toward faithful understanding and compassionate application, not toward finding ways to avoid the responsibilities he has just named.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the movement from public challenge to instruction for Jesus’ own followers. The teaching is not merely a response aimed at hostile questioners; disciples must take it to heart themselves. Christ’s authority shapes the community’s relationships as well as its answers in religious controversy.

Mark 10:11

11He said to them, “Whoever divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery against her.

Arminian

Jesus describes divorce followed by remarriage as adultery against the wife, giving her claim to faithfulness unmistakable weight. Arminian readers emphasize personal responsibility for breaking that bond. The warning cannot be reduced to satisfying a legal procedure while ignoring the person harmed by the husband’s action.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that God’s moral judgment is not exhausted by what a society permits. The phrase against her identifies the wife as a person wronged, not simply an obstacle to the husband’s wishes. The verse demands fidelity while broader questions of permitted divorce require careful attention to the other scriptural texts.

Mark 10:12

12If a woman herself divorces her husband and marries another, she commits adultery.”

Arminian

Jesus applies accountability to a woman who divorces and remarries as well as to a man. Arminian readers see no exemption from faithfulness based on which spouse initiates the breach. The shared moral standard challenges self-serving attempts to judge another person’s conduct more strictly than one’s own.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the marriage bond places both partners under God’s authority. Jesus’ parallel statements prevent the moral warning from being treated as applying only to one sex. Their force should be preserved without pretending this brief passage answers every pastoral question involving coercion, abandonment, or permitted separation.

Mark 10:13

13They were bringing to him little children, that he should touch them, but the disciples rebuked those who were bringing them.

Arminian

People bring children to receive Jesus’ touch, but the disciples attempt to stop them. Arminian readers connect this failure with the earlier lesson about welcoming a child. Followers can obstruct access to Christ when they assume that dependent or socially unimportant people are interruptions to more significant ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the disciples again misunderstanding the character of the Kingdom they serve. Their attempted gatekeeping contrasts with Jesus’ coming welcome. The scene reminds the church that its task is to bring the weak to Christ, not to decide that those without visible status are beneath his attention.

Mark 10:14

14But when Jesus saw it, he was moved with indignation and said to them, “Allow the little children to come to me! Don’t forbid them, for God’s Kingdom belongs to such as these.

Arminian

Jesus’ indignation shows that excluding the children contradicts his welcome. Arminian readers emphasize that dependence is not a barrier to receiving his care and that disciples must not place avoidable obstacles in the way. The verse gives children a positive place in Jesus’ ministry without requiring a claim of universal childhood innocence.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the gracious welcome of those who bring no status or achievements to commend themselves. Some connect this passage with covenant inclusion, while others do not infer an infant-baptism rule from it. Baptismal differences also cross Arminian communities, so the scene should not be framed as a simple dispute between the two traditions.

Mark 10:15

15Most certainly I tell you, whoever will not receive God’s Kingdom like a little child, he will in no way enter into it.”

Arminian

Receiving the Kingdom like a child makes dependence rather than achievement central to entrance. Arminian readers emphasize trusting receptivity as the response God enables and calls for. The comparison does not make faith a meritorious accomplishment or require adults to imitate every feature of children’s behavior.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the receptive posture of someone who cannot secure the Kingdom by rank or accomplishment. Grace must be received as a gift rather than claimed as a debt. The saying humbles every disciple, preparing for the rich man’s difficulty in relinquishing what he possesses.

Mark 10:16

16He took them in his arms and blessed them, laying his hands on them.

Arminian

Jesus takes the children into his arms and blesses them, turning his command of welcome into personal action. Arminian readers see love that gives attention to people the disciples had tried to dismiss. Ministry shaped by Jesus must make room for children’s actual needs rather than use them merely as illustrations.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the initiative and tenderness of Christ’s blessing toward dependent children. His embrace makes the generosity of the Kingdom visible before the story turns to a wealthy adult’s question. The passage encourages confidence in his care without supplying every detail of a sacramental or conversion doctrine.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
