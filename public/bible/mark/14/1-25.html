Passage

Mark 14

Verses 1–25 · WEB

As Passover approaches, Mark places a woman’s costly devotion beside the plot against Jesus and Judas’s betrayal. At the meal Jesus identifies the coming treachery and interprets his death through bread, the covenant cup, and the hope of God’s Kingdom. Divine purpose and human responsibility meet in this narrative, while interpretations of the Supper vary within both Arminian and Reformed traditions.

Mark 14:1

1It was now two days before the Passover and the Feast of Unleavened Bread, and the chief priests and the scribes sought how they might seize him by deception and kill him.

Arminian

The approaching Passover frames a plot to seize and kill Jesus by deception. Arminian readers stress the responsibility of the leaders who choose this course, even as God’s saving purpose advances. Mark names particular agents rather than assigning guilt to all Jewish people.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the passion unfolding within God’s redemptive purpose without making the plotters innocent. Their deceit remains their own wrongdoing. Both traditions should preserve Mark’s specific account and reject collective blame against Jewish people.

Mark 14:2

2For they said, “Not during the feast, because there might be a riot among the people.”

Arminian

Fear of a public disturbance shapes the conspirators’ timing. Arminian readers see calculation rather than concern for justice governing their decision. Human resistance is concrete and responsible, not an impersonal force that excuses those involved.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise observe the leaders’ concern for control and public reaction. God’s providence works through a history containing genuine human motives, including sinful ones. The verse exposes expediency without giving readers grounds to speculate about each individual’s final destiny.

Mark 14:3

3While he was at Bethany, in the house of Simon the leper, as he sat at the table, a woman came having an alabaster jar of ointment of pure nard—very costly. She broke the jar and poured it over his head.

Arminian

An unnamed woman honours Jesus with costly perfume at Bethany. Arminian readers see generous devotion expressed in a concrete act rather than merely inward sentiment. Mark does not identify her here, so her story need not be merged with every other Gospel anointing account.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a striking act of honour as the passion approaches. Its cost contrasts with the betrayal for money that follows. Both traditions can commend her devotion without claiming knowledge of her identity or the full extent of her understanding.

Mark 14:4

4But there were some who were indignant among themselves, saying, “Why has this ointment been wasted?

Arminian

Some observers treat the woman’s gift as waste. Arminian readers see how apparently practical objections can fail to recognise a fitting response to Jesus. Their criticism requires the interpretation he gives rather than the assumption that every expensive religious act is justified.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the gap between the observers’ judgment and Christ’s approval. Financial calculation alone does not measure faithful devotion. Both traditions should avoid using this scene to excuse extravagance whenever religious spending is questioned.

Mark 14:5

5For this might have been sold for more than three hundred denarii and given to the poor.” So they grumbled against her.

Arminian

The large sum explains the intensity of the criticism, but the observers turn their concern against the woman. Arminian readers affirm care for the poor while noting that a worthy cause can be invoked harshly. Jesus will distinguish this particular act from ordinary opportunities for generosity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognise the real value of the gift without endorsing the grumbling it provokes. Concern for stewardship must be joined to attention to Christ’s purpose. The passage does not establish that criticism of religious expenditure is always insincere.

Mark 14:6

6But Jesus said, “Leave her alone. Why do you trouble her? She has done a good work for me.

Arminian

Jesus protects the woman from harassment and calls her action good. Arminian readers see him recognising a faithful response that others have misunderstood. His defence honours her agency and devotion without making the expense a price paid for salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ’s judgment overturning the hostile verdict of the observers. The good of her act rests in its relation to him, not simply its monetary value. Both traditions should notice his defence of a person being shamed.

Mark 14:7

7For you always have the poor with you, and whenever you want to, you can do them good; but you will not always have me.

Arminian

Jesus contrasts continuing opportunities to help the poor with his approaching death. Arminian readers understand this as urgency about his unique presence, not permission to neglect poverty. The statement explicitly leaves the hearers responsible to do good whenever they have opportunity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise reject fatalistic use of this verse as though care for the poor were pointless. The contrast concerns the limited time before Jesus’ death. Honouring Christ and serving vulnerable neighbours are not competing lifelong obligations.

Mark 14:8

8She has done what she could. She has anointed my body beforehand for the burying.

Arminian

Jesus interprets the anointing in relation to his burial. Arminian readers see her available resources and action received as meaningful service, whether or not she foresaw all that was coming. Faithfulness is not measured by possessing complete understanding.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a fitting preparation for death within the unfolding passion. Christ gives her act a significance beyond what the narrative tells us she intended. Both traditions can affirm divine purpose without inventing details about her private motives.

Mark 14:9

9Most certainly I tell you, wherever this Good News may be preached throughout the whole world, that which this woman has done will also be spoken of for a memorial of her.”

Arminian

Jesus joins the memory of her act to the worldwide proclamation of the gospel. Arminian readers see overlooked devotion honoured within the story of his saving work. Her deed is remembered as service to Christ, not as an alternative ground of salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Jesus assigning lasting significance to this act of love. The coming gospel mission extends far beyond the immediate household. Both traditions recognise that the good news centres on Christ while honouring faithful people within its narrative.

Mark 14:10

10Judas Iscariot, who was one of the twelve, went away to the chief priests, that he might deliver him to them.

Arminian

Judas approaches the chief priests from within the circle of the twelve. Arminian readers stress the seriousness of betrayal by one entrusted with close fellowship. The verse records his action but does not by itself settle the whole history of his inward spiritual condition.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the tragedy of betrayal from a place of privilege and ministry. Membership in the apostolic circle does not make the act less culpable. Claims about Judas’s regeneration require more than this narrative detail alone.

Mark 14:11

11They, when they heard it, were glad, and promised to give him money. He sought how he might conveniently deliver him.

Arminian

The leaders welcome Judas’s proposal and promise money, while he looks for an opportunity. Arminian readers see deliberate cooperation in wrongdoing, not coercion that removes responsibility. Mark’s contrast with the woman’s costly gift sharpens the difference between devotion and betrayal.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise acknowledge the willing motives of the participants within the providential course of the passion. Divine purpose does not turn their transaction into obedience. Both traditions should resist explaining away greed and calculation as mere fulfilment of a script.

Mark 14:12

12On the first day of unleavened bread, when they sacrificed the Passover, his disciples asked him, “Where do you want us to go and prepare that you may eat the Passover?”

Arminian

The disciples ask where to prepare the Passover meal. Arminian readers see ordinary obedience forming the setting for Jesus’ interpretation of his approaching death. The festival context matters, but this verse should not be pressed into a complete harmonisation of every Gospel chronology.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to the Passover setting and its significance for the meal that follows. Preparations for shared worship become part of the passion narrative. Both traditions can recognise the context without claiming that Mark answers every historical question about the timing.

Mark 14:13

13He sent two of his disciples and said to them, “Go into the city, and there a man carrying a pitcher of water will meet you. Follow him,

Arminian

Jesus directs two disciples to follow a man carrying water. Arminian readers see trust expressed through a specific task rather than through speculation about how the meeting was arranged. The narrative does not explain every circumstance behind his instruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus guiding the preparations with authority and knowledge. The verse does not require choosing between prior arrangement and extraordinary foreknowledge in order to follow the story. Both traditions should avoid inventing a secret code or background that Mark does not supply.

Mark 14:14

14and wherever he enters in, tell the master of the house, ‘The Teacher says, “Where is the guest room, where I may eat the Passover with my disciples?” ’

Arminian

The disciples are to ask for the room where Jesus will eat with them. Arminian readers see the meal as intentional fellowship before the coming crisis. The request emphasises his direction, not an account of the householder’s identity or earlier dealings with him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Teacher’s authority guiding the arrangements. The room is a provision for the particular Passover meal, not evidence for a universal rule about sacred buildings. Both traditions can attend to the hospitality without filling in an undocumented biography.

Mark 14:15

15He will himself show you a large upper room furnished and ready. Get ready for us there.”

Arminian

A furnished room will be available, and the disciples must prepare it. Arminian readers see divine provision and practical responsibility working together. Trust in Jesus does not excuse them from the ordinary labour needed for the meal.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see preparation as the human task within what Christ provides. His direction gives confidence rather than making their activity unnecessary. Both traditions can recognise grace and responsible service together without manufacturing a disagreement here.

Mark 14:16

16His disciples went out, and came into the city, and found things as he had said to them, and they prepared the Passover.

Arminian

The disciples find the situation as Jesus described and prepare the Passover. Arminian readers see obedience confirming the trustworthiness of his guidance. Their successful task contrasts with the failures of loyalty that the same disciples will soon face.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise find the reliability of Christ’s word displayed in these preparations. A completed task does not mean the disciples have become self-sufficient. Both traditions can hold present faithfulness and coming weakness together in the narrative.

Mark 14:17

17When it was evening he came with the twelve.

Arminian

Jesus comes to the meal with the twelve, including the one who will betray him. Arminian readers see the painful closeness of the coming breach. Sharing a table and holding office do not remove the need for faithful allegiance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that betrayal occurs within intimate fellowship rather than at a safe distance. The narrative challenges confidence based only on religious association. Both traditions should read the scene as a summons to humility, not as grounds for suspecting every fellow believer.

Mark 14:18

18As they sat and were eating, Jesus said, “Most certainly I tell you, one of you will betray me—he who eats with me.”

Arminian

Jesus announces betrayal by someone sharing his food. Arminian readers stress that his foreknowledge does not erase the betrayer’s moral responsibility. The announcement exposes the gravity of treachery without inviting the others to congratulate themselves on their security.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus knowingly approaching his death rather than being overtaken by an unforeseen disaster. His knowledge and purpose do not lessen the wrong of betrayal. Both traditions hear the painful violation of fellowship in the reference to the shared meal.

Mark 14:19

19They began to be sorrowful, and to ask him one by one, “Surely not I?” And another said, “Surely not I?”

Arminian

Sorrow leads the disciples to question their own loyalty. Arminian readers see a fitting humility before a warning that self-confidence cannot answer. The scene invites honest examination rather than the assumption that closeness to Jesus makes failure impossible.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the need for dependence rather than boasting in personal resolve. The disciples’ questions do not establish that every one of them is secretly a betrayer. Both traditions can encourage self-examination without turning it into paralysing suspicion.

Mark 14:20

20He answered them, “It is one of the twelve, he who dips with me in the dish.

Arminian

Jesus identifies the betrayal as coming from one of the twelve who shares the dish with him. Arminian readers stress the seriousness of abused fellowship and entrusted responsibility. The verse narrows the circle but does not license invented details about each disciple’s actions at the table.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the depth of the offence in betrayal by an intimate companion. Privilege magnifies responsibility rather than guaranteeing faithfulness. Both traditions distinguish participation in the visible community from a complete account of someone’s relation to Christ.

Mark 14:21

21For the Son of Man goes as it is written about him, but woe to that man by whom the Son of Man is betrayed! It would be better for that man if he had not been born.”

Arminian

Jesus’ death fulfils Scripture, yet the betrayer remains subject to a terrible warning. Arminian readers commonly understand God as purposefully permitting a foreknown but uncoerced betrayal within his saving plan. Judas is not excused by what God brings from his act; the warning is not a declaration that evil becomes good because it serves redemption.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly understand the betrayal as included within God’s sovereign ordination, while Judas acts willingly and remains culpable. God’s purpose and human responsibility are affirmed together without making God the moral author of sin. The severe woe must not be softened into the claim that Judas simply performed a blameless assignment.

Key contrast Both affirm scriptural fulfilment and the betrayer’s full responsibility. Arminian readers commonly explain God’s plan through purposeful permission of foreknown, uncoerced choices; Reformed readers commonly include those willing choices within sovereign ordination. Neither account treats Judas as blameless or God as morally evil.

Mark 14:22

22As they were eating, Jesus took bread, and when he had blessed it, he broke it and gave to them, and said, “Take, eat. This is my body.”

Arminian

Jesus gives bread as his body, interpreting the meal through his self-giving. Arminian traditions include sacramental understandings of grace received through faith as well as memorial emphases. The wording should be honoured without pretending all Arminian readers agree on the precise mode of Christ’s presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers commonly understand believers as truly feeding on Christ spiritually through faith, while some communities place greater emphasis on remembrance. The bread is not treated as an independent source of grace apart from Christ. Sacramental differences also run within traditions, not simply between Arminian and Reformed accounts of salvation.

Mark 14:23

23He took the cup, and when he had given thanks, he gave to them. They all drank of it.

Arminian

Thanksgiving and the shared cup extend the action of the meal to the disciples. Arminian readers see communion as received gift rather than a display of worthiness. Participation in the outward act must not be confused with salvation irrespective of faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the common cup as a sign of fellowship in what Christ gives. The narrative’s statement that all drank does not establish that every participant shares inwardly in saving grace. Both traditions join grateful reception to the seriousness of allegiance to Christ.

Mark 14:24

24He said to them, “This is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many.

Arminian

The poured-out blood establishes covenant fellowship through Jesus’ approaching death. Arminian readers affirm atonement provided for all and received through faith, understanding many inclusively rather than as a stated limit. The phrase alone does not settle its extent; the central claim is Christ’s self-giving for others.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the covenant established through Christ’s sacrificial death, commonly stressing the definite redemption of his people. Reformed accounts of the atonement’s wider reference vary, and many alone is not a numerical proof excluding all. The verse centres on the saving gift rather than spelling out a complete theory of its extent.

Key contrast Both affirm Christ’s covenant-establishing death for sinners. Classical Arminian readers understand atonement as provided for all and applied through faith; Reformed readers commonly stress a definite saving intention for the elect, with internal variation over wider provision. Many by itself does not resolve the extent of atonement.

Mark 14:25

25Most certainly I tell you, I will no more drink of the fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it anew in God’s Kingdom.”

Arminian

Jesus looks beyond his death to renewed fellowship in God’s Kingdom. Arminian readers see hope grounding the meal’s present call to faithful participation. The promise does not require selecting a millennial timetable to affirm its joy.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the meal anticipating the consummation of Christ’s kingdom. His approaching suffering will not end the fellowship he establishes. Both traditions can hold remembrance and future hope together without resolving every detail of the final banquet.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
