Passage

Mark 14

Verses 66–72 · WEB

While Jesus acknowledges his identity before the high priest, Peter denies his association with him in the courtyard. The escalating denials fulfil Jesus’ warning, and the rooster’s second crow brings remembrance and tears. The earlier promise of reunion after the resurrection leaves restoration hope without minimising the seriousness of the failure.

Mark 14:66

66As Peter was in the courtyard below, one of the maids of the high priest came,

Arminian

The narrative returns to Peter in the courtyard while Jesus faces the hearing above. Arminian readers see a test of loyalty arriving in an ordinary encounter rather than a heroic public contest. Earlier self-confidence offers little protection without watchfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise observe the contrast between Jesus’ faithful testimony and Peter’s approaching denial. The setting prepares for the fulfilment of Christ’s warning. Both traditions should read the encounter as exposure of weakness rather than an occasion for contempt toward vulnerable disciples.

Mark 14:67

67and seeing Peter warming himself, she looked at him and said, “You were also with the Nazarene, Jesus!”

Arminian

The maid recognises Peter as someone who was with Jesus. Arminian readers see the pressure to acknowledge an association that has suddenly become dangerous. The question of loyalty now concerns concrete speech, not merely inward intention.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Peter confronted with a simple identification after his promises of extraordinary courage. The contrast exposes his dependence on grace. Both traditions should avoid making the maid’s ordinary social position a reason to belittle either her or Peter.

Mark 14:68

68But he denied it, saying, “I neither know nor understand what you are saying.” He went out on the porch, and the rooster crowed.

Arminian

Peter answers with denial and withdraws toward the porch. Arminian readers see a real failure of allegiance, not merely a harmless misunderstanding. The WEB retains the first rooster crow; manuscripts differ over that clause, but the main text is followed here without silently harmonising the account.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise treat the denial as grievous sin rather than evidence that preservation prevents every serious lapse. The WEB’s rooster-crow clause is retained, although its manuscript transmission is disputed. Christ’s earlier knowledge of the failure does not excuse Peter’s choice.

Mark 14:69

69The maid saw him and began again to tell those who stood by, “This is one of them.”

Arminian

The maid again identifies Peter to those nearby, and the pressure broadens beyond the first exchange. Arminian readers see how a denial can draw someone further into concealment. The scene calls for humble dependence before temptation intensifies.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Peter’s insecurity grow within circumstances he thought he could withstand. God’s mercy is needed amid repeated exposure, not only at an initial moment of commitment. Both traditions recognise that the narrative describes real responsibility rather than an involuntary reflex alone.

Mark 14:70

70But he again denied it. After a little while again those who stood by said to Peter, “You truly are one of them, for you are a Galilean, and your speech shows it.”

Arminian

Peter repeats his denial while the bystanders appeal to his Galilean identity and speech. Arminian readers see a deepening choice against truthful allegiance. Regional recognition functions within the story and does not justify contempt for any accent or community.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the widening gap between Peter’s earlier promises and his conduct. The evidence confronting him does not produce confession because fear governs his response. Both traditions should let the scene challenge self-confidence rather than stereotypes about a region.

Mark 14:71

71But he began to curse and to swear, “I don’t know this man of whom you speak!”

Arminian

Peter resorts to curses and an oath to deny knowing Jesus. Arminian readers take the act seriously while remembering the promised reunion after the resurrection. The verse does not say that failure is beyond forgiveness or that repentance would be useless.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a grievous fall that does not make Christ’s promised care empty. Preserving grace is not the claim that a believer cannot deny Christ under pressure. Both traditions should resist either minimising the sin or declaring mercy impossible.

Mark 14:72

72The rooster crowed the second time. Peter remembered the words that Jesus said to him, “Before the rooster crows twice, you will deny me three times.” When he thought about that, he wept.

Arminian

The second crow brings Jesus’ warning to Peter’s mind, and he weeps. Arminian readers see sorrow opening toward repentance and restoration through grace, with the promise of reunion still in view. Tears matter, but this scene alone does not narrate a complete restoration or make remorse a payment for forgiveness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ’s truthful warning exposing Peter while his earlier promise leaves hope beyond the collapse. The fall displays the need for preserving and restoring mercy rather than autonomous resolve. Both traditions can acknowledge real sorrow and future hope without treating denial as either trivial or unforgivable.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
