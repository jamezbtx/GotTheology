Passage

Mark 14

Verses 43–52 · WEB

Judas arrives with an armed party, turning a sign of friendship into the means of arrest. Jesus challenges the manner of his capture while locating the passion within scriptural fulfilment, and the companions flee. The unnamed young man’s desperate escape completes the scene of abandonment without resolving his identity.

Mark 14:43

43Immediately, while he was still speaking, Judas, one of the twelve, came—and with him a multitude with swords and clubs, from the chief priests, the scribes, and the elders.

Arminian

Judas arrives with an armed group sent by the religious authorities. Arminian readers see the betrayal taking concrete form through responsible human decisions. Mark’s naming of these agents does not justify blame against an entire religious or ethnic community.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that the passion unfolds through willing actors whose violence is not made righteous by providence. Judas’s place among the twelve deepens the breach. Both traditions should keep culpability tied to the actions narrated rather than to collective ancestry.

Mark 14:44

44Now he who betrayed him had given them a sign, saying, “Whomever I will kiss, that is he. Seize him, and lead him away safely.”

Arminian

The agreed kiss makes an ordinary sign of affection the means of arrest. Arminian readers stress deliberate deception and the misuse of intimacy. Religious familiarity can conceal disloyalty, so the outward sign alone cannot guarantee faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see betrayal chosen and arranged rather than merely stumbled into. The plan depends on trust being exploited. Both traditions can draw a warning about hypocrisy without assuming that every affectionate religious gesture is false.

Mark 14:45

45When he had come, immediately he came to him and said, “Rabbi! Rabbi!” and kissed him.

Arminian

Judas addresses Jesus as teacher while giving the identifying kiss. Arminian readers see words of honour contradicted by action. Acknowledging Jesus with the lips cannot substitute for faithful allegiance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish outward profession from the reality of discipleship. The familiar title intensifies rather than excuses the treachery. Both traditions should attend to the specific betrayal rather than claim access to every feature of Judas’s inner history.

Mark 14:46

46They laid their hands on him and seized him.

Arminian

The group seizes Jesus after the sign is given. Arminian readers see the real vulnerability of the one who willingly faces his passion. His voluntary obedience does not remove the injustice of those laying hands on him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ accepting arrest within his saving mission rather than losing control of his purpose. The arresting party remains responsible for its conduct. Both traditions affirm that providential significance does not turn wrongdoing into a good example.

Mark 14:47

47But a certain one of those who stood by drew his sword and struck the servant of the high priest, and cut off his ear.

Arminian

An unnamed bystander strikes the high priest’s servant with a sword. Arminian readers see how fear can answer danger with further harm. Mark does not name the attacker here, and the episode should not become an endorsement of advancing Christ’s cause by violence.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the blow from Jesus’ own response to arrest. The servant’s injury is not presented as the means of redemption. Both traditions should avoid filling the verse with details from other accounts without identifying that they come from elsewhere.

Mark 14:48

48Jesus answered them, “Have you come out, as against a robber, with swords and clubs to seize me?

Arminian

Jesus challenges the armed treatment of him as though he were a violent robber. Arminian readers see his innocence and the disproportionate force used against him. Naming injustice is compatible with his willingness to suffer.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise hear a truthful protest rather than a denial of the passion’s purpose. Christ’s acceptance of arrest does not require calling the arrest just. Both traditions can affirm patient suffering without silencing those who identify wrongful treatment.

Mark 14:49

49I was daily with you in the temple teaching, and you didn’t arrest me. But this is so that the Scriptures might be fulfilled.”

Arminian

Public teaching contrasts with this manner of arrest, yet Jesus understands the event within scriptural fulfilment. Arminian readers stress God’s faithful purpose working through permitted wrongdoing without excusing it. The verse does not name a specific text that readers must invent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the fulfilment of Scripture assuring that the passion is not outside God’s plan. The captors’ conduct remains open to Jesus’ criticism. Both traditions can affirm providence and responsibility without treating fulfilment as moral approval of the arrest.

Mark 14:50

50They all left him, and fled.

Arminian

All abandon Jesus and flee, fulfilling the warning they had resisted. Arminian readers see the seriousness of disciples’ failure and the need for grace beyond proud intention. The earlier promise of reunion keeps this moment from being read as the final word on their lives.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see genuine failure within the shepherd’s continuing purpose to gather his people again. Preservation is not a claim that believers never fall grievously. Both traditions distinguish the reality of desertion from a conclusion that restoration is impossible.

Mark 14:51

51A certain young man followed him, having a linen cloth thrown around himself over his naked body. The young men grabbed him,

Arminian

An unnamed young follower is caught by his clothing in the confusion. Arminian readers see the spreading fear and vulnerability surrounding Jesus’ arrest. His identity is uncertain; identifying him as Mark goes beyond what this verse tells us.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise treat the young man’s identity as unknown rather than a foundation for theological claims. The detail belongs to the scene of arrest and flight. Both traditions should distinguish a proposed identification from something the Gospel actually states.

Mark 14:52

52but he left the linen cloth and fled from them naked.

Arminian

The young man escapes naked, leaving the cloth behind. Arminian readers see an exposed, undignified flight that deepens the narrative’s picture of abandonment. The text does not require an allegory assigning a separate spiritual meaning to each item.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the desperate character of escape while Jesus remains in custody. Whatever wider literary resonances readers propose, the narrative does not explain the young man’s identity or later life. Both traditions should keep uncertainty explicit.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
