Passage

Mark 14

Verses 26–42 · WEB

Leaving the meal, Jesus warns that the disciples will scatter but promises resurrection and reunion. In Gethsemane his anguished prayer and willing obedience contrast with their inability to watch. The scene presents the reality of his human suffering and the need for prayerful dependence rather than confident promises of loyalty.

Mark 14:26

26When they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives.

Arminian

After singing together, Jesus and the disciples leave for the Mount of Olives. Arminian readers see worship continuing as the crisis approaches, not an escape from the costly path ahead. The verse names a hymn without specifying its exact words.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see praise accompanying the movement toward suffering. Shared worship does not mean the disciples fully understand what is coming. Both traditions should avoid presenting a reconstructed hymn selection as something Mark explicitly identifies.

Mark 14:27

27Jesus said to them, “All of you will be made to stumble because of me tonight, for it is written, ‘I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered.’

Arminian

Jesus warns that all will stumble and interprets the scattering through Scripture’s shepherd imagery. Arminian readers see foreknown failure without a removal of the disciples’ responsibility. Their collapse does not exhaust the story, since the promise of reunion immediately follows.

Reformed

Reformed readers see even the disciples’ scattering within the scriptural pattern of the passion. This does not turn their desertion into an act of faithfulness. Both traditions should read the warning alongside Christ’s intention to gather them again.

Mark 14:28

28However, after I am raised up, I will go before you into Galilee.”

Arminian

Before their failure occurs, Jesus promises resurrection and a meeting in Galilee. Arminian readers see mercy opening a future beyond shame and calling the disciples back to trust. The promise grounds restoration in Christ rather than in their ability to repair themselves.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the shepherd’s preserving care preceding the sheep’s collapse. His purpose is not defeated by their weakness. Both traditions find a basis for restoration hope here without declaring that denial is harmless.

Mark 14:29

29But Peter said to him, “Although all will be offended, yet I will not.”

Arminian

Peter separates his own loyalty from the failure Jesus predicts for everyone. Arminian readers see the danger of relying on determination instead of receiving warning and grace. His confidence is sincere in expression but not an adequate defence against temptation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see self-assurance exposed as insufficient. Christ knows Peter more truly than Peter knows himself. Both traditions can distinguish earnest intention from the sustaining grace needed to act faithfully under pressure.

Mark 14:30

30Jesus said to him, “Most certainly I tell you that you today, even this night, before the rooster crows twice, you will deny me three times.”

Arminian

Jesus predicts Peter’s three denials before the rooster’s second crow. Arminian readers affirm his true foreknowledge without making Peter an unwilling instrument. The specificity prepares for the later recognition of failure, not a licence to abandon resistance to temptation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Christ’s knowledge extending to the details of the coming denial. His warning exposes Peter’s weakness while anticipating the mercy promised in verse 28. Both traditions affirm that foretelling the act does not make it morally innocent.

Mark 14:31

31But he spoke all the more, “If I must die with you, I will not deny you.” They all said the same thing.

Arminian

Peter intensifies his promise, and the others join him. Arminian readers see how strongly expressed commitment can coexist with unrecognised vulnerability. The scene calls believers to humble dependence rather than to assume good intentions make prayer unnecessary.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognise the gap between the disciples’ resolve and their coming actions. Shared confidence does not become reliable merely because everyone repeats it. Both traditions should hear a warning for the whole community, not only a reason to ridicule Peter.

Mark 14:32

32They came to a place which was named Gethsemane. He said to his disciples, “Sit here while I pray.”

Arminian

At Gethsemane Jesus withdraws to pray while asking the disciples to remain nearby. Arminian readers see prayer as genuine dependence in the face of suffering. His example calls for trust without suggesting that anguish reflects deficient faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the incarnate Son’s prayer as real, not a performance concealing indifference to death. Divine purpose and personal supplication belong together. Both traditions can learn dependence from the scene without reducing it to a general lesson detached from Christ’s unique passion.

Mark 14:33

33He took with him Peter, James, and John, and began to be greatly troubled and distressed.

Arminian

Jesus brings three disciples close and experiences profound distress. Arminian readers affirm his full humanity and the reality of the suffering he faces. Emotional anguish must not be treated as proof of disobedience or absence of trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise confess a truly human Saviour whose sorrow is neither pretence nor moral failure. His distress reveals the cost of the path before him. Both traditions should respond tenderly to those who suffer rather than demand emotional invulnerability.

Mark 14:34

34He said to them, “My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death. Stay here and watch.”

Arminian

Jesus names his sorrow and asks his companions to watch. Arminian readers see the compatibility of faithful obedience with seeking another person’s presence. His words resist the idea that strong faith never expresses pain.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognise the depth of Christ’s human sorrow and the real request he makes of the disciples. Reliance on God does not rule out companionship. Both traditions should avoid treating the desire for support as a failure of spiritual maturity.

Mark 14:35

35He went forward a little, and fell on the ground, and prayed that if it were possible, the hour might pass away from him.

Arminian

Jesus prays that the hour might pass if possible, bringing his dread honestly before the Father. Arminian readers see a genuine petition within obedient trust. The request does not mean the saving mission has become an unwanted mistake.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the petition as real within the Son’s willing fulfilment of the Father’s purpose. His human recoil from suffering is not sinful resistance. Both traditions hold together the costliness and the voluntariness of his obedience.

Mark 14:36

36He said, “Abba, Father, all things are possible to you. Please remove this cup from me. However, not what I desire, but what you desire.”

Arminian

Jesus addresses the Father intimately, acknowledges his power, and submits his desire concerning the cup to the Father’s will. Arminian readers see willing obedience rather than coerced submission, with anguish honestly expressed. The cup evokes the suffering of his saving mission; the prayer does not imply rival divine purposes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the incarnate Son’s true human will freely consenting to the Father’s redemptive purpose. His request and submission are equally real, not a conflict between hostile deities. Both traditions affirm his sinless humanity, full deity, and costly obedience rather than reading the scene as a denial of either nature.

Mark 14:37

37He came and found them sleeping, and said to Peter, “Simon, are you sleeping? Couldn’t you watch one hour?

Arminian

Jesus finds the disciples asleep and addresses Peter’s inability to watch. Arminian readers see the immediate failure of confidence unsupported by prayer. The rebuke calls for renewed attentiveness, not contempt for ordinary human need for rest.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Peter’s earlier boasting tested by a small but consequential task. Dependence is needed before the public crisis arrives. Both traditions should hear correction joined to the opportunity to pray, not a claim that physical tiredness is itself apostasy.

Mark 14:38

38Watch and pray, that you may not enter into temptation. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak.”

Arminian

Willing intention does not overcome human weakness without watchful prayer. Arminian readers stress grace-enabled vigilance against a temptation that can truly overcome disciples. The flesh here describes their frailty rather than making the body intrinsically evil.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see prayer and vigilance as means God uses to sustain faithful obedience. Confidence in preservation is no permission to neglect them. The verse distinguishes desire from strength without excusing the disciples or condemning bodily existence.

Mark 14:39

39Again he went away and prayed, saying the same words.

Arminian

Jesus repeats his prayer rather than disguising the depth of his struggle. Arminian readers see persistent dependence that remains faithful even when the request is not granted in the desired form. Repetition can express honest trust rather than empty ritual.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see persevering prayer within obedience to the Father’s purpose. Repeating the petition does not imply that God must be persuaded by technique. Both traditions can distinguish heartfelt persistence from treating prayer as a means of control.

Mark 14:40

40Again he returned and found them sleeping, for their eyes were very heavy; and they didn’t know what to answer him.

Arminian

The disciples again sleep and have no adequate answer. Arminian readers see human weakness becoming culpable neglect despite the warning they received. Their failure invites humility and a renewed appeal for grace, not the belief that failure is inevitable in every temptation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the limits of unaided resolve. God’s preserving care does not make this lapse unreal or commendable. Both traditions should let the narrative expose weakness without imagining that Jesus is surprised by it.

Mark 14:41

41He came the third time and said to them, “Sleep on now, and take your rest. It is enough. The hour has come. Behold, the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners.

Arminian

The repeated return ends with the announcement that betrayal has arrived. Arminian readers see the contrast between Jesus’ readiness and the disciples’ lost opportunity to watch. His surrender to the coming hour does not make the sinners who seize him innocent.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the appointed passion moving forward despite the companions’ failure. Christ faces the hour deliberately rather than being mastered by confusion. Both traditions distinguish his acceptance of suffering from approval of the evil done to him.

Mark 14:42

42Arise! Let’s get going. Behold, he who betrays me is at hand.”

Arminian

Jesus rises to meet the arrival of the betrayer. Arminian readers see active, willing obedience rather than a passive inability to escape. His courage follows honest prayer, not the denial that the coming ordeal is terrifying.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Saviour knowingly entering the suffering through which redemption will come. Dependence on the Father has not removed decisive action. Both traditions recognise that the betrayal remains the betrayer’s deed even as Jesus faces it freely.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
