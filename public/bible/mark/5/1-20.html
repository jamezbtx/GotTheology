Passage

Mark 5

Verses 1–20 · WEB

After calming the storm, Jesus crosses to Gentile territory east of the Sea of Galilee and meets a man tormented by many unclean spirits. Mark stresses the man’s hopeless condition and Jesus’ complete authority: the demons recognize Him, beg for permission, and leave at His word. The townspeople, afraid, ask Jesus to leave, while the healed man is sent home to tell what the Lord has done. Arminian and Reformed readers share nearly all of this; both see Jesus’ sovereign power over evil and His mercy to an outcast.

Mark 5:1

1They came to the other side of the sea, into the country of the Gadarenes.

Arminian

Jesus crosses to the other side of the sea into Gentile country. Arminian readers note that Jesus deliberately takes His mission beyond Israel’s borders, a sign that God’s mercy is meant for all peoples.

Reformed

Reformed readers also see Jesus entering Gentile territory, anticipating the gospel going to the nations. The WEB reads Gadarenes; other manuscripts read Gerasenes or Gergesenes, and the exact location is uncertain.

Mark 5:2

2When he had come out of the boat, immediately a man with an unclean spirit met him out of the tombs.

Arminian

A man with an unclean spirit meets Jesus as soon as He leaves the boat. Arminian readers see the immediacy of the encounter: Jesus is met at once by human misery and spiritual bondage.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the man comes from the tombs, a place of death and ritual uncleanness. Mark presents a person wholly under hostile power, awaiting a stronger deliverer.

Mark 5:3

3He lived in the tombs. Nobody could bind him any more, not even with chains,

Arminian

He lived among the tombs, and no one could bind him. Arminian readers see a picture of human strength failing against spiritual evil. Society had no remedy for him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human helplessness before the power of evil. Only one stronger than the strong man (compare 3:27) can bring release.

Mark 5:4

4because he had been often bound with fetters and chains, and the chains had been torn apart by him, and the fetters broken in pieces. Nobody had the strength to tame him.

Arminian

He broke chains and fetters; nobody could tame him. Arminian readers note that force and restraint could not heal him. What he needed was deliverance, not confinement.

Reformed

Reformed readers see bondage that no human effort could break. The scene prepares for Jesus’ effortless authority in the verses that follow.

Mark 5:5

5Always, night and day, in the tombs and in the mountains, he was crying out, and cutting himself with stones.

Arminian

Night and day he cried out and cut himself with stones. Arminian readers see the destructive aim of evil, which turns a person against himself. Jesus comes to restore what evil ruins.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same misery: sin and evil powers bring isolation, torment, and self-harm. The man’s cries set up the mercy to come.

Mark 5:6

6When he saw Jesus from afar, he ran and bowed down to him,

Arminian

Seeing Jesus from afar, he ran and bowed down. Arminian readers note that even in bondage the man is drawn toward Jesus. Some see a glimmer of the man’s own longing for rescue.

Reformed

Reformed readers often read the bowing as the spirits’ forced submission to Jesus’ authority. Either way, the scene shows that evil cannot stand before Him.

Mark 5:7

7and crying out with a loud voice, he said, “What have I to do with you, Jesus, you Son of the Most High God? I adjure you by God, don’t torment me.”

Arminian

He cries out, naming Jesus as Son of the Most High God and begging not to be tormented. Arminian readers note that the demons know who Jesus is, yet knowledge is not faith (compare James 2:19).

Reformed

Reformed readers see the spirits recognize Jesus’ identity and their coming judgment. The title Most High God fits a Gentile setting and confesses Jesus’ divine sonship.

Mark 5:8

8For he said to him, “Come out of the man, you unclean spirit!”

Arminian

Jesus had commanded the unclean spirit to come out. Arminian readers see Jesus’ word as already at work, explaining the spirit’s desperate plea.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ authoritative command as the decisive act. The demons resist but cannot overrule Him.

Mark 5:9

9He asked him, “What is your name?” He said to him, “My name is Legion, for we are many.”

Arminian

“My name is Legion, for we are many.” Arminian readers see the depth of the man’s bondage. Legion evokes a Roman military unit, suggesting overwhelming force.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same image of many oppressors. Jesus asks the name not because He lacks power but to expose how great the deliverance is.

Mark 5:10

10He begged him much that he would not send them away out of the country.

Arminian

They beg not to be sent out of the country. Arminian readers note that the spirits must ask; they cannot act without Jesus’ permission.

Reformed

Reformed readers see evil powers operating only within limits set by God. Even their requests acknowledge Jesus’ rule.

Mark 5:11

11Now on the mountainside there was a great herd of pigs feeding.

Arminian

A great herd of pigs feeds nearby. Arminian readers note the Gentile setting, since Jews did not keep pigs. The detail prepares for what follows.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the unclean animals matching the unclean spirits. The setting underlines that this is Gentile land.

Mark 5:12

12All the demons begged him, saying, “Send us into the pigs, that we may enter into them.”

Arminian

The demons beg to be sent into the pigs. Arminian readers see the spirits again dependent on Jesus’ permission.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the demons can only request. Jesus remains in full control of the outcome.

Mark 5:13

13At once Jesus gave them permission. The unclean spirits came out and entered into the pigs. The herd of about two thousand rushed down the steep bank into the sea, and they were drowned in the sea.

Arminian

Jesus permits it, and about two thousand pigs rush into the sea and drown. Arminian readers see a visible proof that the man has been freed. Interpreters differ on why Jesus allowed it, but the point is His authority and the man’s worth.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same proof of deliverance and the destructive nature of evil. The sea, often a symbol of chaos, swallows the spirits’ hosts. Questions about the loss of the herd are not answered by the text.

Mark 5:14

14Those who fed the pigs fled, and told it in the city and in the country. The people came to see what it was that had happened.

Arminian

The herders flee and spread the news, and people come to see. Arminian readers note that the event becomes a public testimony.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s works drawing attention, though not everyone who comes will respond in faith.

Mark 5:15

15They came to Jesus, and saw him who had been possessed by demons sitting, clothed, and in his right mind, even him who had the legion; and they were afraid.

Arminian

They find the man sitting, clothed, and in his right mind, and they are afraid. Arminian readers see a full restoration of the person, mind and body.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the transformation grace brings. The fear of the crowd shows that awe at power is not the same as welcome.

Mark 5:16

16Those who saw it declared to them what happened to him who was possessed by demons, and about the pigs.

Arminian

Witnesses tell what happened to the man and the pigs. Arminian readers note that both the healing and the cost are reported.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the townspeople weighing the man’s deliverance against their economic loss.

Mark 5:17

17They began to beg him to depart from their region.

Arminian

They beg Jesus to leave their region. Arminian readers see people refusing the presence of the one who brought freedom. Jesus does not force Himself on them.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human fear and misplaced priorities. Jesus departs, yet He leaves a witness behind.

Key contrast Both see the people’s request as a tragic response to Jesus’ power. Arminian readers often stress that Jesus honors their refusal, showing that grace can be resisted. Reformed readers often stress that seeing a miracle does not change a heart unless God works within, and that Jesus still leaves a witness among them.

Mark 5:18

18As he was entering into the boat, he who had been possessed by demons begged him that he might be with him.

Arminian

The healed man begs to be with Jesus. Arminian readers see the natural response of the delivered: wanting to stay close to the deliverer.

Reformed

Reformed readers see love for Christ as the fruit of mercy received.

Mark 5:19

19He didn’t allow him, but said to him, “Go to your house, to your friends, and tell them what great things the Lord has done for you and how he had mercy on you.”

Arminian

Jesus sends him home to tell what the Lord has done and how He had mercy. Arminian readers see a mission entrusted to a new believer: tell your own story of mercy.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the Lord who acted is identified with Jesus in verse 20. The man’s witness begins at home, among his own people.

Mark 5:20

20He went his way, and began to proclaim in Decapolis how Jesus had done great things for him, and everyone marveled.

Arminian

He proclaims in the Decapolis what Jesus did, and all marvel. Arminian readers see a Gentile witness spreading the news across the region.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the first proclamation in Gentile territory, a foretaste of the gospel to the nations.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
