Passage

Mark 5

Verses 21–43 · WEB

Back on the Jewish side, a synagogue ruler named Jairus begs Jesus to heal his dying daughter. On the way, a woman with a twelve-year illness touches His clothes in faith and is healed; Jesus calls her out of hiding and calls her daughter. Then word comes that Jairus’s daughter has died, but Jesus says, “Don’t be afraid, only believe,” and raises her. Both traditions see Jesus’ power over disease and death and His tender care; they differ mainly in how they describe faith’s role.

Mark 5:21

21When Jesus had crossed back over in the boat to the other side, a great multitude was gathered to him; and he was by the sea.

Arminian

Jesus crosses back, and a great crowd gathers. Arminian readers note the contrast with the region that asked Him to leave. They welcome Him where the Gerasene region had asked Him to leave.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the crowd as the setting for two acts of mercy woven together. Mark often weaves two stories together, and here each one sheds light on the other.

Mark 5:22

22Behold, one of the rulers of the synagogue, Jairus by name, came; and seeing him, he fell at his feet

Arminian

Jairus, a synagogue ruler, falls at Jesus’ feet. Arminian readers see a respected man setting aside status in desperate need. His public humility is costly, since some synagogue leaders were wary of Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers see humility forced by crisis. Faith often comes as a plea from the end of one’s resources. Jairus comes as a father before he comes as an official.

Mark 5:23

23and begged him much, saying, “My little daughter is at the point of death. Please come and lay your hands on her, that she may be made healthy, and live.”

Arminian

He begs Jesus to come and lay hands on his dying daughter. Arminian readers see real faith expressed in a request for Jesus’ touch.

Reformed

Reformed readers see trust that Jesus can heal, even if Jairus does not yet imagine He can raise the dead.

Mark 5:24

24He went with him, and a great multitude followed him, and they pressed upon him on all sides.

Arminian

Jesus goes with him, and the crowd presses around. Arminian readers see Jesus responding to a father’s plea. Jesus’ willingness to go shows His readiness to answer prayer.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the press of the crowd, which sets up the contrast between jostling and the woman’s touch of faith. In this press of people only one touch is singled out, the touch of faith.

Mark 5:25

25A certain woman who had a discharge of blood for twelve years,

Arminian

A woman has suffered bleeding for twelve years. Arminian readers note that her condition brought long suffering and social isolation under purity laws.

Reformed

Reformed readers see years of uncleanness and exclusion. The twelve years link her with the twelve-year-old girl (v.42).

Mark 5:26

26and had suffered many things by many physicians, and had spent all that she had, and was no better, but rather grew worse,

Arminian

She had suffered under many physicians, spent all she had, and only grew worse. Arminian readers see human remedies exhausted, leaving her with nowhere to turn but Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers see helplessness that prepares for grace. She comes with nothing to offer.

Mark 5:27

27having heard the things concerning Jesus, came up behind him in the crowd and touched his clothes.

Arminian

Hearing about Jesus, she comes up behind and touches His clothes. Arminian readers see faith arising from hearing and acting on it. Her approach is real and personal.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faith coming by hearing. Even timid faith reaches toward Jesus.

Mark 5:28

28For she said, “If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well.”

Arminian

She says, “If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well.” Arminian readers see genuine trust, even if mixed with uncertainty.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faith that is real though imperfect. Jesus honors faith not because it is strong but because of its object.

Mark 5:29

29Immediately the flow of her blood was dried up, and she felt in her body that she was healed of her affliction.

Arminian

Immediately her bleeding stops, and she feels healed. Arminian readers see the immediate response of Jesus’ power to faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s power acting at once, through faith, to heal.

Mark 5:30

30Immediately Jesus, perceiving in himself that the power had gone out from him, turned around in the crowd and asked, “Who touched my clothes?”

Arminian

Jesus knows power has gone out and asks who touched Him. Arminian readers see a personal Lord who wants to meet the one He has healed, not just dispense power.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ knowledge and His desire to bring the woman into relationship and public restoration.

Mark 5:31

31His disciples said to him, “You see the multitude pressing against you, and you say, ‘Who touched me?’ ”

Arminian

The disciples point out the crowd. Arminian readers note the difference between crowding and touching in faith. Their question is reasonable on a human level, yet it misses what Jesus has perceived.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the disciples’ incomprehension, a running theme in Mark. They see a crowd; Jesus knows a person has been healed.

Mark 5:32

32He looked around to see her who had done this thing.

Arminian

He looks around to see who did it. Arminian readers see Jesus seeking her out personally. He does not let her slip away as an anonymous beneficiary.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus drawing her into the open to confirm her healing. His searching look is mercy, not rebuke.

Mark 5:33

33But the woman, fearing and trembling, knowing what had been done to her, came and fell down before him, and told him all the truth.

Arminian

Trembling, she falls before Him and tells all the truth. Arminian readers see honest confession and trust overcoming fear. She had expected possible rebuke for touching Him while unclean.

Reformed

Reformed readers see fear turning to confession before the one who healed her. Her full truth-telling is the kind of honest approach the gospel invites.

Mark 5:34

34He said to her, “Daughter, your faith has made you well. Go in peace, and be cured of your disease.”

Arminian

“Daughter, your faith has made you well.” Arminian readers see faith as a real response that God honors. Jesus credits her trust while the power is His.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faith as the instrument through which Jesus’ power came. Faith itself is a gift, and Jesus’ words restore her to community.

Key contrast Both affirm that the power is Christ’s and that faith receives it. Arminian readers usually stress faith as a grace-enabled human response that Jesus honors. Reformed readers usually stress faith as God’s gift that receives what Christ freely gives.

Mark 5:35

35While he was still speaking, people came from the synagogue ruler’s house, saying, “Your daughter is dead. Why bother the Teacher any more?”

Arminian

Word comes that the girl is dead. Arminian readers see the delay testing Jairus’s faith. The messengers assume death ends what Jesus can do.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human hope exhausted, preparing for a greater work. Why bother the Teacher shows how small their view of Jesus still is.

Mark 5:36

36But Jesus, when he heard the message spoken, immediately said to the ruler of the synagogue, “Don’t be afraid, only believe.”

Arminian

“Don’t be afraid, only believe.” Arminian readers see Jesus calling Jairus to keep trusting. Faith must continue when circumstances worsen.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus sustaining faith by His word. The command is also a gracious encouragement.

Mark 5:37

37He allowed no one to follow him except Peter, James, and John the brother of James.

Arminian

Jesus takes only Peter, James, and John. Arminian readers note the inner circle witnessing a key moment. Jesus limits the audience, keeping the moment from becoming a spectacle.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same three later at the transfiguration and Gethsemane. Their presence makes them witnesses to Jesus’ authority over death.

Mark 5:38

38He came to the synagogue ruler’s house, and he saw an uproar, weeping, and great wailing.

Arminian

He sees an uproar of weeping and wailing. Arminian readers see grief in its rawest form. Jesus enters fully into the place of human sorrow.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the mourners may include customary professional mourners. Death seems final. Mark lets the noise of grief contrast with Jesus’ calm command.

Mark 5:39

39When he had entered in, he said to them, “Why do you make an uproar and weep? The child is not dead, but is asleep.”

Arminian

“The child is not dead, but asleep.” Arminian readers see Jesus redefining death in light of His power. For Him, death is not the final word.

Reformed

Reformed readers see death described as sleep because Jesus will wake her. The phrase anticipates resurrection hope.

Mark 5:40

40They ridiculed him. But he, having put them all out, took the father of the child, her mother, and those who were with him, and went in where the child was lying.

Arminian

They laugh at Him, but He takes the parents and disciples in. Arminian readers see unbelief excluded from the moment of grace. Mockery does not stop Jesus, but He shields the moment from scorn.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus choosing witnesses for the miracle. Ridicule of His word echoes later mockery at the cross.

Mark 5:41

41Taking the child by the hand, he said to her, “Talitha cumi!” which means, being interpreted, “Girl, I tell you, get up!”

Arminian

He takes her hand and says, “Talitha cumi.” Arminian readers see tenderness and authority together, as Jesus touches the dead without fear of defilement.

Reformed

Reformed readers note Mark preserves Jesus’ Aramaic words. His command brings life from death.

Mark 5:42

42Immediately the girl rose up and walked, for she was twelve years old. They were amazed with great amazement.

Arminian

She rises and walks; she is twelve. Arminian readers see Jesus’ victory over death. Mark’s note that the onlookers were overwhelmed with amazement shows the impact of what happened.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a foretaste of resurrection power. Her age ties the story back to the woman’s twelve years of suffering.

Mark 5:43

43He strictly ordered them that no one should know this, and commanded that something should be given to her to eat.

Arminian

He orders silence and that she be given food. Arminian readers see Jesus’ care for the girl’s ordinary needs. Food confirms that she is truly alive and restored to ordinary life.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the messianic secret theme. Jesus’ identity is revealed fully through the cross. Jesus does not want to be known merely as a wonder-worker.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
