Passage

Mark 8

Verses 22–30 · WEB

Jesus restores a blind man’s sight in two stages before Peter identifies him as the Christ. The sequence provides a fitting frame for the disciples’ partial perception: Peter names Jesus truly but will soon resist the suffering that defines his mission.

Mark 8:22

22He came to Bethsaida. They brought a blind man to him and begged him to touch him.

Arminian

At Bethsaida, people bring a blind man and ask Jesus to touch him. Arminian readers see neighbors helping someone in need, while the placement after the disciples’ blindness invites reflection on spiritual perception.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm the man’s concrete bodily need before drawing any narrative analogy. His restoration will depend on Christ’s gracious action, not on the crowd’s power.

Mark 8:23

23He took hold of the blind man by the hand, and brought him out of the village. When he had spat on his eyes, and laid his hands on him, he asked him if he saw anything.

Arminian

Jesus leads the man by the hand out of the village and begins the healing personally. Arminian readers see attentive care that meets him in vulnerability rather than exposes him to public scrutiny.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress Christ’s initiative and freedom in the manner of healing. His question invites the man to describe what he sees without suggesting that Jesus lacks knowledge of his need.

Mark 8:24

24He looked up, and said, “I see men, but I see them like walking trees.”

Arminian

The man can see people but only indistinctly. Arminian readers recognize a partial restoration that can illuminate the disciples’ emerging yet confused understanding, without reducing the man’s experience to a mere teaching device.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a narrative parallel to imperfect perception. The verse does not explain why Jesus heals in stages, so it should not be made evidence of deficient power or a fixed pattern for every conversion.

Mark 8:25

25Then again he laid his hands on his eyes. He looked intently, and was restored, and saw everyone clearly.

Arminian

Jesus touches the man again, and his sight becomes clear. Arminian readers see patient grace bringing restoration to completion and hope for disciples who still need clearer understanding.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the completed healing displays Christ’s effective care. The two stages can frame the disciples’ partial sight without implying that the first touch exhausted his ability.

Mark 8:26

26He sent him away to his house, saying, “Don’t enter into the village, nor tell anyone in the village.”

Arminian

Jesus directs the man home rather than back into the village. Arminian readers note that the official WEB includes both the prohibition on entering and on telling anyone there, with no footnote marking a variant.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise preserve the full WEB instruction and emphasize obedience to Christ’s direction. This command concerns this particular healed man and setting, not a universal prohibition on telling others about Jesus.

Mark 8:27

27Jesus went out, with his disciples, into the villages of Caesarea Philippi. On the way he asked his disciples, “Who do men say that I am?”

Arminian

On the way to Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asks about public opinion of him. Arminian readers see a deliberate movement from reports about his works toward the question of his identity.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize that disciples must learn who Christ is, not simply admire what he does. The question prepares a personal confession and then a correction of its meaning.

Mark 8:28

28They told him, “John the Baptizer, and others say Elijah, but others, one of the prophets.”

Arminian

The reported answers place Jesus among John, Elijah, or the prophets. Arminian readers see that respectful estimates can still fall short of recognizing his unique identity and mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish admiration from an adequate confession of Christ. These answers reveal genuine interest but do not yet name him as the Messiah.

Mark 8:29

29He said to them, “But who do you say that I am?” Peter answered, “You are the Christ.”

Arminian

Peter confesses Jesus as the Christ in response to a question directed to the disciples. Arminian readers see a real confession of faith that will immediately need correction about the kind of Messiah Jesus is.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm the truth of Peter’s confession without treating it as complete understanding. Mark’s account here does not explain the mechanics of coming to faith, and both traditions should avoid adding them as though stated.

Mark 8:30

30He commanded them that they should tell no one about him.

Arminian

Jesus commands silence about his identity. Arminian readers read this beside the coming passion prediction: a true title can still communicate a badly mistaken expectation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the need for Jesus himself to define his messianic mission. The command belongs to this moment in the story rather than negating the church’s later task of witness.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
