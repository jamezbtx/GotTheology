Passage

Mark 8

Verses 31–38 · WEB

Jesus first plainly announces his suffering, death, and resurrection, then rebukes Peter for resisting this path. He calls crowd and disciples alike to follow him through self-denial and costly allegiance, placing present choices in the light of the Son of Man’s future glory.

Mark 8:31

31He began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things, and be rejected by the elders, the chief priests, and the scribes, and be killed, and after three days rise again.

Arminian

Jesus begins to explain that his path includes rejection, death, and resurrection. Arminian readers affirm God’s saving purpose and Christ’s willing obedience without excusing the people who reject and kill him.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize the necessity of the Messiah’s suffering within God’s purpose, together with the real guilt of his opponents. Both traditions see resurrection as integral to the announcement, not an afterthought to defeat.

Mark 8:32

32He spoke to them openly. Peter took him and began to rebuke him.

Arminian

Peter responds to Jesus’ plain teaching by rebuking him. Arminian readers see that sincere confession can coexist with resistance to the costly shape of God’s saving work.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish a true title for Christ from a mind submitted to his mission. Peter’s confidence needs correction, not confirmation of his preferred version of messiahship.

Mark 8:33

33But he, turning around and seeing his disciples, rebuked Peter, and said, “Get behind me, Satan! For you have in mind not the things of God, but the things of men.”

Arminian

Jesus sharply rejects Peter’s attempt to redirect him from suffering. Arminian readers see a warning that even a disciple can advance a temptation by placing human expectations above God’s purpose.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand the address to Satan as exposing the adversarial character of Peter’s counsel, not a simple declaration that Peter is beyond redemption. Christ will not abandon the way the Father has appointed.

Mark 8:34

34He called the multitude to himself with his disciples and said to them, “Whoever wants to come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me.

Arminian

Jesus calls both crowd and disciples to self-denial and costly allegiance. Arminian readers stress a genuine invitation and grace-enabled willingness to follow; they do not treat whoever wants to as proof of unaided human ability.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the open summons and the necessity of willing discipleship, while grounding that willingness in renewing grace. Taking up the cross is the fruit of belonging to Christ, not a payment that purchases acceptance.

Key contrast Both affirm the open call, necessary willing obedience, and dependence on grace. Arminian readers stress grace-enabled response that can be resisted; Reformed readers ground the willing response in effective renewal. The invitation itself does not spell out that distinction.

Mark 8:35

35For whoever wants to save his life will lose it; and whoever will lose his life for my sake and the sake of the Good News will save it.

Arminian

Self-preservation at the expense of Christ ends in loss, while losing life for him and the Good News leads to salvation. Arminian readers stress persevering allegiance enabled by grace, not a meritorious bargain or suffering sought for its own sake.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm the necessity of persevering faith and costly loyalty. They understand such endurance as the fruit of God’s preserving grace, not a second basis of justification beside Christ’s work.

Key contrast Both reject earning salvation through suffering and affirm the necessity of enduring allegiance to Christ. Arminian readers understand continued faith as a grace-enabled condition of final salvation; Reformed readers understand perseverance as the necessary fruit of God’s preserving grace.

Mark 8:36

36For what does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his life?

Arminian

Jesus exposes the ruin of gaining everything while forfeiting life. Arminian readers see a searching challenge to choices that trade lasting fellowship with God for immediate advantage.

Reformed

Reformed readers share the warning against making worldly success the measure of blessing. No accumulation of power, security, or possessions can compensate for losing what only God can give.

Mark 8:37

37For what will a man give in exchange for his life?

Arminian

The question rules out any adequate exchange by which a person could buy back a forfeited life. Arminian readers see the folly of treating wealth or achievement as ultimate security.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize the inability of human possessions to secure salvation. Both traditions hear the urgency of following Christ rather than supposing that present gains can later repair the loss.

Mark 8:38

38For whoever will be ashamed of me and of my words in this adulterous and sinful generation, the Son of Man also will be ashamed of him when he comes in his Father’s glory with the holy angels.”

Arminian

Jesus connects shame about him now with the Son of Man’s future response in glory. Arminian readers take this as a real warning against repudiating Christ and a call to enduring allegiance, not a denial of mercy for a repentant failure.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the warning seriously as a means of summoning persevering confession. It concerns the grave reality of disowning Christ, while the promised return shows that the rejected Son of Man will judge in the Father’s glory.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
