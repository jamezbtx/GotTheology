Passage

Mark 8

Verses 1–21 · WEB

Jesus feeds four thousand, refuses a hostile demand for a sign, and warns his disciples about the yeast of the Pharisees and Herod. Their concern about bread exposes hardened understanding despite two abundant feedings, preparing for the next account of sight gradually restored.

Mark 8:1

1In those days, when there was a very great multitude, and they had nothing to eat, Jesus called his disciples to himself and said to them,

Arminian

A great crowd again lacks food, and Jesus initiates the conversation with his disciples. Arminian readers see compassion taking the first step before the hungry people make any recorded request.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise emphasize Jesus’ initiative in addressing ordinary bodily need. The renewed scarcity will expose whether the disciples have learned from his earlier provision.

Mark 8:2

2“I have compassion on the multitude, because they have stayed with me now three days and have nothing to eat.

Arminian

Jesus names his compassion and the crowd’s three days without food. Arminian readers see attentive mercy toward people who have stayed with him, without reducing their hunger to a merely spiritual symbol.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that bodily needs matter to Christ. His care supplies the motive for what follows, rather than any recorded worthiness or social standing of the crowd.

Mark 8:3

3If I send them away fasting to their home, they will faint on the way, for some of them have come a long way.”

Arminian

Jesus considers the danger of people fainting on the journey home. Arminian readers see love that attends to individual circumstances, including the distance some have traveled.

Reformed

Reformed readers share this reading of Christ’s practical concern. Faithful care does not ignore human frailty or treat hunger as irrelevant beside religious instruction.

Mark 8:4

4His disciples answered him, “From where could one satisfy these people with bread here in a deserted place?”

Arminian

The disciples see the deserted setting as an obstacle to feeding the crowd. Arminian readers recognize the recurring failure to let remembered mercy shape present trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that witnessing a miracle does not automatically produce clear spiritual understanding. The disciples’ question prepares a fresh demonstration of Jesus’ sufficiency.

Mark 8:5

5He asked them, “How many loaves do you have?” They said, “Seven.”

Arminian

Jesus asks what bread is available, and the disciples report seven loaves. Arminian readers see an invitation to place limited resources at his disposal, not a demand that they create the supply themselves.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the disciples’ participation from Christ’s power. Naming the small provision brings the inadequacy of their resources into view before he feeds the multitude.

Mark 8:6

6He commanded the multitude to sit down on the ground, and he took the seven loaves. Having given thanks, he broke them and gave them to his disciples to serve, and they served the multitude.

Arminian

Jesus gives thanks, breaks the bread, and uses his disciples to distribute it. Arminian readers see grateful dependence joined to active service; receiving from Christ equips them to care for others.

Reformed

Reformed readers emphasize that the provision originates with Christ while the disciples genuinely serve as his instruments. Both traditions see generosity expressed through concrete, orderly care.

Mark 8:7

7They also had a few small fish. Having blessed them, he said to serve these also.

Arminian

The small fish are also blessed and distributed. Arminian readers notice that Jesus’ care includes the whole meal rather than an abstract display detached from people’s hunger.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see ordinary food taken up into extraordinary provision. Thanksgiving and service belong together; the disciples distribute what Christ supplies.

Mark 8:8

8They ate and were filled. They took up seven baskets of broken pieces that were left over.

Arminian

The people eat their fill, and seven baskets remain. Arminian readers see sufficiency that answers real hunger and abundance that should challenge the disciples’ later anxiety.

Reformed

Reformed readers share that emphasis on Christ’s ample provision. The stated number matters to Jesus’ later reminder, without requiring speculative symbolism for every basket.

Mark 8:9

9Those who had eaten were about four thousand. Then he sent them away.

Arminian

Mark identifies about four thousand people before Jesus sends them away. Arminian readers see compassion reaching a large multitude while the narrative keeps this feeding distinct from the earlier five thousand.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise preserve the distinction between the two feedings, which Jesus himself will recall. The crowd’s departure follows satisfaction rather than being used as a substitute for meeting their need.

Mark 8:10

10Immediately he entered into the boat with his disciples and came into the region of Dalmanutha.

Arminian

Jesus leaves by boat with his disciples for Dalmanutha. Arminian readers see the narrative moving from gracious provision to a confrontation over the demand for further proof.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to the transition without imposing an unverified identification of the region. Being with Jesus will expose the disciples to both his works and his searching instruction.

Mark 8:11

11The Pharisees came out and began to question him, seeking from him a sign from heaven and testing him.

Arminian

The Pharisees seek a sign as a test, not as a humble request for help. Arminian readers distinguish honest questioning from demands that make trust conditional on controlling the terms of revelation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see resistance beneath the request. The problem is not that Christ forbids thought or evidence, but that these questioners approach him adversarially after his works of mercy.

Mark 8:12

12He sighed deeply in his spirit and said, “Why does this generation seek a sign? Most certainly I tell you, no sign will be given to this generation.”

Arminian

Jesus’ deep sigh accompanies his refusal to satisfy this testing demand. Arminian readers see the seriousness of resisting the light already given, rather than a promise to supply whatever evidence hostility requires.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish Christ’s free revelation from an obligation to perform on demand. The WEB’s footnote offers other senses for generation; in context the rebuke addresses the people pressing this challenge.

Mark 8:13

13He left them, and again entering into the boat, departed to the other side.

Arminian

Jesus leaves the questioners and crosses to the other side. Arminian readers see that persistent testing has consequences; access to his presence must not be treated as an entitlement.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note the gravity of his withdrawal. The passage turns toward instruction of the disciples rather than continued argument on the opponents’ terms.

Mark 8:14

14They forgot to take bread; and they didn’t have more than one loaf in the boat with them.

Arminian

The disciples have only one loaf because they forgot to bring bread. Arminian readers see ordinary anxiety setting the stage for a misunderstanding of Jesus’ spiritual warning.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise notice the contrast between actual scarcity in the boat and the abundance they have just witnessed. Their need is not merely for more bread but for a truer understanding of Christ.

Mark 8:15

15He warned them, saying, “Take heed: beware of the yeast of the Pharisees and the yeast of Herod.”

Arminian

Jesus warns about yeast associated with the Pharisees and Herod. Arminian readers understand a caution about corrupting influence, read alongside the testing demand and the disciples’ failure to perceive, rather than a warning about baking.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see danger spreading through unbelief and distorted allegiance. Mark does not explain every aspect of Herod’s yeast here, so the warning should not be expanded into an unsupported catalogue of motives.

Mark 8:16

16They reasoned with one another, saying, “It’s because we have no bread.”

Arminian

The disciples interpret the warning as a complaint about their lack of bread. Arminian readers see how immediate concerns can crowd out attention to Jesus’ actual teaching.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note their spiritual dullness despite repeated exposure to his works. The misunderstanding calls for correction, not ridicule of the ordinary need to eat.

Mark 8:17

17Jesus, perceiving it, said to them, “Why do you reason that it’s because you have no bread? Don’t you perceive yet or understand? Is your heart still hardened?

Arminian

Jesus confronts the disciples’ hardened heart while continuing to teach them. Arminian readers stress that disciples can resist grace and must respond to correction; the question does not itself establish their final spiritual condition.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the persistence of dullness among disciples and their need for Christ’s illuminating work. The rebuke can be a means by which he corrects and preserves his people, not proof that all addressed are finally rejected.

Key contrast Both see real culpable dullness among disciples and a need for divine help. Arminian readers emphasize resistance to grace and the call to respond; Reformed readers emphasize Christ’s correction as a means of overcoming dullness and preserving his people. The question alone does not settle the disciples’ final state.

Mark 8:18

18Having eyes, don’t you see? Having ears, don’t you hear? Don’t you remember?

Arminian

Jesus joins seeing and hearing with remembering what he has done. Arminian readers see an urgent summons to use the light already received rather than allow forgetfulness to deepen resistance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress that physical perception alone is insufficient for spiritual understanding. Christ’s probing questions expose need while directing the disciples back to his faithful provision.

Mark 8:19

19When I broke the five loaves among the five thousand, how many baskets full of broken pieces did you take up?” They told him, “Twelve.”

Arminian

Jesus recalls the five thousand and the twelve baskets they collected. Arminian readers see remembered experience of grace as a reason to trust him in a new shortage.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that Christ directs faith toward his concrete deeds rather than vague optimism. The disciples can recite the number but still need to understand its significance.

Mark 8:20

20“When the seven loaves fed the four thousand, how many baskets full of broken pieces did you take up?” They told him, “Seven.”

Arminian

Jesus then recalls the four thousand and seven baskets. Arminian readers see repeated provision as a patient appeal to trust, not a reason to stop listening or become presumptuous.

Reformed

Reformed readers share that emphasis on Christ’s patient instruction. The two distinct memories leave the disciples without reason to interpret his warning as mere anxiety about bread.

Mark 8:21

21He asked them, “Don’t you understand yet?”

Arminian

Jesus ends with a question that leaves their understanding unsettled. Arminian readers hear an invitation to renewed attention rather than permission to settle into spiritual complacency.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see patient correction that has not yet reached full clarity. The following healing of sight provides a fitting narrative frame for disciples who perceive only partially.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
