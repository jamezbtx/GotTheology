Passage

Mark 4

Verses 21–34 · WEB

The lamp and measure sayings continue the call to hear Jesus attentively: hidden truth is meant for disclosure, and reception brings accountability. The secretly growing seed and the mustard seed picture God’s kingdom advancing beyond human control from small beginnings toward harvest and sheltering greatness. Mark closes this teaching sequence by contrasting public parables with private explanation. Arminian and Reformed readers largely share these emphases on revelation, responsible hearing, and God-given growth.

Mark 4:21

21He said to them, “Is a lamp brought to be put under a basket or under a bed? Isn’t it put on a stand?

Arminian

A lamp belongs on a stand, where it gives light. Arminian readers see the kingdom’s revelation as intended to shine forth, calling disciples to receive and communicate what Jesus teaches.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect the lamp with revelation rather than permanent concealment. What Jesus makes known is not to remain uselessly hidden from view.

Mark 4:22

22For there is nothing hidden except that it should be made known, neither was anything made secret but that it should come to light.

Arminian

Hiddenness serves an eventual disclosure. Arminian readers see hope that the kingdom now explained privately will be made known; temporary obscurity is not the goal of Jesus’ ministry.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm God’s purpose to bring hidden truth to light. The present distinction between public parables and private explanation belongs within the kingdom’s unfolding revelation.

Mark 4:23

23If any man has ears to hear, let him hear.”

Arminian

The renewed call to hear makes disclosure a summons to attention. Arminian readers stress that grace-given opportunity should be met with responsive listening.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise treat listening as a duty, not passive exposure to sound. God’s revelation calls for the attentive faith his word nurtures.

Mark 4:24

24He said to them, “Take heed what you hear. With whatever measure you measure, it will be measured to you; and more will be given to you who hear.

Arminian

In this setting, the measure concerns attentive reception of Jesus’ teaching. Arminian readers see God graciously granting further understanding as hearers heed what they receive, not a scheme for earning revelation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect the measure with hearing the word. God gives increasing understanding through diligent attention, itself dependent on grace rather than personal merit.

Mark 4:25

25For whoever has, to him more will be given; and he who doesn’t have, even that which he has will be taken away from him.”

Arminian

Receptive hearers receive more, while neglected light is lost. Arminian readers see a serious warning against resisting or abandoning what God has given; spiritual opportunity must not be presumed upon.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a warning that neglected revelation brings loss rather than security. The saying calls for fruitful hearing and does not make possession of outward knowledge proof of saving faith.

Mark 4:26

26He said, “God’s Kingdom is as if a man should cast seed on the earth,

Arminian

The farmer sows, but the kingdom’s growth exceeds the farmer’s control. Arminian readers affirm that faithful ministry depends on God’s life-giving work, not techniques that can manufacture results.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the appointed work of sowing from the power that makes it fruitful. The kingdom is God’s work even when human servants carry its message.

Mark 4:27

27and should sleep and rise night and day, and the seed should spring up and grow, though he doesn’t know how.

Arminian

Growth continues beyond the farmer’s observation and understanding. Arminian readers see grounds for patient reliance on God rather than anxious attempts to control every response.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the hidden efficacy of God’s work. The farmer’s ignorance and sleep show that the kingdom does not depend on constant human supervision.

Mark 4:28

28For the earth bears fruit by itself: first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear.

Arminian

The sequence from blade to full grain portrays ordered growth. Arminian readers affirm that God gives life and maturity over time; the earth’s fruitfulness does not make the kingdom independent of him.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see divinely sustained growth proceeding beyond the sower’s power. The picture encourages patience with gradual development rather than demanding immediate fullness.

Mark 4:29

29But when the fruit is ripe, immediately he puts in the sickle, because the harvest has come.”

Arminian

Ripeness brings decisive harvesting. Arminian readers see the kingdom moving toward its appointed completion, with patient growth giving way to the time for gathering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a sure culmination rather than endless hidden development. The harvest recalls the biblical imagery of God’s final gathering and judgment.

Mark 4:30

30He said, “How will we liken God’s Kingdom? Or with what parable will we illustrate it?

Arminian

Jesus introduces another comparison to explain God’s kingdom through familiar experience. Arminian readers join Reformed readers in recognizing that the illustration highlights a particular feature, not every property of the kingdom.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise attend to the contrast the following verses develop. Jesus teaches the kingdom’s surprising greatness through an image of very small beginnings.

Mark 4:31

31It’s like a grain of mustard seed, which, when it is sown in the earth, though it is less than all the seeds that are on the earth,

Arminian

The mustard seed pictures a beginning easily dismissed as insignificant. Arminian readers understand the smallness within the parable’s agricultural setting, directing attention to the extraordinary contrast with its later growth.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the tiny seed as an image of the kingdom’s unimpressive beginnings. Its present appearance is no reliable measure of what God will bring from it.

Mark 4:32

32yet when it is sown, grows up and becomes greater than all the herbs, and puts out great branches, so that the birds of the sky can lodge under its shadow.”

Arminian

The mature plant offers shelter far beyond what its small seed suggested. Arminian readers see the kingdom’s expansive blessing; the birds belong to this sheltering image rather than carrying the negative meaning of verse 4.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see abundant growth and shelter, recalling biblical pictures of great kingdoms as sheltering trees. Both traditions trust God to bring greatness from small beginnings without identifying it with worldly prestige.

Mark 4:33

33With many such parables he spoke the word to them, as they were able to hear it.

Arminian

Jesus accommodates his teaching to what the crowd can hear. Arminian readers see patient instruction that genuinely addresses hearers while still requiring a receptive response.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see teaching suited to the crowd’s capacity. Adaptation does not remove the need for the deeper explanation Jesus gives his disciples.

Mark 4:34

34Without a parable he didn’t speak to them; but privately to his own disciples he explained everything.

Arminian

Mark summarizes this period of public parabolic teaching alongside private explanation. Arminian readers see disciples learning through continued dependence on Jesus rather than possessing automatic insight.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see Christ as the giver and interpreter of revelation. Private explanation develops the gift mentioned in verse 11, while the disciples’ later fear shows their understanding remains incomplete.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
