Passage

Mark 4

Verses 35–41 · WEB

On the evening after the seaside teaching, Jesus directs a crossing of the lake. A threatening storm exposes the disciples’ fear; his command brings a great calm and turns their attention to his identity. The question of who he is carries the narrative forward from his teaching to his authority over creation. Both traditions affirm his true humanity, divine authority, and patient correction of weak disciples.

Mark 4:35

35On that day, when evening had come, he said to them, “Let’s go over to the other side.”

Arminian

The evening crossing follows the day of parables and leads toward the encounter on the other shore. Arminian readers note that obeying Jesus’ direction does not exempt disciples from danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise place the voyage under Jesus’ initiative. The coming storm occurs on the path he directs, not because the disciples have abandoned his command.

Mark 4:36

36Leaving the multitude, they took him with them, even as he was, in the boat. Other small boats were also with him.

Arminian

They leave the crowd in the boat already used for teaching, with other boats accompanying them. Arminian readers see the narrative moving from hearing about the kingdom to encountering Jesus’ authority amid danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see continuity between the seaside teaching and the crossing. The ordinary details locate the coming demonstration of power in the disciples’ shared journey with Jesus.

Mark 4:37

37A big wind storm arose, and the waves beat into the boat, so much that the boat was already filled.

Arminian

The storm threatens to swamp the boat; their danger is real. Arminian readers affirm that trust in Christ need not deny the severity of circumstances or promise a life free from suffering.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise acknowledge the danger rather than treating it as imaginary. God’s providence includes trials in which disciples discover their weakness and dependence.

Mark 4:38

38He himself was in the stern, asleep on the cushion; and they woke him up and asked him, “Teacher, don’t you care that we are dying?”

Arminian

Jesus’ sleep displays his genuine humanity, while the disciples’ appeal questions his care. Arminian readers see that fear can distort trust even as disciples turn to him for help.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm Christ’s true humanity and the disciples’ weak trust. His apparent inactivity is not indifference, and their distress does not place them beyond his care.

Mark 4:39

39He awoke and rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, “Peace! Be still!” The wind ceased and there was a great calm.

Arminian

Jesus commands the wind and sea, and the calm answers immediately. Both traditions see authority recalling God’s rule over the waters in Israel’s Scriptures, not merely a lesson in positive thinking.

Reformed

Reformed readers share this recognition of Christ’s sovereign authority over creation. The great calm answers the great storm through his effective command, revealing who stands among the disciples.

Mark 4:40

40He said to them, “Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?”

Arminian

Jesus rebukes fear that has displaced trust in him. Arminian readers see a call for disciples to grow in faith through his correction, not a declaration that seeking help was wrong.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the disciples’ fearful failure from final abandonment of Christ. His correction exposes their immature trust and teaches reliance on his power and care.

Mark 4:41

41They were greatly afraid and said to one another, “Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?”

Arminian

Fear before the storm becomes awe before Jesus, and the final question presses his identity. Arminian readers join Reformed readers in seeing divine authority over creation, calling for worshipful trust rather than fascination with the miracle alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the question of Jesus’ identity as the climax. The one whom wind and sea obey exercises authority belonging to God; the disciples must reckon with his presence, not merely their rescue.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
