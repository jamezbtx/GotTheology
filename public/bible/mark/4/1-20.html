Passage

Mark 4

Verses 1–20 · WEB

After controversy over his authority and the gathering of his disciples, Jesus teaches a seaside crowd through the parable of the sower and soils. Private explanation identifies the seed as the word and distinguishes fruitful hearing from other responses. Isaiah 6 frames the mystery of the kingdom as graciously given yet judicially concealed. Both traditions affirm the need for grace and enduring fruit; they differ over the basis of withheld understanding and whether the rocky hearers lose genuine faith.

Mark 4:1

1Again he began to teach by the seaside. A great multitude was gathered to him, so that he entered into a boat in the sea and sat down. All the multitude were on the land by the sea.

Arminian

Jesus teaches a large crowd from a boat, continuing the seaside ministry. Arminian readers distinguish public exposure to his teaching from the receptive hearing the parable will require.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the gathered crowd from faithful disciples. The boat provides the setting for teaching that reveals differing responses to the same word.

Mark 4:2

2He taught them many things in parables, and told them in his teaching,

Arminian

Parables draw ordinary experience into the announcement of God’s kingdom. Arminian readers see teaching that calls hearers to attentive, grace-enabled understanding.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see parables as kingdom teaching, not merely moral illustrations. Their revealing and concealing function becomes explicit in verses 11–12.

Mark 4:3

3“Listen! Behold, the farmer went out to sow.

Arminian

The command to listen frames the farmer’s sowing as a summons to receive Jesus’ message. Hearing involves more than being within earshot.

Reformed

Reformed readers share this emphasis on attentive hearing. The farmer’s action directs attention first to the word being proclaimed, before the different soils are explained.

Mark 4:4

4As he sowed, some seed fell by the road, and the birds came and devoured it.

Arminian

Seed on the path remains exposed and is lost to birds. The picture anticipates the removal of the word before it bears fruit, not a failure in the message itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise locate the difference in the reception of the seed rather than its quality. Jesus later identifies the birds’ action with Satan taking the word away.

Mark 4:5

5Others fell on the rocky ground, where it had little soil, and immediately it sprang up, because it had no depth of soil.

Arminian

Shallow soil produces quick growth without depth. Arminian readers hear a warning that an enthusiastic beginning must develop into rooted, enduring trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise warn against confusing immediate enthusiasm with saving faith. The speed of the growth conceals the absence of roots.

Mark 4:6

6When the sun had risen, it was scorched; and because it had no root, it withered away.

Arminian

The sun exposes the plant’s lack of roots. Arminian readers see the coming explanation of persecution: initial reception does not make continued faithfulness automatic.

Reformed

Reformed readers see testing exposing the weakness hidden by rapid growth. Enduring fruit, rather than an impressive beginning, will distinguish good soil.

Mark 4:7

7Others fell among the thorns, and the thorns grew up and choked it, and it yielded no fruit.

Arminian

Thorns compete with the growing seed until it produces nothing. Arminian readers see a warning against allowing rival desires to overcome responsiveness to the word.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see divided attachment as spiritually destructive. The absence of fruit exposes the danger of a heart crowded with competing loyalties.

Mark 4:8

8Others fell into the good ground and yielded fruit, growing up and increasing. Some produced thirty times, some sixty times, and some one hundred times as much.”

Arminian

The harvest shows that sowing is not finally futile despite repeated losses. Both traditions affirm that fruitful reception depends on grace, and that differing yields need not mean faithfulness is identical in every disciple.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see abundant fruit vindicating the sowing of the word. God’s work produces a real harvest with differing measures of fruit, not uniform outward achievement.

Mark 4:9

9He said, “Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear.”

Arminian

Jesus summons each hearer to respond, not merely to admire the story. Arminian readers stress the responsibility to heed the word through God’s enabling grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the summons seriously. The outward call requires attentive hearing, for which sinful people need God to open their understanding.

Mark 4:10

10When he was alone, those who were around him with the twelve asked him about the parables.

Arminian

The questioners include others around Jesus as well as the twelve. Arminian readers see discipleship expressed in drawing near and asking for understanding rather than remaining content with the unexplained story.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise note that this group extends beyond the twelve. Their need to ask shows that closeness to Jesus does not eliminate dependence on his instruction.

Mark 4:11

11He said to them, “To you is given the mystery of God’s Kingdom, but to those who are outside, all things are done in parables,

Arminian

Understanding the kingdom is given, not earned by intellectual skill. Arminian readers affirm divine initiative and connect the outsiders’ obscurity with resistant hearing, while the disciples receive Jesus’ gracious instruction.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise affirm that knowledge of the kingdom is a divine gift. They emphasize God’s sovereign distinction in granting understanding to disciples while leaving outsiders without the same illumination.

Mark 4:12

12that ‘seeing they may see and not perceive, and hearing they may hear and not understand, lest perhaps they should turn again, and their sins should be forgiven them.’ ”

Arminian

Isaiah 6 places uncomprehending hearing within divine judgment. Arminian readers commonly understand the concealment as judicial hardening of people who resist God’s light; turning and forgiveness remain gifts of grace, not unaided achievements.

Reformed

Reformed readers understand the Isaiah quotation as judicial concealment under God’s sovereignty: he grants understanding to some and withholds it from others, leaving them in culpable unbelief. God neither coerces innocent people into sin nor owes saving illumination.

Key contrast Both affirm divine judgment and the necessity of grace. Arminian readers commonly relate withheld understanding to judicial hardening of resistant hearers; Reformed readers emphasize God’s sovereign decision to grant understanding to some and withhold it from others in judgment.

Mark 4:13

13He said to them, “Don’t you understand this parable? How will you understand all of the parables?

Arminian

The disciples’ failure to understand shows that receiving the kingdom’s mystery does not mean instant comprehension. This parable is foundational because it explains responses to the word itself.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see imperfect learners dependent on Christ’s explanation. The gift of understanding works through continued teaching rather than making instruction unnecessary.

Mark 4:14

14The farmer sows the word.

Arminian

The seed is the word, so the story concerns the reception of God’s message. Arminian readers affirm proclamation as a means through which grace addresses hearers.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise identify the word as the appointed means of gospel ministry. The sower serves the message rather than possessing power to create its harvest.

Mark 4:15

15The ones by the road are the ones where the word is sown; and when they have heard, immediately Satan comes and takes away the word which has been sown in them.

Arminian

Satan actively opposes the word’s reception. Arminian readers affirm the reality of this spiritual hostility without making it an excuse for unbelief or a power equal to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognize Satan’s destructive activity. Mere outward hearing does not overcome spiritual blindness; hearers depend on God’s grace to receive and retain the word.

Mark 4:16

16These in the same way are those who are sown on the rocky places, who, when they have heard the word, immediately receive it with joy.

Arminian

The rocky hearers receive the word joyfully, but the next verse reveals their instability. Many Arminian readers understand this as an initial faith that does not endure; joy alone does not establish maturity.

Reformed

Reformed readers generally understand this joyful reception as temporary faith rather than regeneration. Emotional enthusiasm and an outward profession can occur without the inward root of saving faith.

Mark 4:17

17They have no root in themselves, but are short-lived. When oppression or persecution arises because of the word, immediately they stumble.

Arminian

Persecution because of the word exposes the lack of roots. Arminian readers commonly see a warning that genuine initial faith can be abandoned; lasting salvation calls for continued trust sustained by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers generally see persecution exposing temporary, non-saving faith rather than destroying regeneration. True believers can falter seriously, but God preserves them from final apostasy.

Key contrast Both warn that joyful reception without endurance is insufficient. Arminian readers commonly understand these hearers as having genuine initial faith that they abandon; Reformed readers understand their faith as temporary and non-saving, with the lack of roots exposed by persecution.

Mark 4:18

18Others are those who are sown among the thorns. These are those who have heard the word,

Arminian

The thorny hearers have encountered the word, yet competing growth threatens its fruit. Arminian readers see the need to guard an ongoing response against divided loyalties.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise warn that hearing alone does not demonstrate saving reception. The next verse identifies everyday attachments that can crowd out the word.

Mark 4:19

19and the cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches, and the lusts of other things entering in choke the word, and it becomes unfruitful.

Arminian

Anxiety, wealth’s false promises, and other desires choke the message. Arminian readers stress watchfulness: ordinary concerns become destructive when allowed to displace obedient trust.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see riches and desires as rivals to trust in God. The warning calls hearers to repentance and undivided attachment, not merely to avoiding persecution.

Mark 4:20

20Those which were sown on the good ground are those who hear the word, accept it, and bear fruit, some thirty times, some sixty times, and some one hundred times.”

Arminian

Good soil describes those who hear, welcome, and bear fruit from the word. Arminian readers see grace-enabled, continuing reception whose fruit varies in measure; the harvest is not a reward for unaided human goodness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see receptive hearing and fruit as evidence of God’s renewing work. Both traditions expect the word to transform life while allowing differing measures of visible fruit.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
