Passage

Mark 16

Verses 9–20 · WEB

These verses, known as the longer ending of Mark, have a real textual question attached. The two oldest complete Greek manuscripts, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, end at 16:8, and some early writers knew copies without them. Yet the great majority of surviving manuscripts include 9–20, and the WEB translators regard them as reliable on that basis; many modern editions include them in brackets or with a note. Many scholars think they were written by someone after Mark and drawn largely from the other Gospels and Acts. The question cuts across both traditions; it is not an Arminian versus Reformed issue. Both traditions are cautious about building doctrine on these verses alone, and both note that their main teaching is found elsewhere in Scripture.

Mark 16:9

9Now when he had risen early on the first day of the week, he appeared first to Mary Magdalene, from whom he had cast out seven demons.

Arminian

Jesus appears first to Mary Magdalene, from whom he had cast out seven demons (Luke 8:2). Arminian readers note the grace that chooses a once-tormented woman as the first witness.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same, and see God choosing the unlikely. Both traditions note that this matches John 20:11–18.

Mark 16:10

10She went and told those who had been with him, as they mourned and wept.

Arminian

She tells the mourning disciples. Arminian readers see her as a faithful messenger.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. This verse is shared ground.

Mark 16:11

11When they heard that he was alive and had been seen by her, they disbelieved.

Arminian

The disciples do not believe her. Arminian readers note the honest record of the apostles’ doubt.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the disciples’ unbelief shows they did not invent the story. Both traditions value this candor.

Mark 16:12

12After these things he was revealed in another form to two of them as they walked, on their way into the country.

Arminian

He appears “in another form” to two walking into the country, probably the Emmaus account (Luke 24:13–35). Arminian readers note the mystery of the risen body.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the same. This verse is shared ground.

Mark 16:13

13They went away and told it to the rest. They didn’t believe them, either.

Arminian

They report it, but the others still disbelieve. Arminian readers see how hard it was for the disciples to believe.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same. Both traditions note that faith in the resurrection did not come easily even to the apostles.

Mark 16:14

14Afterward he was revealed to the eleven themselves as they sat at the table; and he rebuked them for their unbelief and hardness of heart, because they didn’t believe those who had seen him after he had risen.

Arminian

Jesus appears to the eleven at a meal and rebukes their unbelief and hardness of heart. Arminian readers stress that unbelief is a culpable failure to trust credible witness.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree it is culpable, and they also see the risen Lord overcoming his disciples’ hardness. Both traditions see grace and rebuke together.

Mark 16:15

15He said to them, “Go into all the world and preach the Good News to the whole creation.

Arminian

“Go into all the world and preach the Good News to the whole creation.” Arminian readers stress the universal offer of the gospel, sincerely meant for every person.

Reformed

Reformed readers also hold that the gospel must be preached to all without distinction and freely offered to everyone who hears. Both traditions see this as the church’s mission, also found in Matthew 28:19–20.

Mark 16:16

16He who believes and is baptized will be saved; but he who disbelieves will be condemned.

Arminian

Belief and baptism belong together, but condemnation is tied only to unbelief, so most interpreters on both sides do not read this verse as making baptism strictly necessary for salvation. Arminian readers stress faith as the condition God freely sets for salvation, a condition people can meet by grace or refuse.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree that faith is the means of receiving salvation, but they hold that faith is itself God’s gift to those he chose. Both traditions agree that unbelief, not lack of baptism, brings condemnation.

Key contrast Both see faith as necessary for salvation. Arminians typically understand faith as a grace-enabled response that people can give or refuse; Reformed theology typically understands faith as a gift God gives to the elect.

Mark 16:17

17These signs will accompany those who believe: in my name they will cast out demons; they will speak with new languages;

Arminian

Signs will accompany those who believe: casting out demons and speaking new languages, both found in Acts. Arminian readers note that some traditions within Arminianism, especially Pentecostal and Holiness groups, expect such signs today.

Reformed

Reformed readers often hold that the signs especially confirmed the apostolic witness (Hebrews 2:3–4), although some Reformed and charismatic Christians expect them today. The question of continuing gifts cuts across both traditions.

Mark 16:18

18they will take up serpents; and if they drink any deadly thing, it will in no way hurt them; they will lay hands on the sick, and they will recover.”

Arminian

Serpents and deadly drinks will not harm them, and the sick will recover. Acts 28:3–6 records Paul surviving a snake bite. Arminian and Reformed readers alike warn against treating this as a command to handle snakes or test God.

Reformed

Reformed readers make the same warning (Matthew 4:7). Both traditions read these as promises of protection in mission, not tests to perform.

Mark 16:19

19So then the Lord, after he had spoken to them, was received up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God.

Arminian

Jesus is taken up into heaven and sits at God’s right hand, the place of authority (Psalm 110:1). Arminian readers note the exalted Lord’s reign.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress Christ’s present reign and intercession. Both traditions affirm the ascension, which Luke and Acts also record.

Mark 16:20

20They went out and preached everywhere, the Lord working with them and confirming the word by the signs that followed. Amen.

Arminian

The disciples preach everywhere, and the Lord works with them, confirming the word. Arminian readers see the Lord working with human messengers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Lord as the true power behind the mission. Both traditions close the Gospel on the same note: the risen Lord continues his work through his church.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
