Passage

Mark 13

Verses 24–37 · WEB

The discourse moves from distress to the Son of Man’s glory and the gathering of his people, then ends with illustrations of recognition and watchfulness. Readings centred on AD 70, the future consummation, or both occur within Arminian and Reformed traditions. Jesus’ assurance about his words stands beside his refusal to disclose the day or hour, directing believers toward faithful readiness rather than date-setting.

Mark 13:24

24But in those days, after that oppression, the sun will be darkened, the moon will not give its light,

Arminian

The darkened sun and moon introduce prophetic imagery of decisive upheaval. Arminian readings include symbolic judgment on Jerusalem in AD 70, cosmic events at Christ’s return, and a nearer judgment anticipating the final one. These options cross traditions and should not be turned into competing millennial commitments.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise differ between an AD 70 judgment horizon, a future cosmic transformation, and a layered relation between the two. The imagery evokes God overturning seemingly stable powers. It calls for confidence in Christ rather than certainty about the astronomical form of every detail.

Mark 13:25

25the stars will be falling from the sky, and the powers that are in the heavens will be shaken.

Arminian

The shaking heavens deepen the picture of an order brought under divine judgment. Arminian readers may understand the stars prophetically, cosmically, or with both horizons in view. The common point is that no created power can guarantee security apart from God.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect this language with the prophetic imagery cited by the WEB cross-reference. Interpretations of its relation to Jerusalem and the end differ within the tradition. Both affirm God’s authority without treating current celestial events as a reliable calendar.

Mark 13:26

26Then they will see the Son of Man coming in clouds with great power and glory.

Arminian

The Son of Man is revealed in glory after the discourse’s scenes of distress. Arminian readers may stress his vindication displayed in Jerusalem’s judgment, his visible future return, or the relation between both. The Daniel-like cloud imagery directs attention to Jesus’ authority rather than authorising one unanimous Arminian timetable.

Reformed

Reformed readings likewise include heavenly vindication manifested in AD 70 and the future glorious return, sometimes understood as related horizons. The imagery presents Jesus as ruler and judge rather than a defeated claimant. Differences here do not follow a simple Arminian–Reformed division.

Mark 13:27

27Then he will send out his angels, and will gather together his chosen ones from the four winds, from the ends of the earth to the ends of the sky.

Arminian

Jesus gathers his chosen people from every direction. Arminian readers stress the worldwide community belonging to Christ through faith, whether the gathering is linked chiefly to gospel mission, final deliverance, or both. Salvation is God’s work, not the achievement of those scattered under threat.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress Christ’s effective gathering of those God has chosen. Some emphasise the final assembly of believers, others also the worldwide ingathering following Jerusalem’s judgment. Both traditions hear reassurance that distance and hostility will not defeat Christ’s purpose for his people.

Mark 13:28

28“Now from the fig tree, learn this parable. When the branch has now become tender and produces its leaves, you know that the summer is near;

Arminian

The fig tree teaches attentive recognition rather than hidden calculation. Arminian readers see an ordinary illustration of noticing what is near. The verse itself does not identify the tree with a modern nation or provide a starting date for a countdown.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise take the seasonal comparison as encouragement to attend to Jesus’ warnings. Symbolic interpretations require argument beyond the mere presence of a fig tree. Both traditions should keep the illustration’s simple force in view before building larger prophetic systems.

Mark 13:29

29even so you also, when you see these things coming to pass, know that it is near, at the doors.

Arminian

The disciples are to recognise the approach signalled by these things. Arminian readers differ over whether the nearness concerns Jerusalem’s crisis, the consummation, or connected events. The instruction combines attention with the restraint about exact timing that follows.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise debate the referent of these things and what is near. Confidence in Christ’s warning need not resolve every chronological question. Both traditions should read this verse alongside the subsequent denial that anyone knows the day or hour.

Mark 13:30

30Most certainly I say to you, this generation will not pass away until all these things happen.

Arminian

This generation may mean Jesus’ contemporaries, the generation that sees the final signs, or, in another reading reflected in the WEB footnote, a continuing people. Arminian readers differ over these options and over whether all these things chiefly concerns AD 70 or includes the consummation. Mixed readings distinguish nearer fulfilment from the final day.

Reformed

Reformed readers share the same range of interpretations: Jesus’ contemporaries and Jerusalem’s fall, a future generation witnessing the climax, or a people preserved through history. Layered readings relate AD 70 to final fulfilment without collapsing the two. The meaning of generation and the scope of all these things require contextual argument, not a denominational label.

Mark 13:31

31Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away.

Arminian

Jesus’ words outlast even the created order. Arminian readers find a firm basis for trust when visible securities fail. This assurance concerns his truthfulness rather than the reliability of every interpretation offered in his name.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise stress the enduring authority of Christ’s word over the instability of creation. The discourse’s difficult details do not weaken its call to trust him. Both traditions should distinguish confidence in Jesus from certainty about their own prophetic charts.

Mark 13:32

32“But of that day or that hour no one knows—not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father.

Arminian

The unknown hour excludes confident date-setting, and the Son’s not knowing must be taken seriously. Classical Arminian readers affirm both his true humanity in the incarnation and his full deity, without treating human limitation as proof that he is a lesser god. Accounts of how his divine and human knowledge relate vary.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise confess one divine Son truly incarnate, understanding this limitation in relation to his human life and mission rather than denial of his divine nature. Explanations differ over its precise mode. Both traditions share the practical conclusion: the Father’s timing calls for humility, not a new calculation.

Mark 13:33

33Watch, keep alert, and pray; for you don’t know when the time is.

Arminian

Uncertainty about timing leads to alertness and prayer. Arminian readers stress ongoing dependence on grace rather than a one-time profession that makes vigilance irrelevant. Jesus’ command is a practical summons, not an invitation to live in panic.

Reformed

Reformed readers see watchful prayer as a means through which God sustains his people. Assurance of his purpose does not cancel obedience. Both traditions understand readiness as faithful life before God rather than endless attention to alarming news.

Mark 13:34

34“It is like a man traveling to another country, having left his house and given authority to his servants, and to each one his work, and also commanded the doorkeeper to keep watch.

Arminian

The absent master assigns work as well as watchfulness. Arminian readers stress responsible stewardship during the waiting period, enabled by the master’s provision. Expectation of his coming should deepen service rather than encourage withdrawal.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see entrusted tasks defining the servants’ readiness. The master’s authority gives purpose to ordinary duties. Both traditions recognise that waiting faithfully involves working, not abandoning obligations in order to speculate about the return.

Mark 13:35

35Watch therefore, for you don’t know when the lord of the house is coming—whether at evening, or at midnight, or when the rooster crows, or in the morning;

Arminian

The range of night watches underlines that the master’s arrival cannot be scheduled by the servants. Arminian readers stress persevering attentiveness through ordinary life. The imagery should not be made into a coded timetable of historical eras.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise understand uncertainty as a reason for steady fidelity. The later night of the disciples’ failure gives watchfulness particular narrative force. Both traditions distinguish alert obedience from the physical impossibility of never sleeping.

Mark 13:36

36lest, coming suddenly, he might find you sleeping.

Arminian

Being found asleep pictures failure to remain ready for the master. Arminian readers hear a serious warning against spiritual complacency and a reason to seek sustaining grace. The metaphor does not condemn bodily rest or exhausted believers as such.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the warning as part of Christ’s preserving instruction to his people. Readiness shows itself in faithful allegiance, not constant fear. Both traditions can take the danger seriously without turning it into a demand for anxious self-surveillance.

Mark 13:37

37What I tell you, I tell all: Watch!”

Arminian

The command to watch extends beyond the four disciples who first asked the question. Arminian readers understand an enduring call for believers to respond faithfully through grace. Application must retain the discourse’s restraint about dates and its concern for endurance.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise receive this as instruction for the wider community of disciples. Christ guards his people through warnings that shape their conduct. Shared watchfulness means loyalty, prayer, and service rather than agreement on one detailed eschatological system.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
