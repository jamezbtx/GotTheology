Passage

Mark 13

Verses 1–13 · WEB

Leaving the temple, Jesus answers his disciples’ questions about its coming destruction and warns them about deception, upheaval, and persecution. The discourse begins with the situation facing these hearers while opening questions about the wider completion of God’s purpose. Arminian and Reformed readers share its call to faithful witness, though readings of its historical and final horizons vary within both traditions.

Mark 13:1

1As he went out of the temple, one of his disciples said to him, “Teacher, see what kind of stones and what kind of buildings!”

Arminian

The disciple admires the temple’s imposing buildings as Jesus leaves it. Arminian readers see the danger of mistaking visible religious strength for security before God. The coming warning directs confidence toward Jesus rather than impressive institutions.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish the grandeur of a sanctuary from the faithfulness God requires. The scene prepares for judgment on what appears permanent. Both traditions can honour worship without assuming that a revered institution is beyond correction.

Mark 13:2

2Jesus said to him, “Do you see these great buildings? There will not be left here one stone on another, which will not be thrown down.”

Arminian

Jesus predicts the temple’s destruction rather than endorsing the disciple’s confidence in its stones. Arminian readers understand this as a serious warning of judgment, not the failure of God’s promises. Its fulfilment in Jerusalem’s destruction in AD 70 anchors the discourse in history.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise connect this prediction with the destruction of the Jerusalem temple in AD 70. God’s purpose does not depend on preserving these buildings. How the rest of the discourse relates to that event and to final judgment remains debated within both traditions.

Mark 13:3

3As he sat on the Mount of Olives opposite the temple, Peter, James, John, and Andrew asked him privately,

Arminian

The private question comes from four disciples looking toward the temple from the Mount of Olives. Arminian readers attend to these original hearers before applying the speech to later believers. Its pastoral setting resists treating it as a detached chart of future events.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise begin with Jesus’ warning to these disciples about the temple they can see. The immediate setting matters even where readers also discern a future horizon. Both traditions should interpret the discourse before fitting it into an eschatological system.

Mark 13:4

4“Tell us, when will these things be? What is the sign that these things are all about to be fulfilled?”

Arminian

The disciples ask when the predicted events will happen and how to recognise their approach. Arminian readers note that Jesus answers with instruction for faithfulness as well as signs. Their question does not mean they already distinguish Jerusalem’s fall from the final consummation.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise recognise that the scope of these things is a central interpretive question. Some relate the answer chiefly to AD 70, others to the end, and others to both. No single arrangement of these horizons defines Reformed or Arminian faith.

Mark 13:5

5Jesus, answering, began to tell them, “Be careful that no one leads you astray.

Arminian

Jesus begins with a warning against deception, not a date. Arminian readers stress responsible discernment enabled by grace; hearing Jesus’ warning calls for a faithful response. The first practical task is to remain loyal to him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the warning as a means by which Christ guards his people against false teaching. Confidence in God’s care does not make discernment unnecessary. Both traditions understand watchfulness as obedience to Jesus rather than fascination with alarming predictions.

Mark 13:6

6For many will come in my name, saying, ‘I am he!’ and will lead many astray.

Arminian

Claimants who use Jesus’ name can still deceive. Arminian readers stress testing a claimant’s message and allegiance rather than trusting religious language alone. The main WEB wording identifies the pretenders’ claim without requiring a precise account of every future impostor.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see a warning that apparent spiritual authority can conceal falsehood. Association with Jesus’ name is no guarantee of truth. His followers must judge claims by loyalty to him, not by a leader’s confidence or popularity.

Mark 13:7

7“When you hear of wars and rumors of wars, don’t be troubled. For those must happen, but the end is not yet.

Arminian

War is not by itself evidence that the end has arrived. Arminian readers hear reassurance against panic and a call to continue faithful service amid upheaval. Trust in God must not become indifference to people who suffer in conflict.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise distinguish God’s rule over history from the claim that every conflict identifies a prophetic deadline. Jesus explicitly restrains premature conclusions. Both traditions can seek peace while refusing to let alarming reports govern their hope.

Mark 13:8

8For nation will rise against nation, and kingdom against kingdom. There will be earthquakes in various places. There will be famines and troubles. These things are the beginning of birth pains.

Arminian

Wars, earthquakes, and famine are described as beginnings rather than a complete timetable. Arminian readers see a summons to endurance and compassionate action under distress. Birth pains point beyond present suffering without authorising confident calculations from each disaster.

Reformed

Reformed readers see upheaval within a history that remains under God’s care. The imagery gives suffering a horizon without explaining every event’s particular cause. Both traditions should resist sensationalism and respond to those in need with practical mercy.

Mark 13:9

9“But watch yourselves, for they will deliver you up to councils. You will be beaten in synagogues. You will stand before rulers and kings for my sake, for a testimony to them.

Arminian

Jesus prepares disciples for religious and political hostility that becomes an occasion for testimony. Arminian readers stress grace-enabled courage, not a requirement to seek persecution. The warning concerns particular opponents and must not become hostility toward Jewish people as a whole.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see God able to use opposition for witness before authorities. His providence does not make the violence of persecutors righteous. Both traditions should preserve the passage’s concern for faithful testimony without generalising blame to an entire people.

Mark 13:10

10The Good News must first be preached to all the nations.

Arminian

The gospel’s proclamation to all nations belongs to God’s purpose. Arminian readers emphasise the breadth of the invitation and the responsibility to participate in mission. Interpretations differ over whether the immediate horizon is the first-century world, the final worldwide mission, or both.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress the certainty of God’s purpose advancing through gospel preaching. That certainty encourages mission rather than replacing it. The extent and timing of the proclamation are debated across both traditions and cannot simply be read from modern geographical categories.

Mark 13:11

11When they lead you away and deliver you up, don’t be anxious beforehand or premeditate what you will say, but say whatever will be given you in that hour. For it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit.

Arminian

The Spirit will sustain testimony when disciples are handed over. Arminian readers stress dependence on grace in a crisis that exceeds their resources. This promise is not a general ban on study, preparation, or seeking wise counsel.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise see the Spirit supplying what Christ’s witnesses need under persecution. Divine help does not erase their speaking but enables it. Both traditions can distinguish this particular assurance from a claim that every spontaneous religious statement is inspired.

Mark 13:12

12“Brother will deliver up brother to death, and the father his child. Children will rise up against parents and cause them to be put to death.

Arminian

Faithfulness to Jesus may bring betrayal within the closest relationships. Arminian readers hear preparation for painful choices, not a command to distrust every family member. Grace supports loyalty to Christ while believers continue seeking others’ good.

Reformed

Reformed readers likewise acknowledge that belonging to Christ can expose deep conflict within families. Jesus’ warning describes the evil of betrayal rather than approving it. Both traditions should offer practical care to those whose discipleship has made them vulnerable.

Mark 13:13

13You will be hated by all men for my name’s sake, but he who endures to the end will be saved.

Arminian

Endurance belongs to the path of salvation, not a way of purchasing it. Arminian readers stress continuing faith sustained by grace and take seriously the danger of abandoning Christ. The promise looks beyond immediate hostility and need not guarantee physical survival to every faithful disciple.

Reformed

Reformed readers see enduring faith as the fruit of God’s preserving grace and this warning as one means of sustaining it. Salvation is not earned by surviving enough hardship. Both traditions distinguish final deliverance in Christ from a promise that no witness will be killed.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
