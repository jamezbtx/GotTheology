Passage

Acts 14

Verses 1–18 · WEB

In Iconium many Jews and Greeks believe, but opponents poison minds against the brothers. Paul and Barnabas stay a long time, speaking boldly while the Lord confirms his word with signs, until a plot to stone them drives them to Lystra and Derbe. In Lystra Paul heals a man lame from birth who had faith to be made well, and the crowd hails Barnabas as Zeus and Paul as Hermes and prepares to sacrifice to them. Tearing their clothes, the apostles insist they are only men and urge the people to turn from idols to the living God, who made all things and has never left himself without witness.

Acts 14:1

1In Iconium, they entered together into the synagogue of the Jews, and so spoke that a great multitude both of Jews and of Greeks believed.

Arminian

In Iconium a great number of Jews and Greeks believe. Arminian readers see the same message receiving a wide response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God gathering people from both groups.

Acts 14:2

2But the disbelieving Jews stirred up and embittered the souls of the Gentiles against the brothers.

Arminian

Unbelieving Jews stir up the Gentiles against the brothers. Arminian commentators see unbelief actively spreading hostility.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the familiar pattern of opposition following fruit.

Acts 14:3

3Therefore they stayed there a long time, speaking boldly in the Lord, who testified to the word of his grace, granting signs and wonders to be done by their hands.

Arminian

They stay a long time, speaking boldly, and the Lord confirms the word of his grace with signs. Arminian readers see God backing faithful preaching.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the phrase “the word of his grace” and see the gospel as the message of God’s unearned favor.

Acts 14:4

4But the multitude of the city was divided. Part sided with the Jews and part with the apostles.

Arminian

The city is divided. Arminian expositors see the gospel forcing people to choose.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the gospel separating as well as uniting.

Acts 14:5

5When some of both the Gentiles and the Jews, with their rulers, made a violent attempt to mistreat and stone them,

Arminian

A plot forms to mistreat and stone them. Arminian readers see danger mounting.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s servants facing the suffering foretold.

Acts 14:6

6they became aware of it and fled to the cities of Lycaonia, Lystra, Derbe, and the surrounding region.

Arminian

They learn of it and flee to Lystra and Derbe. Arminian commentators see wisdom in escaping danger when possible.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see providence moving the mission to new cities.

Acts 14:7

7There they preached the Good News.

Arminian

There they preach the gospel. Arminian readers see persistence in mission.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the word continuing despite persecution.

Acts 14:8

8At Lystra a certain man sat, impotent in his feet, a cripple from his mother’s womb, who never had walked.

Arminian

At Lystra a man lame from birth sits listening. Arminian expositors see a man who had never walked.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see helplessness that only God can remedy.

Acts 14:9

9He was listening to Paul speaking, who, fastening eyes on him and seeing that he had faith to be made whole,

Arminian

Paul sees that he has faith to be made well. Arminian readers see faith that responds to the message and receives healing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see faith already at work, which God has given through hearing the word.

Acts 14:10

10said with a loud voice, “Stand upright on your feet!” He leaped up and walked.

Arminian

“Stand upright on your feet!” He leaps up and walks. Arminian commentators see the healing parallel Peter’s in chapter 3.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God working the same signs through Paul as through Peter.

Acts 14:11

11When the multitude saw what Paul had done, they lifted up their voice, saying in the language of Lycaonia, “The gods have come down to us in the likeness of men!”

Arminian

The crowd shouts that the gods have come down in human form. Arminian readers see pagans interpreting the miracle through their own beliefs.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the confusion of hearts without the light of Scripture.

Acts 14:12

12They called Barnabas “Jupiter”, and Paul “Mercury”, because he was the chief speaker.

Arminian

They call Barnabas Zeus and Paul Hermes. Arminian expositors note a local legend about these gods visiting the region.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see idolatry ready to absorb the gospel into its own system.

Acts 14:13

13The priest of Jupiter, whose temple was in front of their city, brought oxen and garlands to the gates, and would have made a sacrifice along with the multitudes.

Arminian

The priest of Zeus brings oxen to sacrifice to them. Arminian readers see danger in misplaced admiration.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the instinct to worship the creature rather than the Creator.

Acts 14:14

14But when the apostles, Barnabas and Paul, heard of it, they tore their clothes and sprang into the multitude, crying out,

Arminian

Barnabas and Paul tear their clothes and rush into the crowd. Arminian commentators see horror at being worshiped.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see zeal for God’s glory.

Acts 14:15

15“Men, why are you doing these things? We also are men of the same nature as you, and bring you good news, that you should turn from these vain things to the living God, who made the sky, the earth, the sea, and all that is in them;

Arminian

“We also are men,” they say, and urge them to turn from vain things to the living God who made everything. Arminian readers see an appeal to people without Scripture, beginning with creation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the call to repentance grounded in God as Creator.

Acts 14:16

16who in the generations gone by allowed all the nations to walk in their own ways.

Arminian

In past generations God allowed the nations to walk in their own ways. Arminian expositors see God’s patience in permitting human choices.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God passing over the nations for a time in his sovereign purpose, as 17:30 also says.

Acts 14:17

17Yet he didn’t leave himself without witness, in that he did good and gave you rains from the sky and fruitful seasons, filling our hearts with food and gladness.”

Arminian

Yet he did not leave himself without witness, giving rain, harvests, and gladness. Arminian readers see general revelation as a witness to all people, leaving everyone accountable.

Reformed

Reformed readers see common grace: God’s goodness to all people, which reveals him yet does not by itself save.

Acts 14:18

18Even saying these things, they hardly stopped the multitudes from making a sacrifice to them.

Arminian

Even so, they barely stop the crowd from sacrificing. Arminian commentators see how hard it is to turn people from idolatry.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the depth of human blindness apart from grace.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
