Passage

Acts 5

Verses 17–42 · WEB

Jealous, the high priest and Sadducees jail the apostles, but an angel opens the prison at night and sends them to preach in the temple. Brought before the council and charged with defying its order, Peter answers that they must obey God rather than men: God raised and exalted Jesus as Prince and Savior to give Israel repentance and forgiveness, and the apostles and the Holy Spirit are witnesses. The council wants to kill them, but Gamaliel advises caution: if this movement is of men it will fail, but if it is of God, they cannot overthrow it. The apostles are beaten and released, rejoicing to suffer for the Name, and keep teaching daily.

Acts 5:17

17But the high priest rose up, and all those who were with him (which is the sect of the Sadducees), and they were filled with jealousy

Arminian

The high priest and the Sadducees are filled with jealousy. Arminian readers see envy driving opposition to God’s work.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the hostility of the religious leaders repeating their response to Jesus.

Acts 5:18

18and laid hands on the apostles, then put them in public custody.

Arminian

They arrest the apostles and put them in the public jail. Arminian commentators note the escalation from warning to imprisonment.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see human power attempting to restrain the gospel.

Acts 5:19

19But an angel of the Lord opened the prison doors by night, and brought them out and said,

Arminian

An angel opens the prison doors by night. Arminian readers see God intervening to free his servants for continued witness.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence overruling the authorities, whose locked doors cannot hold those he sends.

Acts 5:20

20“Go stand and speak in the temple to the people all the words of this life.”

Arminian

“Go stand and speak in the temple to the people all the words of this life.” Arminian expositors see the gospel as a message of life offered publicly.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the commission renewed: the apostles are freed in order to preach.

Acts 5:21

21When they heard this, they entered into the temple about daybreak and taught. But the high priest and those who were with him came and called the council together, with all the senate of the children of Israel, and sent to the prison to have them brought.

Arminian

At daybreak they teach in the temple, while the council convenes and sends for them. Arminian readers see quiet obedience in the face of danger.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the irony of the council summoning prisoners who are already free and preaching.

Acts 5:22

22But the officers who came didn’t find them in the prison. They returned and reported,

Arminian

The officers do not find them in the prison. Arminian commentators note the confusion of those who opposed them.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God making the authorities look powerless.

Acts 5:23

23“We found the prison shut and locked, and the guards standing before the doors, but when we opened them, we found no one inside!”

Arminian

The prison was locked and guarded, but no one was inside. Arminian readers see clear evidence of divine action.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a miracle that leaves the authorities without explanation.

Acts 5:24

24Now when the high priest, the captain of the temple, and the chief priests heard these words, they were very perplexed about them and what might become of this.

Arminian

The leaders are perplexed about what this could lead to. Arminian expositors see people struggling to control something beyond them.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see human plans unsettled by God’s work.

Acts 5:25

25One came and told them, “Behold, the men whom you put in prison are in the temple, standing and teaching the people.”

Arminian

Someone reports that the men are standing in the temple teaching. Arminian readers note the apostles’ courage in returning to the same place.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience to God’s command despite obvious risk.

Acts 5:26

26Then the captain went with the officers, and brought them without violence, for they were afraid that the people might stone them.

Arminian

The captain brings them without force for fear of the people. Arminian commentators see public favor protecting the apostles.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God using ordinary means to restrain violence.

Acts 5:27

27When they had brought them, they set them before the council. The high priest questioned them,

Arminian

They are set before the council and questioned by the high priest. Arminian readers see another opportunity for witness before Israel’s leaders.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Jesus’ promise that his followers would stand before councils fulfilled.

Acts 5:28

28saying, “Didn’t we strictly command you not to teach in this name? Behold, you have filled Jerusalem with your teaching, and intend to bring this man’s blood on us.”

Arminian

The high priest says they have filled Jerusalem with their teaching and want to bring Jesus’ blood on the leaders. Arminian expositors see guilty consciences reacting to the message.

Reformed

Reformed expositors note the echo of Matthew 27:25, where the crowd accepted responsibility for Jesus’ blood.

Acts 5:29

29But Peter and the apostles answered, “We must obey God rather than men.

Arminian

“We must obey God rather than men.” Arminian readers see conscience bound to God over human authority.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the classic statement of the limits of civil and religious authority under God.

Acts 5:30

30The God of our fathers raised up Jesus, whom you killed, hanging him on a tree.

Arminian

God raised Jesus, whom they killed by hanging him on a tree. Arminian commentators see Peter naming the leaders’ guilt again.

Reformed

Reformed commentators note the reference to Deuteronomy 21:23: Jesus bore the curse of the law.

Acts 5:31

31God exalted him with his right hand to be a Prince and a Savior, to give repentance to Israel, and remission of sins.

Arminian

God exalted Jesus as Prince and Savior to give repentance and forgiveness to Israel. Arminian interpreters read “give repentance” as God granting Israel the opportunity and the enabling grace to repent.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters see repentance itself described as a gift that the exalted Christ bestows, not a work people supply on their own.

Acts 5:32

32We are his witnesses of these things; and so also is the Holy Spirit, whom God has given to those who obey him.”

Arminian

The apostles and the Holy Spirit, given to those who obey God, are witnesses. Arminian readers see the Spirit given to those who respond in obedience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see obedience here as the obedience of faith, and they note that the Spirit is the one who makes such obedience possible.

Acts 5:33

33But they, when they heard this, were cut to the heart, and were determined to kill them.

Arminian

The council is enraged and wants to kill them. Arminian expositors note that being cut to the heart can lead to fury instead of repentance, unlike 2:37.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see two opposite responses to the same message, revealing hearts.

Acts 5:34

34But one stood up in the council, a Pharisee named Gamaliel, a teacher of the law, honored by all the people, and commanded to put the apostles out for a little while.

Arminian

Gamaliel, a respected teacher of the law, stands up and has the apostles sent out. Arminian readers see a voice of caution in the council.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God raising up an unexpected protector for his servants.

Acts 5:35

35He said to them, “You men of Israel, be careful concerning these men, what you are about to do.

Arminian

He warns them to be careful about what they do. Arminian commentators see prudence restraining rash action.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God working through Gamaliel’s counsel.

Acts 5:36

36For before these days Theudas rose up, making himself out to be somebody; to whom a number of men, about four hundred, joined themselves. He was slain; and all, as many as obeyed him, were dispersed and came to nothing.

Arminian

Theudas claimed to be somebody, and his followers were scattered when he died. Arminian readers see history as a lesson about movements that fail.

Reformed

Reformed readers see human movements that collapse without God’s blessing.

Acts 5:37

37After this man, Judas of Galilee rose up in the days of the enrollment, and drew away some people after him. He also perished, and all, as many as obeyed him, were scattered abroad.

Arminian

Judas the Galilean rose up and also perished. Arminian expositors note the second example of failed causes.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the pattern: what is merely human does not last.

Acts 5:38

38Now I tell you, withdraw from these men and leave them alone. For if this counsel or this work is of men, it will be overthrown.

Arminian

If this plan or work is of human origin, it will fail. Arminian readers see Gamaliel appealing to the test of time.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Gamaliel’s logic is sound only partly, since many human movements last a long time. They take it as wisdom used by God rather than a universal rule.

Acts 5:39

39But if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow it, and you would be found even to be fighting against God!”

Arminian

If it is of God, they cannot overthrow it and may find themselves fighting God. Arminian commentators see a warning that opposing God is futile.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see an unintended confession that God’s purposes cannot be defeated.

Acts 5:40

40They agreed with him. Summoning the apostles, they beat them and commanded them not to speak in the name of Jesus, and let them go.

Arminian

They beat the apostles, order them not to speak in Jesus’ name, and release them. Arminian readers see persecution intensifying.

Reformed

Reformed readers see suffering permitted by God for the good of his witnesses.

Acts 5:41

41They therefore departed from the presence of the council, rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for Jesus’ name.

Arminian

They leave rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the Name. Arminian expositors see joy that comes from faithfulness under pressure.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see suffering for Christ as a privilege granted, as Philippians 1:29 says.

Acts 5:42

42Every day, in the temple and at home, they never stopped teaching and preaching Jesus, the Christ.

Arminian

Every day in the temple and in homes they keep teaching and preaching Jesus as the Christ. Arminian readers see persistence in proclaiming the gospel.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the word continuing to go out despite every attempt to stop it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
