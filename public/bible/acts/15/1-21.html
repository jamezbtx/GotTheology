Passage

Acts 15

Verses 1–21 · WEB

Men from Judea teach in Antioch that Gentile believers must be circumcised to be saved. Paul and Barnabas dispute this and go to Jerusalem, where some believing Pharisees insist on circumcision and the law. At the council Peter recalls that God gave the Gentiles the Spirit and cleansed their hearts by faith, and declares that Jews and Gentiles alike are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus. Barnabas and Paul recount God’s signs among the Gentiles. James cites Amos on the rebuilt tent of David and proposes not to burden the Gentiles but to ask them to abstain from idol pollution, sexual immorality, strangled meat, and blood.

Acts 15:1

1Some men came down from Judea and taught the brothers, “Unless you are circumcised after the custom of Moses, you can’t be saved.”

Arminian

Some teach that Gentiles must be circumcised to be saved. Arminian readers see a threat to the gospel of grace that both traditions reject.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the same error Paul confronts in Galatians, adding works to faith.

Acts 15:2

2Therefore when Paul and Barnabas had no small discord and discussion with them, they appointed Paul, Barnabas, and some others of them to go up to Jerusalem to the apostles and elders about this question.

Arminian

Paul and Barnabas dispute this, and the church sends them to Jerusalem. Arminian commentators see the church seeking a common answer.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the value of a council of elders to settle doctrine.

Acts 15:3

3They, being sent on their way by the assembly, passed through both Phoenicia and Samaria, declaring the conversion of the Gentiles. They caused great joy to all the brothers.

Arminian

They pass through Phoenicia and Samaria reporting Gentile conversions, bringing joy. Arminian readers see joy among believers at others’ salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the church rejoicing at God’s work.

Acts 15:4

4When they had come to Jerusalem, they were received by the assembly and the apostles and the elders, and they reported everything that God had done with them.

Arminian

They are welcomed in Jerusalem and report what God has done. Arminian expositors see testimony centered on God’s actions.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God as the main actor in the story.

Acts 15:5

5But some of the sect of the Pharisees who believed rose up, saying, “It is necessary to circumcise them, and to command them to keep the law of Moses.”

Arminian

Believing Pharisees say Gentiles must be circumcised and keep the law. Arminian readers see sincere believers holding to an incomplete view.

Reformed

Reformed readers see legalism arising within the church.

Acts 15:6

6The apostles and the elders were gathered together to see about this matter.

Arminian

The apostles and elders meet to consider the matter. Arminian commentators see shared leadership.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see a model for synods and assemblies.

Acts 15:7

7When there had been much discussion, Peter rose up and said to them, “Brothers, you know that a good while ago God made a choice among you that by my mouth the nations should hear the word of the Good News and believe.

Arminian

Peter recalls that God chose that through his mouth the Gentiles would hear and believe. Arminian readers see God choosing the messenger and the hearers responding in faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s choice directing the way the gospel reached the Gentiles.

Acts 15:8

8God, who knows the heart, testified about them, giving them the Holy Spirit, just like he did to us.

Arminian

God, who knows the heart, testified by giving them the Spirit. Arminian expositors see the Spirit as God’s seal on genuine faith.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God bearing witness to his own work in their hearts.

Acts 15:9

9He made no distinction between us and them, cleansing their hearts by faith.

Arminian

He made no distinction, cleansing their hearts by faith. Arminian readers see faith as the means of cleansing for everyone.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God as the one who cleanses, with faith as the instrument.

Acts 15:10

10Now therefore why do you tempt God, that you should put a yoke on the neck of the disciples which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear?

Arminian

Why test God by placing an unbearable yoke on the disciples? Arminian commentators see the law as a burden no one could carry.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the law unable to justify.

Acts 15:11

11But we believe that we are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they are.”

Arminian

“We believe that we are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they are.” Arminian readers see salvation by grace through faith for all alike.

Reformed

Reformed readers see this as a decisive statement of salvation by grace alone, with Jews and Gentiles on the same footing.

Acts 15:12

12All the multitude kept silence, and they listened to Barnabas and Paul reporting what signs and wonders God had done among the nations through them.

Arminian

The assembly listens as Barnabas and Paul describe signs among the Gentiles. Arminian expositors see testimony confirming doctrine.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God’s works confirming his word.

Acts 15:13

13After they were silent, James answered, “Brothers, listen to me.

Arminian

James responds. Arminian readers see the Lord’s brother as a leading voice.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the elder of the Jerusalem church giving judgment.

Acts 15:14

14Simeon has reported how God first visited the nations to take out of them a people for his name.

Arminian

God first visited the Gentiles to take from them a people for his name. Arminian commentators see God reaching out to the nations.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God taking out a people for himself, language that fits his gathering of the elect.

Acts 15:15

15This agrees with the words of the prophets. As it is written,

Arminian

This agrees with the prophets. Arminian readers see Scripture confirming experience.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Scripture as the final authority in the council.

Acts 15:16

16‘After these things I will return. I will again build the tabernacle of David, which has fallen. I will again build its ruins. I will set it up

Arminian

God will rebuild the fallen tent of David. Arminian expositors see the promise fulfilled in Christ’s kingdom.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the church as the restored people of God under David’s Son.

Acts 15:17

17that the rest of men may seek after the Lord: all the Gentiles who are called by my name, says the Lord, who does all these things.’

Arminian

So that the rest of mankind may seek the Lord, all the Gentiles called by his name. Arminian readers see “the rest of men” seeking the Lord as an invitation open to all.

Reformed

Reformed readers note “all the Gentiles who are called by my name” and see God’s own people among the nations.

Acts 15:18

18“All of God’s works are known to him from eternity.

Arminian

All God’s works are known to him from eternity. Arminian commentators see God’s eternal foreknowledge of his plan.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God’s eternal purpose behind the Gentile mission.

Acts 15:19

19Therefore my judgment is that we don’t trouble those from among the Gentiles who turn to God,

Arminian

James judges that they should not trouble the Gentiles who turn to God. Arminian readers see an end to unnecessary barriers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the gospel protected from additions.

Acts 15:20

20but that we write to them that they abstain from the pollution of idols, from sexual immorality, from what is strangled, and from blood.

Arminian

They should abstain from idol pollution, sexual immorality, strangled things, and blood. Arminian expositors see practical guidance for fellowship between Jewish and Gentile believers.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see these as matters of love and holiness, not conditions of salvation.

Acts 15:21

21For Moses from generations of old has in every city those who preach him, being read in the synagogues every Sabbath.”

Arminian

Moses is read in every synagogue each Sabbath. Arminian readers see sensitivity to Jewish neighbors.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the requests promoting peace in mixed congregations.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
