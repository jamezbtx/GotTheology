Passage

Acts 27

Verses 27–44 · WEB

On the fourteenth night the sailors sense land and try to escape in the lifeboat, but Paul tells the centurion that unless they stay in the ship, the others cannot be saved, and the soldiers cut the boat loose. At dawn Paul urges everyone to eat, gives thanks to God before them all, and they are encouraged. The ship runs aground and breaks apart; the soldiers plan to kill the prisoners, but the centurion, wanting to save Paul, stops them. Some swim and others float on planks, and all 276 reach land safely.

Acts 27:27

27But when the fourteenth night had come, as we were driven back and forth in the Adriatic Sea, about midnight the sailors surmised that they were drawing near to some land.

Arminian

On the fourteenth night, drifting across the Adriatic Sea, as the term then covered the central Mediterranean, the sailors sense land is near.

Reformed

Two weeks of drifting carried them some 475 miles from Cauda to Malta, a distance modern sailors have found consistent with the storm.

Acts 27:28

28They took soundings and found twenty fathoms. After a little while, they took soundings again, and found fifteen fathoms.

Arminian

Their soundings show twenty fathoms, then fifteen. The bottom is rising fast.

Reformed

The precise depths are another mark of an eyewitness account.

Acts 27:29

29Fearing that we would run aground on rocky ground, they let go four anchors from the stern, and wished for daylight.

Arminian

Fearing rocks, they drop four anchors from the stern and pray for day.

Reformed

Anchoring from the stern kept the bow toward shore, ready to run aground at dawn.

Acts 27:30

30As the sailors were trying to flee out of the ship and had lowered the boat into the sea, pretending that they would lay out anchors from the bow,

Arminian

The sailors try to escape in the ship’s boat, pretending to lay out anchors from the bow.

Reformed

Those with the skills to beach the ship try to save themselves and abandon everyone else.

Acts 27:31

31Paul said to the centurion and to the soldiers, “Unless these stay in the ship, you can’t be saved.”

Arminian

Paul tells the centurion and soldiers, “Unless these stay in the ship, you can’t be saved.” Arminian theologians point to this verse to show that God’s promise of verse 24 was fulfilled through, and in some sense conditioned on, human cooperation; the outcome depended on what people did.

Reformed

Reformed theologians see a classic case of God ordaining both the end and the means. The lives were promised with certainty, yet the promise would be kept precisely by the sailors staying aboard, so Paul’s warning is itself one of the means God uses.

Acts 27:32

32Then the soldiers cut away the ropes of the boat and let it fall off.

Arminian

The soldiers cut the ropes of the boat and let it fall away. They act decisively on Paul’s word.

Reformed

The pagan soldiers’ obedience to the prisoner’s warning becomes part of how God keeps his promise.

Acts 27:33

33While the day was coming on, Paul begged them all to take some food, saying, “Today is the fourteenth day that you wait and continue fasting, having taken nothing.

Arminian

As day is about to dawn, Paul urges everyone to eat, since they have gone fourteen days in suspense without food.

Reformed

Paul cares for practical needs while waiting on God’s promise. Faith does not neglect the body.

Acts 27:34

34Therefore I beg you to take some food, for this is for your safety; for not a hair will perish from any of your heads.”

Arminian

He urges them to eat for their survival, for not a hair will perish from anyone’s head, a phrase Jesus used in Luke 21:18.

Reformed

The promise of safety does not make food unnecessary. Paul joins God’s assurance with ordinary means.

Acts 27:35

35When he had said this and had taken bread, he gave thanks to God in the presence of all; then he broke it and began to eat.

Arminian

He takes bread, gives thanks to God in front of them all, breaks it, and begins to eat.

Reformed

The words take, give thanks, and break recall the Last Supper and the feeding miracles, though this is a meal among mostly pagan sailors. Luke lets the echo stand without making it a sacrament.

Acts 27:36

36Then they all cheered up, and they also took food.

Arminian

Everyone is encouraged and they eat too.

Reformed

One man’s calm trust in God steadies a whole ship.

Acts 27:37

37In all, we were two hundred seventy-six souls on the ship.

Arminian

There are 276 people aboard.

Reformed

Luke counts everyone because everyone will be saved. The number makes the fulfillment checkable.

Acts 27:38

38When they had eaten enough, they lightened the ship, throwing out the wheat into the sea.

Arminian

When they have eaten, they lighten the ship by throwing the wheat into the sea.

Reformed

The last of the cargo goes overboard so the ship can ride higher and run farther onto the beach.

Acts 27:39

39When it was day, they didn’t recognize the land, but they noticed a certain bay with a beach, and they decided to try to drive the ship onto it.

Arminian

At daylight they do not recognize the land but see a bay with a beach and decide to run the ship ashore if they can.

Reformed

Traditionally identified as St. Paul’s Bay on Malta.

Acts 27:40

40Casting off the anchors, they left them in the sea, at the same time untying the rudder ropes. Hoisting up the foresail to the wind, they made for the beach.

Arminian

They cut the anchors, loosen the rudder ropes, hoist the foresail, and make for the beach.

Reformed

Every maneuver is careful seamanship. God’s promise does not make skill unnecessary.

Acts 27:41

41But coming to a place where two seas met, they ran the vessel aground. The bow struck and remained immovable, but the stern began to break up by the violence of the waves.

Arminian

They strike a sandbar where two currents meet; the bow sticks fast and the stern is broken apart by the surf.

Reformed

The wreck comes exactly as Paul foretold in verse 26.

Acts 27:42

42The soldiers’ counsel was to kill the prisoners, so that none of them would swim out and escape.

Arminian

The soldiers plan to kill the prisoners so none can swim away and escape.

Reformed

Roman guards could be executed for losing prisoners. Their plan is cruel but grimly logical.

Acts 27:43

43But the centurion, desiring to save Paul, stopped them from their purpose, and commanded that those who could swim should throw themselves overboard first to go toward the land;

Arminian

The centurion, wanting to save Paul, stops them and orders those who can swim to jump first and make for land.

Reformed

Julius’s regard for Paul spares every prisoner. God’s grant of all lives in verse 24 is kept through a soldier’s decision.

Acts 27:44

44and the rest should follow, some on planks and some on other things from the ship. So they all escaped safely to the land.

Arminian

The rest follow on planks or pieces of the ship, and so all are brought safely to land.

Reformed

The chapter ends on the fulfillment of the promise: all 276 reach shore, as God said.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
