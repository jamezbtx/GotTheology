Passage

Acts 27

Verses 1–26 · WEB

Paul sails for Italy as a prisoner under a centurion named Julius, who treats him kindly. Contrary winds slow the voyage, and at Fair Havens Paul warns that continuing will bring loss, but the centurion follows the pilot and owner. A violent northeaster drives the ship for days until all hope of rescue is abandoned. Paul then stands up and tells them to take heart: an angel of God has assured him that he must stand before Caesar and that God has granted him the lives of all who sail with him, though they will run aground on an island.

Acts 27:1

1When it was determined that we should sail for Italy, they delivered Paul and certain other prisoners to a centurion named Julius, of the Augustan band.

Arminian

Paul and other prisoners are handed over to Julius, a centurion of the Augustan Cohort. The “we” returns, so Luke is on board for the voyage.

Reformed

Luke’s account of the voyage is so detailed that historians of ancient seafaring still use it.

Acts 27:2

2Embarking in a ship of Adramyttium, which was about to sail to places on the coast of Asia, we put to sea, Aristarchus, a Macedonian of Thessalonica being with us.

Arminian

They embark on a ship from Adramyttium bound for ports along Asia, accompanied by Aristarchus, a Macedonian from Thessalonica.

Reformed

Aristarchus had been seized in the Ephesian riot (19:29) and later appears as Paul’s fellow prisoner (Colossians 4:10).

Acts 27:3

3The next day, we touched at Sidon. Julius treated Paul kindly and gave him permission to go to his friends and refresh himself.

Arminian

At Sidon Julius treats Paul kindly and lets him visit friends to receive care.

Reformed

A Roman officer’s courtesy becomes one of the means by which Paul is sustained.

Acts 27:4

4Putting to sea from there, we sailed under the lee of Cyprus, because the winds were contrary.

Arminian

Contrary winds force them to sail under the lee of Cyprus, on its sheltered side.

Reformed

Late-summer westerlies made the passage slow, a detail any Mediterranean sailor would recognize.

Acts 27:5

5When we had sailed across the sea which is off Cilicia and Pamphylia, we came to Myra, a city of Lycia.

Arminian

They cross the open sea off Cilicia and Pamphylia and land at Myra in Lycia.

Reformed

Myra was a major port for the Egyptian grain fleet bound for Rome.

Acts 27:6

6There the centurion found a ship of Alexandria sailing for Italy, and he put us on board.

Arminian

There Julius finds an Alexandrian grain ship sailing for Italy and puts them aboard.

Reformed

Grain ships were vital to Rome’s food supply, large enough to carry the 276 people counted in verse 37.

Acts 27:7

7When we had sailed slowly many days, and had come with difficulty opposite Cnidus, the wind not allowing us further, we sailed under the lee of Crete, opposite Salmone.

Arminian

They sail slowly for many days and reach Cnidus with difficulty, then run under the lee of Crete off Salmone.

Reformed

The winds keep blocking their course, foreshadowing the storm to come.

Acts 27:8

8With difficulty sailing along it we came to a certain place called Fair Havens, near the city of Lasea.

Arminian

Coasting with difficulty, they reach a place called Fair Havens near the town of Lasea.

Reformed

Fair Havens was an exposed bay, not a good winter harbor, which sets up the decision that follows.

Acts 27:9

9When much time had passed and the voyage was now dangerous because the Fast had now already gone by, Paul admonished them

Arminian

Much time has passed and sailing is now dangerous, since the Fast, the Day of Atonement, has already gone by. That placed them in late September or October, near the end of the sailing season.

Reformed

Luke dates the voyage by the Jewish calendar, a reminder that Paul still marked Israel’s holy days.

Acts 27:10

10and said to them, “Sirs, I perceive that the voyage will be with injury and much loss, not only of the cargo and the ship, but also of our lives.”

Arminian

Paul warns that the voyage will bring disaster and heavy loss, not only of cargo and ship but also of their lives. His advice comes from experience; he had already been shipwrecked three times (2 Corinthians 11:25).

Reformed

This is Paul’s judgment, not yet a revelation. God’s later word in verse 22 will correct the forecast about lives.

Acts 27:11

11But the centurion gave more heed to the master and to the owner of the ship than to those things which were spoken by Paul.

Arminian

The centurion trusts the pilot and the ship’s owner more than Paul.

Reformed

Expert opinion and commercial interest outweigh the prisoner’s warning, a natural choice that turns out wrong.

Acts 27:12

12Because the haven was not suitable to winter in, the majority advised going to sea from there, if by any means they could reach Phoenix and winter there, which is a port of Crete, looking southwest and northwest.

Arminian

Because Fair Havens is unsuitable for wintering, the majority decide to try for Phoenix, a Cretan harbor facing southwest and northwest.

Reformed

A majority vote decides the course, and the story will show its limits.

Acts 27:13

13When the south wind blew softly, supposing that they had obtained their purpose, they weighed anchor and sailed along Crete, close to shore.

Arminian

A gentle south wind seems to give them what they want, so they weigh anchor and sail close along Crete.

Reformed

The fair breeze looks like confirmation. Luke shows how quickly circumstances can mislead.

Acts 27:14

14But before long, a stormy wind beat down from shore, which is called Euroclydon.

Arminian

Soon a violent wind called the Euraquilo, a northeaster, rushes down from the island.

Reformed

The name, a mix of Greek and Latin, is the sailors’ own term. Luke records the storm as they knew it.

Acts 27:15

15When the ship was caught and couldn’t face the wind, we gave way to it and were driven along.

Arminian

The ship is caught and cannot head into the wind, so they give way and are driven along.

Reformed

Human control ends here. From this point the narrative turns to how God will keep his word.

Acts 27:16

16Running under the lee of a small island called Clauda, we were able, with difficulty, to secure the boat.

Arminian

Running under the lee of a small island called Cauda, they barely manage to secure the ship’s boat.

Reformed

Luke’s “we” suggests he helped haul the dinghy aboard.

Acts 27:17

17After they had hoisted it up, they used cables to help reinforce the ship. Fearing that they would run aground on the Syrtis sand bars, they lowered the sea anchor, and so were driven along.

Arminian

They pass cables under the hull to brace it and, fearing the Syrtis sandbars off Libya, lower the gear and let the ship drift.

Reformed

Undergirding was standard emergency practice. The crew does everything sound seamanship requires.

Acts 27:18

18As we labored exceedingly with the storm, the next day they began to throw things overboard.

Arminian

Violently storm-tossed, they begin throwing cargo overboard the next day.

Reformed

The jettison echoes Jonah 1:5, another storm in which sailors lightened a ship.

Acts 27:19

19On the third day, they threw out the ship’s tackle with their own hands.

Arminian

On the third day they throw out the ship’s tackle with their own hands.

Reformed

Even passengers join in, giving up equipment needed to sail.

Acts 27:20

20When neither sun nor stars shone on us for many days, and no small storm pressed on us, all hope that we would be saved was now taken away.

Arminian

With neither sun nor stars visible for many days and the storm still raging, all hope of being saved is finally abandoned.

Reformed

Without celestial navigation they are lost as well as battered. Luke marks this as the low point before God’s word arrives.

Acts 27:21

21When they had been long without food, Paul stood up in the middle of them and said, “Sirs, you should have listened to me, and not have set sail from Crete and have gotten this injury and loss.

Arminian

After long abstinence from food, Paul stands among them and reminds them they should have listened to him and not left Crete.

Reformed

The rebuke is not gloating. It establishes that Paul’s word can be trusted before he delivers God’s promise.

Acts 27:22

22Now I exhort you to cheer up, for there will be no loss of life among you, but only of the ship.

Arminian

He urges them to take heart, for no life will be lost, only the ship. This corrects his own earlier prediction in verse 10.

Reformed

The difference between verse 10 and verse 22 is the difference between Paul’s judgment and God’s revelation.

Acts 27:23

23For there stood by me this night an angel, belonging to the God whose I am and whom I serve,

Arminian

An angel of the God to whom he belongs and whom he serves stood by him that night.

Reformed

Paul introduces God to pagan sailors through his own relationship: belonging comes before serving.

Acts 27:24

24saying, ‘Don’t be afraid, Paul. You must stand before Caesar. Behold, God has granted you all those who sail with you.’

Arminian

The angel said, “Don’t be afraid, Paul. You must stand before Caesar. God has granted you all who sail with you.” Arminian interpreters often read “granted” as God answering Paul’s intercession, so the lives of others were given in response to prayer.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters see the “must” of 23:11 reaffirmed in the middle of the storm. God’s purpose for Paul guarantees the lives of everyone aboard.

Acts 27:25

25Therefore, sirs, cheer up! For I believe God, that it will be just as it has been spoken to me.

Arminian

“So take heart, men, for I believe God that it will be just as I have been told.” Faith here is trust in a specific promise.

Reformed

Paul’s confidence rests entirely on God’s word, not on any sign of improving weather.

Acts 27:26

26But we must run aground on a certain island.”

Arminian

“But we must run aground on some island.” The promise includes the wreck, not an escape from it.

Reformed

God’s plan includes loss of the ship and the hardship of the landing. Deliverance comes through the disaster, not around it.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
