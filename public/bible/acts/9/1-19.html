Passage

Acts 9

Verses 1–19 · WEB

Saul, still breathing threats against the disciples, obtains letters to arrest followers of the Way in Damascus. Near the city a light from heaven flashes around him, and he hears Jesus ask why Saul is persecuting him. Blinded, Saul is led into Damascus and fasts for three days. The Lord sends a reluctant Ananias to him, calling Saul a chosen vessel to carry his name to Gentiles, kings, and Israel. Ananias lays hands on him, Saul regains his sight, is filled with the Spirit, and is baptized.

Acts 9:1

1But Saul, still breathing threats and slaughter against the disciples of the Lord, went to the high priest

Arminian

Saul is still breathing threats and murder against the disciples. Arminian readers see a man fully committed to his course.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a sinner in open rebellion, which makes the coming grace all the more striking.

Acts 9:2

2and asked for letters from him to the synagogues of Damascus, that if he found any who were of the Way, whether men or women, he might bring them bound to Jerusalem.

Arminian

He requests letters to arrest any belonging to the Way. Arminian commentators note the early name for the faith, the Way.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see Saul’s zeal directed against Christ himself.

Acts 9:3

3As he traveled, he got close to Damascus, and suddenly a light from the sky shone around him.

Arminian

Near Damascus a light from heaven suddenly flashes around him. Arminian readers see God interrupting Saul’s path unexpectedly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see sovereign grace taking the initiative without any request from Saul.

Acts 9:4

4He fell on the earth, and heard a voice saying to him, “Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?”

Arminian

He falls and hears, “Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?” Arminian expositors see Jesus addressing Saul personally and calling him to account.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see Christ’s union with his church: to persecute believers is to persecute him.

Acts 9:5

5He said, “Who are you, Lord?” The Lord said, “I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.

Arminian

“Who are you, Lord?” “I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.” Arminian readers see Saul confronted with the truth he had resisted.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the moment of revelation that overturns Saul’s understanding.

Acts 9:6

6But rise up and enter into the city, then you will be told what you must do.”

Arminian

“Rise up and enter into the city, then you will be told what you must do.” Arminian commentators see Saul called to obey and to respond in action.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see Christ directing the next steps of the one he has seized.

Acts 9:7

7The men who traveled with him stood speechless, hearing the sound, but seeing no one.

Arminian

His companions hear the sound but see no one. Arminian readers note that the call is personal to Saul.

Reformed

Reformed readers see an effectual call aimed at one man, while others nearby are not converted.

Acts 9:8

8Saul arose from the ground, and when his eyes were opened, he saw no one. They led him by the hand and brought him into Damascus.

Arminian

Saul rises but sees nothing and is led by the hand. Arminian expositors see the proud persecutor humbled.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see a picture of spiritual blindness before illumination.

Acts 9:9

9He was without sight for three days, and neither ate nor drank.

Arminian

For three days he neither sees, eats, nor drinks. Arminian readers see a time of repentance and reflection.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the inward work of conviction preparing him for what follows.

Acts 9:10

10Now there was a certain disciple at Damascus named Ananias. The Lord said to him in a vision, “Ananias!” He said, “Behold, it’s me, Lord.”

Arminian

The Lord speaks to Ananias in a vision, and he answers, “Here I am.” Arminian commentators see a willing disciple.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God using another believer in Saul’s conversion.

Acts 9:11

11The Lord said to him, “Arise and go to the street which is called Straight, and inquire in the house of Judah for one named Saul, a man of Tarsus. For behold, he is praying,

Arminian

Go to Straight Street and ask for Saul of Tarsus, for he is praying. Arminian readers see prayer as evidence of Saul’s new direction.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that the persecutor now prays because God has changed him.

Acts 9:12

12and in a vision he has seen a man named Ananias coming in and laying his hands on him, that he might receive his sight.”

Arminian

Saul has seen Ananias in a vision coming to restore his sight. Arminian expositors see God preparing both men.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God arranging every detail of the encounter.

Acts 9:13

13But Ananias answered, “Lord, I have heard from many about this man, how much evil he did to your saints at Jerusalem.

Arminian

Ananias protests that Saul has done much harm to the saints. Arminian readers see honest fear expressed to God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see how unlikely Saul’s conversion appeared from a human view.

Acts 9:14

14Here he has authority from the chief priests to bind all who call on your name.”

Arminian

Saul has authority to arrest all who call on Jesus’ name. Arminian commentators note the danger Ananias faces.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see that God’s choice overrides every human calculation.

Acts 9:15

15But the Lord said to him, “Go your way, for he is my chosen vessel to bear my name before the nations and kings, and the children of Israel.

Arminian

“He is my chosen vessel to bear my name before the nations, kings, and the children of Israel.” Arminian readers usually take this as election to service and mission, an appointment that Saul then freely embraced.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Saul as a clear example of sovereign election, chosen and called while still an enemy, as he later says of himself in Galatians 1:15–16.

Acts 9:16

16For I will show him how many things he must suffer for my name’s sake.”

Arminian

“I will show him how many things he must suffer for my name’s sake.” Arminian expositors see a calling that will demand costly faithfulness.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see suffering appointed as part of God’s plan for his servant.

Acts 9:17

17Ananias departed and entered into the house. Laying his hands on him, he said, “Brother Saul, the Lord, who appeared to you on the road by which you came, has sent me that you may receive your sight and be filled with the Holy Spirit.”

Arminian

Ananias lays hands on him, calls him “Brother Saul,” and says the Lord sent him so he may see and be filled with the Spirit. Arminian readers see grace extended through reconciliation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the persecutor welcomed as family through God’s work.

Acts 9:18

18Immediately something like scales fell from his eyes, and he received his sight. He arose and was baptized.

Arminian

Something like scales falls from his eyes; he sees again and is baptized. Arminian commentators see his baptism as his public response to Christ.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see sight restored as a picture of spiritual illumination, followed by the sign of union with Christ.

Acts 9:19

19He took food and was strengthened. Saul stayed several days with the disciples who were at Damascus.

Arminian

He eats, regains strength, and stays with the disciples. Arminian readers see the new believer joined to the community he once attacked.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the converted persecutor received into the church.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
