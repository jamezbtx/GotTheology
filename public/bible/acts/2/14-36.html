Passage

Acts 2

Verses 14–36 · WEB

Peter stands with the eleven and explains that the believers are not drunk; this is the outpouring of the Spirit promised through Joel, and whoever calls on the Lord’s name will be saved. He proclaims Jesus of Nazareth, approved by God through miracles, delivered up by God’s set purpose and foreknowledge and crucified by lawless men, but raised by God because death could not hold him. Citing Psalms 16 and 110, Peter argues that David foresaw the resurrection and exaltation of the Christ, and he concludes that God has made the crucified Jesus both Lord and Christ.

Acts 2:14

14But Peter, standing up with the eleven, lifted up his voice and spoke out to them, “You men of Judea and all you who dwell at Jerusalem, let this be known to you, and listen to my words.

Arminian

Peter, who denied Jesus weeks earlier, now speaks boldly. Arminian readers see a restored disciple whose renewed faithfulness shows God’s grace working with human response.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the transformation of Peter as the Spirit’s work, fulfilling Christ’s prayer that his faith would not fail (Luke 22:32).

Acts 2:15

15For these aren’t drunken, as you suppose, seeing it is only the third hour of the day.

Arminian

It is only nine in the morning, too early for drunkenness. Arminian commentators appreciate Peter’s plain reasoning.

Reformed

Reformed commentators note that Peter answers objections before proclaiming Christ.

Acts 2:16

16But this is what has been spoken through the prophet Joel:

Arminian

This is what Joel prophesied. Arminian expositors see the fulfillment of God’s promise to his people.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see Scripture interpreting events, as the apostles read history through the prophets.

Acts 2:17

17‘It will be in the last days, says God, that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh. Your sons and your daughters will prophesy. Your young men will see visions. Your old men will dream dreams.

Arminian

God will pour out his Spirit on all flesh in the last days. Arminian readers see “all flesh” as a sign of the gift’s universal scope, available to anyone who believes.

Reformed

Reformed readers usually take “all flesh” to mean all kinds of people, young and old, men and women, Jew and Gentile, rather than every individual.

Acts 2:18

18Yes, and on my servants and on my handmaidens in those days, I will pour out my Spirit, and they will prophesy.

Arminian

Even servants, men and women, will prophesy. Arminian commentators see social barriers crossed by the Spirit.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the whole people of God equipped to speak his word, not only an elite class.

Acts 2:19

19I will show wonders in the sky above, and signs on the earth beneath: blood, and fire, and billows of smoke.

Arminian

Wonders in the sky and signs on the earth will appear. Arminian interpreters connect these signs to the events surrounding the cross and to the end of the age.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters see the cosmic language of the prophets describing God’s decisive intervention in history.

Acts 2:20

20The sun will be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and glorious day of the Lord comes.

Arminian

The sun will be darkened and the moon turned to blood before the day of the Lord. Arminian readers see the urgency of responding before the day comes.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the last days as already begun at Pentecost and moving toward the final day.

Acts 2:21

21It will be that whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved.’

Arminian

“Whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved.” Arminian readers see an open invitation extended to every hearer, so anyone may call and be saved.

Reformed

Reformed readers affirm the promise without reservation and add that calling on the Lord is the fruit of God’s prior work in the heart, as verse 39 will say.

Acts 2:22

22“Men of Israel, hear these words! Jesus of Nazareth, a man approved by God to you by mighty works and wonders and signs which God did by him among you, even as you yourselves know,

Arminian

Jesus of Nazareth was attested by God through mighty works, wonders, and signs. Arminian commentators note that Peter appeals to what his hearers already know.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God himself working through Jesus, confirming his identity and mission.

Acts 2:23

23him, being delivered up by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God, you have taken by the hand of lawless men, crucified and killed;

Arminian

Jesus was delivered up “by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God,” yet lawless men crucified him. Arminian interpreters read God’s counsel as his plan to provide salvation through the cross, formed with full foreknowledge of what free agents would do. The hearers remain fully responsible.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters see the cross as decreed by God, with foreknowledge grounded in his determined purpose. They see here a clear case where divine sovereignty and human guilt stand together without either cancelling the other.

Acts 2:24

24whom God raised up, having freed him from the agony of death, because it was not possible that he should be held by it.

Arminian

God raised him up because death could not hold him. Arminian readers see God reversing the verdict of human courts.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the resurrection as the Father’s vindication of the Son and the necessary outcome of who Jesus is.

Acts 2:25

25For David says concerning him, ‘I saw the Lord always before my face, for he is on my right hand, that I should not be moved.

Arminian

David spoke of seeing the Lord always before him. Arminian expositors see David’s trust in God now fulfilled in Christ.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see Psalm 16 as a messianic psalm that speaks through David about the Christ.

Acts 2:26

26Therefore my heart was glad, and my tongue rejoiced. Moreover my flesh also will dwell in hope,

Arminian

David’s heart was glad and his flesh rested in hope. Arminian readers see the confidence of one who trusts God through death.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the hope of resurrection already present in the Old Testament.

Acts 2:27

27because you will not leave my soul in Hades, neither will you allow your Holy One to see decay.

Arminian

God would not let his Holy One see decay. Arminian commentators note that David’s words could not apply fully to himself.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see a prophecy fulfilled precisely in Jesus’ resurrection.

Acts 2:28

28You made known to me the ways of life. You will make me full of gladness with your presence.’

Arminian

God made known the paths of life. Arminian readers see joy in God’s presence as the end of the psalm and the end of salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the risen Christ entering the fullness of joy in the Father’s presence.

Acts 2:29

29“Brothers, I may tell you freely of the patriarch David, that he both died and was buried, and his tomb is with us to this day.

Arminian

David died and was buried, and his tomb is still there. Arminian expositors note that the evidence is public.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see Peter’s argument: David’s body did decay, so the psalm must speak of someone else.

Acts 2:30

30Therefore, being a prophet, and knowing that God had sworn with an oath to him that of the fruit of his body, according to the flesh, he would raise up the Christ to sit on his throne,

Arminian

David was a prophet who knew God had sworn to seat one of his descendants on his throne. Arminian readers see God keeping a covenant promise across centuries.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the Davidic covenant fulfilled in Christ, central to the unfolding covenant of grace.

Acts 2:31

31he foreseeing this, spoke about the resurrection of the Christ, that his soul wasn’t left in Hades, and his flesh didn’t see decay.

Arminian

David foresaw and spoke of Christ’s resurrection. Arminian commentators see prophecy reaching its intended subject.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the unity of Scripture, in which the Old Testament points to Christ.

Acts 2:32

32This Jesus God raised up, to which we all are witnesses.

Arminian

God raised Jesus, and all the apostles are witnesses. Arminian readers see personal testimony at the center of the message.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the resurrection as a public fact attested by God’s chosen witnesses.

Acts 2:33

33Being therefore exalted by the right hand of God, and having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, he has poured out this which you now see and hear.

Arminian

Exalted at God’s right hand, Jesus received the promised Spirit and poured him out. Arminian expositors see the Spirit’s coming as proof of Christ’s exaltation.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the Trinity at work: the Father gives the Spirit through the exalted Son.

Acts 2:34

34For David didn’t ascend into the heavens, but he says himself, ‘The Lord said to my Lord, “Sit by my right hand

Arminian

David did not ascend to heaven but wrote, “The Lord said to my Lord.” Arminian readers see Peter using Psalm 110 as Jesus himself did.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the psalm as a clear testimony to Christ’s divine lordship.

Acts 2:35

35until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet.”’

Arminian

“Until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet.” Arminian commentators see Christ’s reign continuing until every enemy is defeated.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see Christ’s present rule as certain to bring every enemy into subjection.

Acts 2:36

36“Let all the house of Israel therefore know certainly that God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified.”

Arminian

God has made the crucified Jesus both Lord and Christ. Arminian readers see the climax of the sermon pressing the hearers toward a decision.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s verdict overturning the world’s, and they stress that the hearers’ guilt is named plainly before grace is offered.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
