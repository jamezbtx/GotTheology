Passage

Acts 2

Verses 1–13 · WEB

On the day of Pentecost the believers are gathered when a sound like a rushing wind fills the house and tongues like fire rest on each of them. Filled with the Holy Spirit, they speak in other languages, and Jews from every nation living in Jerusalem hear the mighty works of God in their own languages. Some are amazed and ask what it means, while others mock and say the disciples are drunk.

Acts 2:1

1Now when the day of Pentecost had come, they were all with one accord in one place.

Arminian

The believers are together in one place on Pentecost, the harvest feast. Arminian readers see their unity as preparation for the gift.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the harvest feast as fitting for the beginning of the great ingathering of God’s people.

Acts 2:2

2Suddenly there came from the sky a sound like the rushing of a mighty wind, and it filled all the house where they were sitting.

Arminian

A sound like a mighty wind fills the house. Arminian commentators recall that the Hebrew and Greek words for spirit also mean wind or breath.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the life-giving breath of God, as in Ezekiel 37, now bringing life to the church.

Acts 2:3

3Tongues like fire appeared and were distributed to them, and one sat on each of them.

Arminian

Tongues like fire rest on each one. Arminian expositors stress that every believer, not only the apostles, receives the Spirit.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see fire as a sign of God’s presence, as at Sinai and the burning bush, now resting on his people.

Acts 2:4

4They were all filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak with other languages, as the Spirit gave them the ability to speak.

Arminian

All are filled with the Spirit and speak in other languages as the Spirit gives them utterance. Pentecostal and many Holiness Arminians see this as a pattern of Spirit filling that believers may still expect.

Reformed

Reformed readers generally see the tongues at Pentecost as known languages that signaled the gospel’s reach to all nations, and many hold that this sign belonged to the apostolic foundation.

Acts 2:5

5Now there were dwelling in Jerusalem Jews, devout men, from every nation under the sky.

Arminian

Devout Jews from every nation are staying in Jerusalem. Arminian readers see God gathering a worldwide audience for the first proclamation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s providence arranging the crowd so the gospel would spread back to the nations.

Acts 2:6

6When this sound was heard, the multitude came together and were bewildered, because everyone heard them speaking in his own language.

Arminian

Each person hears in his own language. Arminian interpreters see God meeting people where they are.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters see the reversal of Babel, as God begins to gather the scattered nations into one people.

Acts 2:7

7They were all amazed and marveled, saying to one another, “Behold, aren’t all these who speak Galileans?

Arminian

They are amazed that Galileans are speaking. Arminian commentators note the surprise that ordinary men carry such a message.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God choosing what the world considers lowly, as 1 Corinthians 1:27 says.

Acts 2:8

8How do we hear, everyone in our own native language?

Arminian

“How do we hear, everyone in our own native language?” Arminian readers see a sign meant to awaken questions and lead to faith.

Reformed

Reformed readers see a miracle that points beyond itself to the message it carries.

Acts 2:9

9Parthians, Medes, Elamites, and people from Mesopotamia, Judea, Cappadocia, Pontus, Asia,

Arminian

Luke begins listing the nations: Parthians, Medes, Elamites, and others. Arminian expositors see the breadth of the gospel’s intended reach.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see a preview of the redeemed from every tribe and tongue in Revelation 7:9.

Acts 2:10

10Phrygia, Pamphylia, Egypt, the parts of Libya around Cyrene, visitors from Rome, both Jews and proselytes,

Arminian

The list continues through Egypt, Libya, and Rome, including both Jews and proselytes. Arminian readers note that converts to Judaism are already included.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the inclusion of proselytes as a sign of Gentiles drawing near to the covenant.

Acts 2:11

11Cretans and Arabians—we hear them speaking in our languages the mighty works of God!”

Arminian

They hear the mighty works of God declared. Arminian commentators see praise as the first content of the Spirit-filled speech.

Reformed

Reformed commentators note that the message centers on what God has done, not on human experience.

Acts 2:12

12They were all amazed and were perplexed, saying to one another, “What does this mean?”

Arminian

Everyone asks, “What does this mean?” Arminian readers see hearts opened to inquiry, ready for the explanation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that miracles alone do not produce faith. The sign requires the preached word to explain it.

Acts 2:13

13Others, mocking, said, “They are filled with new wine.”

Arminian

Others mock and claim the disciples are drunk. Arminian expositors see that people can witness the same event and respond in opposite ways.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the division that the gospel always produces between those who receive it and those who scoff.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
