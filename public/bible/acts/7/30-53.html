Passage

Acts 7

Verses 30–53 · WEB

Forty years later God speaks to Moses from a burning bush in the wilderness, calls the ground holy, and sends him to deliver Israel. The same Moses whom the people rejected became their ruler and deliverer, led them out with signs, received living words at Sinai, and foretold a prophet like himself. Yet the fathers refused him, turned to the golden calf, and later worshiped the host of heaven. Israel had the tabernacle and later Solomon’s temple, but the Most High does not dwell in houses made by hands. Stephen ends by charging his hearers with resisting the Holy Spirit as their fathers did, betraying and murdering the Righteous One, and failing to keep the law they received.

Acts 7:30

30“When forty years were fulfilled, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in the wilderness of Mount Sinai, in a flame of fire in a bush.

Arminian

An angel appeared to Moses in a burning bush near Sinai. Arminian readers note again that God revealed himself outside the land and temple.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s holy presence in the wilderness, independent of any building.

Acts 7:31

31When Moses saw it, he wondered at the sight. As he came close to see, the voice of the Lord came to him,

Arminian

Moses drew near and heard the Lord’s voice. Arminian commentators see curiosity leading to an encounter with God.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God speaking first, drawing Moses to himself.

Acts 7:32

32‘I am the God of your fathers: the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob.’ Moses trembled and dared not look.

Arminian

“I am the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.” Arminian readers see God identifying himself by covenant relationships.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the covenant God remaining faithful from generation to generation.

Acts 7:33

33The Lord said to him, ‘Take off your sandals, for the place where you stand is holy ground.

Arminian

The ground is holy, and Moses must remove his sandals. Arminian expositors note that holiness depends on God’s presence, not location.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see Stephen answering the charge against the temple: God makes any place holy.

Acts 7:34

34I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt, and have heard their groaning. I have come down to deliver them. Now come, I will send you into Egypt.’

Arminian

God has seen the suffering of his people and will send Moses to rescue them. Arminian readers see God’s compassion moving him to act.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God remembering his covenant and sending a deliverer.

Acts 7:35

35“This Moses whom they refused, saying, ‘Who made you a ruler and a judge?’—God has sent him as both a ruler and a deliverer by the hand of the angel who appeared to him in the bush.

Arminian

God sent as ruler and redeemer the same Moses whom they rejected. Arminian commentators see Stephen drawing the parallel with Jesus clearly.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God vindicating the rejected one as redeemer.

Acts 7:36

36This man led them out, having worked wonders and signs in Egypt, in the Red Sea, and in the wilderness for forty years.

Arminian

Moses led them out with wonders in Egypt, at the Red Sea, and in the wilderness. Arminian readers see God confirming his servant with signs.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the exodus as the great type of Christ’s redemption.

Acts 7:37

37This is that Moses who said to the children of Israel, ‘The Lord our God will raise up a prophet for you from among your brothers, like me.’

Arminian

Moses foretold a prophet like himself. Arminian expositors see Stephen naming Jesus as that prophet.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see Moses himself pointing beyond the law to Christ.

Acts 7:38

38This is he who was in the assembly in the wilderness with the angel that spoke to him on Mount Sinai, and with our fathers, who received living revelations to give to us,

Arminian

Moses was with the assembly in the wilderness and received living words. Arminian readers note the law is called living words, not a dead letter.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law as God’s good gift, which Stephen does not despise.

Acts 7:39

39to whom our fathers wouldn’t be obedient, but rejected him and turned back in their hearts to Egypt,

Arminian

The fathers refused to obey and turned back to Egypt in their hearts. Arminian commentators see a free choice to reject God’s leader.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the persistent unbelief of the human heart even among the redeemed nation.

Acts 7:40

40saying to Aaron, ‘Make us gods that will go before us, for as for this Moses who led us out of the land of Egypt, we don’t know what has become of him.’

Arminian

They asked Aaron to make gods to go before them. Arminian readers see impatience turning to idolatry.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the root of idolatry in a heart that wants a god it can control.

Acts 7:41

41They made a calf in those days, and brought a sacrifice to the idol, and rejoiced in the works of their hands.

Arminian

They made a calf and rejoiced in the work of their hands. Arminian expositors note the phrase “works of their hands,” which Stephen will use again of the temple.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the folly of worshiping what humans make.

Acts 7:42

42But God turned away and gave them up to serve the army of the sky, as it is written in the book of the prophets, ‘Did you offer to me slain animals and sacrifices forty years in the wilderness, O house of Israel?

Arminian

God gave them over to worship the host of heaven, as Amos wrote. Arminian readers see God allowing people to follow the course they insisted on.

Reformed

Reformed readers see judicial hardening, as in Romans 1:24, where God gives people over to their desires.

Acts 7:43

43You took up the tabernacle of Moloch, the star of your god Rephan, the figures which you made to worship, so I will carry you away beyond Babylon.’

Arminian

They carried the shrine of Moloch and the star of Rephan, and God would exile them beyond Babylon. Arminian commentators see idolatry leading to judgment.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the exile as God’s just response to persistent covenant unfaithfulness.

Acts 7:44

44“Our fathers had the tabernacle of the testimony in the wilderness, even as he who spoke to Moses commanded him to make it according to the pattern that he had seen;

Arminian

The fathers had the tabernacle of testimony, made as God directed Moses. Arminian readers see the tabernacle as a portable sign of God’s presence.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the tabernacle pointing ahead to God dwelling with his people in Christ.

Acts 7:45

45which also our fathers, in their turn, brought in with Joshua when they entered into the possession of the nations whom God drove out before the face of our fathers to the days of David,

Arminian

They brought it in with Joshua until the days of David. Arminian expositors note the tabernacle moved with the people.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God going with his people rather than being confined to one place.

Acts 7:46

46who found favor in the sight of God, and asked to find a habitation for the God of Jacob.

Arminian

David found favor and asked to provide a dwelling for God. Arminian readers see David’s desire as sincere devotion.

Reformed

Reformed readers see David’s request answered by God’s promise to build David a house.

Acts 7:47

47But Solomon built him a house.

Arminian

But Solomon built him a house. Arminian commentators see Stephen’s brief phrasing as a subtle contrast with the tabernacle.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the temple as legitimate but temporary.

Acts 7:48

48However, the Most High doesn’t dwell in temples made with hands, as the prophet says,

Arminian

The Most High does not dwell in houses made with hands. Arminian readers see Stephen answering the charge that he spoke against the temple.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s transcendence, which no building can contain.

Acts 7:49

49‘heaven is my throne, and the earth a footstool for my feet. What kind of house will you build me?’ says the Lord. ‘Or what is the place of my rest?

Arminian

Heaven is his throne and earth his footstool. Arminian expositors see Isaiah 66 confirming Stephen’s point.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God’s majesty filling all creation.

Acts 7:50

50Didn’t my hand make all these things?’

Arminian

“Didn’t my hand make all these things?” Arminian readers see the Creator who cannot be confined by what he made.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereignty as Creator over every place and structure.

Acts 7:51

51“You stiff-necked and uncircumcised in heart and ears, you always resist the Holy Spirit! As your fathers did, so you do.

Arminian

“You always resist the Holy Spirit; as your fathers did, so do you.” Arminian readers see this as clear evidence that the Spirit’s gracious work can be resisted to the end. The hearers are not passive; they have refused God’s drawing again and again.

Reformed

Reformed readers distinguish the outward call of the Spirit through prophets and preaching, which sinners always resist, from the Spirit’s inward regenerating work, which brings the elect to faith. They see the verse describing resistance to the Spirit speaking through his messengers.

Acts 7:52

52Which of the prophets didn’t your fathers persecute? They killed those who foretold the coming of the Righteous One, of whom you have now become betrayers and murderers.

Arminian

Their fathers persecuted the prophets and they betrayed and murdered the Righteous One. Arminian commentators see a pattern of choices repeated across generations.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the hostility of the unregenerate heart to God’s messengers throughout history.

Acts 7:53

53You received the law as it was ordained by angels, and didn’t keep it!”

Arminian

They received the law delivered by angels and did not keep it. Arminian readers see Stephen turning the charge of lawbreaking back on his accusers.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the law exposing sin, which drives people to need a Savior.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
