Passage

Acts 17

Verses 16–34 · WEB

Waiting in Athens, Paul is distressed by the city full of idols and reasons in the synagogue and the marketplace. Epicurean and Stoic philosophers bring him to the Areopagus to explain his new teaching. Paul begins from their altar to an unknown god and proclaims the Creator who does not live in temples, gives life to all, made every nation from one man, and set their times and boundaries so that they would seek him. God overlooked the times of ignorance but now commands all people everywhere to repent, because he has fixed a day to judge the world by the man he raised from the dead. Some mock, some want to hear more, and a few believe.

Acts 17:16

16Now while Paul waited for them at Athens, his spirit was provoked within him as he saw the city full of idols.

Arminian

Paul’s spirit is provoked by the city full of idols. Arminian readers see grief over people lost in error.

Reformed

Reformed readers see zeal for God’s glory, which idols steal.

Acts 17:17

17So he reasoned in the synagogue with the Jews and the devout persons, and in the marketplace every day with those who met him.

Arminian

He reasons in the synagogue and the marketplace daily. Arminian commentators see Paul meeting people where they are.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the gospel brought to both religious and secular audiences.

Acts 17:18

18Some of the Epicurean and Stoic philosophers also were conversing with him. Some said, “What does this babbler want to say?” Others said, “He seems to be advocating foreign deities,” because he preached Jesus and the resurrection.

Arminian

Epicurean and Stoic philosophers call him a babbler who proclaims foreign gods. Arminian readers see misunderstanding of “Jesus and the resurrection.”

Reformed

Reformed readers see worldly wisdom unable to grasp the gospel, as 1 Corinthians 1 says.

Acts 17:19

19They took hold of him and brought him to the Areopagus, saying, “May we know what this new teaching is, which you are speaking about?

Arminian

They bring him to the Areopagus to explain his teaching. Arminian expositors see an open forum.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God giving Paul a hearing before the city’s thinkers.

Acts 17:20

20For you bring certain strange things to our ears. We want to know therefore what these things mean.”

Arminian

They want to know what these strange things mean. Arminian readers see curiosity as a starting point.

Reformed

Reformed readers see interest that falls short of faith.

Acts 17:21

21Now all the Athenians and the strangers living there spent their time in nothing else, but either to tell or to hear some new thing.

Arminian

Athenians spend their time hearing new ideas. Arminian commentators see a culture fascinated with novelty.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see restless minds that need truth, not novelty.

Acts 17:22

22Paul stood in the middle of the Areopagus and said, “You men of Athens, I perceive that you are very religious in all things.

Arminian

Paul says he sees they are very religious. Arminian readers see Paul finding common ground.

Reformed

Reformed readers see recognition of the human religious impulse, which goes astray without revelation.

Acts 17:23

23For as I passed along and observed the objects of your worship, I also found an altar with this inscription: ‘TO AN UNKNOWN GOD.’ What therefore you worship in ignorance, I announce to you.

Arminian

He found an altar to an unknown god and proclaims that God to them. Arminian expositors see Paul building on their search.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see Paul exposing their ignorance while offering the knowledge of God.

Acts 17:24

24The God who made the world and all things in it, he, being Lord of heaven and earth, doesn’t dwell in temples made with hands.

Arminian

God made the world and does not live in temples made by hands. Arminian readers see God as Creator and Lord of all.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s independence from all creation.

Acts 17:25

25He isn’t served by men’s hands, as though he needed anything, seeing he himself gives to all life and breath and all things.

Arminian

He is not served by human hands as if he needed anything; he gives life and breath to all. Arminian commentators see God’s generosity to every person.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God’s self-sufficiency and providence.

Acts 17:26

26He made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the surface of the earth, having determined appointed seasons and the boundaries of their dwellings,

Arminian

From one man he made every nation and determined their times and boundaries. Arminian readers see the unity of humanity and God’s care for all nations.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God’s sovereign ordering of history and peoples.

Acts 17:27

27that they should seek the Lord, if perhaps they might reach out for him and find him, though he is not far from each one of us.

Arminian

This was so they would seek God and perhaps reach out and find him, though he is not far from any of us. Arminian readers see God’s intention that all people seek him, and his nearness as an invitation.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the purpose of God’s providence and note the word “perhaps”: humans left to themselves grope without finding him, which is why revelation is needed.

Acts 17:28

28‘For in him we live, move, and have our being.’ As some of your own poets have said, ‘For we are also his offspring.’

Arminian

In him we live and move and have our being, and as the poets said, we are his offspring. Arminian expositors see Paul quoting Greek poets to show common ground.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see all people as God’s creatures, sustained by him, though not all are his children by adoption.

Acts 17:29

29Being then the offspring of God, we ought not to think that the Divine Nature is like gold, or silver, or stone, engraved by art and design of man.

Arminian

Since we are God’s offspring, we should not think God is like gold or stone. Arminian readers see idolatry as unworthy of humans made by God.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the folly of worshiping what is lower than ourselves.

Acts 17:30

30The times of ignorance therefore God overlooked. But now he commands that all people everywhere should repent,

Arminian

God overlooked the times of ignorance but now commands all people everywhere to repent. Arminian readers see a universal command that implies a universal opportunity, since God does not command what people have no way to obey by grace.

Reformed

Reformed readers agree the command goes to every person and holds all responsible, while holding that the ability to obey comes from God’s regenerating grace, so the command reveals duty rather than natural ability.

Acts 17:31

31because he has appointed a day in which he will judge the world in righteousness by the man whom he has ordained; of which he has given assurance to all men, in that he has raised him from the dead.”

Arminian

He has appointed a day to judge the world by a man he raised from the dead. Arminian expositors see the coming judgment as the reason to repent now.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the resurrection as God’s public proof of Christ’s authority to judge.

Acts 17:32

32Now when they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some mocked; but others said, “We want to hear you again concerning this.”

Arminian

At the mention of resurrection, some mock and others want to hear more. Arminian readers see different responses to the same message.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the resurrection as the stumbling block for Greek thought.

Acts 17:33

33Thus Paul went out from among them.

Arminian

Paul leaves them. Arminian commentators see the conversation ending without coercion.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the faithful messenger leaving the results to God.

Acts 17:34

34But certain men joined with him and believed, including Dionysius the Areopagite, and a woman named Damaris, and others with them.

Arminian

Some believe, including Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman named Damaris. Arminian readers see individuals responding even in a skeptical city.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God calling a few from among the learned.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
