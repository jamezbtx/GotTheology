Passage

Acts 22

Verses 1–21 · WEB

Speaking in Hebrew from the barracks steps, Paul describes his upbringing under Gamaliel and his zeal in persecuting the Way. He recounts his encounter with the risen Jesus on the road to Damascus, his blindness, and Ananias’s message that the God of their fathers had appointed him to know his will, see the Righteous One, and be his witness to all people. Ananias urged him to be baptized and wash away his sins, calling on the Lord’s name. Later, while praying in the temple, Paul saw the Lord telling him to leave Jerusalem because he would send him far away to the Gentiles.

Acts 22:1

1“Brothers and fathers, listen to the defense which I now make to you.”

Arminian

Paul opens with “Brothers and fathers,” the same address Stephen used before the council (7:2). He speaks to the crowd as kin, not enemies.

Reformed

Calling his speech a “defense” (apologia), Paul begins the first of several formal defenses that fill the rest of Acts.

Acts 22:2

2When they heard that he spoke to them in the Hebrew language, they were even more quiet. He said,

Arminian

The crowd grows even quieter when they hear him speak in their own language. Choosing Aramaic over Greek signals that he is one of them.

Reformed

A small linguistic choice earns a hearing that argument alone could not.

Acts 22:3

3“I am indeed a Jew, born in Tarsus of Cilicia, but brought up in this city at the feet of Gamaliel, instructed according to the strict tradition of the law of our fathers, being zealous for God, even as you all are today.

Arminian

Paul lists his credentials: born in Tarsus, raised in Jerusalem, trained strictly under Gamaliel, and zealous for God “as you all are today.” He credits their zeal before showing where his own went wrong.

Reformed

Paul later describes this zeal as one “not according to knowledge” (Romans 10:2). Reformed writers see in it the limit of religious effort without grace.

Acts 22:4

4I persecuted this Way to the death, binding and delivering into prisons both men and women,

Arminian

He persecuted “this Way” to the death, binding and imprisoning men and women. He hides nothing of his past.

Reformed

Paul calls himself the chief of sinners for this (1 Timothy 1:15). The worse his record, the more clearly his conversion shows mercy.

Acts 22:5

5as also the high priest and all the council of the elders testify, from whom also I received letters to the brothers, and traveled to Damascus to bring them also who were there to Jerusalem in bonds to be punished.

Arminian

The high priest and the council of elders can confirm that he carried their letters to Damascus to bring believers back in chains.

Reformed

He appeals to the very authorities now hostile to him as witnesses of his past. His testimony can be checked.

Acts 22:6

6“As I made my journey and came close to Damascus, about noon suddenly a great light shone around me from the sky.

Arminian

Near Damascus, about noon, a great light from heaven suddenly shines around him. This is the first of two retellings of chapter 9, each shaped for its audience.

Reformed

The midday detail matters: this light outshone the sun at its brightest, as 26:13 will say.

Acts 22:7

7I fell to the ground and heard a voice saying to me, ‘Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?’

Arminian

He falls and hears, “Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?” The double name is the way God called Abraham, Jacob, Moses, and Samuel.

Reformed

The question identifies Jesus with his followers. To harm the church is to strike its Lord.

Acts 22:8

8I answered, ‘Who are you, Lord?’ He said to me, ‘I am Jesus of Nazareth, whom you persecute.’

Arminian

“Who are you, Lord?” “I am Jesus of Nazareth, whom you persecute.” Only in this telling does Jesus add “of Nazareth,” a phrase that would land hard on this Jerusalem crowd.

Reformed

The name the crowd despises is the name of the risen Lord who spoke from heaven.

Acts 22:9

9“Those who were with me indeed saw the light and were afraid, but they didn’t understand the voice of him who spoke to me.

Arminian

His companions saw the light but did not understand the voice. This harmonizes with 9:7, where they heard a sound but saw no one.

Reformed

The call was meant for Saul. Others witnessed the event without receiving its message.

Acts 22:10

10I said, ‘What shall I do, Lord?’ The Lord said to me, ‘Arise, and go into Damascus. There you will be told about all things which are appointed for you to do.’

Arminian

Paul asks, “What shall I do, Lord?” and is told to go into Damascus, where he will learn all that is appointed for him. Arminian readers note that he is given a next step to take in obedience.

Reformed

The phrase “appointed for you to do” points Reformed readers to a calling already arranged by God before Saul knew it.

Acts 22:11

11When I couldn’t see for the glory of that light, being led by the hand of those who were with me, I came into Damascus.

Arminian

Blinded by the brilliance of the light, he is led by the hand into Damascus. The persecutor who came to drag others in chains is himself led like a child.

Reformed

Paul tells this detail without embarrassment. His helplessness became part of his testimony.

Acts 22:12

12“One Ananias, a devout man according to the law, well reported of by all the Jews who lived in Damascus,

Arminian

Ananias is described as devout according to the law and well spoken of by all the Jews in Damascus, details absent from chapter 9 but suited to this audience.

Reformed

Paul chooses the most Jewish description of his mentor to show that his conversion came through a law-respecting Jew.

Acts 22:13

13came to me, and standing by me said to me, ‘Brother Saul, receive your sight!’ In that very hour I looked up at him.

Arminian

Ananias stands by him and says, “Brother Saul, receive your sight,” and in that moment he sees. The first word Saul hears from a believer is “brother.”

Reformed

Sight returns at the word spoken in Christ’s name, a sign of the spiritual sight now given.

Acts 22:14

14He said, ‘The God of our fathers has appointed you to know his will, and to see the Righteous One, and to hear a voice from his mouth.

Arminian

Ananias says the God of their fathers has appointed Saul to know his will, to see the Righteous One, and to hear his voice. Arminian interpreters typically read this as appointment to a specific apostolic task.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters see the language of prior appointment and link it with Paul’s own testimony that God set him apart before birth (Galatians 1:15).

Acts 22:15

15For you will be a witness for him to all men of what you have seen and heard.

Arminian

Saul will be a witness to all people of what he has seen and heard. His testimony is grounded in personal encounter.

Reformed

“All people” anticipates the commission to the Gentiles in verse 21, the word that will set the crowd off.

Acts 22:16

16Now why do you wait? Arise, be baptized, and wash away your sins, calling on the name of the Lord.’

Arminian

“Why are you waiting? Get up, be baptized, and wash away your sins, calling on his name.” Arminian readers see the call to act at once; calling on the Lord in faith is what receives forgiveness, and baptism expresses it.

Reformed

Reformed readers take the washing as signified and sealed in baptism and received by faith in calling on Christ. The water does not cleanse by itself; the name of the Lord does.

Acts 22:17

17“When I had returned to Jerusalem and while I prayed in the temple, I fell into a trance

Arminian

Back in Jerusalem, Paul was praying in the temple when he fell into a trance. He tells this crowd that he worshiped in the temple they accuse him of profaning.

Reformed

Paul places a vision of Christ in the temple, quietly answering the charge in 21:28.

Acts 22:18

18and saw him saying to me, ‘Hurry and get out of Jerusalem quickly, because they will not receive testimony concerning me from you.’

Arminian

The Lord told him to leave quickly because Jerusalem would not accept his testimony about him.

Reformed

The warning shows that the rejection now happening was foreseen long ago.

Acts 22:19

19I said, ‘Lord, they themselves know that I imprisoned and beat in every synagogue those who believed in you.

Arminian

Paul objected that the people knew he had imprisoned and beaten believers in every synagogue. He thought his past would make his witness persuasive.

Reformed

Paul assumed his credentials would win his old allies. The Lord knew better.

Acts 22:20

20When the blood of Stephen, your witness, was shed, I also was standing by, consenting to his death, and guarding the cloaks of those who killed him.’

Arminian

He reminds the Lord that he stood by approving when Stephen’s blood was shed, guarding the clothes of the killers. He calls Stephen “your witness,” the word that would later mean martyr.

Reformed

Stephen’s prayer in 7:60 hangs over this confession. The man who held the coats became the next great witness.

Acts 22:21

21“He said to me, ‘Depart, for I will send you out far from here to the Gentiles.’ ”

Arminian

“Depart, for I will send you far away to the Gentiles.” The Lord overrode Paul’s preference for Jerusalem.

Reformed

God directs the mission where he wills. Paul’s apostleship to the Gentiles was not his own strategy but his Lord’s command.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
