Passage

Acts 1

Verses 12–26 · WEB

The apostles return to Jerusalem and devote themselves to prayer with the women, Mary the mother of Jesus, and his brothers. Peter explains that Judas’s betrayal and death fulfilled Scripture, and that another must take his office. Two candidates who had been with Jesus from John’s baptism to the ascension are put forward; the believers pray, cast lots, and Matthias is added to the eleven.

Acts 1:12

12Then they returned to Jerusalem from the mountain called Olivet, which is near Jerusalem, a Sabbath day’s journey away.

Arminian

The apostles walk back from the Mount of Olives to Jerusalem. Arminian readers see them obeying Jesus’ instruction to wait.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the obedience and the sense of expectation in their return.

Acts 1:13

13When they had come in, they went up into the upper room where they were staying, that is Peter, John, James, Andrew, Philip, Thomas, Bartholomew, Matthew, James the son of Alphaeus, Simon the Zealot, and Judas the son of James.

Arminian

Luke lists the eleven in the upper room. Arminian commentators see the same men who fled at the arrest now gathered and restored.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see Christ preserving his chosen apostles despite their earlier failure.

Acts 1:14

14All these with one accord continued steadfastly in prayer and supplication, along with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus, and with his brothers.

Arminian

They devote themselves to prayer together with the women, Mary, and Jesus’ brothers. Arminian expositors see prayer as the church’s first response while it waits for God’s promise.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see prayer as the means God uses to accomplish what he has already promised. They also note that Jesus’ brothers, once unbelieving, are now among the disciples.

Acts 1:15

15In these days, Peter stood up in the middle of the disciples (and the number of names was about one hundred twenty), and said,

Arminian

Peter stands up among about one hundred twenty believers. Arminian readers see the restored Peter taking leadership.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the small company as the core of the new covenant people.

Acts 1:16

16“Brothers, it was necessary that this Scripture should be fulfilled, which the Holy Spirit spoke before by the mouth of David concerning Judas, who was guide to those who took Jesus.

Arminian

The Scripture spoken by the Spirit through David “had to be fulfilled” concerning Judas. Arminian interpreters hold that God foresaw Judas’s betrayal without causing his choice, and Scripture recorded what God knew beforehand.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters see the necessity of fulfillment as evidence that even Judas’s act fell within God’s plan, though Judas remained fully guilty.

Acts 1:17

17For he was counted with us, and received his portion in this ministry.

Arminian

Judas was counted among the apostles and shared in the ministry. Arminian readers see a man with a genuine calling who forfeited it, a warning that privilege does not guarantee perseverance.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that outward membership and ministry are not the same as saving union with Christ, as Jesus’ words in John 6:70 suggest.

Acts 1:18

18Now this man obtained a field with the reward for his wickedness; and falling headlong, his body burst open and all his intestines gushed out.

Arminian

Judas bought a field with his wages of wickedness and died a gruesome death. Arminian commentators see the consequences of a chosen path.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God’s justice reaching the betrayer. They usually harmonize this account with Matthew 27 by noting the priests bought the field with Judas’s money.

Acts 1:19

19It became known to everyone who lived in Jerusalem that in their language that field was called ‘Akeldama,’ that is, ‘The field of blood.’

Arminian

The field became known as Akeldama, the field of blood. Arminian readers note that the event was public knowledge in Jerusalem.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the name serving as a lasting memorial of Judas’s crime.

Acts 1:20

20For it is written in the book of Psalms, ‘Let his habitation be made desolate. Let no one dwell in it;’ and, ‘Let another take his office.’

Arminian

Peter cites Psalms 69 and 109: another must take his office. Arminian expositors see the psalms applied to Judas as a type of the enemies of God’s anointed.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see Scripture directing the church’s action, so that filling the office is obedience to God’s word.

Acts 1:21

21“Of the men therefore who have accompanied us all the time that the Lord Jesus went in and out among us,

Arminian

The replacement must have accompanied Jesus throughout his ministry. Arminian readers see qualifications rooted in firsthand knowledge.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the apostolic office tied to eyewitness testimony, which is why it was not continued in later generations.

Acts 1:22

22beginning from the baptism of John to the day that he was received up from us, of these one must become a witness with us of his resurrection.”

Arminian

He must be a witness of the resurrection from John’s baptism to the ascension. Arminian commentators note that the resurrection is the center of the apostolic message.

Reformed

Reformed commentators stress the unique, foundational role of the apostles as witnesses.

Acts 1:23

23They put forward two: Joseph called Barsabbas, who was also called Justus, and Matthias.

Arminian

Two men are put forward, Joseph Barsabbas and Matthias. Arminian interpreters see the community taking responsibility for discernment.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters see the church acting within limits, presenting candidates while leaving the choice to God.

Acts 1:24

24They prayed and said, “You, Lord, who know the hearts of all men, show which one of these two you have chosen

Arminian

They pray to the Lord who knows every heart, asking him to show which one he has chosen. Arminian readers see the disciples seeking God’s guidance for a decision they cannot make alone.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that the choice is already God’s; the prayer asks him to reveal it.

Acts 1:25

25to take part in this ministry and apostleship from which Judas fell away, that he might go to his own place.”

Arminian

Judas turned aside from this ministry “to go to his own place.” Arminian expositors see his fall as his own departure, a solemn reminder that an apostle could forsake his calling.

Reformed

Reformed expositors read “his own place” as the destiny that matched his character, and they stress that his apostasy did not thwart God’s purpose.

Acts 1:26

26They drew lots for them, and the lot fell on Matthias; and he was counted with the eleven apostles.

Arminian

They cast lots, and the lot fell on Matthias. Arminian readers note that this practice disappears after Pentecost, when the Spirit guides the church directly.

Reformed

Reformed readers see Proverbs 16:33 at work: the lot is cast, but the decision is the Lord’s.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
