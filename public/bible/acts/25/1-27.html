Passage

Acts 25

Verses 1–27 · WEB

The new governor Festus visits Jerusalem, where the chief priests ask him to bring Paul there, planning to ambush him. Festus hears the case in Caesarea, where the accusers bring serious but unproven charges. When Festus, wanting to please the Jews, asks whether Paul will stand trial in Jerusalem, Paul appeals to Caesar, and Festus grants the appeal. King Agrippa and Bernice arrive, and Festus explains that the dispute concerns their religion and a dead man named Jesus whom Paul claims is alive. Agrippa asks to hear Paul, and the next day Festus presents him to the assembled dignitaries, admitting he has nothing definite to write to the emperor.

Acts 25:1

1Festus therefore, having come into the province, after three days went up to Jerusalem from Caesarea.

Arminian

Three days after arriving in his province, Festus goes up from Caesarea to Jerusalem. A new governor would want to meet the Jewish leadership quickly.

Reformed

Festus governed briefly, from about AD 59 to 62, and Josephus remembers him as more just than Felix or his successors.

Acts 25:2

2Then the high priest and the principal men of the Jews informed him against Paul, and they begged him,

Arminian

The high priest and leading men immediately bring their case against Paul. Two years have not cooled their determination.

Reformed

The persistence of the accusers shows that the case has always been about Paul’s message rather than any civil offense.

Acts 25:3

3asking a favor against him, that he would summon him to Jerusalem, plotting to kill him on the way.

Arminian

They ask as a favor that Paul be transferred to Jerusalem, planning to ambush him on the way. The plot of 23:12–15 is revived.

Reformed

They try to use a new governor’s desire for goodwill to accomplish what the earlier conspiracy could not.

Acts 25:4

4However Festus answered that Paul should be kept in custody at Caesarea, and that he himself was about to depart shortly.

Arminian

Festus answers that Paul is being kept at Caesarea and that he himself will return there soon.

Reformed

A simple administrative decision closes the door on the ambush. Luke shows the promise of 23:11 protected again.

Acts 25:5

5“Let them therefore”, he said, “that are in power among you go down with me, and if there is anything wrong in the man, let them accuse him.”

Arminian

“Let your leading men come down with me, and if there is anything wrong in the man, let them accuse him.” Festus insists on a proper hearing.

Reformed

Roman procedure, with its requirement that accusers face the accused, keeps working in Paul’s favor.

Acts 25:6

6When he had stayed among them more than ten days, he went down to Caesarea, and on the next day he sat on the judgment seat, and commanded Paul to be brought.

Arminian

After eight or ten days Festus returns to Caesarea and, the next day, takes his seat on the tribunal and orders Paul brought.

Reformed

The bema, the judge’s seat, signals a formal Roman trial.

Acts 25:7

7When he had come, the Jews who had come down from Jerusalem stood around him, bringing against him many and grievous charges which they could not prove,

Arminian

The Jews from Jerusalem surround Paul and bring many serious charges they cannot prove.

Reformed

Luke does not list the charges; the repeated failure of proof is the point.

Acts 25:8

8while he said in his defense, “Neither against the law of the Jews, nor against the temple, nor against Caesar, have I sinned at all.”

Arminian

Paul answers in one sentence: he has committed no offense against the Jewish law, the temple, or Caesar. The three charges are religious, cultic, and political.

Reformed

Paul’s summary denial covers each realm of authority. Luke presents him as a loyal Jew and a law-abiding subject.

Acts 25:9

9But Festus, desiring to gain favor with the Jews, answered Paul and said, “Are you willing to go up to Jerusalem and be judged by me there concerning these things?”

Arminian

Festus, wanting to do the Jews a favor, asks whether Paul is willing to go to Jerusalem to be tried there before him.

Reformed

The question puts Paul’s life at risk to buy political goodwill, and Paul recognizes the danger at once.

Acts 25:10

10But Paul said, “I am standing before Caesar’s judgment seat, where I ought to be tried. I have done no wrong to the Jews, as you also know very well.

Arminian

Paul replies that he is standing before Caesar’s tribunal, where he ought to be tried, and that he has done no wrong to the Jews, as Festus knows very well.

Reformed

Paul insists on the court with lawful jurisdiction. Reformed writers have long drawn from this the legitimacy of appealing to civil authority for justice.

Acts 25:11

11For if I have done wrong and have committed anything worthy of death, I don’t refuse to die; but if none of those things is true that they accuse me of, no one can give me up to them. I appeal to Caesar!”

Arminian

If he has done something deserving death, he does not refuse to die; but if the charges are empty, no one can hand him over. “I appeal to Caesar.” A citizen’s right of provocatio moved the case to the emperor, then Nero.

Reformed

The appeal becomes the means by which God brings Paul to Rome. Paul acts on his legal rights, and through that act the promise of 23:11 moves forward.

Acts 25:12

12Then Festus, when he had conferred with the council, answered, “You have appealed to Caesar. To Caesar you shall go.”

Arminian

After consulting his council, Festus answers: “You have appealed to Caesar. To Caesar you shall go.”

Reformed

A Roman governor’s formal ruling now carries the apostle toward the capital. Luke lets the official words echo God’s earlier “must.”

Acts 25:13

13Now when some days had passed, King Agrippa and Bernice arrived at Caesarea and greeted Festus.

Arminian

Some days later King Agrippa II and his sister Bernice arrive in Caesarea to welcome Festus. Agrippa ruled territories north and east of Galilee and oversaw the temple and high priestly appointments.

Reformed

Jesus had said his witnesses would stand before kings (Luke 21:12), and the Lord told Ananias that Paul would bear his name before kings (9:15).

Acts 25:14

14As he stayed there many days, Festus laid Paul’s case before the king, saying, “There is a certain man left a prisoner by Felix;

Arminian

Because they stay many days, Festus lays Paul’s case before the king. He wants an expert’s help with a puzzling prisoner.

Reformed

Agrippa, raised in Rome and familiar with Jewish affairs, was an obvious adviser.

Acts 25:15

15about whom, when I was at Jerusalem, the chief priests and the elders of the Jews informed me, asking for a sentence against him.

Arminian

Festus recounts how the chief priests and elders asked for a sentence against Paul when he was in Jerusalem.

Reformed

His retelling shows how the case looks from a Roman administrator’s point of view.

Acts 25:16

16I answered them that it is not the custom of the Romans to give up any man to destruction before the accused has met the accusers face to face and has had opportunity to make his defense concerning the matter laid against him.

Arminian

He told them that Romans do not hand anyone over before he has met his accusers face to face and had a chance to defend himself.

Reformed

Festus presents himself as a defender of due process. The principle is good even if his own motives were mixed.

Acts 25:17

17When therefore they had come together here, I didn’t delay, but on the next day sat on the judgment seat and commanded the man to be brought.

Arminian

He convened the court without delay the next day and ordered Paul brought.

Reformed

Festus emphasizes his own promptness to Agrippa.

Acts 25:18

18When the accusers stood up, they brought no charges against him of such things as I supposed;

Arminian

When the accusers stood up, they brought no charge of the evil he had expected.

Reformed

Festus assumed a serious crime and found none. The case has never matched its intensity.

Acts 25:19

19but had certain questions against him about their own religion and about one Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul affirmed to be alive.

Arminian

Instead they had points of dispute about their own religion and about a certain Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul asserted to be alive.

Reformed

A pagan governor sums up the gospel’s core claim with casual precision: the dispute is whether Jesus, who died, is alive.

Acts 25:20

20Being perplexed how to inquire concerning these things, I asked whether he was willing to go to Jerusalem and there be judged concerning these matters.

Arminian

Unsure how to investigate such questions, he asked if Paul would go to Jerusalem.

Reformed

Festus admits his confusion, though he leaves out his wish to please the Jews (25:9).

Acts 25:21

21But when Paul had appealed to be kept for the decision of the emperor, I commanded him to be kept until I could send him to Caesar.”

Arminian

When Paul appealed to be held for the emperor’s decision, Festus ordered him kept until he could send him to Caesar.

Reformed

The appeal is irreversible. The path to Rome is now fixed in law.

Acts 25:22

22Agrippa said to Festus, “I also would like to hear the man myself.” “Tomorrow,” he said, “you shall hear him.”

Arminian

Agrippa says he would like to hear the man himself. Festus answers, “Tomorrow you shall hear him.”

Reformed

The request recalls Herod Antipas’s wish to see Jesus (Luke 23:8). Luke draws the parallel between the two trials.

Acts 25:23

23So on the next day, when Agrippa and Bernice had come with great pomp, and they had entered into the place of hearing with the commanding officers and the principal men of the city, at the command of Festus, Paul was brought in.

Arminian

The next day Agrippa and Bernice enter the audience hall with great pomp, accompanied by tribunes and leading men of the city.

Reformed

Luke contrasts royal display with the chained prisoner whose message outlasts them all.

Acts 25:24

24Festus said, “King Agrippa, and all men who are here present with us, you see this man about whom all the multitude of the Jews petitioned me, both at Jerusalem and here, crying that he ought not to live any longer.

Arminian

Festus presents Paul as the man about whom the whole Jewish people petitioned, shouting that he ought not to live any longer.

Reformed

Festus exaggerates the unanimity, but the description shows how much was at stake for Paul.

Acts 25:25

25But when I found that he had committed nothing worthy of death, and as he himself appealed to the emperor, I determined to send him,

Arminian

He found that Paul had done nothing worthy of death, and since Paul appealed to the emperor, he decided to send him.

Reformed

Another Roman official declares Paul innocent. Like Pilate with Jesus, Festus finds no fault yet does not release him.

Acts 25:26

26of whom I have no certain thing to write to my lord. Therefore I have brought him out before you, and especially before you, King Agrippa, that, after examination I may have something to write.

Arminian

He has nothing definite to write to “my lord,” the emperor, so he brings Paul before them all, especially Agrippa, to have something to write after the examination.

Reformed

Sending a prisoner to Caesar without a clear charge was an embarrassment. The hearing is partly to save Festus’s face.

Acts 25:27

27For it seems to me unreasonable, in sending a prisoner, not to also specify the charges against him.”

Arminian

It seems unreasonable to send a prisoner without stating the charges against him.

Reformed

Festus’s dilemma highlights the emptiness of the case: an appeal to the emperor with nothing to report.
