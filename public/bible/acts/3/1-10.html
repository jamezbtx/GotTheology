Passage

Acts 3

Verses 1–10 · WEB

At the hour of prayer Peter and John meet a man lame from birth who begs at the Beautiful Gate of the temple. Peter has no money to give, but in the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth he tells the man to walk and lifts him up. The man’s feet are made strong at once, and he enters the temple walking, leaping, and praising God, while the people who knew him are filled with wonder.

Acts 3:1

1Peter and John were going up into the temple at the hour of prayer, the ninth hour.

Arminian

Peter and John go up to the temple at the afternoon hour of prayer. Arminian readers see the first believers still keeping the rhythms of Jewish worship as they await Israel’s response to Jesus.

Reformed

Reformed readers see continuity between the old covenant people and the church, since the apostles pray where Israel had always prayed.

Acts 3:2

2A certain man who was lame from his mother’s womb was being carried, whom they laid daily at the door of the temple which is called Beautiful, to ask gifts for the needy of those who entered into the temple.

Arminian

A man lame from birth is carried daily to the temple gate. Arminian commentators note that his condition was lifelong and well known, which makes the coming healing impossible to explain away.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see a picture of human helplessness: the man can do nothing to bring himself into the temple.

Acts 3:3

3Seeing Peter and John about to go into the temple, he asked to receive gifts for the needy.

Arminian

He asks Peter and John for money. Arminian readers see a man who expects only small help and is about to receive far more.

Reformed

Reformed readers see that he asks for less than he needs, much as sinners seek relief without knowing their deepest need.

Acts 3:4

4Peter, fastening his eyes on him, with John, said, “Look at us.”

Arminian

Peter fixes his eyes on him and says, “Look at us.” Arminian expositors see personal attention that draws the man into an encounter.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the apostles directing his attention before pointing him beyond themselves to Christ.

Acts 3:5

5He listened to them, expecting to receive something from them.

Arminian

He looks at them, expecting a gift. Arminian interpreters see a response of attention, however limited his expectation.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters see God about to act beyond anything the man had in mind.

Acts 3:6

6But Peter said, “I have no silver or gold, but what I have, that I give you. In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, get up and walk!”

Arminian

“I have no silver or gold, but what I have, that I give you.” Arminian readers see healing offered in the name of Jesus, with the authority belonging to him rather than to Peter.

Reformed

Reformed readers stress that the power lies wholly in Christ’s name. The apostles have nothing of their own to give.

Acts 3:7

7He took him by the right hand and raised him up. Immediately his feet and his ankle bones received strength.

Arminian

Peter takes his hand and raises him, and his feet and ankles grow strong. Arminian commentators note the cooperation in the scene: Peter reaches out, and the man rises.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see strength given immediately, before the man could take a step. The power to stand comes first.

Acts 3:8

8Leaping up, he stood and began to walk. He entered with them into the temple, walking, leaping, and praising God.

Arminian

He leaps up, walks, and enters the temple praising God. Arminian readers see joy pouring out of a restored life.

Reformed

Reformed readers recall Isaiah 35:6, where the lame leap like deer in the age of salvation.

Acts 3:9

9All the people saw him walking and praising God.

Arminian

All the people see him walking and praising God. Arminian expositors see a public sign that prepares the crowd for Peter’s message.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God gathering an audience for his word through his own work.

Acts 3:10

10They recognized him, that it was he who used to sit begging for gifts for the needy at the Beautiful Gate of the temple. They were filled with wonder and amazement at what had happened to him.

Arminian

They recognize him as the beggar from the Beautiful Gate and are amazed. Arminian readers note that the miracle cannot be denied by those who knew him.

Reformed

Reformed readers see wonder as the right first response, though wonder alone does not save.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
