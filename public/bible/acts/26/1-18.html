Passage

Acts 26

Verses 1–18 · WEB

Given permission to speak, Paul tells King Agrippa that he has lived as a strict Pharisee and now stands on trial for the hope promised to the fathers, asking why it should seem incredible that God raises the dead. He describes how he once fiercely persecuted the saints, then recounts the light from heaven on the Damascus road and Jesus’ commission: to be a servant and witness, sent to the Gentiles to open their eyes, turn them from darkness to light and from Satan’s power to God, so that they receive forgiveness and a place among those sanctified by faith in Jesus.

Acts 26:1

1Agrippa said to Paul, “You may speak for yourself.” Then Paul stretched out his hand, and made his defense.

Arminian

Agrippa grants permission, and Paul stretches out his hand, the orator’s gesture, and begins his defense.

Reformed

This is the third account of Paul’s conversion in Acts and the most fully shaped as a courtroom speech.

Acts 26:2

2“I think myself happy, King Agrippa, that I am to make my defense before you today concerning all the things the Jews accuse me of,

Arminian

Paul counts himself fortunate to make his defense before Agrippa against all the Jewish accusations.

Reformed

His courtesy is sincere: here at last is a judge who understands the questions involved.

Acts 26:3

3especially because you are expert in all customs and questions which are among the Jews. Therefore I beg you to hear me patiently.

Arminian

Agrippa is an expert in Jewish customs and controversies, so Paul asks him to listen patiently.

Reformed

Paul tailors his argument to an informed hearer, appealing to Scripture and Jewish hope rather than general philosophy.

Acts 26:4

4“Indeed, all the Jews know my way of life from my youth up, which was from the beginning among my own nation and at Jerusalem;

Arminian

All the Jews know how he lived from his youth among his own nation and in Jerusalem.

Reformed

His early life was public and conventional, which makes his change all the more striking.

Acts 26:5

5having known me from the first, if they are willing to testify, that after the strictest sect of our religion I lived a Pharisee.

Arminian

They have known for a long time, if they would testify, that he lived as a Pharisee, according to the strictest party of their religion.

Reformed

Paul’s religious credentials were impeccable. Reformed writers note that this strictness did not save him; grace did.

Acts 26:6

6Now I stand here to be judged for the hope of the promise made by God to our fathers,

Arminian

Now he stands on trial because of his hope in the promise God made to their fathers.

Reformed

Paul frames the gospel as the fulfillment of Israel’s own promise, not a rival to it.

Acts 26:7

7which our twelve tribes, earnestly serving night and day, hope to attain. Concerning this hope I am accused by the Jews, King Agrippa!

Arminian

The twelve tribes, worshiping earnestly night and day, hope to attain this promise, and for this hope he is accused, “O king.”

Reformed

The irony is sharp: Paul is accused by Jews for believing what Israel itself hopes for.

Acts 26:8

8Why is it judged incredible with you if God does raise the dead?

Arminian

“Why is it judged incredible with you if God raises the dead?” Paul turns from defense to direct appeal, addressing everyone present.

Reformed

If God is the Creator, resurrection is not beyond him. The question answers both Sadducean doubt and Greek skepticism.

Acts 26:9

9“I myself most certainly thought that I ought to do many things contrary to the name of Jesus of Nazareth.

Arminian

Paul once thought he ought to do many things against the name of Jesus of Nazareth. His opposition was a matter of conviction, not mere malice.

Reformed

Sincere conviction can be sincerely wrong. Paul’s testimony is a warning against trusting zeal without truth.

Acts 26:10

10I also did this in Jerusalem. I both shut up many of the saints in prisons, having received authority from the chief priests; and when they were put to death I gave my vote against them.

Arminian

He imprisoned many saints with authority from the chief priests and cast his vote against them when they were put to death.

Reformed

The phrase “I cast my vote” may be literal or figurative; either way Paul owns his share in the deaths of believers.

Acts 26:11

11Punishing them often in all the synagogues, I tried to make them blaspheme. Being exceedingly enraged against them, I persecuted them even to foreign cities.

Arminian

He punished them often in all the synagogues, tried to make them blaspheme, and, raging against them, pursued them even to foreign cities.

Reformed

The confession heightens the mercy that follows. Paul never minimizes what he was.

Acts 26:12

12“Whereupon as I traveled to Damascus with the authority and commission from the chief priests,

Arminian

On one such mission to Damascus he traveled with authority and commission from the chief priests.

Reformed

He was acting as an official agent of the very council now accusing him.

Acts 26:13

13at noon, O king, I saw on the way a light from the sky, brighter than the sun, shining around me and those who traveled with me.

Arminian

At midday, “O king,” he saw a light from heaven brighter than the sun, shining around him and his companions.

Reformed

Each telling heightens the light. Here it surpasses the noonday sun, a sign of divine glory.

Acts 26:14

14When we had all fallen to the earth, I heard a voice saying to me in the Hebrew language, ‘Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me? It is hard for you to kick against the goads.’

Arminian

All fell to the ground, and he heard a voice in Hebrew: “Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me? It is hard for you to kick against the goads.” Arminian readers often take the proverb to mean that Saul had been resisting God’s promptings, a resistance that was painful but not yet final.

Reformed

Reformed readers see the goads as God’s pressure on Saul’s conscience, which he could not ultimately escape. The proverb describes the futility of opposing God.

Acts 26:15

15“I said, ‘Who are you, Lord?’ “He said, ‘I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.

Arminian

“Who are you, Lord?” “I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.”

Reformed

The same exchange in all three accounts is the fixed center of Paul’s testimony.

Acts 26:16

16But arise, and stand on your feet, for I have appeared to you for this purpose: to appoint you a servant and a witness both of the things which you have seen and of the things which I will reveal to you;

Arminian

“Get up and stand on your feet, for I have appeared to you to appoint you a servant and a witness.” The language recalls the commissioning of Ezekiel (Ezekiel 2:1).

Reformed

This version compresses Ananias’s message into Jesus’ own words. The appointment comes directly from the risen Lord.

Acts 26:17

17delivering you from the people and from the Gentiles, to whom I send you,

Arminian

Jesus promises to rescue Paul from his own people and from the Gentiles, to whom he is sending him.

Reformed

The promise of rescue stands behind every escape Luke has narrated, including this very hearing.

Acts 26:18

18to open their eyes, that they may turn from darkness to light and from the power of Satan to God, that they may receive remission of sins and an inheritance among those who are sanctified by faith in me.’

Arminian

Paul is sent to open their eyes, to turn them from darkness to light and from Satan’s power to God, so they may receive forgiveness and a share among those sanctified by faith in Jesus. Arminian theologians read the opening of eyes as the work of preaching and grace that enables people to turn, while the turning remains their own act.

Reformed

Reformed theologians point to the language of Isaiah 42:7 and see the preached word as God’s instrument for giving sight to the blind. They note that the inheritance belongs to those “sanctified by faith in me,” tying the whole process to union with Christ.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
