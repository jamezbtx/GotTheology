Passage

Acts 26

Verses 19–32 · WEB

Paul says he was not disobedient to the heavenly vision but preached in Damascus, Jerusalem, Judea, and to the Gentiles that they should repent, turn to God, and do works worthy of repentance. For this he was seized, but with God’s help he stands testifying only what the prophets and Moses said: that the Christ would suffer, rise first from the dead, and proclaim light to Jews and Gentiles. Festus shouts that Paul is out of his mind, but Paul appeals to Agrippa, who asks whether Paul hopes to make him a Christian so quickly. Paul answers that he prays all his hearers would become as he is, except for the chains. The officials agree Paul could have been released had he not appealed to Caesar.

Acts 26:19

19“Therefore, King Agrippa, I was not disobedient to the heavenly vision,

Arminian

“So, King Agrippa, I was not disobedient to the heavenly vision.” Paul presents his ministry as simple obedience.

Reformed

Both traditions see here the right response to God’s call. Reformed writers add that the vision itself produced the obedience it required.

Acts 26:20

20but declared first to them of Damascus, at Jerusalem, and throughout all the country of Judea, and also to the Gentiles, that they should repent and turn to God, doing works worthy of repentance.

Arminian

He preached first in Damascus, then Jerusalem, throughout Judea, and to the Gentiles, that they should repent, turn to God, and perform deeds worthy of their repentance. The order follows the outline of 1:8.

Reformed

Repentance and works are linked as root and fruit. Reformed theology insists the deeds demonstrate repentance rather than earn forgiveness, a point Arminian theology also affirms.

Acts 26:21

21For this reason the Jews seized me in the temple and tried to kill me.

Arminian

For this reason the Jews seized him in the temple and tried to kill him.

Reformed

Paul names the real cause of the riot: his message to the Gentiles, not any temple crime.

Acts 26:22

22Having therefore obtained the help that is from God, I stand to this day testifying both to small and great, saying nothing but what the prophets and Moses said would happen,

Arminian

With God’s help he stands to this day, testifying to small and great, saying nothing beyond what the prophets and Moses said would happen.

Reformed

Paul attributes his survival to God’s help, the fulfillment of the promise in verse 17.

Acts 26:23

23how the Christ must suffer, and how, by the resurrection of the dead, he would be first to proclaim light both to these people and to the Gentiles.”

Arminian

The Christ must suffer, and by rising first from the dead he would proclaim light both to the people and to the Gentiles.

Reformed

Christ is “first” to rise, the firstfruits of the resurrection Paul describes in 1 Corinthians 15:20. Light to the Gentiles echoes Isaiah 49:6.

Acts 26:24

24As he thus made his defense, Festus said with a loud voice, “Paul, you are crazy! Your great learning is driving you insane!”

Arminian

Festus interrupts loudly: “Paul, you are out of your mind! Your great learning is driving you mad.” To a Roman, resurrection sounded like lunacy.

Reformed

The outburst illustrates Paul’s own words that the cross is foolishness to Gentiles (1 Corinthians 1:23).

Acts 26:25

25But he said, “I am not crazy, most excellent Festus, but boldly declare words of truth and reasonableness.

Arminian

Paul answers that he is not mad, “most excellent Festus,” but speaks words of truth and soberness.

Reformed

Paul refuses the dichotomy between faith and reason. The gospel is sober truth about public events.

Acts 26:26

26For the king knows of these things, to whom also I speak freely. For I am persuaded that none of these things is hidden from him, for this has not been done in a corner.

Arminian

The king knows about these things, and Paul speaks freely to him, confident that none of this has escaped his notice, “for this has not been done in a corner.”

Reformed

The Christian claim concerns events done in public view, open to investigation by anyone who cares to look.

Acts 26:27

27King Agrippa, do you believe the prophets? I know that you believe.”

Arminian

“King Agrippa, do you believe the prophets? I know that you believe.” Paul presses for a personal response.

Reformed

The question traps Agrippa: denying the prophets would offend his Jewish subjects, but affirming them leads straight to Paul’s conclusion.

Acts 26:28

28Agrippa said to Paul, “With a little persuasion are you trying to make me a Christian?”

Arminian

Agrippa replies, “With a little persuasion are you trying to make me a Christian?” His words may be sarcastic, evasive, or half-serious. Arminian preachers have long used the verse to warn against coming close to faith and stopping short.

Reformed

Reformed preachers make a parallel point: intellectual conviction falls short of saving faith without the Spirit’s inward work. Agrippa feels the force of the argument and still deflects it.

Acts 26:29

29Paul said, “I pray to God, that whether with little or with much, not only you, but also all that hear me today, might become such as I am, except for these bonds.”

Arminian

Paul answers that he prays to God that, with little or much, not only Agrippa but all who hear him today would become as he is, except for these chains. Arminian readers find here the evangelist’s desire for every hearer’s salvation.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that Paul prays to God for their conversion, recognizing that only God can bring it about, and still pleads with all.

Acts 26:30

30The king rose up with the governor and Bernice, and those who sat with them.

Arminian

The king rises, and with him the governor, Bernice, and those seated with them.

Reformed

The hearing ends at the king’s initiative. The gospel has been laid before the highest audience Paul has faced so far.

Acts 26:31

31When they had withdrawn, they spoke to one another, saying, “This man does nothing worthy of death or of bonds.”

Arminian

As they leave, they agree that Paul is doing nothing worthy of death or imprisonment.

Reformed

Another official verdict of innocence, now including a Jewish king.

Acts 26:32

32Agrippa said to Festus, “This man might have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar.”

Arminian

Agrippa says to Festus that the man could have been released had he not appealed to Caesar.

Reformed

Humanly, the appeal now seems to have trapped Paul. In Luke’s story it is the very means by which God keeps his promise to bring Paul to Rome.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
