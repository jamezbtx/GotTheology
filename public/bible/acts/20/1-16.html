Passage

Acts 20

Verses 1–16 · WEB

After the uproar Paul encourages the disciples and travels through Macedonia to Greece, where he spends three months before a plot changes his route. Companions from several churches travel with him. At Troas, on the first day of the week, the believers gather to break bread, and Paul talks until midnight; a young man named Eutychus falls asleep, drops from a third-story window, and is taken up dead, but Paul embraces him and he is restored to life. Paul then hurries along the coast, sailing past Ephesus to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost.

Acts 20:1

1After the uproar had ceased, Paul sent for the disciples, took leave of them, and departed to go into Macedonia.

Arminian

Paul encourages the disciples and leaves for Macedonia. Arminian readers see care for believers before departing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see pastoral encouragement as a means of grace.

Acts 20:2

2When he had gone through those parts and had encouraged them with many words, he came into Greece.

Arminian

He travels through the region encouraging them and comes to Greece. Arminian commentators see a ministry of strengthening.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see the churches established in the faith.

Acts 20:3

3When he had spent three months there, and a plot was made against him by Jews as he was about to set sail for Syria, he determined to return through Macedonia.

Arminian

After three months a plot changes his route. Arminian readers see wisdom adjusting plans.

Reformed

Reformed readers see providence redirecting Paul.

Acts 20:4

4These accompanied him as far as Asia: Sopater of Beroea, Aristarchus and Secundus of the Thessalonians, Gaius of Derbe, Timothy, and Tychicus and Trophimus of Asia.

Arminian

Companions from Berea, Thessalonica, Derbe, and Asia go with him. Arminian expositors see representatives of the churches, likely carrying the collection.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the unity of Gentile churches in caring for Jerusalem.

Acts 20:5

5But these had gone ahead, and were waiting for us at Troas.

Arminian

They go ahead and wait at Troas. Arminian readers see coordinated travel.

Reformed

Reformed readers note the return of the “we” narration.

Acts 20:6

6We sailed away from Philippi after the days of Unleavened Bread, and came to them at Troas in five days, where we stayed seven days.

Arminian

They sail from Philippi after Unleavened Bread and reach Troas. Arminian commentators see Paul still marking Jewish feasts.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see time ordered by God’s calendar.

Acts 20:7

7On the first day of the week, when the disciples were gathered together to break bread, Paul talked with them, intending to depart on the next day; and continued his speech until midnight.

Arminian

On the first day of the week they gather to break bread, and Paul talks until midnight. Arminian readers see early Sunday worship.

Reformed

Reformed readers see word and sacrament together on the Lord’s Day.

Acts 20:8

8There were many lights in the upper room where we were gathered together.

Arminian

There are many lamps in the upper room. Arminian expositors see an eyewitness detail explaining the heat and drowsiness.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see the scene vividly remembered.

Acts 20:9

9A certain young man named Eutychus sat in the window, weighed down with deep sleep. As Paul spoke still longer, being weighed down by his sleep, he fell down from the third floor and was taken up dead.

Arminian

Eutychus falls asleep, falls from the third story, and is taken up dead. Arminian readers see a tragic accident.

Reformed

Reformed readers see God allowing a crisis to display his power.

Acts 20:10

10Paul went down and fell upon him, and embracing him said, “Don’t be troubled, for his life is in him.”

Arminian

Paul embraces him and says, “His life is in him.” Arminian commentators see Paul acting like Elijah and Elisha.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see God restoring life through his servant.

Acts 20:11

11When he had gone up, had broken bread and eaten, and had talked with them a long while, even until break of day, he departed.

Arminian

Paul goes back up, breaks bread, eats, and talks until dawn. Arminian readers see fellowship continuing.

Reformed

Reformed readers see ordinary worship resuming after the miracle.

Acts 20:12

12They brought the boy in alive, and were greatly comforted.

Arminian

They bring the boy alive and are comforted. Arminian expositors see comfort for the church.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see God’s mercy to the young.

Acts 20:13

13But we, going ahead to the ship, set sail for Assos, intending to take Paul aboard there; for he had so arranged, intending himself to go by land.

Arminian

They sail to Assos while Paul goes on foot. Arminian readers see Paul choosing a solitary walk.

Reformed

Reformed readers see detailed travel narrative.

Acts 20:14

14When he met us at Assos, we took him aboard and came to Mitylene.

Arminian

They meet at Assos and go to Mitylene. Arminian commentators see the journey continuing.

Reformed

Reformed commentators see steady progress toward Jerusalem.

Acts 20:15

15Sailing from there, we came the following day opposite Chios. The next day we touched at Samos and stayed at Trogyllium, and the day after we came to Miletus.

Arminian

They pass Chios, Samos, and reach Miletus. Arminian readers see the eyewitness log.

Reformed

Reformed readers see historical precision.

Acts 20:16

16For Paul had determined to sail past Ephesus, that he might not have to spend time in Asia; for he was hastening, if it were possible for him, to be in Jerusalem on the day of Pentecost.

Arminian

Paul sails past Ephesus, hurrying to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost. Arminian expositors see urgency.

Reformed

Reformed expositors see Paul pressing toward the suffering foretold.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
