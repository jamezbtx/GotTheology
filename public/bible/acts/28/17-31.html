Passage

Acts 28

Verses 17–31 · WEB

Paul calls the Jewish leaders in Rome and explains that he is in chains for the hope of Israel, though he has done nothing against his people. They have heard nothing against him but want to hear about this sect that is spoken against everywhere. On an appointed day he explains from morning to evening about the kingdom of God, persuading them about Jesus from the Law and the Prophets. Some are persuaded and others disbelieve. As they leave, Paul quotes Isaiah’s words about a people who hear but do not understand and declares that God’s salvation has been sent to the Gentiles, who will listen. For two years Paul lives in his own rented house, welcoming all and proclaiming the kingdom and the Lord Jesus with boldness, without hindrance.

Acts 28:17

17After three days Paul called together those who were the leaders of the Jews. When they had come together, he said to them, “I, brothers, though I had done nothing against the people or the customs of our fathers, still was delivered prisoner from Jerusalem into the hands of the Romans,

Arminian

After three days Paul calls together the local Jewish leaders and explains that, though he did nothing against the people or the ancestral customs, he was handed over as a prisoner from Jerusalem to the Romans.

Reformed

Even in Rome, Paul goes to the Jews first, maintaining the pattern of his whole ministry (Romans 1:16).

Acts 28:18

18who, when they had examined me, desired to set me free, because there was no cause of death in me.

Arminian

The Romans examined him and wanted to release him because there was no ground for the death penalty.

Reformed

Paul summarizes the verdicts Luke has recorded: Lysias, Felix in effect, Festus, and Agrippa all found no capital crime.

Acts 28:19

19But when the Jews spoke against it, I was constrained to appeal to Caesar, not that I had anything about which to accuse my nation.

Arminian

When the Jews objected, he was forced to appeal to Caesar, though he has no charge to bring against his own nation.

Reformed

Paul takes care not to come as Israel’s accuser. His appeal was defensive, not an attack.

Acts 28:20

20For this cause therefore I asked to see you and to speak with you. For because of the hope of Israel I am bound with this chain.”

Arminian

He is wearing this chain because of the hope of Israel.

Reformed

The phrase sums up Paul’s whole defense: his message is Israel’s own hope fulfilled in the risen Messiah.

Acts 28:21

21They said to him, “We neither received letters from Judea concerning you, nor did any of the brothers come here and report or speak any evil of you.

Arminian

The leaders say they have received no letters from Judea about him, and no one has reported anything bad.

Reformed

The silence may mean the accusers gave up the case, which would help explain Paul’s later release that many scholars infer from the Pastoral Letters.

Acts 28:22

22But we desire to hear from you what you think. For, as concerning this sect, it is known to us that everywhere it is spoken against.”

Arminian

They want to hear his views, since they know this sect is spoken against everywhere.

Reformed

Their phrase echoes Simeon’s word that the child would be a sign spoken against (Luke 2:34).

Acts 28:23

23When they had appointed him a day, many people came to him at his lodging. He explained to them, testifying about God’s Kingdom, and persuading them concerning Jesus, both from the law of Moses and from the prophets, from morning until evening.

Arminian

On an appointed day many come to his lodging, and from morning until evening he explains the kingdom of God and tries to persuade them about Jesus from the Law of Moses and the Prophets.

Reformed

Paul’s method is the one Jesus used on the Emmaus road (Luke 24:27): reading the whole of Scripture as pointing to the Messiah.

Acts 28:24

24Some believed the things which were spoken, and some disbelieved.

Arminian

Some are persuaded by what he says, while others refuse to believe. Arminian interpreters see the same message meeting different responses that each hearer is responsible for.

Reformed

Reformed interpreters see the gospel dividing its hearers. Faith is given to some and others remain in unbelief, as Jesus taught in the parable of the sower.

Acts 28:25

25When they didn’t agree among themselves, they departed after Paul had spoken one message: “The Holy Spirit spoke rightly through Isaiah the prophet to our fathers,

Arminian

As they leave, disagreeing among themselves, Paul adds one statement: the Holy Spirit spoke rightly through Isaiah the prophet to their fathers.

Reformed

Paul attributes Isaiah’s words directly to the Holy Spirit, a clear statement of the Spirit speaking through Scripture.

Acts 28:26

26saying, ‘Go to this people and say, in hearing, you will hear, but will in no way understand. In seeing, you will see, but will in no way perceive.

Arminian

He quotes Isaiah 6:9: “Go to this people and say, ‘You will hear, but will not understand; you will see, but will not perceive.’” Jesus cited the same words about his parables (Matthew 13:14–15), and John applied them to Israel’s unbelief (John 12:40).

Reformed

This text appears at three key points of rejection in the New Testament: Jesus’ parables, the end of his public ministry, and here at the end of Acts.

Acts 28:27

27For this people’s heart has grown callous. Their ears are dull of hearing. Their eyes they have closed. Lest they should see with their eyes, hear with their ears, understand with their heart, and would turn again, then I would heal them.’

Arminian

“This people’s heart has grown callous, their ears are dull, and they have closed their eyes, lest they should see, hear, understand, turn again, and be healed.” Arminian readers stress that the people closed their own eyes; the hardening follows and confirms a refusal they chose.

Reformed

Reformed readers note that in Isaiah 6 the prophet is sent to make the heart dull, so the hardening is both human refusal and God’s judicial act. They connect this with Paul’s treatment of Israel’s partial hardening in Romans 11:7–10, 25.

Acts 28:28

28“Be it known therefore to you that the salvation of God is sent to the nations, and they will listen.”

Arminian

“Therefore know that this salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles, and they will listen.” Arminian theologians read this as the gospel going out to all nations, with no group excluded from the offer.

Reformed

Reformed theologians see the same pattern Paul describes in Romans 11: Israel’s partial hardening opens the way for the fullness of the Gentiles, all within God’s saving purpose.

Acts 28:29

29When he had said these words, the Jews departed, having a great dispute among themselves.

Arminian

When he has said this, the Jews leave, debating vigorously among themselves.

Reformed

The debate does not end; Luke leaves it open, as the question of Israel and the gospel remains open in Romans 9–11.

Acts 28:30

30Paul stayed two whole years in his own rented house and received all who were coming to him,

Arminian

Paul stays two full years in his own rented quarters and welcomes all who come to him.

Reformed

Two years of house arrest become two years of open ministry. Philippians, Colossians, Ephesians, and Philemon may come from this period.

Acts 28:31

31preaching God’s Kingdom and teaching the things concerning the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness, without hindrance.

Arminian

He proclaims the kingdom of God and teaches about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness, unhindered. The last word of Acts in Greek is akōlutōs, “without hindrance.”

Reformed

Luke ends not with Paul’s trial or death but with the word going out freely from the heart of the empire. The book that began in Jerusalem closes in Rome with the message unchained.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
