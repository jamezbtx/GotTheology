Passage

Acts 28

Verses 1–16 · WEB

The survivors find themselves on Malta, where the islanders show unusual kindness and build a fire. A viper fastens on Paul’s hand, and the islanders think he is a murderer pursued by justice, but when he shakes it off unharmed, they decide he is a god. Paul heals the father of Publius, the chief official, and many other sick islanders, and they honor the travelers and supply their needs. After three months they sail on to Italy, believers from Rome come out to meet them, and Paul thanks God and takes courage. In Rome he is allowed to live by himself with a soldier guarding him.

Acts 28:1

1When we had escaped, then they learned that the island was called Malta.

Arminian

Once safe, they learn the island is Malta, called Melita in the text.

Reformed

Malta lay on the grain route to Italy, so a ship bound for Rome would eventually call there.

Acts 28:2

2The natives showed us uncommon kindness; for they kindled a fire and received us all, because of the present rain and because of the cold.

Arminian

The islanders, whom Luke calls barbarians simply because they did not speak Greek, show unusual kindness and build a fire against the rain and cold.

Reformed

Kindness from strangers outside the faith is a recurring note in Acts. Reformed theology calls this common grace, God’s goodness working through people who do not yet know him.

Acts 28:3

3But when Paul had gathered a bundle of sticks and laid them on the fire, a viper came out because of the heat and fastened on his hand.

Arminian

Paul gathers a bundle of sticks and lays them on the fire, and a viper driven out by the heat fastens on his hand.

Reformed

The apostle is collecting firewood with everyone else. Luke shows him serving, not being served.

Acts 28:4

4When the natives saw the creature hanging from his hand, they said to one another, “No doubt this man is a murderer, whom, though he has escaped from the sea, yet Justice has not allowed to live.”

Arminian

The islanders say he must be a murderer whom Justice, the goddess Dike, will not allow to live even though he escaped the sea.

Reformed

Pagan conscience assumes a moral order in which guilt catches up with the guilty.

Acts 28:5

5However he shook off the creature into the fire, and wasn’t harmed.

Arminian

Paul shakes the creature into the fire and suffers no harm, as Jesus promised his messengers in Luke 10:19.

Reformed

God’s protection continues on land as it did at sea. The promise of 23:11 still governs events.

Acts 28:6

6But they expected that he would have swollen or fallen down dead suddenly, but when they watched for a long time and saw nothing bad happen to him, they changed their minds and said that he was a god.

Arminian

They wait for him to swell up or drop dead, and when nothing happens, they change their minds and say he is a god.

Reformed

The same crowd moves from murderer to god in moments, much as the Lystrans did in 14:11–19.

Acts 28:7

7Now in the neighborhood of that place were lands belonging to the chief man of the island, named Publius, who received us and courteously entertained us for three days.

Arminian

Nearby are estates belonging to Publius, the island’s chief official, who welcomes them and entertains them hospitably for three days.

Reformed

Inscriptions confirm that “first man” was the official title of Malta’s leading magistrate.

Acts 28:8

8The father of Publius lay sick of fever and dysentery. Paul entered in to him, prayed, and laying his hands on him, healed him.

Arminian

Publius’s father lies sick with fever and dysentery. Paul visits, prays, lays hands on him, and heals him.

Reformed

Luke, a physician, uses precise medical terms. Paul prays before laying on hands, making clear the healing comes from God.

Acts 28:9

9Then when this was done, the rest also who had diseases in the island came and were cured.

Arminian

After this, others on the island who have diseases come and are cured.

Reformed

Luke records no preaching here, though the healings in Jesus’ name surely carried the message.

Acts 28:10

10They also honored us with many honors; and when we sailed, they put on board the things that we needed.

Arminian

They honor the travelers in many ways and, when it is time to sail, supply what they need.

Reformed

God provides for the shipwrecked company through the gratitude of the islanders.

Acts 28:11

11After three months, we set sail in a ship of Alexandria which had wintered in the island, whose figurehead was “The Twin Brothers.”

Arminian

After three months they sail on an Alexandrian ship that had wintered at the island, with the Twin Brothers, Castor and Pollux, as its figurehead.

Reformed

The pagan sailors’ patron gods ride on the prow, while the God who actually kept them safe is the one Paul serves.

Acts 28:12

12Touching at Syracuse, we stayed there three days.

Arminian

They put in at Syracuse in Sicily and stay three days.

Reformed

The voyage now follows the regular grain route toward Italy.

Acts 28:13

13From there we circled around and arrived at Rhegium. After one day, a south wind sprang up, and on the second day we came to Puteoli,

Arminian

From there they sail around to Rhegium, at the toe of Italy, and with a south wind reach Puteoli in two days.

Reformed

Puteoli, near Naples, was the main port for grain ships before Ostia was expanded.

Acts 28:14

14where we found brothers, and were entreated to stay with them for seven days. So we came to Rome.

Arminian

At Puteoli they find believers and are invited to stay seven days. “And so we came to Rome.”

Reformed

The gospel had already reached Italy before Paul. He arrives to find brothers waiting.

Acts 28:15

15From there the brothers, when they heard of us, came to meet us as far as The Market of Appius and The Three Taverns. When Paul saw them, he thanked God and took courage.

Arminian

Believers from Rome come as far as the Forum of Appius and Three Taverns, about forty and thirty miles out, to meet them. Seeing them, Paul thanks God and takes courage.

Reformed

Paul had written to these believers years earlier (Romans 1:11–12). The encouragement he hoped to give, he first receives.

Acts 28:16

16When we entered into Rome, the centurion delivered the prisoners to the captain of the guard, but Paul was allowed to stay by himself with the soldier who guarded him.

Arminian

In Rome Paul is allowed to live by himself with a soldier guarding him.

Reformed

God’s word in 23:11 is fulfilled. The prisoner has reached the capital exactly as promised.

About these notes. Commentary here is pastoral and educational. It is not a substitute for reading primary sources, confessions, or trusted teachers in either tradition. Both Arminian and Reformed families contain internal diversity (Wesleyan, classical Remonstrant, confessional Calvinist, and more). GotTheology aims for fair representation—not a final verdict.
